Your instruction gets 200,000 compute units by default.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" />

By Anchor's own published figures, loading a single 10 KB account with `Account<'info, T>` costs
about 50,000 of them — a quarter of the budget spent before your logic has run, on the act of copying
bytes out of a buffer into a struct.<Cite src="anchor-docs-zero-copy" />

That is what this lesson is about: the framework's convenience is code, code costs compute, and
compute is a hard per-instruction ceiling rather than a bill you settle afterwards. Then the two
things you do about it — an escape hatch for the accounts that dominate the cost, and a test setup
fast enough that measuring becomes routine instead of an event.

## The unit is compute units, and no EVM number transfers

State this once, bluntly, because it is the single most common import error from the EVM track.
Solana meters **compute units**. CU are not gas, are not convertible to gas, and have no shared cost
table. Every constant you carry from the EVM — 2,100 for a cold `SLOAD`, 2,600 for a cold account
access, 20,000 for a zero-to-nonzero `SSTORE` — describes a different instruction set with a different
pricing model, and none of them tells you anything about a Solana program. Do not port them, do not
analogise from them, and be suspicious of any comparison table that puts the two in the same column.

The second row is the one that changes how you design. **1.4 million CU is a wall, not a bill.** An
EVM transaction that needs more gas gets more gas if the sender will pay for it, up to the block
limit. A Solana transaction that needs more than 1.4 million CU does not exist — it must be split
into several transactions, which means the atomicity you were relying on has to be re-established by
your own program state. "This operation is too expensive" is an architectural constraint on Solana
and a pricing one on Ethereum.

### Where Anchor's compute actually goes

Four buckets, roughly in ascending order of how much they matter.

**Each constraint is a comparison.** `mut`, `has_one`, `address`, `constraint = expr` — each expands
to an `if` over a few words of memory.<Cite src="anchor-account-constraints" /> Individually
negligible. Collectively a floor you pay every invocation.

**The container checks are a comparison plus a deserialization.** Owner check and discriminator check
are cheap; the Borsh decode is not, and it is the item that scales.<Cite src="anchor-docs-account-types" />

**PDA derivation is priced explicitly, and this is the one you can control.** A bare `bump` searches;
`bump = state.bump` derives once. The Solana documentation gives 1,500 CU for a single
`create_program_address` and 1,500 on entry plus 1,500 for each failed bump in the search — so a
constraint on an account whose canonical bump is a few steps down the range costs several thousand CU
more than the stored-bump form, on every call, forever.<Cite src="solana-docs-pda" />

**Serialization on exit.** An account marked `mut` is written back when the instruction
ends.<Cite src="anchor-account-constraints" /> That is a second pass over the same bytes, and it is
the half people forget when they reason about the cost of loading state.

Two of those four scale with account size, which is why the escape hatch below is about size rather
than about count.

## What you pay for the units you asked for

The fee model has a property that inverts an EVM habit, and getting it backwards is expensive at
scale.

The base fee is 5,000 lamports per signature, split 50% burned and 50% to the
validator.<Cite src="solana-docs-fees" /> The prioritization fee is
`ceil(compute_unit_price * compute_unit_limit / 1,000,000)` lamports, all of it to the
validator.<Cite src="solana-docs-fees" /> You have met that formula as
[the priority fee formula](/c/priority-fee-formula); the term to stare at is `compute_unit_limit`.

**The priority fee is determined by the compute unit limit you requested, not the units you
used.**<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" /> Leave the default 200,000 in place on an instruction
that consumes 2,153 and you have paid priority on roughly 198,000 units of nothing. There is no
refund; the EVM's "unused gas comes back" reflex is simply wrong here. The documented method is to
simulate the transaction to measure consumption and add about a ten percent safety
margin.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" />

Which makes CU measurement a *product* concern rather than an optimisation hobby. Every instruction
you ship with an unmeasured limit overpays on every call, and the overpayment scales with your
traffic and with network contention.

<Aside kind="warn">
Two more facts that bite teams arriving from the EVM. Fees are charged on failed transactions as well
as successful ones — a reverted EVM transaction also costs gas, but Solana's
[fees charged on failure](/c/fees-charged-on-failure) apply to a wider set of failure modes, so a
client that retries aggressively is paying for every attempt. And the compute-budget mechanism itself
is changing: the Solana documentation states that in the upcoming v1 transaction format,
ComputeBudget instructions are ignored for configuration and execute as no-ops that still cost 150 CU
and an instruction slot, with limits and the priority fee set directly in the message config — and
unset limits defaulting to **zero** rather than to today's
values.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" /> Treat that as scheduled, not shipped, and re-read the
page before you write anything that depends on defaults.
</Aside>

## When the cost is irrelevant, and when it is the whole argument

Be honest about the size of this, in both directions.

**For an application instruction, it does not matter.** A program whose instructions are called a few
thousand times a day pays for its constraints in an amount of SOL that does not appear on any budget
line. The compute those checks consume buys you the elimination of the two most common Solana
vulnerability classes, and that trade is not close. Optimising a well-constrained application program
for CU before you have measured anything is the Solana version of premature optimisation, with the
added downside that the things you remove are the safety checks.

**For a primitive invoked by CPI inside other people's transactions, the constant is the product.**
If your program is a leg in a route, every transaction touching that route pays your per-invocation
overhead, and it competes with your budget rather than yours. Below some threshold, integrating with
you stops being affordable. That is the argument for dropping to a lower-level framework, and it is
an argument about who bears the cost, not about elegance.

<Aside kind="note">
Circulating comparisons between Anchor and lower-level frameworks quote large percentage savings, and
the ones we could trace come from conference-talk summaries with unstated methodology and unstated
program shapes. Treat them as directional only. The number that matters is the one you measure on
your own instruction, with the harness described below, and the measurement takes minutes once the
harness exists.
</Aside>

## The escape hatch: `zero_copy` and `AccountLoader`

Traditional deserialization with `Account<'info, T>` copies data from the account into a
heap-allocated struct, and Anchor names three limits that follow: stack (4 KB) and heap (32 KB)
constrain account sizes, deserialization consumes significant compute, and the data is duplicated in
memory.<Cite src="anchor-docs-zero-copy" />

Zero-copy with `AccountLoader<'info, T>` casts the raw account bytes to the struct type instead of
copying, supporting accounts up to 10 MB (10,485,760 bytes) and a substantial reduction in CU for
large accounts.<Cite src="anchor-docs-zero-copy" />

Anchor publishes its own comparison, and it is worth reproducing with the caveat attached — these are
the documentation's figures, with methodology unstated, and your account layout is not theirs:

The shape is the message: below a kilobyte the difference is noise against a 200,000 CU budget, and
somewhere in the tens of kilobytes `Account<T>` stops being an option at all rather than merely being
slow.

**Using it.** Annotate the struct with `#[account(zero_copy)]`, which derives `Copy`, `Clone`,
`bytemuck::Zeroable`, `bytemuck::Pod` and `#[repr(C)]`, and add `bytemuck` with the
`min_const_generics` feature.<Cite src="anchor-docs-zero-copy" /> Then load through
`AccountLoader<'info, T>`, using `load_init` the first time — it also sets the account
discriminator — and `load` or `load_mut` thereafter.<Cite src="anchor-docs-zero-copy" />

**The allocation ceiling that surprises people.** The `init` constraint can allocate at most 10,240
bytes, because under the hood it CPIs to the System Program and that is the CPI
limit.<Cite src="anchor-docs-zero-copy" /> For anything larger, create the account in a separate
instruction by calling the System Program directly and use the `zero` constraint, which verifies the
account has not been initialized by checking that its discriminator has not been
set.<Cite src="anchor-docs-zero-copy" /><Cite src="anchor-account-constraints" /> With eight bytes
reserved for the discriminator, the maximum data size is 10,485,752
bytes.<Cite src="anchor-docs-zero-copy" />

**What you give up, which is the part the performance table does not show.** `#[repr(C)]` plus
`Pod` means fixed-layout, plain-old-data fields only: no `Vec`, no `String`, no `HashMap`, and no
enums with payloads. Alignment and padding become your problem, and a struct whose fields you reorder
is a struct whose on-chain bytes now mean something else. Anchor's own guidance is to use zero-copy
for accounts over roughly a kilobyte, arrays with many elements such as orderbooks and event queues,
high-frequency operations and compute-sensitive programs, and to stay on `Account<T>` for small
accounts, dynamic data structures and frequently changing
schemas.<Cite src="anchor-docs-zero-copy" />

That trade — layout obligations in exchange for not copying — is the same one the whole of the next
module makes. Anchor offers it here one account at a time, which is exactly the right way to meet it
first.

  why="Performance tables show the axis where the technique wins and never the axis where it costs, and 'zero-copy' sounds like a strictly-better version of 'copy'."

/>

<Aside kind="note">
Two cheaper steps before reaching for zero-copy. `Box<Account<'info, T>>` changes no checks at all and
moves the deserialized struct off the stack onto the heap, which resolves a stack-overflow build error
without changing your data model.<Cite src="anchor-docs-account-types" /> And Anchor carries a
`LazyAccount` type behind a `lazy-account` feature for deferring field decoding, which has continued
to receive fixes through 1.x — check its status for your version before designing around
it.<Cite src="anchor-repo-changelog" />
</Aside>

## Three places to run a test, and the one you should live in

Now the half of the lesson that changes your day.

**An in-process SVM harness.** LiteSVM creates an in-process Solana VM optimised for program
developers, and is much faster to run and compile than `solana-program-test` and
`solana-test-validator`; it is available in Rust, TypeScript/JavaScript and
Python.<Cite src="anchor-docs-litesvm" /> Programs are loaded from compiled bytes with
`add_program` or `add_program_from_file`, and you can rewrite sysvars — including the clock — to test
time-dependent logic directly.<Cite src="anchor-docs-litesvm" /> Being able to set the clock rather
than wait for it is the equivalent of Foundry's `warp` cheatcode, and it lands in the same place in
your workflow.

**A minified harness with no runtime at all.** Mollusk goes further: it does not create any semblance
of a validator runtime, but provisions a program execution pipeline directly from lower-level SVM
components — a minified program cache, transaction context and invoke context — and executes the
program's ELF through the BPF loader without AccountsDB, Bank or any other large Agave
component.<Cite src="anchor-docs-mollusk" /> The consequence is that it is exceptionally fast, and
the cost is that you must supply an explicit list of accounts because it has nowhere to load them
from.<Cite src="anchor-docs-mollusk" />

Mollusk is also where CU measurement is a first-class assertion. `process_and_validate_instruction`
takes a list of `Check`s, and `Check::compute_units(...)` is one of
them.<Cite src="anchor-docs-mollusk" /> That turns compute consumption into a regression test: a
change that adds 3,000 CU to a hot instruction fails CI rather than being discovered in a fee report.

**A validator-shaped local network.** Anchor 1.0 changed the default here — the CHANGELOG records
Surfpool being used by default for `anchor test` and `anchor localnet`, and a `litesvm` test template
added and made the default option on `anchor init`.<Cite src="anchor-repo-changelog" /> Verify both
against the version you have installed rather than inheriting them from this
page.<Cite src="anchor-changelog" />

**And mainnet itself**, which is not a test environment but is where the accounts are. Everything
your program touches that you did not create — an oracle account, a token mint with extensions, a
pool owned by somebody else — exists only there.

The ordering is fidelity against milliseconds, and the discipline is to know which one each test
needs.

**Run the fast harness constantly and reserve the realistic ones for what actually depends on real
accounts.** That sentence is the whole judgement, and it is the same one you already made in the
Foundry track: unit tests in milliseconds, [fork tests](/c/fork-testing-purpose) for the handful of
cases that need live state, in full knowledge that [fork tests are slow](/c/fork-tests-are-slow) and
that a slow suite is a suite people stop running. Solana's version of that split is sharper, because
the gap between an in-process SVM and a validator is larger than the gap between a local EVM and a
fork.

<Check type="explain" concept="anchor-test-stack">
Your team ships a Solana program used by three partner protocols via CPI. The suite is 40 tests
against a local validator, takes eleven minutes, and is run on merge only. Fees are rising and one
partner has said your instruction is "expensive to route through".

Write the plan: what you change about testing, what you measure, and how you decide whether to
optimise the program at all.

<Answer>
**First, get a number, because "expensive" is not one.** Before touching the program, measure the
instruction's CU consumption. Two routes, and use both. The cheap one is the transaction log: every
invocation prints `consumed X of Y compute units`, so a devnet or mainnet transaction already tells
you what a real call costs.<Cite src="anchor-docs-errors" /> The durable one is a Mollusk test
asserting `Check::compute_units(...)`, which turns the figure into something CI
defends.<Cite src="anchor-docs-mollusk" />

**Second, check the cheapest possible win before optimising anything: the requested limit.** The
priority fee is charged on the CU limit requested, not the CU
consumed.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" /> If your client is not setting
`SetComputeUnitLimit`, or is setting a padded number, the partner is being billed for units nobody
uses. Simulate and add roughly ten percent.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" /> This can resolve
the complaint entirely without a program change, and it should be checked first because it is a
client fix rather than a redeploy.

**Third, restructure the suite as a ladder rather than a single tier.** Move the bulk to an
in-process harness: LiteSVM for multi-instruction flows and anything needing clock manipulation,
Mollusk for per-instruction unit tests and every CU
assertion.<Cite src="anchor-docs-litesvm" /><Cite src="anchor-docs-mollusk" /> Keep on the validator
only the tests whose subject *is* validator behaviour, and add a small fork-based tier for the
partners' real accounts. Eleven minutes on merge becomes seconds on save for most of the suite, and
the reason that matters here is specific: you cannot iterate on compute cost with an eleven-minute
feedback loop, so the test restructure is a precondition for the optimisation rather than a parallel
piece of hygiene.

**Fourth, look at the cost drivers in order of size, not in order of visibility.**

*Account size and serialization.* If any instruction loads a large account with `Account<'info, T>`,
that is likely the dominant term — Anchor's own figures put a 10 KB account at roughly 50,000 CU
copied versus roughly 5,000 mapped.<Cite src="anchor-docs-zero-copy" /> Converting a hot, large,
fixed-layout account to `#[account(zero_copy)]` plus `AccountLoader` is the single biggest available
change, and it is the one with real obligations attached: no dynamic types, fixed layout, and a
migration for the existing accounts.<Cite src="anchor-docs-zero-copy" />

*PDA derivation.* Every constraint using a bare `bump` runs a search. Storing the canonical bump at
creation and switching to `bump = state.bump` replaces 1,500 CU plus 1,500 per failed bump with a
single 1,500 CU derivation, per constrained account, per
call.<Cite src="solana-docs-pda" /><Cite src="anchor-account-constraints" /> Cheap to do, no data
model change, and it also fixes the canonicalization hazard.

*Accounts marked `mut` that are not written.* Each costs a serialization pass on exit and takes an
exclusive write lock that hurts your partners' throughput as well as your
fees.<Cite src="anchor-account-constraints" />

*Constraints.* Last, and mostly do not. Individual constraint checks are small, and each one you
remove is a security check you removed. If the measured constraint overhead is the dominant term,
that is a finding about the shape of your program rather than a licence to
delete.<Cite src="anza-compute-units" />

**Fifth, decide about the framework itself, with the numbers in hand.** If after the above the
instruction is still too expensive for a CPI leg, then you have the real version of the question the
next module asks: is the remaining overhead worth what the framework generates. That decision needs a
measured before-and-after on your own program, because the public comparisons come with unstated
methodology and are directional at best.

**What you tell the partner today.** The measured CU figure, the requested limit you are now setting
and why it was wrong, and a date for the zero-copy change if the measurement supports it. Those are
three concrete things, and the first two are available this week.

**The meta-point.** Every step above depends on measurement being cheap. That is why the test-stack
change comes before the optimisation and not after it: with an eleven-minute suite the honest answer
to "should we optimise" is "we can't tell", and with a millisecond harness it is a number.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

This is the end of the module, so the payoff is a way of deciding rather than a next mechanism.

- **The next module is the same trade, taken further.** Pinocchio drops the generated entrypoint, the
  generated validation and the IDL to buy compute headroom. Everything you now know about what Anchor
  generates is the list of what you would be writing yourself, and everything you know about what it
  costs is the reason somebody would.
- **The Solana security module** is where the vulnerability classes live, and you have met most of
  them already as the shape of a check: owner, discriminator, signer, seeds, canonical bump,
  re-initialization, revival. Arriving there having seen where each one comes from in the framework
  makes it a review checklist instead of a list to memorise.<Cite src="anchor-docs" />
- **The habit worth keeping** is the one this lesson is built around. Measure before you optimise,
  and make measuring cheap enough that "measure first" is not an act of discipline. A Mollusk test
  with a `compute_units` check costs you an afternoon once and answers the question forever
  after.<Cite src="anchor-docs-mollusk" />

And the sentence to carry out of the module as a whole. Anchor is the parts of a Solidity compiler
that Solana does not have, re-implemented as macros that emit runtime checks — which means it can only
enforce the invariants you thought to declare, it charges you for each one on every call, and the
guarantee you did not write down is not enforced by anything at all. Reading an `Accounts` struct as
a list of claims, and asking what is missing from it, is the skill this module exists to build.
