The previous lesson made IBC sound like something every chain should obviously adopt. So here is the
question that follows: if the design is that good, why did it spend most of a decade being a thing
Cosmos chains do with other Cosmos chains?

Part of the answer is integration cost, and it was structural rather than incidental. IBC Classic put
a multi-step negotiation in front of every pair of chains and every application on top of them:
connections, channels, a port router, and handshakes to establish each.<Cite src="ibc-go-docs" /> Each
new counterparty was a project. Each new application on an existing counterparty was another one.

IBC v2 — the work also carried under the name Eureka — is the attempt to remove that cost without
touching the trust model. This lesson is about what it changed, what it deliberately did not, and
where the honest edge of current knowledge sits, because this is the most actively moving material in
the whole track.

## What v2 removed, from the specification

The specification directory for IBC v2 states the compression directly: it compresses "only the
necessary aspects of connection and channel layers into a single packet handler with no
handshakes".<Cite src="ibc-v2-spec" />

Four concrete changes fall out of that, and each has a consequence you can feel.

**The connection layer becomes a registration.** A `RegisterCounterparty` message "associate[s] the
counterparty client of our chain with our client of the counterparty", replacing the connection
layer.<Cite src="ibc-v2-spec" /> Instead of a handshake dance, each side records which client on the
other side corresponds to it.

**Channel negotiation becomes per-packet metadata.** In v2, "each packet carries the information about
how its individual data should be processed", including version and encoding — information that "was
negotiated in the channel handshake" in the previous
model.<Cite src="ibc-v2-spec" /> The negotiation did not disappear; it moved onto the wire, where it
is decided per message rather than per relationship.

**Routing becomes dynamic.** "A sending application can route its packet to ANY OTHER application on
the receiving application by simply specifying its portID", rather than through a pre-negotiated
channel.<Cite src="ibc-v2-spec" />

**Sending becomes optimistic.** With no handshake, packet sending is "asynchronous and optimistic",
with immediate error handling if incompatible versions are received.<Cite src="ibc-v2-spec" />

The reference implementation's documentation describes the same shift architecturally: v2 "reduces
architectural complexity" down to three main components — clients, a router, and applications —
where IBC Classic relied on connections, channels, and the port
router.<Cite src="ibc-go-docs" />

<Aside kind="warn">
**The fourth change moves a security check onto you.** Because routing is dynamic, the specification
states that applications must validate source and destination port IDs
themselves.<Cite src="ibc-v2-spec" /> Under Classic, "which application on which chain am I talking
to" was fixed when the channel was established; under v2 it is an assertion carried in each packet
that your application code is responsible for checking.

That is a new bug class, and it should feel extremely familiar: it is the same shape as validating an
account's owner on Solana, or checking `msg.sender` rather than trusting a passed-in address. Removing
a handshake removed a place where the protocol was doing a check on your behalf. If you write an IBC
v2 application, the port-ID validation is not optional and no framework will notice its absence for
you.
</Aside>

## What did not change, which is the reason any of it matters

The trust anchor is exactly where it was. Clients verify the counterparty's state; a misbehaviour
proof freezes a client; relayers carry packets with proofs and cannot forge
them.<Cite src="ics-002-client-semantics" /><Cite src="ibc-go-docs" /> Everything the previous lesson
established survives intact.

One protocol detail did change in a way that tells you exactly what v2 is for. IBC v2 packets carry
"a single Unix timestamp timeout (in seconds)",<Cite src="ibc-go-docs" /> and the stated reason is
that "block heights are chain-specific and don't translate across heterogeneous chains like
Ethereum".<Cite src="ibc-go-docs" />

Read that as a statement of intent. Height-based timeouts are fine between two CometBFT chains with
similar block times. Timestamp-based timeouts are what you need when the counterparty might be
something with an entirely different notion of a block. The v2 design was made for a world where the
other end is not a Cosmos chain.

## Two lines at once, and how to know which one you are talking to

The transition is not a cutover. Both models coexist in the reference implementation: ibc-go v10
carries IBC Classic and IBC v2 together, and "a connection uses either IBC classic or IBC
v2".<Cite src="ibc-go-docs" />

And the release history says the same thing more bluntly. As of 26 August 2026 the ibc-go repository
shows an active v11 line — v11.0.0 in April 2026, v11.2.0 in July 2026 — alongside a v10 line still
receiving releases, with v10.7.0 in May 2026.<Cite src="ibc-go-releases" />

**The operational consequence.** "Does this chain support IBC" is not a question with a yes/no answer
any more. The useful questions are which ibc-go line the chain runs, whether a given path is Classic
or v2, and what your application must therefore do about port validation and timeouts. Read the
releases page and the chain's own configuration; do not infer a version from a blog post's
date.<Cite src="ibc-go-releases" />

## Leaving Cosmos: what has actually been built

Here is where the strategic argument lives. The point of removing handshakes and moving to timestamp
timeouts was to make it feasible for a chain that is not a Cosmos SDK chain to speak IBC — and the
protocol was always agnostic about what a counterparty is. The client specification says so
explicitly: from IBC's perspective, "a state machine is defined entirely by its light client
validation and misbehaviour detection logic".<Cite src="ics-002-client-semantics" /> If you can write
those two functions for Ethereum, Ethereum is an IBC counterparty.

The 2026 Cosmos stack roadmap records what shipped and what is planned. IBC v2 was released in 2025,
"simplifying the protocol by removing channel and connection
handshakes".<Cite src="cosmos-stack-roadmap-2026" /> IBC Solidity and a CosmWasm Ethereum light client
were delivered, "paving the way for IBC to make its way to
Ethereum".<Cite src="cosmos-stack-roadmap-2026" /> Planned work includes "first-class IBC connectivity
to Solana" with two light clients, and support for Solana and EVM through new token and messaging
standards.<Cite src="cosmos-stack-roadmap-2026" />

The technical walkthrough fills in the mechanism, and the asymmetry in it is the most instructive
detail in this lesson. An **Ethereum light client is deployed as a CosmWasm contract on the Cosmos
Hub**, while verification of a Tendermint light client **on Ethereum** uses Succinct's SP1
zkVM.<Cite src="cosmoslabs-eureka" /> The same walkthrough describes light clients as "the gold
standard in cross-chain communication, which enables communication through trusting the underlying
blockchains", and says Go and Solidity deployments together "provide the means to bridge tokens
between Cosmos and Ethereum through the implementation of token transfer (ICS20) in each
domain".<Cite src="cosmoslabs-eureka" />

**Subgoal: understand why the two directions need different machinery.** Running an Ethereum light
client on a Cosmos chain is a normal program on cheap execution. Running a Tendermint light client on
Ethereum means verifying a large validator set's signatures inside the EVM, repeatedly, at mainnet
gas prices — which is the classic reason on-chain light clients of BFT chains were considered
impractical on Ethereum. Replacing that direct verification with a succinct proof is the standard
move from the ZK track, applied here to consensus verification.

**Subgoal: draw the general lesson.** Whether the light-client design is *available* to you is a
function of the destination chain's cost of verification, not of the source chain's honesty. Two
chains can want the same trust model and be able to afford it in only one direction. Proving systems
are what make the expensive direction payable, which is why interoperability and ZK stopped being
separate topics.

<Aside kind="stop">
**Do not treat the deployment status as settled, in either direction.** The research behind this
module could not confirm whether IBC over Eureka is fully live for **Ethereum mainnet** transfers
today. The roadmap describes IBC v2 and IBC Solidity as *enabling* Ethereum connectivity and lists
Solana and EVM support as planned work,<Cite src="cosmos-stack-roadmap-2026" /> while the technical
walkthrough describes Eureka as connecting Cosmos and Ethereum with "more rollups and domains to be
added in the future", naming Babylon and Seda among test chains and referring to future access to
rollups such as Base or Arbitrum as not yet implemented.<Cite src="cosmoslabs-eureka" /> Secondary
coverage implies it is live. These are not reconcilable from the sources available, and this lesson
will not pick one. Check the current state directly before building on it — and note that "an
implementation exists" and "a production path is live between two named chains" are different claims
that marketing routinely merges.
</Aside>

## Where this pays off, and where to go next

**The idea, stripped of the ecosystem.** Verify the counterparty's consensus on chain instead of
trusting a committee — and if the destination cannot afford that verification directly, prove it
succinctly and verify the proof instead.<Cite src="cosmoslabs-eureka" /> That sentence is the whole
transferable content of this module, and it applies to designs that have never heard of Cosmos.

**The reading habit for hot material.** Everything in this lesson is dated and much of it will move.
The durable artifacts are the specification directories and the release
pages;<Cite src="ibc-v2-spec" /><Cite src="ibc-spec" /><Cite src="ibc-go-releases" /> the roadmap is a
dated statement of intent<Cite src="cosmos-stack-roadmap-2026" /> and the walkthroughs are dated
statements of progress.<Cite src="cosmoslabs-eureka" /> When those three disagree, the specification
and the releases win, and the honest output is a range rather than a claim.

**Where to go deeper, since this was a stub.** For the protocol itself, read the ICS specifications —
ICS-002 for client semantics is the one that repays the most reading, and the IBC_V2 directory for
what changed.<Cite src="ics-002-client-semantics" /><Cite src="ibc-v2-spec" /> For implementation,
ibc-go and its release notes.<Cite src="ibc-go-releases" /> For the appchain argument itself, build
one: the shortest path to understanding what sovereignty costs is to stand up a chain with one custom
module and watch how much of your time goes to operations rather than to product.

**And the thing this whole track was for.** You came in knowing the EVM extremely well. You have now
seen a language where an asset is a type, a chain that verifies bytecode before admitting it, three
different answers to running transactions at once, and an ecosystem that argues you should not be
renting execution at all. None of those make the EVM look worse. They make its choices visible as
choices — which is the only way to know what you are actually standing on.
