In February 2022 an attacker bypassed a bridge's verification step, generated a message specifying
that 120,000 wETH be minted, and minted it — 120,000 wETH on Solana with no matching collateral, while
the escrow on Ethereum sat untouched throughout.<Cite src="certik-bridge-exploits-2022" /> Six months
later a different bridge's total value locked went from around $190 million to roughly
$12,000.<Cite src="certik-bridge-exploits-2022" />

You catalogued these in Track 10 and you drew the right conclusion: the money was lost where a small
set of parties gets to decide that a message is genuine. The [bridge is the honeypot](/c/bridge-is-the-honeypot)
because the honeypot is the verifier set.

IBC is the design that does not have one. Not a better-run committee, not a larger one, not one with
better key management — no committee. This lesson is about how that works, and, more usefully, about
the trust assumption that replaced it, because it is not zero and anyone who tells you it is has not
read the specification.

## The mechanism: each chain verifies the other's consensus itself

The core idea in one sentence from the reference implementation's documentation: "each side of the
connection uses the light client of the other chain to quickly verify incoming
messages".<Cite src="ibc-go-docs" />

The specification is more precise about what a light client *is*, and the definition is worth having
exactly. IBC calls the algorithm used to verify state updates of a remote state machine a **validity
predicate**; "pairing a validity predicate with a trusted state (i.e., a state that the verifier
assumes to be correct), implements the functionality of a *light client* ... for a remote state
machine on the host state machine".<Cite src="ics-002-client-semantics" />

Unpack that into three objects, because they are the whole protocol.

**A `ConsensusState` is "the *trusted view* of the state of a state machine at a particular
`Height`".**<Cite src="ics-002-client-semantics" /> This is what chain A stores about chain B: a
snapshot it has accepted as correct.

**A `ValidityPredicate` "validates a ClientMessage sent by a relayer in order to update the
client",** and using it "SHOULD be more computationally efficient than executing
`Consensus`".<Cite src="ics-002-client-semantics" /> This is the whole trick. Chain A does not re-run
chain B. It runs a cheap check that is sound given chain B's consensus rules — for a CometBFT chain,
checking that more than two thirds of a known validator set signed a header.

**A `ClientState` "MUST expose an interface to higher-level protocol abstractions, e.g., functions to
verify proofs of the existence of particular values at particular paths at particular
`Height`s".**<Cite src="ics-002-client-semantics" /> Once chain A has a trusted root for chain B at
some height, it can verify a Merkle proof that some specific value was in chain B's state at that
height. That is how "a token was escrowed on B" becomes a checkable fact on A.

**Subgoal: see why CometBFT makes this cheap.** A commit on a CometBFT chain is a set of precommits
from more than two thirds of voting power.<Cite src="cometbft-consensus-spec" /> Verifying it is
signature arithmetic against a validator set you already track — no re-execution, no waiting for
confirmation depth, no probabilistic reasoning. Deterministic finality is not just nice for users; it
is what makes an on-chain light client of another chain affordable in the first place. This is one of
those cases where two design decisions made years apart turn out to have been the same decision.

## Misbehaviour: what happens when the counterparty lies

Here is the part that separates IBC from "a bridge with more steps", and it comes straight out of the
fork-accountability property from the previous lesson.

Every light client also has a **misbehaviour predicate** — a function that "checks whether the rules
of `Consensus` were broken, in which case the client MUST be *frozen*, i.e., no subsequent
`ConsensusState`s can be generated".<Cite src="ics-002-client-semantics" /> The proof it consumes is
concrete: "in the case the state machine is a blockchain, a `Misbehaviour` might consist of two signed
block headers with different `CommitmentRoot`s, but the same
`Height`".<Cite src="ics-002-client-semantics" />

Two signed headers at the same height with different roots. That is exactly what CometBFT's fork
accountability lemma says a fork must produce: double-signing by at least a third of the validator
set, attributable to the specific validators who did it.<Cite src="cometbft-consensus-spec" />

**Subgoal: connect the two into one story.** A counterparty chain cannot equivocate without leaving a
signed contradiction. Anyone who has the contradiction can submit it. Submitting it freezes the
client, which stops the lying chain's messages from being believed and requires higher-level
intervention to resolve.<Cite src="ics-002-client-semantics" /> The specification names the situation
directly: two consensus states at the same height with different commitment roots is called an
"equivocation" and "MUST be classified as misbehaviour", with a proof submitted so the client can be
frozen "and previous state roots invalidated as necessary".<Cite src="ics-002-client-semantics" />

Compare that to a committee bridge. When a committee signs a false message, there is no contradiction
to detect, because the committee's signature *is* the truth condition. There is nothing to submit and
nothing to freeze. That is not a difference of degree.

## Relayers move things and cannot forge them

The other half of the design is the part that looks like a weakness and is not.

Relayers are "off-chain processes that observe packet commitments on the sending chain and submit them
with Merkle proofs to the receiving chain".<Cite src="ibc-go-docs" /> The documentation states the
access model plainly: "Relaying is permissionless by default."<Cite src="ibc-go-docs" /> Many relayers
can serve the same paths.<Cite src="ibc-go-docs" />

**Subgoal: work out why permissionless is safe here.** A relayer submits a packet together with a
proof. The receiving chain verifies that proof against its own light client of the
sender.<Cite src="ics-002-client-semantics" /><Cite src="ibc-go-docs" /> A forged packet fails
verification, so a malicious relayer accomplishes nothing by submitting one. There is no signature
from the relayer anywhere in the trust path — the relayer is a courier, and the envelope is sealed by
mathematics the recipient checks itself.

**Subgoal: name what a relayer *can* do, because it is not nothing.** It can decline to relay. If no
relayer runs a path, packets sit unsent and eventually time out. **Relayers are a liveness dependency,
not a trust anchor**, and that distinction is the whole reason "who runs the relayer" is a different
question from "who runs the bridge". The first is an operations question with a cost attached; the
second is a custody question with a loss attached.

**Subgoal: understand the timeout that makes the liveness failure safe.** IBC v2 packets "carry a
single Unix timestamp timeout (in seconds)",<Cite src="ibc-go-docs" /> so a packet nobody relays does
not hang forever — it expires, and the sending side can reclaim what it escrowed. The design uses a
timestamp rather than a block height because "block heights are chain-specific and don't translate
across heterogeneous chains like Ethereum".<Cite src="ibc-go-docs" /> A liveness failure therefore
degrades to "your transfer did not happen" rather than "your funds are in limbo", which is the
correct shape for a failure you cannot prevent.

Steps 2 and 3 are the relayer's entire contribution, and they are exactly the steps described in the
documentation: observe packet commitments on the sending chain and submit them with Merkle proofs to
the receiving chain.<Cite src="ibc-go-docs" /> Step 4 is chain B checking, itself, with no input from
the relayer beyond the bytes.<Cite src="ics-002-client-semantics" /> Step 7 is what makes step 2 not
mattering survivable.<Cite src="ibc-go-docs" />

**Subgoal: notice what the sender has to know.** Chain B's belief about chain A rests on a
`ConsensusState` — a trusted view of A at some height — that had to get there
somehow.<Cite src="ics-002-client-semantics" /> That initial trusted state is a bootstrapping
assumption: somebody created the client with a starting view, and everything after it is derived by
the validity predicate from there. It is the one moment in the protocol where a human made a
judgement, and it is worth knowing that it exists.

**Subgoal: turn that into a check you can actually run.** The specification notes that stored client
instances "can be introspected by a third party actor, such as a user inspecting the state of the
state machine and deciding whether or not to send an IBC packet".<Cite src="ics-002-client-semantics" />
That is an explicit invitation, and it is the closest thing this ecosystem has to reading a bridge's
deployed configuration before using it. Before routing product value over a path, look at the client:
which chain it tracks, how recently it was updated, and whether it is frozen. On a committee bridge
the equivalent inspection is reading a signer list and hoping; here it is querying protocol state.

## The trust assumption that replaced the committee

Now the honest part, and it is the reason to read the specification rather than the marketing.

IBC does not eliminate trust. It relocates it onto the counterparty's consensus. And the specification
is explicit that "the counterparty's consensus" can be almost anything: "A state machine may consist
of a single process signing operations with a private key (the so-called 'solo machine'), a quorum of
processes signing in unison, many processes operating a Byzantine fault-tolerant consensus algorithm
(e.g., Tendermint), or other configurations yet to be invented — from the perspective of IBC, a state
machine is defined entirely by its light client validation and misbehaviour detection
logic."<Cite src="ics-002-client-semantics" />

Read that carefully. **A quorum of processes signing in unison is a valid IBC counterparty.** So is a
single machine with one key. The protocol's guarantee is not "no committees"; it is "you verify
whatever the counterparty's consensus actually is, honestly, on chain, and its failures are detectable
according to its own rules". If you connect to a chain whose consensus is a five-of-nine signing
group, you have an IBC connection to a five-of-nine signing group, and the fact that it arrived over
IBC does not upgrade it.

Three consequences you should carry into any integration decision.

**The counterparty's security becomes yours.** This is the appchain trade-off from lesson one arriving
by a different route. A chain with a small validator set, connected to yours, is a path by which a
cheap attack on their consensus becomes an expensive loss on yours. IBC transports value across a
security boundary faithfully; it does not raise the floor.

**Liveness is an operational dependency you must fund or monitor.** Permissionless does not mean
automatic.<Cite src="ibc-go-docs" /> Somebody runs the relayer for the path your users need, and if
your product depends on that path, "somebody" should probably be you or someone you pay.

**A frozen client is a real operational state.** Misbehaviour freezes the client and "higher-level
intervention" is then necessary.<Cite src="ics-002-client-semantics" /> That is a governance-scale
incident, not a retry, and it is worth knowing that it exists before you meet it.

<Aside kind="note">
Where this sits in the classification you already learned: ethereum.org's central security question
about any cross-chain design is "who verifies the system", and its answer is that bridges secured by
external validators are typically less secure than those secured locally or natively by the
underlying blockchain's own validators.<Cite src="ethereum-org-bridges" /> Its trustless category is
defined as relying on the chains being connected and their validators, adding no new trust
assumptions.<Cite src="ethereum-org-bridges" /> IBC is a full realisation of that corner, with the
caveat above about what the counterparty's validators actually are. Note also the price the same
classification says you pay in that corner: light-client designs buy security at the cost of
connectivity.<Cite src="ethereum-org-bridges" /> The next lesson is about the work aimed squarely at
that cost.
</Aside>

<Answer>
**Proposal A — the committee bridge.** (a) Nine signers. That is the entire answer, and it does not
depend on the other chain's security at all — the committee can sign a message about a deposit that
never happened.<Cite src="certik-bridge-exploits-2022" /> (b) Unbacked supply appears on your chain
and trades at par until someone notices; nothing on chain contradicts it, because the signature *is*
the truth condition.<Cite src="wormhole-postmortem" /> (c) If the signers stop, messages stop and your
users' funds sit on whichever side they were on.

**Proposal B — IBC.** (a) Whoever can break the counterparty chain's consensus — for a CometBFT chain,
more than a third of its voting power for a fork, and note that the resulting equivocation is a signed
contradiction.<Cite src="cometbft-consensus-spec" /><Cite src="ics-002-client-semantics" /> There is no
smaller set: no relayer, no signer group, no admin. (b) The equivocation can be submitted as a
misbehaviour proof, which freezes the client so no further state updates from that chain are accepted,
with previous roots invalidated as necessary and higher-level intervention
required.<Cite src="ics-002-client-semantics" /> Detection and containment exist, which they do not in
case A. (c) If relayers stop, packets are not delivered and time out on their Unix-timestamp
deadline;<Cite src="ibc-go-docs" /> escrowed value is reclaimable rather than stranded, and anyone may
start relaying because relaying is permissionless.<Cite src="ibc-go-docs" />

**The question that decides it, and it is not the one people ask.** Not "is IBC safer than a
multisig" — it plainly is on this comparison. It is **what is the counterparty chain's consensus
actually made of**. The specification permits a counterparty that is a quorum signing in unison or
even a single machine with a key.<Cite src="ics-002-client-semantics" /> If the chain on the other end
is secured by a small validator set, proposal B's answer to (a) may in practice be a set not much
larger than proposal A's — arrived at honestly, verified on chain, and still small. IBC guarantees
that you are trusting exactly what you think you are trusting. It does not guarantee that what you
are trusting is worth trusting.

## Where this pays off

**As a design pattern, independent of Cosmos.** "Verify the counterparty's consensus on chain instead
of trusting a committee" is the single most transferable idea in this module. It is what storage-proof
bridges and zk light clients on Ethereum are reaching for, and reading the IBC specification is the
cheapest way to understand the shape they are converging on — including the misbehaviour handling
that most designs discover they need
later.<Cite src="ics-002-client-semantics" /><Cite src="ibc-spec" />

**As a due-diligence checklist.** For any cross-chain path: who verifies, what exactly do they verify,
what proof would demonstrate they lied, what happens when it is submitted, and who is merely moving
bytes. Those five questions separate the designs, and four of them have no good answer for a
committee bridge.

**As a correction to your own vocabulary.** "Bridge" is a category of *outcome*, not of mechanism.
Sorting cross-chain systems by whether the destination verifies the source's consensus or believes
someone's signature is the sort that predicts losses; sorting them by branding is not.

**What this unlocks.** Everything above is intra-Cosmos: two CometBFT chains, each cheaply verifiable
by the other. The next lesson is about the attempt to take the same design somewhere much harder —
IBC v2, the removal of the connection and channel handshakes, and light-client verification
implemented for chains that are not Cosmos SDK chains at all.
