You have a working state machine and you want it on a Cosmos chain. So you ask the obvious question —
how do I deploy this? — and on most Cosmos chains the honest answer is: you do not. There is no deploy
transaction. The chain runs a binary, your logic goes *in* the binary, and putting it there means
persuading the chain's governance to run a new version.

That single fact reorganises everything. It is not a packaging inconvenience. It is a different answer
to who is allowed to ship code, and it follows directly from the way the stack is put together.

## Three layers with a specified seam

The architecture is unusually clean, and the SDK's own documentation states each piece.

**The bottom layer is consensus and networking.** "CometBFT is a Byzantine Fault Tolerant consensus
engine that provides fast, deterministic finality."<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-app-architecture" /> It knows
nothing about your application. It orders bytes.

**The top layer is your application.** "A Cosmos SDK application is a deterministic state machine that
defines a blockchain's behavior."<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-app-architecture" /> Not a contract inside a
chain — the chain's behaviour itself.

**The seam between them is ABCI.** "ABCI is a strict request/response interface between CometBFT and
a Cosmos SDK blockchain application."<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-app-architecture" /> The methods that
matter: `CheckTx` "validates transactions before adding them to the mempool"; `FinalizeBlock` "is
invoked after consensus on a block is reached" and is "where state transitions occur"; `Commit` "is
called after FinalizeBlock to persist the finalized state to the nodes' local
disk".<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-app-architecture" />

The division of labour is stated in one sentence worth memorising: CometBFT "determines when blocks
happen and which transactions they contain through its consensus mechanism" while the SDK "defines
what those transactions mean and how they change state
deterministically".<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-app-architecture" />

**Subgoal: locate yourself on that diagram.** A Solidity developer lives on the bottom row. A Cosmos
developer lives on the third row, and the fourth row may not exist at all on their chain. That is the
whole module in one observation: **a Cosmos application sits where the EVM sits.**

**Subgoal: notice that Ethereum has the same seam.** The Engine API split consensus from execution on
Ethereum too, and the argument for it was the same one the SDK gives: "Separating consensus from
application logic provides several benefits. Security improves because isolating consensus logic
prevents application bugs from affecting block production or network
stability."<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-app-architecture" /> The difference is not that Cosmos invented
modularity. It is that Cosmos expects *you* to be the thing above the seam, whereas Ethereum ships a
single well-known implementation up there and rents you space inside it.

## Finality that means something different from the one you know

You arrive from a track that taught finality as [a spectrum](/c/finality-is-a-spectrum) — more
confirmations, more cost to reverse, eventually a checkpoint that is economically final. CometBFT's
version is categorically different, and the specification's own proofs are the right place to see
why.

**Safety.** "Assume that at most -1/3 of the voting power of validators is byzantine. If a validator
commits block `B` at round `R`, it's because it saw +2/3 of precommits at round `R`."<Cite src="cometbft-consensus-spec" />
Under that assumption, no conflicting block can be committed. A commit is not a probability that
improves with depth. It is a decision.

**What happens when the assumption fails is the interesting half.** The specification also proves fork
accountability: when a fork is detected by the existence of two conflicting commits, the union of the
justification sets "must include double-signing by at least 1/3+ of the validator
set".<Cite src="cometbft-consensus-spec" /> As a corollary, an external process can determine the
blame by requiring each validator to justify its votes, and will find at least a third who
double-signed or cannot justify a vote.<Cite src="cometbft-consensus-spec" />

So a BFT fork is not merely possible-in-principle-but-unlikely. It is *attributable*. Somebody signed
two things, and the evidence is a pair of signatures anyone can check. That is what makes slashing a
credible deterrent rather than a gesture, and it is a property Nakamoto-style chains structurally
cannot offer, because there mining a competing chain is not a signed contradiction — it is just
mining.

**The price is liveness.** Safety here is bought by requiring more than two thirds of voting power to
agree before anything commits.<Cite src="cometbft-consensus-spec" /> Turn that around: if more than a
third of voting power is offline, malfunctioning, or refusing, nothing commits. The chain does not
fork, does not reorg, and does not produce blocks. It stops. The protocol's own liveness argument is
conditional on enough honest validators eventually converging and on proposals being fully
gossiped;<Cite src="cometbft-consensus-spec" /><Cite src="tendermint-gossip-paper" /> when those
conditions do not hold, the correct behaviour of the protocol is to wait.

  why="The word 'finality' is heard as 'robustness'. It is a statement about what a commit means, not about how likely the chain is to keep producing them."
  src="cometbft-consensus-spec"
/>

**What it changes in code you would actually write.** Every integration you have built against an EVM
chain carries a confirmation-depth constant somewhere — wait N blocks before crediting a deposit,
re-check after a reorg, keep a rollback path in the indexer. On a CometBFT chain that constant is one
block, and the rollback path is dead code, because a committed block is a
decision.<Cite src="cometbft-consensus-spec" /> That deletes a genuinely error-prone piece of
infrastructure from your indexer and your deposit pipeline. It also deletes your safety net: if your
assumption about the chain's validator set turns out to be wrong, there is no confirmation depth
tuned up to absorb it, and the failure arrives as an attributable fork with slashable evidence rather
than as a reorg you quietly survive.<Cite src="cometbft-consensus-spec" />

**Design consequence, stated as a product question.** Which do you prefer: a chain that keeps
producing blocks but where recent history might be revised, or a chain whose history is never revised
but which may stop entirely for hours? Exchanges, custodians and payment processors answer that
question differently from games and social apps, and the answer should drive the chain choice rather
than follow it. Nothing about "instant finality" is better in the abstract; it is better for
workloads that cannot tolerate revision and can tolerate downtime.

## Modules, where the contracts would be

The application is assembled from modules. The SDK's own description: "Modules generally define a
subset of the state using one or more `KVStore`s in the main multistore, as well as a subset of
message types", and "SDK modules implement the bulk of the logic of applications, while the core does
the wiring and enables modules to be composed together".<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-building-modules" />

A chain gets accounts, tokens, staking and governance by including the modules that provide them, and
gets your product by including the module you wrote. The documentation frames the whole SDK this way:
blockchains are "composed of interoperable modules, each responsible for a specific piece of
functionality", and developers can use predefined ones or build custom ones "tailored to their
application's requirements".<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-overview" />

Now the part that changes the permission model. The full node "presents itself as a binary, generally
suffixed by `-d` for 'daemon'", and "This binary is built by running a simple `main.go` function
placed in `./cmd/appd/`".<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-app-anatomy" /> Modules are instantiated inside the
application's constructor and registered with a module manager that also fixes their order of
execution for genesis, begin-block and end-block hooks.<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-app-anatomy" />

**Subgoal: derive the permission model from the build model.** If your logic is compiled into the
daemon, then shipping new logic means shipping a new daemon, which means every validator must run it,
which means the chain's governance must agree. There is no permissionless deploy because there is
nothing to deploy *into*.

**Subgoal: price both sides honestly.** What you lose: the thing that made Ethereum what it is —
anyone can put code on the chain without asking, and the composability that follows from a public
square of deployed contracts. What you gain: no unaudited stranger can add state or logic to your
chain, upgrades are coordinated events with a governance record rather than a proxy admin key held by
three people, and the entire class of problems around
[upgrade authority on a proxy](/c/upgrade-risk) is replaced by a different, more visible process.

**Subgoal: notice that this also changes ordering guarantees.** Module execution order for the
begin-block and end-block hooks is *set explicitly* in the application
constructor.<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-app-anatomy" /> On Ethereum there is no such thing as "the order in
which contracts run at the end of a block", because contracts do not run unless called. Per-block
logic being first-class, with a deterministic and configurable ordering, is a real capability, and it
is also a real footgun: the ordering is a security-relevant configuration line in a Go file that no
contract auditor's checklist covers.

### State and messages, next to the two models you already hold

Two more mappings finish the picture, and both are places where EVM intuition silently misleads.

**State is one key-value store, sliced by module.** A module "define[s] a subset of the state using
one or more `KVStore`s in the main multistore".<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-building-modules" /> Note the
word *main*: there is a single store for the whole application, and modules own regions of it. That is
much closer to Solana's model — where [state is externalised](/c/state-externalized) and a program is
code that operates on accounts — than to the EVM's, where each contract has a private 256-bit
[slot space](/c/storage-slot) that nothing else can address.

The consequence is one that catches EVM developers: **another module can be given a reference to your
module's store keeper and read or write your state.** That is not an exploit, it is the composition
mechanism — the core "does the wiring and enables modules to be composed
together"<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-building-modules" /> and the wiring is Go code in the application
constructor.<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-app-anatomy" /> Access control between modules is a property of how
the app was assembled, not of the runtime. There is no equivalent of "this storage slot is mine
because the EVM says so".

**Messages are typed and routed, not selected by four bytes.** A module handles "a subset of message
types",<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-building-modules" /> registered through a `Msg` service on the module
manager.<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-app-anatomy" /> A transaction carries messages; the router hands each to
the module that declared it.

Put that beside the ABI machinery you know. On Ethereum, a call is calldata whose first four bytes are
a hash of a signature, dispatched by a compiler-generated jump table, with no type information on the
wire beyond what the caller and callee separately agreed to believe. Here the message is a declared
type with a schema, and routing is a registration rather than a collision-prone hash.

Two practical differences fall straight out of that.

**Selector-collision problems do not exist**, and neither do the proxy-related dispatch hazards built
on them. There is no four-byte space to collide in.

**Introspection is better and versioning is worse.** A client can enumerate a chain's message types
from the running node; you cannot enumerate an EVM contract's functions from its bytecode without a
verified ABI. But changing a message's schema is a chain upgrade with a migration, not a new function
added beside the old one — so the flexibility you get from "just deploy V2 next to V1" is unavailable.

<Misconception
  belief="A Cosmos module is the equivalent of a Solidity contract, so the same isolation applies."
  reality="A contract's storage is isolated by the EVM. A module's state region is isolated by convention and by which keepers the application constructor chose to hand it. Composition happens in Go at build time, not through calls at run time."
  why="Both are 'the unit you write', so the isolation properties get assumed to carry across. They do not — the boundary moved from the runtime to the build."
  src="cosmos-sdk-building-modules"
/>

### CosmWasm is a choice a chain makes, not a property Cosmos has

If a chain does want permissionless deployment, it adds a module for it. The CosmWasm documentation
describes precisely that: "The x/wasm module, the root of CosmWasm, is a Cosmos SDK module enabling
smart contracts to execute on the CosmWasm virtual machine."<Cite src="cosmwasm-docs" /> Contracts are
written in Rust and compiled to WebAssembly, a choice the docs justify by Rust's popularity among
blockchain developers, with stated priorities of security, performance and interoperability, and
particular attention to tight integration with the Cosmos SDK and to IBC-speaking
contracts.<Cite src="cosmwasm-docs" />

The practical consequence for you as an integrator: "can I deploy to this Cosmos chain" is not a
question about Cosmos. It is a question about that chain's module list, and the answer differs chain
by chain. Check before you plan, not after.

## Reading versions in this ecosystem, which is a skill rather than a fact

This module refuses to hand you version numbers, and here is a worked demonstration of why using the
Cosmos stack itself.

The 2026 Cosmos stack roadmap records two retractions people still teach as the future. CometBFT v1
and v2 development was abandoned — "Due to the breaking changes in v1 and v2, we will not be pursuing
further development on these versions" — with effort refocused on the v0.38 line and a planned v0.39
carrying BLS signing and experimental libp2p networking.<Cite src="cosmos-stack-roadmap-2026" /> The
previously promoted "Cosmos SDK v2" direction was likewise dropped in favour of continuing the v0.5x
line, with v0.53 released in 2025 and v0.54 planned for end of Q1 or early Q2
2026.<Cite src="cosmos-stack-roadmap-2026" />

Now read the releases pages instead of the roadmap. As of 26 August 2026 the Cosmos SDK repository's
releases include a v0.55.0 published on 28 July 2026, alongside continuing v0.54.x and v0.53.x
lines,<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-releases" /> and the CometBFT repository's releases include a v0.38.26
published on 13 August 2026, a v0.39.4, and a v0.40.0 published on 27 July
2026.<Cite src="cometbft-releases" />

<Aside kind="warn">
Three lessons, none of them about Cosmos specifically.

**The roadmap was right about direction and stale about specifics.** It said v0.39 was next; there is
now a v0.40.0 as well.<Cite src="cometbft-releases" /><Cite src="cosmos-stack-roadmap-2026" /> Roadmaps
are dated intentions.

**Version numbers here are not semver intuition.** A "v0.x" line is the production line, and a higher
major number was abandoned rather than promoted.<Cite src="cosmos-stack-roadmap-2026" /> Anyone
reasoning "v1 must be newer than v0.38" reaches the wrong conclusion, and it is exactly the wrong
conclusion an LLM or a two-year-old tutorial will hand you.

**Multiple lines are maintained at once.** v0.53, v0.54 and v0.55 all receive releases; so do v0.38,
v0.39 and v0.40.<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-releases" /><Cite src="cometbft-releases" /> "The current
version" is not a well-formed question. "Which line is this chain on, and what is the newest release
in that line" is.
</Aside>

<Check type="explain" concept="sdk-modules">
Your team ships a lending market. On an EVM chain, the release process is: audit, deploy, point the
proxy at the new implementation, done in an afternoon. Describe what the equivalent release looks
like on a sovereign Cosmos SDK chain where your market is a custom module, and name three things that
get better and two that get worse.

<Answer>
**The mechanics.** The new logic is Go code compiled into the daemon binary that every validator
runs.<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-app-anatomy" /> Shipping it means cutting a release, getting a governance
proposal passed, and having validators upgrade at a coordinated height. Your state migration is part
of the upgrade rather than a separate script run against a proxy. There is no moment where new code
is live for some users and not others, because there is no "deploy" — there is a height before which
the chain runs the old binary and after which it runs the new one.

**Better — the upgrade key stops existing.** There is no proxy admin, so there is no
[upgrade authority](/c/upgrade-risk) to compromise, and no address on a dashboard that could take
everything. The authority is a governance process with a public record.

**Better — no unknown code appears on your chain.** Nothing gets added without the same process. The
entire class of "a malicious contract was deployed and drained a pool that had integrated it" is not
available on a chain with no permissionless deployment.

**Better — block-boundary logic is first-class.** Interest accrual and liquidation sweeps can run in
begin-block or end-block hooks with an execution order you set
explicitly,<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-app-anatomy" /> instead of depending on keeper bots you fund and
monitor.

**Worse — your release cadence is now social.** Validators must upgrade. A hotfix is a coordination
problem measured in hours or days, and there is no way to make it faster by being a better engineer.
An EVM team can ship a fix in minutes if their process allows it; you cannot.

**Worse — you have taken on chain operations.** State migrations at an upgrade height, consensus
version compatibility, validator communications, and the possibility that a bad binary halts the
chain rather than reverting a transaction.<Cite src="cometbft-consensus-spec" /> The blast radius of a
bug moved from "my contract" to "the network".

**The one-line summary.** The proxy upgrade key was not removed; it was replaced by a governance
process and an operations team. Whether that is an improvement depends entirely on which of those two
your organisation is actually better at.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**In evaluating any Cosmos chain you might integrate with.** Three questions, in order: which SDK and
CometBFT lines is it on;<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-releases" /><Cite src="cometbft-releases" /> does it
include a contract module, or is all logic in the binary;<Cite src="cosmwasm-docs" /> and what is its
governance process for upgrades. Those three answers tell you more about your integration risk than
any amount of reading its documentation site.

**In understanding your own stack better.** Ethereum has the same three layers with the same kind of
seam. What differs is who occupies the top one and how many parties are allowed to add code inside
it. Seeing an ecosystem that made the other choice makes the EVM's choice visible as a choice.

**What this unlocks.** You now have chains that are individually sovereign and individually isolated,
each with a validator set of its own and no shared settlement layer. Connecting them is the obvious
next problem, and the obvious solutions are the committee bridges whose failures you catalogued in
Track 10. The next lesson is about the design that does not do that: each chain runs a light client
of its counterparty and verifies the other chain's consensus itself, which makes "who runs the
relayer" a fundamentally different question from "who runs the bridge".
