A governance process you are not part of votes to change a parameter. Six weeks later your product's
economics are different: a fee curve moved, a precompile got repriced, a sequencer policy changed.
You had no vote, you were not consulted, and you find out from a changelog.

You have absorbed this as weather. It is the ordinary condition of building on a shared chain, and
the entire curriculum up to this point has been about becoming excellent at operating inside somebody
else's machine — its gas schedule, its opcode semantics, its upgrade cadence, its neighbours.

Cosmos exists because a set of people looked at that arrangement and said: why are you renting?

## The thesis, in the words of the people making it

The Cosmos SDK describes itself as "a secure, open-source framework for building application-specific
blockchains and decentralized ledgers".<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-overview" /> The argument against the
alternative is made directly on the same page: general-purpose VM chains deploy applications as smart
contracts into shared execution environments, which "can impose constraints around performance,
customization, and protocol-level control", whereas an application-specific chain lets developers
"tailor execution logic, fee models, governance rules, and state transitions directly at the protocol
level".<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-overview" />

Read that list again with your Solidity hat on, because each item is something you currently cannot
touch.

**Execution logic.** Not your contract's logic — the machine's. Transaction validity rules,
scheduling, what happens between blocks. On Ethereum that is the client's business and yours only
through EIPs.

**Fee models.** Not "we subsidise gas with a paymaster", which is a workaround built on top of a fee
market you do not control. Actually deciding what a fee is, what it is denominated in, whether it
exists for certain message types, and where it goes.

**Governance rules.** Who can change the chain, on what timeline, with what quorum. Your app's
upgrade key stops being a multisig you defend and starts being a chain-level process you designed.

**All of the block space.** The neighbour's mint cannot price you out, because there is no neighbour.
This is the item people lead with and it is the least interesting of the four.

The claim is not marginal in adoption terms either: the documentation states that "the SDK powers over
200 live networks across the Cosmos ecosystem".<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-overview" />

<Aside kind="note">
An important framing you will need for the whole module: a Cosmos application sits where the **EVM**
sits, not where a **contract** sits. The SDK's own architecture page describes a Cosmos SDK
application as "a deterministic state machine that defines a blockchain's
behavior".<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-app-architecture" /> You are not writing a tenant. You are writing the
building.
</Aside>

### What "protocol-level control" concretely means

That phrase does a lot of work in Cosmos marketing, so make it concrete using the interface the stack
is actually built on. The SDK's architecture page describes ABCI as "a strict request/response
interface between CometBFT and a Cosmos SDK blockchain application", and names the methods: `CheckTx`
"validates transactions before adding them to the mempool", `FinalizeBlock` "is invoked after
consensus on a block is reached" and is "where state transitions occur", and `Commit` "is called after
FinalizeBlock to persist the finalized state to the nodes' local
disk".<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-app-architecture" />

You implement those. That is what owning the state machine means, and each one is a lever a contract
developer does not have.

**You own mempool admission.** `CheckTx` is yours,<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-app-architecture" /> so "which
transactions are even allowed to wait" is application logic. On Ethereum that policy belongs to
client implementations and to whatever the builder market decides; you influence it with fees and
nothing else.

**You own what happens at block boundaries.** `FinalizeBlock` is where your state transitions
run,<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-app-architecture" /> which means logic that fires once per block —
settlement, funding-rate accrual, batch matching, liquidation sweeps — is a thing the chain does
rather than a thing a keeper bot must be paid to trigger. Anyone who has operated a liquidation
keeper on an EVM chain knows exactly how much of a product this replaces.

**You own the boundary itself.** The division is stated cleanly: CometBFT "determines when blocks
happen and which transactions they contain through its consensus mechanism" while the SDK "defines
what those transactions mean and how they change state
deterministically".<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-app-architecture" /> The page also names the reason the seam
exists: "Separating consensus from application logic provides several benefits. Security improves
because isolating consensus logic prevents application bugs from affecting block production or
network stability."<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-app-architecture" />

None of those levers matter to most applications. They matter enormously to a few — exchanges,
games with per-block ticks, anything whose product *is* an ordering policy. That asymmetry is the
real selection rule, and it is far more useful than "do you want your own chain".

## The bill, which the official page does not print

Here is something worth noticing about that documentation page, and it is a reading skill more than a
Cosmos fact: it lists the advantages of application-specific chains and does not mention what they
cost.<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-overview" /> No paragraph about recruiting validators. No paragraph about
paying for them. That is not dishonesty — it is a product page doing what product pages do — but a
curriculum that repeats it without the missing half is teaching marketing.

So state the missing half precisely, using a concept you already have.

A chain's safety is bounded by the cost of corrupting the set that produces its blocks. On a
sovereign chain, that set is *yours*: you recruit it, you incentivise it, and you pay for it out of
your own application's economics, usually by inflating your own token. Which means the question
"is my application safe" reduces to "is the market value of the stake securing my chain large
relative to what my application holds" — and for a young appchain the honest answer is frequently no.

This is the [weaker link](/c/security-is-the-weaker-link) argument, pointed at the chain instead of at
a bridge. A hundred million dollars of value sitting on a chain secured by ten million dollars of
staked token is not a hundred-million-dollar system. It is a ten-million-dollar system with a
hundred million dollars in it.

The threshold is not a guess. CometBFT's consensus specification proves safety under the assumption
that at most one third of the voting power of validators is Byzantine,<Cite src="cometbft-consensus-spec" />
so c is just over a third for any chain running it — which is every Cosmos SDK chain in this module.
With that threshold and any realistic slippage multiplier, S has to be the same order of magnitude as
V, not a tenth of it. That is the sentence to carry: **a sovereign
chain must carry a stake comparable to the value it holds**, and it must do so from the day the value
arrives, not from the day the token matures.

Three consequences worth pre-computing before anyone builds anything.

**The gap is worst exactly when the product is working.** Value arrives with adoption; stake value
follows token price and staking ratio, which move on their own schedule. The dangerous window is a
successful launch, not a failed one.

**Inflation is the usual funding mechanism and it is a real cost.** Paying validators by minting your
own token is a transfer from holders to validators. It is not free because it does not appear on an
income statement.

**Two of the variables are not yours.** Token price and staking ratio are set by a market. You can
influence them; you cannot set them. An architecture whose safety depends on a number you do not
control is a specific, nameable risk, and it should be written down as one.

**A rollup makes exactly the opposite trade.** It does not recruit a validator set, because it
borrows security from an L1 — that *is* the trade a [rollup](/c/rollup) is making. And it pays for the
borrowing in the currency you spent Track 10 learning to price: dependence on someone else's
sequencing, someone else's data availability, and someone else's
[upgrade politics](/c/upgrade-risk). Neither answer is free. They are two different bills.

## The second half of the bill: liquidity and the network effect

Security is the one people name. Liquidity is the one that kills products.

A contract on a shared L1 is born adjacent to every asset, every lending market, every DEX and every
wallet on that chain. Composability is not a feature you integrate; it is the ambient condition. Move
the same application to its own chain and every one of those adjacencies becomes an integration
project: assets must be brought over, market makers must be persuaded, wallets must add support,
oracles must be deployed and paid for.

Cosmos's answer to this is IBC, and it is a genuinely good answer — the next two lessons are about
why its trust model is structurally different from the bridges that lost billions.<Cite src="ibc-go-docs" />
But be precise about what it does and does not fix. IBC lets assets and messages move between chains
under a strong trust model. It does not make your chain's order book deep, and moving an asset does
not move the liquidity that was around it. A general classification of cross-chain designs puts the
same point in trust-model terms: what you get from any of them is connectivity, and connectivity is
not the same thing as being in the same place.<Cite src="ethereum-org-bridges" />

The honest formulation, and the one to be able to say out loud at the end of this module: **you gain
sovereignty over the execution environment and you lose the shared security and shared liquidity of
building inside somebody else's.**

## Renting a validator set: the convergence from the other side

The security half of that trade is well enough understood inside Cosmos that the ecosystem built a
response. Interchain Security lets a consumer chain be secured by a provider chain's validator set
rather than one it recruits itself.<Cite src="interchain-security-docs" /> The documentation describes
Partial Set Security and permissionless launch features whose stated purpose is making it "easier
than ever to leverage the Cosmos Hub validators, stake, and community for your chain", including the
ability to shape which validators serve it.<Cite src="interchain-security-docs" />

Look at the shape of that and not at the branding. A chain that does not produce its own security,
and instead borrows a larger system's, in exchange for accepting a relationship with that system, is
**the rollup trade** — reached from the opposite direction. Ethereum's ecosystem started from shared
security and spent a decade building ways to get execution independence. Cosmos started from
execution independence and built ways to get shared security. Both are converging on the same
insight, which is that most applications want one of each and the interesting engineering is in the
seam.

<Aside kind="warn">
This is the most volatile material in the module, and the research behind this stub did not settle
it. Names, adoption levels and operational status of shared-security programmes change; the
documentation is a live product site rather than a specification.<Cite src="interchain-security-docs" />
Treat the *shape* of the arrangement as the durable content — borrowed validators, reduced
sovereignty, some economic relationship with the provider — and verify any specific programme, its
current name, and who is actually using it before making a decision on it.
</Aside>

<Check type="explain" concept="appchain-tradeoff">
You are the technical founder of a perpetuals exchange expecting, within a year, a few hundred
million dollars of open interest and a latency-sensitive matching workload. Three deployment shapes
are on the table: a contract on a shared L1, a rollup, and a sovereign appchain. For each, answer
four questions — who provides security and what it costs, who controls upgrades, what the fee token
is, and how long until you can launch. Then name the single assumption that, if false, flips your
answer.

<Answer>
**Contract on a shared L1.** Security is the L1's, and you pay for it in gas, priced by a market you
share with everyone else — which is exactly the problem for a latency-sensitive matching engine,
because your costs and your inclusion depend on other people's activity. Upgrades: the chain's, on
its schedule, plus your own contract upgrade key. Fee token: the L1's. Time to launch: shortest, and
you inherit liquidity and wallet support on day one.

**Rollup.** Security is borrowed from the L1 under the constraints of the stack you chose — which, at
the stages you studied in Track 10, usually means an upgrade multisig is the real answer to "who can
take everything".<Cite src="ethereum-org-bridges" /> Upgrades: yours for the application, constrained
for the chain. Fee token: typically the L1's asset. Time to launch: medium; you are launching a
deployment, not a network. You get block space that is yours in practice and a much better latency
story than the shared L1, and you keep proximity to the ecosystem's liquidity.

**Sovereign appchain.** Security is yours to produce and to pay for, most likely by inflating a token
you have not launched yet — unless you take a shared-security arrangement, which converts this row
into something close to the rollup's.<Cite src="interchain-security-docs" /> Upgrades: entirely yours,
through chain governance, which is a real advantage for a product that wants to change matching rules
without asking anyone.<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-overview" /> Fee token: yours, and you can decide that
some message types are free — a genuinely differentiated capability for an exchange, where charging
per order cancellation is a product decision rather than a protocol
constraint.<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-overview" /> Time to launch: longest, and it includes recruiting a
validator set, which is a business-development problem rather than an engineering one.

**The assumption that flips it.** *Whether your users' assets originate elsewhere.* If the product's
value is denominated in assets that live on another chain, the appchain's liquidity island is a
permanent tax paid on every deposit and withdrawal, and the sovereignty is being bought with the
thing your users actually care about. If the product mints and holds its own asset, the island costs
much less and the sovereignty is nearly free.

Notice that the flipping assumption is not about security or performance, which are where the
argument is usually conducted. It is about where the money already is.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**In your architecture reviews.** "Should this be its own chain" is now a question with a structured
answer rather than an aesthetic one. Ask what you need at the protocol level that a contract cannot
give you, then ask what your security budget will be and where your users' assets currently sit. Most
applications, honestly evaluated, do not need protocol-level control; the ones that do usually need
it for fee semantics or for ordering, and they know it.

**In reading other ecosystems.** The appchain-versus-rollup argument recurs under other names — L3s,
sovereign rollups, validiums, hyperchains. The axis is always the same: what does this thing produce
itself, and what does it borrow, and what does the lender get in return.

**What this unlocks.** The next lesson opens the box. A Cosmos chain is three layers with a defined
seam between them — CometBFT for consensus and networking, the SDK for the application, and ABCI as
the interface — and the unit of deployment is a module compiled into the chain binary rather than a
contract anyone can publish.<Cite src="cosmos-sdk-app-architecture" /> That last point is a different
*permission model*, not a different packaging format, and it changes who is allowed to ship on your
chain at all.
