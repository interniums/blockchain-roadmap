Two transactions do the same thing: move one token from A to B. Same program, same accounts, same
compute-unit price. One pays about seven times the other's priority fee, and lands *later*.

Nothing is wrong with either. The expensive one left its compute budget at the default. That is the
entire difference, and it is not a rounding error — it is a factor of seven on price and a factor of
several on scheduling position, both in the same direction, both derivable from published formulas.

If you arrive from the EVM this reads as absurd, because there setting a generous gas limit is
*free*: unused gas is refunded, so the limit is a safety margin with no economic content and everyone
over-sets it deliberately. Import that habit here and you overpay on every transaction you ever send.

This lesson makes the arithmetic explicit, and then shows why the mistake costs twice.

## Three quantities that all sound like "gas"

The first job is to stop using one word for three things. Anza's own framing separates
them:<Cite src="anza-compute-units" />

**Compute units consumed.** What the runtime meters during execution. The smallest runtime operation
consumes 1 CU.<Cite src="anza-compute-units" /> This is the quantity closest to gas as a
[countdown](/c/gas-is-a-countdown): exhaust your budget mid-execution and the transaction
fails.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" />

**Transaction cost.** A pre-execution *estimate*, also denominated in compute units, that the leader
uses to schedule and to enforce block limits. It is the sum of five components — signature cost, write
lock cost, instruction data cost, program execution cost, and loaded-accounts-data-size
cost.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" /> Note the fourth: "program execution cost" is not what
your program used, it is *the CU limit you requested*.<Cite src="anza-compute-units" />

**Transaction fee.** Lamports, and mostly independent of how many CU you actually
used.<Cite src="anza-compute-units" />

Keeping these apart is the whole lesson. The EVM collapses all three: gas used drives gas paid drives
block-space accounting. Here they are three different numbers with three different formulas, and the
one you control most directly is the *estimate*.

## The budget

Without any explicit instruction, a transaction's CU limit is computed from its instruction mix rather
than being a single default:<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" />

```text
default_cu_limit = (non-builtin instructions      × 200,000)
                 + (builtin instructions          ×   3,000)
                 + (migrated-builtin instructions × 200,000)
clamped to MAX_COMPUTE_UNIT_LIMIT = 1,400,000
```

`DEFAULT_INSTRUCTION_COMPUTE_UNIT_LIMIT` is 200,000 CU per user-deployed SBF instruction;
`MAX_BUILTIN_ALLOCATION_COMPUTE_UNIT_LIMIT` is 3,000 CU for builtins that have not migrated to SBF,
per SIMD-0170; the result is clamped to 1,400,000.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" />

To override it you include a `SetComputeUnitLimit` instruction from the Compute Budget program, which
has four variants in
total — `RequestHeapFrame`, `SetComputeUnitLimit`, `SetComputeUnitPrice`,
`SetLoadedAccountsDataSizeLimit`.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" /> Three rules bite in
practice: only one of each variant is allowed per transaction, and a duplicate fails the *whole
transaction* with `DuplicateInstruction`; any `u32` is accepted for the limit but the effective value
is clamped to 1,400,000; and an unrecognised Compute Budget instruction fails with
`InvalidInstructionData`.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" />

The documented method for choosing the number is not folklore: simulate the transaction to measure CU
consumption, then add a 10% safety margin.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" />

## The fee, exactly

Total fee is base fee plus prioritization fee, deducted from the fee payer *before execution
begins*.<Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" />

**Base fee.** `signature_count × lamports_per_signature`, where `lamports_per_signature` is currently
5,000 and the count includes the transaction's own Ed25519 signatures plus any verified by the
Ed25519, Secp256k1 and Secp256r1 precompile programs.<Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" /> Of the
base fee, 50% is burned and 50% goes to the block-producing validator — `DEFAULT_BURN_PERCENT` is
50.<Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" />

**Prioritization fee.**

```text
micro_lamport_fee  = compute_unit_price × compute_unit_limit
prioritization_fee = ceil(micro_lamport_fee / 1,000,000)
```

<Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" /> The price is in micro-lamports per CU and defaults to 0, so
the default prioritization fee is 0.<Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" /> All of it goes to the
validator; none is burned, per SIMD-0096.<Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" />

Now read the second factor in that multiplication. It is `compute_unit_limit` — the requested
maximum — not consumed CU, and if you set no limit the *default* computed above is what gets
multiplied.<Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" /> There is no refund path anywhere in the formula.

**So the seven-times claim is one division.** A transaction that lazily rides the 1,400,000 ceiling
pays `1,400,000 / 200,000 = 7×` what a tuned 200,000 request pays at the same
price.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" /><Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" /> At a price of
10,000 micro-lamports per CU that is 14,000 lamports against 2,000 — a 12,000-lamport gap on a
transaction whose base fee is 5,000.

## Why over-requesting costs you twice

The fee is the obvious half. The scheduling half is larger and almost nobody mentions it.

The leader ranks buffered transactions with a published formula:<Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" />

```text
Priority = reward × 1,000,000 / (cost + 1)
```

where `reward` is the validator's fee income — prioritization fee plus the non-burned half of the base
fee — and `cost` is the scheduler's five-component estimate.<Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" />
The multiplier preserves precision because cost usually exceeds reward in raw lamports; the `+1`
prevents division by zero.<Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" /> That priority determines the order
in which transactions are dequeued from the scheduler's buffer.<Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" />

Your requested CU limit appears in **both** terms. It multiplies the numerator through the
prioritization fee, and it is the program-execution component of the denominator. Raising it scales
the numerator linearly — and scales the denominator by the same absolute amount while the rest of the
denominator stays fixed. Which means past a certain point you are buying denominator faster than
numerator.

### Working it

A token transfer. One signature, two writable accounts, and — from Anza's example — roughly 6,000 CU
of actual execution.<Cite src="anza-compute-units" />

**Subgoal 1 — assemble the fixed part of the cost.** Signature cost is 720 CU; write locks are 300 CU
each, so two writable accounts contribute 600 CU.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" /> If you set
no loaded-accounts-data limit, the transaction is assumed to load the 64 MiB maximum, and at 8 CU per
32 KiB that is about 16,000 CU of overhead.<Cite src="anza-compute-units" /> Fixed overhead: roughly
17,300 CU before a single instruction executes.

**Subgoal 2 — price the lazy version.** Leave the CU limit at the default 200,000 for one non-builtin
instruction, set a price of 10,000 micro-lamports per CU.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" />
Prioritization fee = `10,000 × 200,000 / 1,000,000` = **2,000 lamports**.<Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" />
Reward = 2,000 + 2,500 (the non-burned half of the 5,000-lamport base fee) = 4,500. Cost ≈ 200,000 +
17,300 = 217,300. Priority ≈ `4,500 × 1,000,000 / 217,301` ≈ **20,709**.

**Subgoal 3 — price the maximally lazy version.** Request the 1,400,000 ceiling at the same price.
Prioritization fee = **14,000 lamports**. Reward = 16,500. Cost ≈ 1,417,300. Priority ≈
`16,500 × 1,000,000 / 1,417,301` ≈ **11,642**. You paid seven times as much and your priority
*halved*.

**Subgoal 4 — price the tuned version.** Simulate, read about 6,000 CU consumed, add the recommended
10% margin, request 6,600.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" /> Prioritization fee =
`ceil(10,000 × 6,600 / 1,000,000)` = **66 lamports**. Reward = 2,566. Cost ≈ 6,600 + 17,300 = 23,900.
Priority ≈ `2,566 × 1,000,000 / 23,901` ≈ **107,359**.

**Subgoal 5 — read the table.**

Tuning cut the priority fee by a factor of over two hundred against the lazy ceiling *and* raised
scheduling priority by roughly nine times. Both, from one instruction.

**Subgoal 6 — take the last free win.** Add `SetLoadedAccountsDataSizeLimit` sized to what you
actually load and the 16,000 CU of default overhead mostly evaporates, shrinking the denominator
again.<Cite src="anza-compute-units" /><Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" /> Anza recommends it
explicitly, both for priority today and against rejection risk as block limits
rise.<Cite src="anza-compute-units" />

<Aside kind="note">
Do not read the absolute priority numbers as portable. They depend on the base fee, the burn split and
the cost constants, all of which can change, and the leader's ranking is against whatever else is in
its buffer. What *is* portable is the shape: **your requested limit sits in the numerator once and the
denominator once, and the denominator has a floor you cannot go below.** That is why the curve turns
over.
</Aside>

## Where the compute actually goes

Two more things worth knowing about the runtime's meter, because they explain why tuned CU limits
are so much smaller than EVM instincts predict.

**The floor is one.** The smallest runtime operation costs 1 CU.<Cite src="anza-compute-units" /> Not
three, not five. Arithmetic and memory access on Solana are close to free by design, and the budget
gets spent on the things that are genuinely expensive: cross-program invocations, syscalls,
serialization and account handling.

**Structural work has published constants.** Agave's execution budget lists them: one CPI invocation
costs `DEFAULT_INVOCATION_COST` = 1,000 CU, dropping to 946 CU under SIMD-0339; heap costs
`DEFAULT_HEAP_COST` = 8 CU per 32 KiB page, with the heap frame settable between 32 KiB and 256 KiB in
1,024-byte multiples; the instruction stack depth is 5 (top level plus CPIs), raised to 9 under
SIMD-0268; and SBF-to-SBF call depth within a program is capped at 64 with a 4,096-byte stack
frame.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" />

Read the CPI number against the arithmetic floor. **One cross-program call costs about as much as a
thousand arithmetic operations.** On Solana, composition is the expensive thing and computation is
nearly free — which is close to the inverse of the shape you internalised optimising Solidity, where a
single storage read outweighs a great deal of arithmetic and an external call is comparatively
unremarkable.

### There is no cold and warm here

The absence is the interesting part.

The EVM prices the *first* access to an account or storage slot far above subsequent ones — the
[cold versus warm](/c/cold-vs-warm-access) distinction you measured yourself, where a cold `SLOAD`
came in at 2,100 gas against 100 warm.<Cite src="measured-forge-1-7-1" /> That mechanism exists
because the EVM discovers what it touches during execution: the first touch may require fetching state
the node did not have loaded, so it is charged for, and the warm price reflects that the work is
already done.

Solana has no equivalent, and could not have one. Every account the transaction will touch is named
before execution starts, so the loading is bulk work done up front and priced by *size* rather than by
order of first use: 8 CU per 32 KiB of loaded account data, defaulting to an assertion of the 64 MiB
maximum unless you say otherwise.<Cite src="anza-compute-units" /> There is no "first" access to price
differently, because there is no discovery.

That is the same trade this whole track keeps circling. **Declared access removes an entire pricing
mechanism from the runtime and replaces it with a size declaration you are responsible for making.**
The EVM's cold/warm rule is a clever way to price a problem Solana does not have; Solana's
loaded-data limit is a chore the EVM does not impose. Neither machine is being sloppy — they are
charging for different things because they learn about state at different times.

## Included means paid

Now the failure semantics, which are two-thirds familiar and one-third completely new.

The fee is deducted from the fee payer before execution, and if the transaction fails it is still
charged.<Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" /> Instructions are atomic — if any instruction fails,
the entire transaction fails and all state changes are reverted — but the fee is not
reverted.<Cite src="solana-docs-transactions" /> So far this is exactly the EVM's "a revert still
burns gas", with one refinement: the amount charged does not depend on how far execution got, because
the fee was computed from the requested limit before anything ran.

The third case is the one the EVM barely has. **A transaction that never gets included costs
nothing** — and produces no receipt, no logs, no on-chain record of any kind. It is not a cheaper
failure. It is an *invisible* one. Under load it is also the common one, and the entire final lesson
of this module is about it.

Three outcomes, then, where your EVM instincts have two:

### Reading your own numbers back

None of this needs to be taken on trust, because both quantities are published per transaction. The
documented profiling route is `getBlock`, whose transaction metadata carries **`computeUnitsConsumed`**
— what the runtime actually metered — alongside **`costUnits`**, the scheduler's cost
estimate.<Cite src="anza-compute-units" />

Pull both for a day of your own traffic and three questions answer themselves:

- **How much headroom am I paying for?** `requested limit − computeUnitsConsumed`, multiplied by your
  CU price, is money spent on compute nobody used.<Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" />
- **How much of my cost is not execution at all?** `costUnits − requested limit` is the signature,
  write-lock, instruction-data and loaded-data overhead.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" /> If
  that difference is large relative to your execution budget, the cheapest available optimisation is
  the account list, not the program.
- **How stable is my consumption?** The spread of `computeUnitsConsumed` for one instruction shape is
  what sizes your margin. A tight distribution justifies the documented 10%; a long tail says the
  margin belongs at a percentile of the observed distribution rather than at a round
  number.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" />

That last one is the difference between tuning and guessing, and it is the kind of measurement that
does not exist as a habit in EVM work — because there, over-setting the limit was free and nobody ever
had to know.

## A change worth watching

Everything above describes legacy and v0 transactions. The documentation carries a standing warning
that the upcoming v1 format works differently, and the differences invert the advice in this lesson
rather than refining it.<Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" />

Under v1 as documented: Compute Budget instructions are ignored for configuration — they execute as
no-ops that still cost 150 CU and still occupy an instruction slot — and the limits plus the priority
fee are set directly in the message config instead.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" /> The
priority fee becomes an absolute lamport total with no per-CU price, no multiplication and no
rounding.<Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" /> And the trap: **unset limits default to zero, not to
the values above**.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" />

That last clause deserves alarm. Today an omitted limit means "generous default"; under v1 it means
"no compute at all". The same code that merely overpays now would fail outright. SIMD-0296 is the
proposal behind the larger v1 payload, and its own status line reads Review with the feature key
unfilled — so treat the whole of this section as direction, verify activation yourself, and make sure
any transaction-building code you write has one place where limits are set rather than
five.<Cite src="simd-0296" />

<Check type="predict" concept="priority-fee-formula">
A trading bot sends bursts of transactions. Its builder sets `SetComputeUnitLimit` to 1,400,000 on
everything "so nothing ever runs out of compute", and sets the CU price from a provider's fee API.
Under load, the team observes that its landing rate falls even after doubling the CU price, while a
competitor lands consistently at what appears to be a lower price.

Diagnose it, and say what you would change first and how you would prove the change worked.

<Answer>
**The diagnosis: they are bidding against their own denominator.**

Priority is `reward × 1,000,000 / (cost + 1)`, and the requested CU limit appears in both
terms.<Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" /> At a 1,400,000 limit the cost is dominated by the
requested compute — roughly 1.4M against maybe 20,000 of everything else — so doubling the price
doubles the numerator against a denominator that is already 70 times larger than a tuned
transaction's. The competitor with a 30,000 limit gets a comparable priority from a fraction of the
fee, because their denominator is two orders of magnitude smaller.

Worse, they are paying for it. The prioritization fee is price × requested limit, and at 1,400,000 it
is seven times what a 200,000 request costs at the same price and over two hundred times a tuned
6,600.<Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" /><Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" /> Doubling the
price doubled a bill that was already inflated sevenfold.

**Change first: set the limit from simulation, not from the ceiling.** Simulate each transaction
shape, read the consumed CU, add the documented 10% margin, and set that.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" />
Do it per shape, not globally — a swap and a cancel have different profiles and one number for both
reintroduces the problem in miniature.

**Change second: set `SetLoadedAccountsDataSizeLimit`.** Leaving it unset asserts the 64 MiB maximum
and adds roughly 16,000 CU to cost for free.<Cite src="anza-compute-units" /> On a tuned transaction
that overhead can exceed the execution budget itself.

**How to prove it.** Instrument three numbers per transaction and compare distributions before and
after, on the same market conditions:

1. `computeUnitsConsumed` and `costUnits` from the transaction metadata in `getBlock`, which is the
   documented way to profile this.<Cite src="anza-compute-units" />
2. Lamports paid — priority fee is a formula you can recompute exactly from the limit and price you
   set, so any divergence means your builder is not sending what you think.
3. Landing rate: signatures submitted versus confirmed inside the blockhash lifetime.

**The trap in the fix.** A tuned limit is a real limit. Simulation runs against a recent bank, and a
program whose CU consumption depends on state — how many order book levels it walks, whether an
account needs initialising — can consume more when it lands than when it simulated. The 10% margin is
a margin, not a guarantee, and the correct response is to size the margin per instruction shape from
its observed distribution rather than to retreat to 1,400,000. This is the same lesson as
[simulation is not a guarantee](/c/simulation-is-not-a-guarantee), arriving with a price attached.

**One thing to be suspicious of.** The provider fee API is itself a vendor artifact rather than a
protocol quantity: every major RPC offers its own, they disagree, and practitioner analysis has
flagged both the divergence and the lock-in risk.<Cite src="helius-local-fee-markets" /> Recompute the
fee you are being advised to pay against the published formula before trusting it.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**Next**, the ceiling that all this competes under. Compute is limited per block, and — separately,
and much lower — per writable account. That second limit is the mechanical basis of local fee markets,
and its current value is not what most published material says it is.

**Two lessons out**, the reason a fee war on one account does not stay on that account: the scheduler
you now know ranks a single global queue with the formula above, so a big numerator anywhere reorders
everything.<Cite src="agave-greedy-scheduler" />

**And carry the two-sided rule.** *On Solana, asking for less makes you cheaper and faster at the same
time.* There is no equivalent statement about the EVM, and if you find yourself reaching for a
generous limit for safety margin alone, that is the EVM's refund rule talking through you.
