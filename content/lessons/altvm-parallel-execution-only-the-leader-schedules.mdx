An engineer joins a team shipping a Solana product and does what they have always done first: opens a
mempool explorer to watch pending transactions.

There isn't one. Not "there isn't a good one" — the object it would display does not exist. Solana's
own documentation states it flatly in the guide on retrying transactions: on Solana there is no
concept of a mempool, and transactions are routed to leaders directly instead of gossiped to
everyone.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-retry" />

That single absence rearranges a surprising amount. No pending pool means no global queue to inspect,
no `eth_getTransactionByHash` returning a transaction that has not landed, no
[mempool broadcast](/c/mempool-is-a-public-broadcast) for searchers to watch, and no shared view that
your monitoring can query. It also means the thing you actually care about — will my transaction be
in the next block — is decided inside one machine's process memory, for a few hundred milliseconds,
and then that machine stops being the one that matters.

This lesson follows a transaction from your client into that machine and out the other side.

## The path, end to end

**Client to RPC.** The overwhelming majority of transactions are submitted by proxy: the client calls
`sendTransaction` on an RPC node.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-retry" /> The alternative is speaking the
leader's transport directly with a TPU client, in which case rebroadcast and leader forwarding become
your own software's job.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-retry" />

The critical property of `sendTransaction`: it relays, and that is all it does. A successful response
returns the transaction id and says nothing about whether the transaction will be processed or
finalized.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-retry" /> Treating a 200 from `sendTransaction` as "submitted, now
poll for the receipt" is the single most common porting error from the EVM, and lesson seven is about
what it costs.

**RPC to leader.** Because the leader schedule for an epoch — about two days — is known in advance,
the RPC does not gossip. It sends the transaction directly to the current leader and the next
one.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-retry" /> By default it re-forwards every two seconds until the
transaction is finalized or its blockhash expires, and if its outstanding rebroadcast queue exceeds
10,000 transactions, newly submitted ones are dropped.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-retry" />

Stop on that number. **Your transaction can be discarded by your own RPC provider, before it ever
reaches a validator, because someone else's traffic filled a queue.** Nothing on-chain records this.
No fee is charged. `sendTransaction` already returned success.

**Into the leader.** Arriving traffic hits the leader's Transaction Processing Unit, which is
organised into five stages — Fetch, SigVerify, Banking, Proof of History Service, Broadcast — with the
Fetch stage sorting inbound packets across three ports: `tpu` for ordinary transactions, `tpu_vote`
for consensus votes, and `tpu_forwards` for packets a leader could not process and is passing
on.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-retry" />

Forwarding is deliberately weak. There is a limit on how much can be forwarded, and **each forward is
a single hop**: a transaction received on `tpu_forwards` is not forwarded again.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-retry" />
So "the leader was too busy, it will get passed along" buys you exactly one more attempt at exactly
one more validator.

<Aside kind="warn">
Ingress is not first-come-first-served. Leader connection capacity is rationed by stake:
stake-weighted quality of service gives a stake-weighted share of the leader's TPU capacity to peers,
with a validator holding 1% of stake entitled to transmit up to 1% of packets to the
leader.<Cite src="solana-swqos-guide" /> The documentation records the current allocation as covering
**80% of a leader's TPU capacity**, split proportionally among peers by stake, with configuration
options that can assign virtual stake to specific peers.<Cite src="solana-swqos-guide" /> The practical
effect is that *which RPC you send through* is an input to whether you land. Lesson seven takes this
apart properly.
</Aside>

## Inside the banking stage

Everything so far was transport. The scheduling happens in the Banking stage, and this is where the
account locks from lesson one get used.

Agave's structure at the version this lesson was checked against, v4.2.0, is a set of named threads
spawned per scheduler:<Cite src="agave-banking-stage-source" />

- **one scheduling thread** (`solBnkTxSched`), which holds the buffered set and decides everything;
- **four consume workers** (`solCoWorker00`–`03`) — `DEFAULT_NUM_WORKERS` is 4 — which execute the
  batches they are handed;
- **one vote worker**, kept separate from non-vote traffic;
- **eight check workers**, which do the pre-scheduling validity checks;
- **bounded channels of capacity 10,000** between the scheduler and each worker.

The scheduler's loop is the machine you should carry. It pops the highest-priority transaction from a
container, asks the lock table whether any worker can take it, and either dispatches it into a batch
or sets it aside — repeating until the block budget is spent, until it has scanned its per-pass limit
of 100,000 transactions, until every worker is saturated, or until the buffer is
empty.<Cite src="agave-greedy-scheduler" />

Two of those stopping conditions deserve names.

**Worker saturation is a compute-unit condition, not a queue-length condition.** The scheduler tracks
compute units in flight per thread and removes a thread from the schedulable set once its in-flight
CU reaches a per-thread target derived from the configured `target_scheduled_cus`, whose default is
the block unit constant divided by four, split again across the workers.<Cite src="agave-greedy-scheduler" />
A single very expensive transaction can therefore take a worker out of circulation on its own.

**Set-aside is not rejection.** Transactions that cannot be placed accumulate in an `unschedulables`
list and are pushed back into the priority queue when the pass ends — the pessimistic behaviour from
lesson one, visible in the control flow.<Cite src="agave-greedy-scheduler" />

### One queue, four workers

The architectural fact to hold above all the thread counts: **there is one priority-ordered view of
all buffered traffic, and one thread walking it.**

That was the point of the redesign. The client team's write-up of the original central scheduler
describes the predecessor as four non-vote threads pulling from a common queue and independently
attempting to lock transactions, with the root cause of its slowdowns being that no working thread
had any context about what the others were doing.<Cite src="anza-central-scheduler" /> The fix was to
add one thread that does have that context, and demote the workers to executing what they are
handed.<Cite src="anza-central-scheduler" />

Hold onto this, because it is the mechanism behind the most-repeated overclaim in the ecosystem. A
single global priority queue means priority is compared *across the whole buffer*, not within a lane.
Your transaction on a quiet account is ordered against every transaction on the busiest account in the
same list. Lesson six is entirely about what that does to "local" fee markets.

### Putting a clock on it

The pipeline is easier to reason about with durations attached, and the durations are derivable from
two published facts.

**Subgoal 1 — establish the slot budget.** Slots are configured to last about 400 ms and may run
between 400 and 600 ms in practice.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-confirmation" /> A validator produces
blocks for four consecutive slots before the next leader takes over.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-confirmation" />
So **a given leader owns roughly 1.6 to 2.4 seconds**, and then the machine holding your transaction
in its buffer stops being the one that can include it.

**Subgoal 2 — compare that to your retry cadence.** The RPC re-forwards every two
seconds.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-retry" /> Those two numbers are the same order of magnitude, which
is the whole reason submission is sent to the current *and next* leaders rather than just the
current: at a two-second cadence against a roughly two-second leader window, a retry that targets only
"whoever is leader right now" would frequently arrive during a handover.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-retry" />

**Subgoal 3 — establish the total budget.** A blockhash is valid for 150 slots, and because the age
is zero-indexed there are actually 151 blockhashes recent enough to process; at 400–600 ms slots that
is roughly **60 to 90 seconds** of usable life.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-confirmation" /> Divide by
the retry cadence: somewhere between thirty and forty-five delivery attempts, spread across on the
order of twenty to fifty distinct leaders.

**Subgoal 4 — subtract what you spend before you start.** Fetching a blockhash at the default
`finalized` commitment costs you time: the finalized block typically trails the most recent confirmed
block by at least 32 slots, which the documentation puts at roughly **13 seconds** of your expiration
window, and more when the cluster is unsteady.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-confirmation" /> Add human
signing latency and a wallet round trip and a naive client can burn a quarter of its budget before the
first packet leaves.

**Subgoal 5 — read off the design rule.** *Fetch the blockhash as late and as recently as your
fork-risk tolerance allows, then spend the entire remaining window resubmitting the same signed
bytes.* The documentation's own recommendation is `confirmed` for almost all requests: only a few
slots behind `processed`, with a very low chance of belonging to a dropped fork, where `processed`
gives you the longest window but exposes you to the roughly 5% of blocks that never get
finalized.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-confirmation" />

That last figure is worth carrying on its own. **About one block in twenty does not end up finalized**,
and a transaction whose blockhash came from an abandoned block can never be considered recent by any
block on the finalized chain.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-confirmation" /> On the EVM you learned to
worry about reorgs as a settlement risk after inclusion; here forks additionally poison the
*prerequisite* your transaction carries before inclusion.

## The scheduler is software, and it has already changed

Now a discipline point that matters more than any specific algorithm.

The central scheduler shipped in Agave v1.18 as an **opt-in** feature: disabled by default, enabled
with `--block-production-method central-scheduler`, with the previous
`thread-local-multi-iterator` method as the default and a stated intention to flip that
later.<Cite src="anza-central-scheduler" /> The same release changed how priority was computed, moving
from a formula dividing the priority fee by compute units to one incorporating requested compute and
the base fee.<Cite src="anza-central-scheduler" />

What is true today, read from the source rather than from any blog: Agave v4.2.0 exposes exactly two
values for `--block-production-method`, `central-scheduler` and `central-scheduler-greedy`. The greedy
one carries the `#[default]` attribute, and selecting the other logs a warning that it is deprecated
and will be removed in a future release.<Cite src="agave-validator-block-production" /> In the same
tag, the dispatch function routes *both* values to the same `GreedyScheduler`, with a source comment
saying exactly that.<Cite src="agave-banking-stage-source" /> The `thread-local-multi-iterator` option
the v1.18 blog documents is gone entirely.<Cite src="agave-validator-block-production" />

<Aside kind="stop">
**One thing this curriculum could not settle.** The greedy scheduler replaced an earlier prio-graph
dependency-ordering algorithm as the shipped default at some point between v1.18 and now. Search
results attribute the flip to a specific release in the 2.x series; that pin was not confirmed against
release notes for this course and is therefore not printed here. The *existence* of the change is well
supported and directly visible in the code above; the version is not. If you need the version, read
the release notes yourself — and notice that you had to, which is the actual lesson.
</Aside>

And Agave is not the only client. Firedancer is a second production implementation with its own
banking and scheduling design.<Cite src="firedancer-repo" /> Which makes any sentence beginning
"Solana's scheduler…" a category error. There is no such object. There are clients, each of which
schedules, and the protocol validates the *block*, not the algorithm that produced it.

## What this changes about your product

**Pre-confirmation is a different animal.** Solana leaders build blocks continuously as transactions
arrive rather than assembling one at a fixed interval, which is why confirmation can be close to a
single round trip between client and leader.<Cite src="lightspeed-local-fee-markets" /> The same
property means a high-fee transaction that arrives late in a slot may miss it regardless of
what it paid, because the space it wanted has already been recorded.<Cite src="lightspeed-local-fee-markets" />
Fee is a ranking input, not a guarantee of inclusion.

**Your observability has to be built from confirmations, not from a pool.** There is no equivalent of
"how many transactions like mine are waiting". What you can measure is landing rate — signatures
submitted versus signatures confirmed inside the blockhash lifetime — and that is the metric to
instrument from day one, because it is the one that degrades under exactly the conditions where your
users complain.

**Leader identity is an operational variable.** Since the schedule is known an epoch ahead, both the
transaction's route and its odds change over a timescale you can predict rather than a random one.
Two operators running different validator software, different hardware and different stake-weighted
peering arrangements will not give your traffic the same treatment, and the schedule tells you in
advance which of them your next few seconds belong to. Nothing equivalent exists on Ethereum, where
the proposer for the next slot is known but the mempool is common property and reaching it is not the
hard part.

**RPC topology becomes part of the design.** The retry guide is explicit that nodes serving
data-intensive requests can fall behind and are poorly suited to also forwarding transactions, and
recommends dedicated nodes that only handle `sendTransaction` for latency-sensitive
work.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-retry" /> It also names a failure mode with no EVM analogue: an RPC
*pool* where one backend runs ahead of another, so a blockhash fetched from the advanced node is
unrecognised by the lagging node you submit through, and the transaction is dropped before it goes
anywhere.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-retry" /> Preflight checks catch that one; skipping preflight
hides it.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-retry" />

<Aside kind="note">
The retry guide's description of the RPC-to-leader hop says the node converts the transaction into a
UDP packet before forwarding.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-retry" /> The stake-weighted quality of
service documentation, on the same site, describes leader ingress in terms of QUIC connections and a
QUIC TPU port.<Cite src="solana-swqos-guide" /> These two first-party pages disagree because one of
them predates the transport change and has not been fully revised. Where two pages from the same
publisher conflict, prefer the one describing the mechanism you can independently confirm — here, the
one whose configuration flags you can read in a validator's help output.
</Aside>

<Check type="predict" concept="banking-stage-pipeline">
Your team ports an EVM monitoring stack to Solana. Three of its components are:

1. a mempool watcher that alerts when a competitor's transaction targeting your pool appears pending;
2. a "stuck transaction" detector that alerts when a submitted transaction stays pending for more than
   thirty seconds;
3. a fee-bumping worker that replaces an under-priced pending transaction with a higher-fee copy.

What happens to each, and what replaces it?

<Answer>
**1 — the mempool watcher has no input and cannot be repaired.** There is no gossiped pending pool to
watch; transactions go to the current and next leaders directly.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-retry" />
The information it consumed is not merely hard to get, it is not published. What partially replaces it
is *post-hoc*: read confirmed blocks and detect the competitor's activity after it lands, which is a
different product with a different latency budget. Anything promising you a Solana mempool feed is
selling a view of one operator's own inbound traffic, which is a sample, not the pool.

**2 — the stuck detector has the wrong clock and the wrong terminal state.** "Pending" is not a state
the network reports. The correct construction is: capture `lastValidBlockHeight` when you fetch the
blockhash, poll block height, and treat *blockhash expiry* as the terminal
outcome.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-retry" /> The window is short by EVM standards — a blockhash is
valid for 150 slots, roughly 60 to 90 seconds depending on how slot times are running — so a
thirty-second threshold is not a warning, it is half the total budget.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-confirmation" />

**3 — the fee-bumping worker is the dangerous one, because it looks like it works.** There is no
nonce-ordered queue and no replace-by-fee: a Solana transaction is not superseded by a higher-fee
copy, so the "replacement" is a second, independently valid transaction. If both reach a
leader before the shared blockhash expires, both execute. On the EVM the [nonce](/c/nonce-gap) makes
replacement safe by construction; here nothing does.

What replaces it is a *resubmission* loop of the identical signed transaction — same signature, so
duplicate execution is prevented by the runtime's own deduplication — repeated at a chosen interval
until the blockhash expires, with `maxRetries` set to 0 so the RPC's generic retry logic does not
fight your own.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-retry" /> If you want a different fee, you must let the
first transaction expire before signing a new one, and the retry guide's warning is exactly this:
confirm the original blockhash has expired before re-signing anything.<Cite src="solana-cookbook-retry" />

**The pattern behind all three.** Each component depended not on a chain feature but on a *shared,
queryable, ordered waiting area*. Solana has no waiting area, so the port is not a translation — it is
a redesign around a different question: not "where is my transaction in the queue" but "has it landed
yet, and how much of its blockhash lifetime is left".
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**Next**, the currency the scheduler ranks with. Compute units are not gas with a different name: you
are billed on the budget you *request*, unused units are not refunded, and the request itself changes
your position in the queue you just watched the scheduler walk.<Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" />

**Three lessons out**, this lesson's one-global-queue observation becomes the honest correction to
local fee markets — the argument that shared workers and shared block space leak contention across
accounts that never touch each other.<Cite src="helius-local-fee-markets" />

**And carry the shape.** Ethereum publishes a waiting area and lets everyone see and bid into it.
Solana publishes a leader schedule and lets you send directly to whoever is next. Everything else in
this module — landing rates, RPC choice, retry logic, fee estimation — is downstream of which of those
two designs you are standing in.
