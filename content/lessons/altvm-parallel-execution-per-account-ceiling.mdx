On 26 August 2026 I opened four first-party pages about the same limit and got three different answers.

The Solana documentation's compute budget page lists `MAX_WRITABLE_ACCOUNT_UNITS` as **12,000,000**,
alongside `MAX_BLOCK_UNITS` of 60,000,000 and a note that SIMD-0286 *proposes* raising the block limit
to 100,000,000.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" />

The Solana Foundation's own upgrade page says the 100M block limit went live on mainnet on 29 July
2026 at the start of epoch 1009, and that the per-writable-account cap **stayed at 12M**, so a hot
account's share of a block fell from 20% to 12%.<Cite src="solana-100m-cu-blocks" />

SIMD-0286's table agrees: 12M before, 12M after.<Cite src="simd-0286" />

And Agave v4.2.0 — the client version the public mainnet RPC reports running — declares
`MAX_WRITABLE_ACCOUNT_UNITS: u64 = 24_000_000`.<Cite src="agave-block-cost-limits" />

One of these is the number the network actually enforces. This lesson finds out which, because the
method is more useful than the answer, and because the answer changes what you should build.

## What the limit is

Independently of how much compute a whole block may contain, there is a cap on how much compute may
be attributed to any *single writable account* within one block.

The enforcement lives in the leader's cost tracker, which keeps a map from public key to accumulated
cost — `cost_by_writable_accounts` — and checks each account against the account limit as it adds a
transaction's cost.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" /> The relationship between the two limits
is asserted at compile time: the writable-account limit must not exceed the block
limit.<Cite src="agave-block-cost-limits" />

The reason it exists is the whole architecture of this track. A block's compute limit exists to bound
how long a leader may make everyone else spend replaying it. But compute against *one* writable
account cannot be parallelised — lesson one showed that conflicting work serializes onto a single
worker thread. So a block that spent its entire budget on one account would be a block that every
validator must replay strictly serially, at the pace of one core. The per-account cap is a bound on
worst-case *serial* execution, which is a different quantity from total work, and that is why it needs
its own constant.

SIMD-0286 says this in one sentence: keeping the writable-account units unchanged while raising the
block units "allows for additional parallel capacity".<Cite src="simd-0286" /> SIMD-0306's drawbacks
section says the converse: raising the per-account limit increases worst-case serialized execution,
which could lengthen block verification and slot times.<Cite src="simd-0306" />

## Resolving the disagreement

Four documents, three numbers. Rather than pick the most authoritative-looking page, read the
mechanism and then read the network.

**Step 1 — find where the constant is turned into an enforced limit.** In Agave v4.2.0 the bank
computes its cost-tracker limits from a function that takes a single boolean — whether the
`raise_block_limits_to_100m` feature is active. If it is, **both** the account cost and the block cost
are multiplied by 100 and divided by 60; if not, both pass through
unchanged.<Cite src="agave-slot-params" />

That is the crux, and it is easy to miss. The 100M feature does not only raise the block limit. It
scales the per-account limit by the identical factor, which by construction *holds the ratio between
them constant*.

**Step 2 — substitute the constants.** The baseline pair in the same release is
`MAX_BLOCK_UNITS = 60,000,000` and `MAX_WRITABLE_ACCOUNT_UNITS = 24,000,000` — a ratio of exactly
40%.<Cite src="agave-block-cost-limits" /> Apply the ×100/60 scaling and you get 100,000,000 and
**40,000,000**. Still 40%.

Both of those numbers come straight out of SIMD-0306, whose design is not a new constant at all but a
rule: set the per-account limit to 40% of the block limit, which yields 24M under a 60M block and 40M
under a 100M block.<Cite src="simd-0306" />

**Step 3 — check whether the features are actually active, on the network, today.** Feature gates on
Solana are accounts. The gate ids are declared in the client, with human-readable descriptions
attached: `P1BCUMpAC7V2GRBRiJCNUgpMyWZhoqt3LKo712ePqsz` is described as "SIMD-0286: Raise block limit
to 100M", and `htsptAwi2yRoZH83SKaUXykeZGtZHgxkS2QwW1pssR8` as "SIMD-0306: Raise account CU limit to
40% max".<Cite src="agave-feature-set" />

Read them with `getAccountInfo`:<Cite src="solana-docs-rpc-getaccountinfo" />

```bash
curl -sS https://api.mainnet-beta.solana.com -X POST \
  -H 'Content-Type: application/json' \
  -d '{"jsonrpc":"2.0","id":1,"method":"getAccountInfo",
       "params":["htsptAwi2yRoZH83SKaUXykeZGtZHgxkS2QwW1pssR8",{"encoding":"base64"}]}'
```

A feature account is nine bytes owned by the feature program: a one-byte `Option` tag followed by a
little-endian `u64` activation slot. Tag `0x00` means not activated. Tag `0x01` means activated at the
slot that follows.

**Step 4 — validate the method before trusting it.** Decode the SIMD-0286 account first, because its
answer is already published. Queried on 26 August 2026 it returned tag `0x01` and slot
**435,888,000**. Divide by the 432,000 slots in an epoch and you get **epoch 1009** — exactly the
epoch the Foundation's upgrade page names.<Cite src="solana-100m-cu-blocks" /> The decode is sound.

**Step 5 — read the answer.** The SIMD-0306 account returned tag `0x01` and slot **379,296,000**,
which is **epoch 878** — many months *before* the block-limit raise.

So the live configuration on mainnet, as of this lesson's verification date, is a block limit of
100,000,000 CU and a per-writable-account limit of **40,000,000 CU**, and the per-account share of a
block is 40%.

**Do not carry the 40M forward as a fact about Solana.** Carry the procedure. The block limit has gone

release;<Cite src="agave-feature-set" /> and query the feature accounts on the cluster you care about

correct on the day you do it — which is more than any published article can promise.
</Aside>

## Why the ceiling is the whole basis of local fee markets

Now the economics, and the contrast this track exists for.

Ethereum's base fee is one price for one commodity. It rises when blocks run above target and falls
when they run below, and it applies identically to every transaction in the block regardless of what
that transaction touches.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> A popular mint congests the chain for a lending
protocol that shares no state with it whatsoever, because the two are bidding for the same undivided
resource. You know this shape from the [EIP-1559 fee fields](/c/eip-1559-fee-fields): one base fee,
one market, one price signal.

Solana has no protocol base fee that moves at all — `lamports_per_signature` is a flat per-signature
charge, and the variable component is a first-price bid you set
yourself.<Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" /> Contention is bounded per writable account by the
ceiling above. So the auction that matters is not "who gets into the block" but "who gets one of the
40M compute units attributable to *this* account", and that auction is, mechanically, separate from
the block-wide one.

That structural difference is real and it shows up in data. Practitioner analysis compares median and
average priority fees as a diagnostic: fees on uncontested state should stay low while fees on
contested state surge, dragging the mean away from the median. In November 2024 the average fee for
non-vote transactions reached an all-time high above 0.0003 SOL while the median held at 0.00000861
SOL — about 35 times lower.<Cite src="helius-local-fee-markets" /> A single global price cannot
produce that spread.

### What is bounded, and what is not

Four block-level limits sit alongside each other, and confusing them produces confident wrong capacity
plans. As enumerated in SIMD-0286's terminology section and the client's constants:<Cite src="simd-0286" /><Cite src="agave-block-cost-limits" />

  alt="Four block-level limits: Max Block Units bounds total compute attributable to a block; Max Writable Account Units bounds compute attributable to any one writable account; Max Vote Units bounds compute consumed by simple vote transactions; and Max Block Accounts Data Size Delta bounds how much account data may be newly allocated in a block."
/>

The vote limit is the one people forget exists. Consensus votes are transactions and they consume
block capacity; a separate ceiling keeps them from crowding out user traffic and keeps user traffic
from crowding out consensus.<Cite src="simd-0286" /> The data-size delta is the one that bites
launches: it caps *allocation*, so a mint that creates one new account per buyer is bounded by how
many accounts a block may allocate, not only by compute.

And one limit that does not exist: there is no per-account cap on *reads*. Read locks are compatible
across threads, so an account that thousands of transactions read but nobody writes is not contended
at all and consumes no per-account ceiling. Making an account read-only where you can is the cheapest
contention fix available, and it is a data-modelling decision, not a tuning one.

<Aside kind="warn">
**A proposal you will be told is live, and should not teach as live.** SIMD-0110 proposes dynamic
write-lock fees: track an exponential moving average of each contended account's CU utilisation and
raise the cost of write-locking accounts that stay above a target, with the write-lock cost rate
falling again when utilisation drops.<Cite src="helius-local-fee-markets" /> It is the mechanism that
would make Solana's per-account pricing genuinely *dynamic* rather than merely bounded — the thing
people often assume already exists. This curriculum's research could not confirm mainnet activation,
and the honest position is that it is a proposal. If it matters to your design, resolve it the same
way this lesson resolved the per-account cap: find the feature gate id in the client and query the
feature account.<Cite src="agave-feature-set" /><Cite src="solana-docs-rpc-getaccountinfo" />
</Aside>

## Sizing your own application against the ceiling

The ceiling stops being abstract the moment you divide by your own transaction's cost.

**Subgoal 1 — measure your cost, not your compute.** The quantity the cap counts is the scheduler's
*transaction cost*, not the CU your program consumed: execution budget requested, plus 720 CU per
signature, plus 300 CU per writable account, plus instruction data, plus loaded-account-data
size.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" /> Read `costUnits` alongside `computeUnitsConsumed` from
transaction metadata to get the real figure.<Cite src="anza-compute-units" />

**Subgoal 2 — decide which account is the bottleneck.** Take every writable key in your transaction
and ask which one *every* user's transaction also writes. The cap applies per key, so a shared pool
account, a global config, a single mint's supply field — each is its own ceiling, and your throughput
is bounded by the tightest one.

**Subgoal 3 — divide.** With a per-account limit of 40M CU and a transaction cost of `C`, at most
`40,000,000 / C` transactions touching that account fit in a block.<Cite src="agave-slot-params" /> At
40,000 CU of cost that is 1,000 per block; at 120,000 CU it is 333.

**Subgoal 4 — convert to a rate.** Slots run at about 400 ms.<Cite src="solana-100m-cu-blocks" /> So
1,000 per block is roughly 2,500 per second against that one account, and 333 per block is roughly
830 per second.

**Subgoal 5 — sanity-check against the block.** The same 40M is 40% of the 100M block limit, so if
your hot account genuinely saturates its ceiling, you are consuming close to half the block on your
own and everything else on the network is competing for the rest. Being near your per-account ceiling
is not a comfortable place to be even when it is legal.

**Subgoal 6 — notice what the number does *not* tell you.** Fitting under the ceiling says nothing
about *ordering*. Under the ceiling, all your users' transactions serialize onto one worker thread and
are ranked by a priority formula, and the losers do not fail — they wait for the next pass, and then
for the pass after that, until their blockhash expires. The ceiling caps throughput; the scheduler
decides who gets it.

A launchpad is preparing a mint. Every purchase writes: the mint account (supply), a shared treasury
account (proceeds), the buyer's new token account, and the buyer's wallet. Simulation puts each

The team plans for 100,000 buyers in the first minute. Will it work, what breaks first, and what would
you change?

<Answer>
**It will not work, and the binding constraint is a per-account ceiling — but not the one people
usually name.**

**The arithmetic.** Two accounts are shared by every buyer: the mint and the treasury. Each is capped
independently, and each caps at the same place. At 55,000 CU per purchase, one shared account admits
`40,000,000 / 55,000 ≈ 727` purchases per block.<Cite src="agave-slot-params" /> At roughly 400 ms
per slot that is about 150 slots in a minute, so **on the order of 109,000 purchases per minute** is
the theoretical ceiling — if the launchpad is the only thing on the network competing for that
account, if every block is available, and if every purchase lands first try.

**So the plan is at roughly 92% of a theoretical maximum**, which in practice means it fails. Real
blocks are shared, some slots are skipped, and the buyers are not arriving smoothly — they arrive in
the first three seconds. Three seconds is about seven or eight slots, or on the order of 5,500
purchases of capacity against a demand of 100,000.

**What actually breaks, in order:**

1. **Nothing reverts.** The excess purchases are not rejected by the program. They are not
   scheduled — set aside, returned to the buffer, tried again.
2. **Priority fees on the mint account spike**, because that is the only lever a buyer has, and the
   auction is first-price with no published clearing price.<Cite src="solana-docs-fee-structure" />
3. **Blockhashes expire.** Buyers who cannot outbid see nothing at all: no receipt, no error, no fee
   charged. Support gets "the site is broken", which is the subject of the last lesson in this module.

**What to change.**

- **Split the treasury.** Proceeds do not need to accumulate in one account during the mint. Shard it
  — by buyer key prefix, by time bucket, by anything the client can derive — and sweep afterwards.
  That removes one of the two ceilings entirely, at the cost of client-side derivation and a sweep
  job. This is exactly the tax from lesson two, paid deliberately.
- **Interrogate the mint account.** If supply must be tracked in one place, that account is a hard
  ceiling you cannot design around, and the honest answer is that the mint must be spread over time
  rather than sold in one minute. A queue with a fair ordering mechanism is a product decision, not
  an infrastructure one.
- **Cut per-purchase cost.** Every 10% off `C` is 10% more throughput under the same ceiling. Tuning
  the requested CU limit and the loaded-accounts-data-size limit is the cheapest available
  win.<Cite src="anza-compute-units" />

**Verify the ceiling before you plan against it.** The 40M figure above is what the live network
enforced on this lesson's verification date, derived from the client's cost limits and confirmed
against the activation state of the feature gate.<Cite src="agave-slot-params" /><Cite src="agave-feature-set" />
Several widely-cited first-party pages still print 12M.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" /><Cite src="solana-100m-cu-blocks" />
A launch plan is exactly the situation where you re-run the check yourself rather than trusting a
number from a lesson written in August.
</Answer>

## What this unlocks

**Next**, the honest correction. Everything above describes a mechanism that *should* isolate one
account's congestion from everyone else's, and it partly does — but the scheduler you met in lesson
three ranks a single global queue over shared worker threads and shared block space, and that leaks.
The next lesson is about how much.

**And carry the audit, not the constant.** Two limits, one client release, one feature-gate lookup,
one RPC call. When someone tells you what Solana's per-account compute ceiling is — including this
lesson — the correct response is to spend fifteen minutes finding out whether it is still true.
