Most programs should not be written in Pinocchio, and the honest version of this module ends by saying
so plainly rather than by being enthusiastic.

The reason is arithmetic, not taste. Everything this module has described removes a **fixed cost paid
once per invocation**. A fixed cost divided by a large workload is a rounding error; divided by a tiny
one it is the whole bill. That single sentence decides the question for almost every program, and the
rest of this lesson is about running it properly and about what the saving is actually for — because it
is not what most teams think.

## The shape of the saving

Take the two extremes from the previous lesson and hold them next to each other.

`InitializeImmutableOwner` sets a flag. It fell from 1,404 CU to 38 — to 2.7% of its original
cost.<Cite src="simd-0266-proposal" /> `InitializeMultisig2` iterates over signer accounts and does real
work. It fell from 2,826 CU to 318 — to 11.2%.<Cite src="simd-0266-proposal" /> Same library, same
techniques, same authors, one instruction apart in the same file. The difference is entirely how much
the instruction was doing.

Extrapolate the trend line honestly and it goes somewhere uncomfortable for the enthusiast case. An
instruction doing 90,000 CU of real work has roughly the same few thousand CU of removable overhead as
one doing 400. In the first case that is a saving of a few per cent. In the second it is 90%.

So the question is never "is Pinocchio faster". It is:

**What fraction of this instruction's compute is paid before my first line of logic runs, and how many
times per block does that happen?**

Both halves matter. A program with 80% overhead that is invoked twice a day is not worth rewriting. A
program with 5% overhead invoked ten thousand times a block might be.

## Measure before you reach for the sharp tool

This is the Track 05 discipline unchanged, with different instruments. Three of them, in increasing
order of effort.

**The invocation log.** Every program invocation reports `consumed X of Y compute units`. That is your
total, free, on every transaction you have ever sent.<Cite src="simd-0266-proposal" />

**The syscall.** `sol_log_compute_units()` logs consumption at a specific point in
execution.<Cite src="helius-optimizing-solana-programs" /> Call it as the first statement of your
handler and again as the last. The delta is your logic. The difference between that delta and the
invocation total is the overhead a lower-level entrypoint could address. That subtraction *is* the
business case, and it takes an afternoon.

**A local harness.** For per-instruction numbers you can put in CI, use an execution harness rather than
a validator. p-token's own test suite depends on Mollusk for exactly
this.<Cite src="p-token-program-manifest" /> Making CU a tracked number in CI is more valuable than any
one-off optimisation, because it turns a regression into a failing build instead of an incident.

<Aside kind="warn">
Vendor advice worth repeating, from a source with every commercial incentive to tell you to optimise:
for most applications the risk of introducing critical bugs outweighs the performance benefit, and
reaching for the lowest-level techniques will most likely land you in the trap of premature
optimisation.<Cite src="helius-optimizing-solana-programs" /> When the people selling infrastructure
tell you not to do the thing, listen.
</Aside>

## Compute units are not gas, and the difference decides your argument

Before you can say what the saving is worth, you have to be precise about what compute units are, and
here the EVM intuition actively misleads.

**Fees do not scale with what you consume.** A Solana transaction's base fee is 5,000 lamports **per
signature**, split half burned and half to the validator, with no compute term in
it.<Cite src="solana-docs-fees" /> The optional prioritization fee is
`ceil(compute_unit_price * compute_unit_limit / 1,000,000)` lamports, and all of it goes to the
validator.<Cite src="solana-docs-fees" />

Read that formula again. The multiplier is `compute_unit_limit` — the limit you **requested** — not the
units you used. The documentation says so outright: the priority fee is determined by the requested
compute unit limit, not the actual number consumed, and if you set the limit too high or leave the
default you pay for compute you never used.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" />

That inverts the EVM habit completely. On Ethereum, unused gas is refunded and the limit is a safety
ceiling. Here the limit is the **price tag**. Optimising your program to consume less changes nothing
about what anyone pays until the transaction builder also lowers the request. The recommended
procedure is explicit: simulate the transaction to measure consumption, then set the limit to that
value plus about ten per cent.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" />

**And fees are the wrong argument anyway.** At 5,000 lamports a signature, the money involved is not
what makes compute optimisation worth an engineer's quarter.

## The prize is headroom

Three ceilings decide what a Solana program can do, and cheaper instructions buy room under all three.

**1,400,000 CU per transaction.** `MAX_COMPUTE_UNIT_LIMIT`, the hard cap on what one transaction may
consume.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" /> This is the composability ceiling: how many hops a
router can chain, how many legs an arbitrage can have, how many protocols a single user action can
touch atomically. It is also, incidentally, where
[the compute budget](/c/compute-unit-budget) stops being an accounting detail and becomes a product
constraint — a route that does not fit is a route you cannot offer.

**12,000,000 CU per writable account per block.** `MAX_WRITABLE_ACCOUNT_UNITS` is the throughput ceiling
for any hot account — an AMM pool, an orderbook, an oracle feed — because every transaction writing it
serialises against every other and they share this budget.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" /> This
is [the per-account write cap](/c/per-account-write-cu-cap) you met when the local fee market was
introduced, seen from the other side: there it explained why your transactions were being priced out of
a contended account, and here it is the resource your own instruction's cost consumes on behalf of every
user who touches it. Cheapening the instruction is the only lever that raises the ceiling without
changing the account layout.

**The block limit**, which recently moved and the per-account cap did not. SIMD-0286 raised
`MAX_BLOCK_UNITS` from 60,000,000 to 100,000,000 CU, activated on mainnet at the start of epoch 1009 on
29 July 2026, while max writable account compute units stayed at 12,000,000 and the block accounts data
size delta stayed at 100 MB.<Cite src="solana-100m-cu-blocks" /><Cite src="simd-0286-proposal" />

That combination is the one to internalise. Total block capacity rose by two thirds; the per-account
cap did not move. **Local contention got relatively tighter, not looser.** If your product's bottleneck
is one hot writable account, the block-limit increase did nothing for you at all, and reducing the
compute cost of writing that account is one of the few levers that does.

<Aside kind="warn">
Solana's own documentation currently disagrees with itself here: the compute-budget reference page still
lists `MAX_BLOCK_UNITS` as 60,000,000 and describes SIMD-0286 as a
proposal,<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" /> while the upgrade page records the change as live
since 29 July 2026.<Cite src="solana-100m-cu-blocks" /> Reference pages lag activations. Check the
feature-gate status on the cluster you are targeting before you build a capacity model on either number.
</Aside>

### The subtlety that undoes a naive headroom argument

There is a trap here that will bite anyone who reasons only from execution costs.

The validator's scheduler decides what fits in a block using a **pre-execution cost model**, separate
from the CU metering that happens during execution. Its `programs_execution_cost` component is the CU
limit from the compute budget instruction, or the default when none is set — plus a signature cost, a
`WRITE_LOCK_UNITS` charge of 300 CU per writable account, an instruction-data-bytes term, and a loaded-
accounts-data-size term.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" />

So the packing decision uses the *requested* limit. If no `SetComputeUnitLimit` is present, the default
is computed per instruction — 200,000 CU for each non-builtin instruction, 3,000 for each builtin, the
whole thing clamped to 1,400,000.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" />

The consequence is direct and frequently missed: **making your program cheaper buys nothing against the
block and per-account caps until the transactions that call it request less.** A wallet that never sets
a compute unit limit reserves 200,000 CU per instruction whether the instruction burns 76 CU or 76,000.
The optimisation and the request have to move together.

### Working the throughput argument

This is the calculation to put in a design document, because it converts "our swap is cheaper" into a
number a product manager can act on.

**Subgoal 1 — identify the contended account.** For an AMM, every swap writes the pool state account.
That account is the serialisation point, so `MAX_WRITABLE_ACCOUNT_UNITS` at 12,000,000 CU per block is
the ceiling that matters, not the block limit.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" />

**Subgoal 2 — establish the per-swap cost as the scheduler sees it.** Suppose a swap transaction
requests a limit of 100,000 CU. Then roughly 120 swaps against that pool can be scheduled per block
before the account's budget is exhausted.

**Subgoal 3 — apply a saving, and apply it to the request too.** Suppose optimisation brings real
consumption to 40,000 CU and the client re-simulates and requests 44,000. The pool now supports around
270 swaps per block — a 2.2× throughput increase on the same account, with no protocol change and no
extra liquidity.

**Subgoal 4 — sanity-check against block time.** Blocks target 400 ms, so per-block figures multiply by
roughly 2.5 to become per-second figures.<Cite src="solana-100m-cu-blocks" /> Convert before you present
them, because "270 per block" persuades nobody and "675 swaps per second on our busiest pool" persuades
everybody.

**Subgoal 5 — state the argument in the right currency.** Not "our swap is 60% cheaper". *"Our pool
serves 2.2 times as many users per block at peak, and peak is exactly when we were losing them."* Same
engineering, and only one of those sentences survives contact with a roadmap discussion.

## Where the overhead is worth removing

Frequency decides, so the answer points at a narrow class of programs.

**Yes, plausibly:** token-like primitives; oracle updates; DEX and AMM hot paths; anything invoked by
CPI inside other people's transactions, where your cost is a tax on somebody else's composition. The
extremes are instructive — one write-up reports oracle updates optimised to 143 CU on one platform and
21 CU on another.<Cite src="helius-p-token" /> Those are programs where the fixed overhead was hundreds
of times the work.

**No:** governance, admin and configuration instructions; anything invoked by a human rather than by a
bot; anything where your own logic already dominates the budget; anything a small team maintains without
funding for repeated external review. Recall what the easiest possible case cost to make safe — four
external audit reports and a formal-verification report, plus differential fuzzing against replayed
mainnet traffic — for a program reimplementing a format that already existed and could be checked
against a reference implementation.<Cite src="anza-security-audits-spl" /><Cite src="simd-0266-proposal" />

**Maybe, at a smaller granularity:** the decision is per-instruction, and sometimes per call site. The
satellite CPI helper crates — `pinocchio-system`, `pinocchio-token`, `pinocchio-token-2022`,
`pinocchio-associated-token-account` — can be used from an otherwise conventional program to make its
hot cross-program calls cheaper without touching its account model, its constraints or its
IDL.<Cite src="pinocchio-crate-versions" /> That is the highest-value, lowest-risk version of this
trade, and it is the one most teams should actually consider.

## You already got the win

The most important sentence in this module is the least exciting one.

The single most frequent thing your program does by CPI is almost certainly a token operation, and that
operation now costs a small fraction of what it did — `Transfer` at 76 CU against 4,645, `TransferChecked`
at 105 against 6,200 — because the implementation was replaced behind the address you already
call.<Cite src="simd-0266-proposal" /><Cite src="solana-upgrades-p-token" /> You did not write a line of
Pinocchio to get it, and you never will.

That is what "most developers should benefit from Pinocchio without writing Pinocchio" means concretely.
It is not a consolation prize. It is the largest compute saving available to your program, it arrived
without a migration, and it is already in production.

What is left for you is the follow-on work: re-tune the compute-unit limits your clients request, so the
saving becomes headroom instead of unused reservation, and look at whether `batch` collapses several
token CPIs in your hot instruction into one — because at 1,000 CU per invocation, the call is now more
expensive than the callee.<Cite src="solana-docs-compute-budget" /><Cite src="simd-0266-proposal" />
Both are days of work, not quarters, and both capture more than a rewrite would.

**Step 3 — check the free win first.** Two questions before any rewrite. Does the transaction set a
`SetComputeUnitLimit`, or is it reserving the 200,000 CU default per instruction? If it is defaulting,

margin recovers about 10% of throughput for a one-line

3,000 of 180,000, the Pinocchio proposal is answered: it is a 1.7% improvement in exchange for
hand-writing every account check in the protocol's most dangerous instruction. If the internal split
shows 120,000 CU in oracle-price maths or account iteration, that is the target, and it is an algorithm
problem the framework has nothing to do with.

**Step 5 — reframe what the project is.** The goal is not "make `liquidate` cheaper". It is "raise
liquidations-per-block on the market account", and the levers in order of value are: split the shared
state so liquidations do not all contend on one writable account; reduce the requested limit to
simulated-plus-margin; batch the token CPIs; then optimise the maths. Replacing the entrypoint is last,
and it is worth the smallest amount.

**The number that would change my mind.** If measurement showed that the instruction's own logic
accounted for under 20,000 CU and the remaining 160,000 was framework overhead, the arithmetic would
invert and the rewrite would be the highest-value option available. That is not what a 180,000 CU
instruction looks like — but it is the measurement that decides it, and it costs an afternoon to take.

## What this unlocks

You can now answer the question this module was named for, in the form it should be asked: not "should
we use Pinocchio" but "what fraction of this instruction is paid before my logic runs, how often does
that happen, which ceiling binds us, and can we fund the review that hand-written validation requires".
You can price the answer in throughput rather than in fees, and you can defend it with two numbers you
measured rather than a percentage you read.

You also leave with a pattern that is not about Solana. The dominant cost in a hot path was fixed
per-invocation overhead that appeared in nobody's profile because it appeared in nobody's source. The fix
was deployable underneath the callers rather than in front of them, so it reached everyone at once. And
the moment it landed, the bottleneck moved to the cost of *calling* the thing that had just been
optimised. Every part of that generalises, and the reflection at the end of this module asks you to find
the same shape in a system you actually work on.
