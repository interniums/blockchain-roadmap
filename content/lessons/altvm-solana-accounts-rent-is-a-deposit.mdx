Write one new storage slot on Ethereum and you pay 20,000 gas, plus 2,100 if the slot is
cold.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /> Then you pay nothing, ever again, and every full node on
the network stores those 32 bytes for as long as the chain exists. That is
[the state growth problem](/c/state-growth-problem) stated as an invoice: a one-off payment buying
an unbounded stream of storage from thousands of machines.

Solana priced the same thing differently, and the difference is not the number. It is the *kind* of
payment. Creating an account requires depositing lamports in proportion to its size, and the deposit
comes back in full when the account is closed.

That single change — fee to deposit — is worth ten minutes on its own, because it decides who funds
your users' accounts, what "clean up" means in your protocol, and why a design that would be merely
inefficient on Ethereum is arithmetically impossible here.

## The formula, and what it costs to be wrong about it

An account must hold a minimum lamport balance proportional to its data size to remain on
chain.<Cite src="solana-docs-accounts" /> The published formula is:

which the documentation gives exactly that way, in a table of account
limits.<Cite src="solana-docs-accounts" /> Three things in it deserve attention. The **128 bytes of

permanence rather than two years.

Every row is the published formula applied to a published size — the deposit is not looked up, it is
computed, and `getMinimumBalanceForRentExemption` is the RPC that does the computing for
you.<Cite src="solana-docs-account-structure" />

Read the last row twice. A maximum-size account costs roughly seventy-three SOL to hold open —
refundable, but locked for the account's whole life. A design that wants a large on-chain buffer is
a design that wants a large amount of capital sitting idle, which makes "store it all on chain" a
decision with a balance sheet behind it rather than a preference.

## The name is a fossil, and the mechanism it names is gone

"Rent" is not merely a misleading label for a deposit. The recurring fee it once described has been
switched off at the protocol level, and two SIMDs record the demolition.

**SIMD-0084, "Disable rent fees collection", status Implemented.** With the feature active, rent is
no longer collected from accounts nor distributed to validators. Its motivation notes that creating
new rent-paying accounts was already impossible — any attempt returns a transaction error — so the
remaining population was phasing out anyway.<Cite src="simd-0084" />

**SIMD-0267, "Sets rent_epoch to a constant in the VM", status Activated.** Its reasoning is the
whole story in one sentence: `rent_epoch` is no longer meaningful, because all accounts must be
rent-exempt and rent fee collection has been disabled. The value serialized into the VM is fixed at
the maximum `u64`, so validators cannot disagree about it and diverge.<Cite src="simd-0267" />

The field you met in lesson one is therefore a constant, kept because removing a serialized field is
a hard fork, and it now signals only "this account is
rent-exempt".<Cite src="solana-docs-account-structure" />

## Three states, and one you can never enter

The runtime models this as a small state machine, and the transitions are enforced rather than
advisory. Every account is in one of three `RentState` values: **Uninitialized** (zero lamports),
**RentPaying** (above zero but below the exempt minimum), or **RentExempt** (at or above
it).<Cite src="solana-docs-account-modification-rules" />

The permitted transitions are:<Cite src="solana-docs-account-modification-rules" />

- Any account may move to **Uninitialized** — that is closing it.
- Any account may move to **RentExempt**.
- No account may enter **RentPaying** from Uninitialized or from RentExempt.

A disallowed transition fails the transaction with `InsufficientFundsForRent`. Which means the rule
"all new accounts must be rent-exempt" is not a convention or a linting suggestion; it is a
transition the state machine refuses to make.<Cite src="solana-docs-account-modification-rules" />

The same rule reaches the fee payer. After paying fees, the fee-payer account must either remain
rent-exempt or go to *exactly* zero lamports — it cannot end up between zero and the exempt
minimum.<Cite src="solana-docs-account-runtime" /> You cannot dribble a wallet down to dust.

<Aside kind="warn">
That is a real failure mode for testing and treasury tooling. A wallet holding slightly more than
the exempt minimum can fail a transaction a naive balance check says it can afford, because paying
the fee would land it in the forbidden band. Budget the exempt minimum as unavailable balance.
</Aside>

## Closing is a payment, not a cleanup

Because the deposit is refundable, deleting state pays somebody. That is a genuinely different
incentive landscape from the EVM's, and it changes how protocols are designed.

Closing an account means sending its lamports to a target and resetting its data — which is exactly
what a framework's `close` constraint does for you.<Cite src="anchor-account-constraints" /> The
target is a parameter. Whoever is named there receives the deposit.

Set that against the EVM's version. Clearing a storage slot there produces a gas *refund*, capped at
4,800 gas per slot, with the whole transaction's refund bounded at one fifth of the gas
used.<Cite src="eip-3529" /> It goes to the transaction sender, it is capped, and it is denominated
in the gas that made the transaction expensive in the first place. On Solana the refund is uncapped —
it is the entire deposit — and it goes to an account of your choosing.

**So cleanup can be incentivised.** A protocol can let anyone close an expired position, order or
escrow and keep the deposit as the reward. There is no equivalent on Ethereum, where a keeper bounty
has to be funded separately.

**And cleanup can be stolen.** If the closer names the target and your program does not constrain
it, the wrong party gets paid. Worse, draining an account's lamports does not by itself put it
beyond reach within the same transaction — the deposit can be restored before the runtime would have
reclaimed the account, which is the shape of
[the account revival attack](/c/account-revival-attack). Closing is security-relevant here in a way
`delete` never was.

## Every account has a funder, and you have to choose one

Here is the part that is easy to miss and expensive to discover late.

Creating an account is a CPI to the System Program in which some account's lamports move into the
new one. That paying account is named explicitly in the instruction. A framework spells this as a
`payer` on the `init` constraint.<Cite src="anchor-account-constraints" /> It is a parameter, so it
is a product decision.

On Ethereum this decision does not exist. Whoever sends the transaction pays the gas, and there is no
"someone else funds this user's slot" because there is no slot to fund — the storage is the
contract's and its cost was folded into the write.

Here you have at least three options. **The user funds their own accounts**: simple, refundable to
them, and it means a new user needs SOL before they can do anything — the onboarding tax every
Solana product designs around. **The protocol funds them from a treasury**: removes that tax, but
account creation becomes a capital line with a refund that returns to the treasury rather than the
user, and an attacker who can trigger unbounded creation ties up your balance a couple of
thousandths of a SOL at a time. **A third party funds them**: the Associated Token Account program
exists partly for this, since an associated token account may be created by anybody and is fully
owned by the wallet it was derived for regardless of who paid.<Cite src="solana-program-ata" />

That third option is expressible only because the funder, the owner and the address are three
separate things. Fusing them, as contract storage does, removes the question and the answer
together.

<Check type="predict" concept="rent-exemption">
A protocol lets users open leveraged positions. Each position is a program-owned account of 240
bytes, funded by the protocol treasury at open, and closed by the user at settlement with the
deposit returned to the treasury.

A growth push brings 400,000 new positions in a week. Answer three things:

1. What does that cost the treasury, and is it a cost?
2. An attacker opens and closes positions in a tight loop, thousands of times. What have they
   actually done to you?
3. The team proposes storing a 30-day price history inside each position account instead of a
   separate oracle account, adding 8 KiB per position. What does that do to the numbers?

<Answer>
**1 — about 1,025 SOL, and it is locked capital rather than an expense.** Apply the
formula: (240 + 128) × 3,480 × 2 = 2,561,280 lamports per account, about 0.00256
SOL.<Cite src="solana-docs-accounts" /> Times 400,000 positions is 1,024,512,000,000 lamports, about
1,025 SOL. Every lamport of it returns when the positions close, so the correct line item is not
"cost" but "working capital tied up for the average life of a position". If positions live a month,
the treasury needs a month's worth of openings funded simultaneously. That is a cash-flow model, and
it is the kind of model that does not appear anywhere in an EVM design.

**2 — they have not stolen anything, and they may still have hurt you.** Each open-and-close cycle
returns the deposit, so the loop is capital-neutral for the treasury. What it is not is free: the
attacker pays transaction fees, and you pay in the treasury's momentary exposure and in whatever the
account churn does downstream — indexers, snapshots, anything that reacts to account creation. The
real risk is a design where the close path returns the deposit somewhere other than where the open
path took it from. Then the loop is a pump, and it drains at a couple of thousandths of a SOL per
iteration until the treasury is empty. Check that the funder on open and the target on close are the
same account, and that the program enforces it rather than trusting the instruction's arguments.

**3 — it multiplies the deposit by more than twenty and changes what the design is.** (8,192 + 240
+ 128) × 6,960 = 59,577,600 lamports, roughly 0.0596 SOL per position — against 0.00256 before. At
400,000 positions that is about 23,830 SOL of locked capital instead of 1,025. Nothing has broken;
the arithmetic has simply made the proposal a capital-allocation decision rather than a schema
decision. And note what the alternative looks like: one shared oracle account that every position
*reads*, costing one deposit instead of 400,000 — which also happens to be the arrangement that lets
those positions be processed concurrently, since read locks do not conflict with each other. Lesson
four is about why that second property matters at least as much as the first.

**The transferable habit.** Price the schema before you write it. On Solana the storage cost of a
data model is computable from the formula in this lesson, exactly, before a line of Rust exists —
which is not true of an EVM contract, where you can only estimate write frequency.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

You can now cost a Solana data model on paper, which completes the account-model picture: **an
account is an address, an owner, a size, and a refundable deposit that scales with the size.**

**The economics thread.** Rent-exemption is why account closure is a first-class operation with a
payee, why keeper-style cleanup is self-funding here, and why
[the revival attack](/c/account-revival-attack) earns a lesson of its own in the security module. It
is also, quietly, why Solana's state does not grow in the unbounded, unpriced way Ethereum's does —
the pressure behind a decade of upstream work on [statelessness](/c/statelessness).

**The architecture thread.** You have now met all three properties of Solana state: external to the
program, globally addressed, and individually sized and funded. What remains is the property all of
this was arranged to produce. If every piece of state is a separately addressed object, a
transaction can *name* the ones it will touch — and a runtime that knows what a transaction will
touch before running it can run two of them at once. That is the next lesson, and it is the reason
the account model looks the way it does.
