Thirty-one withdrawal transactions, roughly twelve minutes, and the contracts were fine.

On 1 April 2026 attackers drained approximately $285 million in user assets from Drift Protocol, the
largest decentralised perpetual futures exchange on Solana, in about twelve minutes, with most of the
stolen funds bridged to Ethereum within hours.<Cite src="trm-drift" /> It is the largest decentralised
finance loss of 2026 and the second-largest exploit in Solana's history, behind only the $326 million
Wormhole bridge hack of 2022.<Cite src="trm-drift" />

Every technique in the previous seven lessons was irrelevant to it. No missing owner check. No missing
signer check. No account substitution, no seed collision, no revival, no wrapped subtraction. The
critical vulnerability was not a smart contract bug.<Cite src="trm-drift" />

This lesson is the module's turn, and it is the reason the module exists in 2026 rather than in 2022.
The taxonomy you have just learned is correct, well documented, and largely closed for programs built
on the framework.<Cite src="sealevel-attacks" /><Cite src="helius-solana-security-guide" /> The money
stopped leaving through it. Following where it went instead changes what you would put in a security
budget.

<Aside kind="warn">
**On the Drift loss figure, sources disagree and this lesson will not pick one.** Chainalysis and TRM
Labs both report approximately $285 million;<Cite src="chainalysis-drift" /><Cite src="trm-drift" />
CoinDesk's contemporaneous reporting puts it at approximately $270
million.<Cite src="coindesk-drift-durable-nonce" /> Different valuation snapshots of a basket of
assets seized over minutes and bridged over hours. State the range, or state the mechanism. Every
figure in this lesson carries the same caveat, and where two published numbers exist you will find
both.
</Aside>

## The chain of events, with no code step in it

Read this as a sequence and notice that every link is a decision made by a person or by a market, not
by a program.

**Autumn 2025 to March 2026 — relationships.** Drift's own investigation suggests the social
engineering campaign may have begun as early as autumn 2025. The attackers deposited over $1 million
of capital, participated in detailed strategy and product discussions, and built trust with multiple
contributors over time — proximity and credibility inside the ecosystem, purchased with real
money.<Cite src="chainalysis-drift" />

**11 March 2026 — infrastructure.** On-chain staging began with a single withdrawal of 10 ETH from
Tornado Cash. Those funds began moving hours later, around midnight GMT on 12 March — roughly 09:00
Pyongyang time — and shortly afterwards funded the deployment of the token used to manipulate
Drift.<Cite src="trm-drift" />

**12 March 2026 — a manufactured asset.** The attacker created CarbonVote Token, minting 750 million
units, seeding just a few thousand dollars of liquidity on Raydium, and wash trading to build a price
history near $1.<Cite src="trm-drift" /> Chainalysis adds that they controlled around 80% of the
supply and seeded a pool holding roughly $500 of real liquidity, and that they deployed a price oracle
of their own to feed the artificial price to Drift.<Cite src="chainalysis-drift" /> To an outside
system, the token had a price, a trading history, and a stable
value.<Cite src="chainalysis-drift" />

**23 to 30 March 2026 — signatures that do not expire.** The attacker created multiple durable nonce
accounts, a legitimate Solana feature allowing transactions to be pre-signed and executed later
without expiring, and used social engineering to induce Drift Security Council multisig signers into
pre-signing transactions that appeared routine but carried hidden authorisations for critical admin
actions.<Cite src="trm-drift" />

**27 March 2026 — the defence removed.** Drift migrated its Security Council to a new two-of-five
threshold configuration with zero timelock, eliminating the delay that would have allowed detection
and intervention. TRM's assessment is direct: that migration created the exploitable
gap.<Cite src="trm-drift" />

**1 April 2026 — execution.** The pre-signed transactions were deployed. The attacker listed the fake
token as valid collateral, raised withdrawal limits to extreme levels, and deposited hundreds of
millions of it against the manufactured price; Chainalysis puts the deposit at 500 million
units.<Cite src="trm-drift" /><Cite src="chainalysis-drift" /> Then thirty-one withdrawal transactions
executed in roughly twelve minutes, draining real assets.<Cite src="trm-drift" /> Because the
transactions carried valid admin signatures, standard security did not
intervene.<Cite src="chainalysis-drift" />

**Aftermath.** Drift suspended deposits and withdrawals, the protocol's token fell more than 40%, and
most of the stolen funds were bridged to Ethereum within hours — each bridging transaction moving
hundreds of thousands or millions of dollars, faster and more aggressively than the Bybit laundering
of 2025.<Cite src="trm-drift" /> TRM's initial investigation suggests North Korean
actors;<Cite src="trm-drift" /> Chainalysis notes the operation resembles previously attributed
operations of that kind while formal attribution remains
pending.<Cite src="chainalysis-drift" />

**Subgoal: locate the step where a program was at fault.** There is not one. Drift's post-mortem, as
summarised by Chainalysis, has not yet been independently verified by a completed third-party
investigation<Cite src="chainalysis-drift" /> — so hold the details loosely — but the shape is not in
dispute across the sources: audited, uncompromised contracts executing valid instructions authorised
by valid signatures.

## The mechanism worth learning: durable nonces

One technical component deserves a proper explanation, because it is a feature you may want to use
and because the security property it removes is one you have been relying on without noticing.

### Why transactions normally expire

Solana's double-spend defence is a bounded replay window. Transactions are checked against the last
150 blocks: if the signature is found there it is rejected as a duplicate, and it is also rejected if
it is older than 150 blocks. The only case in which a transaction is accepted is when it is unique and
its blockhash is more recent than 150 blocks, which is around 80 to 90
seconds.<Cite src="solana-durable-nonces-guide" /> The core documentation gives the same figure as a
limit: a transaction's recent blockhash is valid for 150 slots.<Cite src="solana-docs-transactions" />

The guide names the side effect in its own words: a forced mortality of the transaction even before
its submission.<Cite src="solana-durable-nonces-guide" /> That is a genuine ergonomic problem. Hardware
wallets, air-gapped signing, multi-party approval flows and scheduled operations all need more than
ninety seconds between the signature and the broadcast.

### What a durable nonce replaces it with

A nonce account stores a nonce value, is owned by the System Program, and must be rent-exempt,
maintaining a minimum balance of around 0.0015 SOL.<Cite src="solana-durable-nonces-guide" /> A nonce
authority controls it and can generate a new nonce, advance it, or withdraw the account's
SOL.<Cite src="solana-durable-nonces-guide" /> By default the account's creator is the authority,
though authority can be transferred to a different
keypair.<Cite src="solana-durable-nonces-guide" />

The mechanism: use the stored nonce in place of a recent blockhash, and make the first instruction of
the transaction a nonce-advance, which changes the nonce. That guarantees every transaction signed
against it is unique whether or not it was successfully submitted, which is what preserves the
double-spend property.<Cite src="solana-durable-nonces-guide" />

**Subgoal: state exactly what changed.** Uniqueness is preserved. *Mortality is not.* A transaction
signed against a durable nonce remains executable until the nonce advances — which, if the attacker
controls when it advances, or simply if nobody advances it, means indefinitely.

**Subgoal: state the security consequence in one sentence.** *Signature expiry is an operational
control, and this feature removes it by design.* An approval obtained under false pretences on a
Tuesday is executable in April.

  why="The 150-block expiry is taught as if it were a universal property of Solana transactions rather than a property of the blockhash mode, and durable nonces are usually introduced as a convenience feature for offline signing."
  src="coindesk-drift-durable-nonce"
/>

You have met the same structure on the EVM and it is worth connecting deliberately. An EIP-712 typed
signature — a token permit, a limit order, a session grant — is likewise a bearer instrument that
lives until something consumes or invalidates it, and the only reason most of them expire is that
somebody put a `deadline` field in the payload and checked it. Solana's blockhash window is a
platform-level deadline that applies to everything; a durable nonce opts out of it. The
[replay-protection question](/c/nonce-replay-protection) is answered in both designs; the *expiry*
question is answered in neither unless a human specifies it.

<Aside kind="note">
The feature is not the villain and should not be treated as one. Durable nonces are how custody
providers, air-gapped signers and multi-party approval flows work on Solana at all, and removing them
would remove the safest signing setups along with the attack. The correct posture is a policy: which
of your keys may sign durable-nonce transactions, who advances the nonce, how long a pre-signed
transaction may sit before it is either broadcast or invalidated by an advance, and what the signer
sees on screen before signing one.<Cite src="solana-durable-nonces-guide" />
</Aside>

## The other direction the money went: collateral and oracles

Drift is one of two shapes in the modern record. The other is older, better understood, and still
producing losses.

**Mango Markets, October 2022.** The attacker manipulated the protocol's price oracle by inflating a
token's price through leveraged perpetual futures trades, using two accounts holding $10 million in
USDC to move the token from $0.038 to $0.91 across exchanges, then borrowing against the inflated
collateral.<Cite src="helius-solana-hacks" /> The catalogue records approximately $116 million drained
and $67 million returned after negotiation;<Cite src="helius-solana-hacks" /> figures near $114 million
also circulate, so treat the total as a range. No account-validation bug was involved at any point.

**Loopscale, April 2025.** A Solana lending protocol lost $5.8 million to an issue with how the
protocol priced a particular class of yield-bearing collateral token; the write-up describes it as a
price oracle issue within the project's smart contracts, where the protocol incorrectly assessed the
token's value and an attacker exploited the mismatch between the perceived and actual
price.<Cite src="halborn-loopscale" /> The funds were subsequently
recovered.<Cite src="helius-solana-hacks" />

**Drift's collateral half is the same class, industrialised.** The oracle was not manipulated by
trading against a real market; the market itself was manufactured — a token with 750 million units, a
few thousand dollars of seeded liquidity, wash trades to establish a price, and an attacker-run oracle
to report it.<Cite src="trm-drift" /><Cite src="chainalysis-drift" /> The step that made it fatal was
not the manufacture; it was the listing, which is a governance action.

**Subgoal: extract the invariant these three share.** *A protocol's solvency is bounded by the
weakest asset it will accept as collateral, priced by the weakest oracle it will believe.* That is
[oracle manipulation](/c/oracle-manipulation) as the DeFi track defined it, and every mitigation the
track gave you applies unchanged: minimum liquidity thresholds before an asset is listable,
time-weighted price validation rather than spot, and circuit breakers — which is precisely the
defence-in-depth TRM recommends in its own post-incident
lessons.<Cite src="trm-drift" />

## What the record actually looks like

Two cautions before any aggregate, because both have bitten this project.

**Cross-chain loss statistics are not Solana statistics.** The widely quoted annual totals for smart
contract losses cover every chain. Do not present them as Solana figures.

**Scope every catalogue.** The most complete Solana-specific incident catalogue available sorts
incidents into application exploits, supply chain attacks, network-level attacks and core protocol
vulnerabilities, and its own summary states it is listed up to Q1
2025.<Cite src="helius-solana-hacks" /> Within that scope it records approximately $600 million in
total losses, approximately $469 million mitigated through reimbursements, recoveries and protocol
interventions, and net losses of approximately $131
million.<Cite src="helius-solana-hacks" /> Those are useful numbers with an explicit boundary, and the
boundary predates both Drift and everything after it.

The recovery figure is the interesting one. Wormhole's $326 million was reimbursed by its parent
company; Loopscale's $5.8 million was recovered; Mango's attacker returned $67
million.<Cite src="helius-solana-hacks" /> Headline loss numbers and realised loss numbers differ by
a factor of several in this ecosystem's history, which is a reason to distrust any single figure you
see quoted without a date attached.

Three incidents in that catalogue are worth carrying for what they are *not*.

- **Slope, August 2022.** A mobile wallet transmitted encrypted seed phrases to a central logging
  server, and the wallets were drained. The catalogue records approximately $8 million from over 9,000
  wallets;<Cite src="helius-solana-hacks" /> other write-ups give a materially lower figure and a more
  precise wallet count, so the total is disputed. This is routinely miscited as "Solana was hacked".
  It was a logging pipeline in one application.
- **Cashio, March 2022.** A genuine account-validation bug, and the one incident in this list that
  belongs to lesson 3 rather than here.<Cite src="helius-solana-hacks" />
- **Drift, April 2026.** No contract bug at all.<Cite src="trm-drift" />

Those three, in that order, are the ecosystem's arc in miniature: key handling, then account
validation, then operations.

## The counter-pressure

One honest complication, because the conclusion "the classic classes are closed" has an expiry date of
its own.

The classes are closed *for programs that use the framework*. A lower-level framework is now in
active use for compute-constrained programs, and it validates nothing about the accounts it hands
you.<Cite src="pinocchio-repo" /> Every guarantee a constraint derived — owner equals program id,
discriminator matches, this account signed, this key equals that field — has to be written by hand,
in the right order, in every instruction. For those programs, the seven lessons before this one are
not history. They are the specification.

That makes the [security trade-off](/c/pinocchio-security-tradeoff) a budget question rather than a
style question: if you cannot fund a review that specifically enumerates owner, signer, discriminator
and relational checks per instruction, the compute saving is borrowed rather than free. And it means
this module's shape — a taxonomy that is mostly closed, plus a set of operational failures that are
not — describes 2026 and will need re-reading whenever the framework mix shifts.

<Check type="explain" concept="not-all-exploits-are-code">
Your protocol has a clean audit from a reputable firm, uses the framework throughout, has no
`UncheckedAccount` without a justified `CHECK` comment, and passes an invariant suite. You are asked
to name the three controls most likely to have prevented a Drift-shaped loss, and to say why none of
them would appear in a code audit's scope.

<Answer>
**Control 1 — a non-zero timelock on every administrative action, treated as a protocol invariant
rather than a configuration value.** Drift migrated its Security Council to a two-of-five threshold
with zero timelock on 27 March, five days before the drain, and TRM's assessment is that this
eliminated the delay that would have allowed detection and intervention and created the exploitable
gap.<Cite src="trm-drift" /> Its own first stated lesson is that timelocks on governance and admin
actions are a critical safeguard.<Cite src="trm-drift" />

Why an audit misses it: the timelock length is deployed state and a governance decision, not source
code. An audit of the contracts would find the timelock mechanism correctly implemented. Whether it
was set to zero last Tuesday is a different artefact entirely, and it is the artefact that mattered.
This is the same finding shape as reading deployed storage rather than source after an upgrade — a
control the security track already gave you, applied to a parameter nobody re-read.

**Control 2 — an asset-listing process with quantitative gates, applied to collateral before it can
back anything.** The attacker's token had 750 million units, a few thousand dollars of seeded
liquidity, a wash-traded price near $1 and an attacker-operated
oracle.<Cite src="trm-drift" /><Cite src="chainalysis-drift" /> Every one of those is measurable
before listing. TRM's recommendation is explicit: minimum liquidity thresholds, time-weighted price
validation, and circuit breakers before accepting any asset as
collateral.<Cite src="trm-drift" />

Why an audit misses it: an audit verifies that the listing instruction works and is access-controlled.
It cannot tell you what the humans holding that access will list, and the listing here was performed
with entirely valid authority. The vulnerability was in the admission policy, which lives in a
governance document rather than a repository.

**Control 3 — a signing procedure that makes a pre-signed transaction's full effect legible, plus an
explicit durable-nonce policy.** The signers pre-signed transactions that appeared routine but carried
hidden authorisations for critical admin actions, held under durable nonces so they never
expired.<Cite src="trm-drift" /> TRM's third lesson is multisig hygiene: signers should have robust
processes for independently verifying the full content of any transaction before signing, particularly
those touching admin functions.<Cite src="trm-drift" /> The durable-nonce half adds a second
requirement the general advice does not cover — a policy on how long a signature may remain live, and
a nonce authority that advances the nonce on a schedule so that an unbroadcast signature dies. The
nonce authority can advance the nonce at will, so this is available as a
control.<Cite src="solana-durable-nonces-guide" />

Why an audit misses it: what a signer sees on a screen before approving is a wallet and tooling
question, and how long their signature stays valid is a property of the nonce mode chosen by whoever
built the transaction. Neither is in the program.

**The pattern across all three.** Each control lives in *deployed state*, in *policy*, or in
*tooling* — and a code audit's scope is source. That is not a criticism of audits, which do the job
they are scoped to; it is an argument that the security budget of a protocol should not be
predominantly the audit budget. The reflection prompt for this module asks you to run both exercises
on the same protocol — the three questions on every account in one instruction, and then the
operational inventory — and to say which would have caught more of 2026's losses. Having read this
lesson, you already know the answer. The value of doing it anyway is finding out how much longer the
second exercise takes, and how much of it nobody at your company currently owns.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks, and what the track leaves you with

**An inventory you can build this week, for any protocol you depend on or ship.** Who can sign an
administrative action today, by name and by organisation. What timelock stands between their signature
and its effect, read from chain state rather than from documentation. What they see on screen before
they sign, and whether the transaction's full effect is legible there. And how long a signature they
produce stays executable — which on Solana is a question about blockhash versus durable nonce, with
answers of ninety seconds and forever
respectively.<Cite src="solana-durable-nonces-guide" /><Cite src="solana-docs-transactions" />

**A correction to how you would spend a security budget.** The taxonomy in lessons 1 through 7 is
real, cheap to close, and worth closing — the canonical reference and the audit-firm checklist between
them cover it completely and both are free.<Cite src="sealevel-attacks" /><Cite src="cantina-securing-solana" />
Close it, then stop treating it as the perimeter. The perimeter in 2026 is collateral admission,
oracle design, and the operational security of a small number of privileged
signers.<Cite src="trm-drift" />

**And the thing the track was really for.** You came into Track 12 knowing the EVM well. What Solana
did was take two properties you never had to think about — an authenticated sender and
callee-controlled state — and remove them, in exchange for parallelism. Every class in this module is
the invoice for that trade. That is the shape of the whole track: the EVM's choices are choices, each
with a price, and other machines paid differently. `msg.sender` is not a law of nature. Storage
isolation is not a law of nature. Checked arithmetic is a compiler flag somebody set.

You will read Solidity differently now, which was the point.
