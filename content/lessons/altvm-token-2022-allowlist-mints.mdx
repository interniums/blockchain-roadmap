"Do we support Token-2022?" is the question every team asks, and it has no answer, because it is not
a question about a program. Supporting the program means accepting **every mint anyone creates under
it**, including the extension sets you have never handled and the ones that did not exist when you
wrote the integration. It is the same category error as asking whether you support ERC-20.

Neodyme's version of this, at the end of a long catalogue of foot-guns, is one sentence and a
wink — after reading all of that, you probably think you should support mints only with certain
extensions, and if not, they suggest you
reconsider.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> This lesson turns that into a posture you can operate,
and then closes the track by saying what the whole contrast was for.

## The symmetry, and where it breaks

Set the two ecosystems side by side, because the shape is genuinely mirrored and the mirror is what
misleads people.

**Ethereum.** A token is an interface, so the behaviour space is unbounded and unknowable in advance.
The [weird-token taxonomy](/c/weird-erc20-taxonomy) is an empirical list that can never be completed.
The posture that follows is forced: allowlist specific token addresses, because there is nothing else
you could enumerate.

**Solana.** A token's behaviour lives in one audited program and each mint declares which of a finite,
enumerated set of extensions it enabled.<Cite src="spl-token-2022-docsrs-extensiontype" /> The
behaviour space *is* knowable in advance. So the tempting conclusion is that the ERC-20 posture is
obsolete here: handle every extension once, accept the program, done.

Three things break that conclusion, and each has appeared in an earlier lesson.

**The enumeration grows.** `ScaledUiAmount`, `Pausable`, `PermissionedBurn` and `ConfidentialMintBurn`
sit at the high end of the `ExtensionType` numbering,<Cite src="spl-token-2022-docsrs-extensiontype" />
which is a polite way of saying they arrived after the hazard write-ups everyone learned from. A
program that handles "all the extensions" handles the ones that existed when it shipped. This is
exactly why the allowlist has to be over extensions you have *handled*, never a denylist of ones you
have heard of.

**A mint's set is not permanent.** A closeable mint can be destroyed and rebuilt at the same address
with a different extension set, leaving accounts created under the old rules
intact.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> Whatever bucket you put a mint in, it can move without
telling you.

**And the extension list does not describe the interaction with your architecture.** `PermanentDelegate`
is a single flag; whether it can bankrupt your protocol depends on whether you hold user funds in one
pooled account or many.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> The mint tells you the capability. Only your
own design tells you the blast radius.

## The three buckets, filled in

The module's reflection prompt asks you to put every extension into exactly one of reject, handle, or
harmless. Here is a starting assignment, with the reason each one is where it is. Treat it as a
template to argue with rather than a table to copy — the right answer differs by protocol, and
producing your own is the exercise.

**Reject, or accept only as an explicit counterparty decision.**

- `PermanentDelegate` — unlimited privilege to transfer or burn from any account of the
  mint,<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> bypassing CPI
  Guard.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> Not a code problem; a question of whether you
  accept the holder as a party who can reduce your vault.
- `TransferHook` — third-party code in every transfer, replaceable by the mint
  authority.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" />
- `NonTransferable` — nothing a protocol does with a token is possible.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" />
- `MintCloseAuthority` — the mint's rules can be replaced under
  you.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" />
- `Pausable` — an authority can abort all transfers, mints and burns, which is a liveness dependency
  on your liquidation path.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" />
- `PermissionedBurn` — the standard `Burn` and `BurnChecked` instructions are rejected once a burn
  authority is set,<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> so any redemption flow that burns
  breaks.

**Handle, with named code in a named place.**

- `TransferFeeConfig` / `TransferFeeAmount` — `transfer_checked_with_fee` on the way in, credit the
  measured delta, and use the extension's `closable()` predicate before closing an
  account.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /><Cite src="offside-token-2022-part1" />
- `DefaultAccountState` — every account you create can arrive frozen; check and adapt rather than
  assuming.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" />
- `ConfidentialTransfer*` — you cannot read the balance, only the owner can configure the account,
  and closure requires the balances to be emptied
  first.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /><Cite src="offside-token-2022-part1" />
- `InterestBearingConfig` / `ScaledUiAmount` — the ledger is untouched and the feature is entirely
  cosmetic,<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> so the work is in your boundary: raw
  amounts internally, convert only for display.
- `MemoTransfer` (account extension) — incoming transfers require a memo instruction immediately
  before the transfer, including in CPI contexts.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" />
- `CpiGuard` (account extension) — with it enabled, a program must follow the delegation flow to move
  the user's tokens by CPI.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" />

**Harmless, with one caveat that is not optional.**

- `ImmutableOwner` — prevents ownership reassignment, and the Associated Token Account program always
  uses it for Token-2022 accounts.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> This one makes your
  life easier.
- `MetadataPointer`, `TokenMetadata`, `GroupPointer`, `TokenGroup`, `GroupMemberPointer`,
  `TokenGroupMember` — Neodyme's assessment is that no dangers arise because they are solely
  cosmetic.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> **The caveat:** to avoid phony mints claiming to be
  something they are not, a client must check that the mint and the metadata point at *each other*,
  and the same mutual check applies to the group and member
  pointers.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> A pointer that only points one way is a
  claim, not a fact.

Notice how many of the "handle" entries are two lines of code and how many of the "reject" entries are
not code at all. That distribution is the honest summary of the module: Token-2022's mechanical
hazards are cheap to defuse once named, and its governance hazards cannot be defused by an integrator
at all.

## What an allowlist actually is

Not a set of addresses. Neodyme's sketch allowlists **extension types** and rejects a mint if any of
its extensions falls outside the permitted set,<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> which is the on-chain
half. The operational half is a record, kept off-chain, of what you decided and what you decided it
about:

```text
per listed mint, store and monitor:
  mint address
  token program (owner of the mint account)          -- lesson 1
  expected extension set                             -- exact, not a subset test
  for TransferFeeConfig:  bps, max fee, both authorities
  for TransferHook:       hook program id, its upgrade authority, the mint's hook authority
  for PermanentDelegate:  the delegate address
  freeze authority, mint authority, close authority
  provenance: has this address ever been closed and reinitialised?

alert when any of these differs from the record.
```

Everything in that block is a value some authority can change: the fee, the hook program, the
multiplier, the auditor key, the default account state, the delegate. A listing decision made once
and never re-checked is a decision about a mint that no longer exists.

<Aside kind="note">
Two of those rows are the ones teams skip. **The token program row** is the whole of lesson one — a
hardcoded program id silently narrows every derivation, and reading the mint account's owner is the
one field that settles it.<Cite src="solana-program-token-2022" /> **The provenance row** is the only
one that cannot be answered by reading state, because a mint does not record its own history, and it
is the row that catches the close-and-reinit case a `MintCloseAuthority` check
cannot.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" />
</Aside>

<Check type="recall" concept="allowlist-mints-not-programs">
A protocol lists a Token-2022 mint after verifying it carries only `MetadataPointer` and
`TokenMetadata` — both in the harmless bucket. Name the two things that could still go wrong, and the
check that addresses each.

<Answer>
**1 — The metadata may be lying about what the mint is.** The pointer designates an address that
describes the mint, and to stop phony mints claiming to be, for example, a stablecoin, a client must
verify that the **mint and the metadata point at each
other**.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> A one-way pointer is an unverified assertion,
and a listing pipeline that reads a name and symbol without the mutual check has taken the token's
word for its own identity.

**2 — The extension set can be replaced.** If the mint also carries `MintCloseAuthority`, it can be
closed at zero supply and reinitialised at the same address with a completely different set — a fee, a
hook, a permanent delegate — while any accounts created under the old mint survive under the old
rules.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> And Neodyme's warning applies even if the close authority is
absent today, since a mint may have been closeable, been reinitialised, and come back without
it.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> The check is provenance plus continuous monitoring of the
extension set against the record, not a single read at listing time.

**The point the question is making.** "Harmless" is a property of an extension, and a listing is a
decision about a mint. Even an entirely cosmetic extension set leaves the identity question and the
mutability question open, and both are answered outside the extension list.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks — and what the track was for

**An integration posture you can defend in a review.** Allowlist mints together with the specific
extension sets you have handled; store what you expected; monitor for divergence; and treat every
mint-level authority as a counterparty you have accepted rather than a field you have
read.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> That is a smaller claim than "we support Token-2022" and it is
one you can actually keep.

**A finished answer to the question this module opened with.** ERC-20's weird-token problem exists
because a token is an interface and behaviour cannot be enumerated. Token-2022 answers it from the
opposite direction: behaviour lives in one program, the menu is finite, and the extension records are
readable off the mint.<Cite src="solana-docs-token-extensions" /> That is a real improvement and it
is not a solution, because the readable answer is about the present, the menu grows, and the mint
itself can be replaced. The correct summary is that Token-2022 converts an *unknowable* risk into a
*checkable* one — and a checkable risk that nobody checks is worse than an unknowable one, because it
comes with a feeling of safety.

**And the thing Track 12 was for.** You came into this track knowing the EVM deeply enough that its
choices had stopped looking like choices. Eight modules later, each of them should have a visible
alternative attached: state inside contracts or state in accounts you declare; implicit access or
declared access; a token as an interface or a token as a row in a program; gas metering priced around
storage or compute units priced around execution; behaviour you discover by reading bytecode or
behaviour you read off a mint. None of those pairs has a universally right answer. Every one of them
is a trade someone made, for reasons, and the reasons are now legible to you on both sides.

That is the payoff, and it is not about Solana. It is that you can now say what the EVM chose and what
it gave up to choose it — which is a different and much more useful thing than knowing how it works.
