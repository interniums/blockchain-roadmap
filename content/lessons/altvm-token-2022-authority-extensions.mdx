An ERC-20 allowance has four properties you rely on without ever articulating them. It is granted by
the holder. It is granted to a named spender. It has an amount. And the holder can revoke it. All
four live in the holder's own storage, and every one of them is a consequence of the same fact: on
Ethereum, the only party who can move your tokens is a party you told the contract about.

This lesson is about the Token-2022 extensions where that sentence stops being true. Not because
anyone made a mistake, but because the extension list contains, as first-class declared features: an
address that can move or burn tokens from **any** account of the mint without asking anyone, accounts
that arrive frozen, tokens that cannot move at all, a switch that stops every transfer at once, and a
burn that requires the issuer's signature. Each one is a legitimate answer to a real regulatory or
product requirement. Each one also breaks an assumption underneath ordinary vault code.

They belong together because they share a structure: **a capability that lives on the mint and is
exercised by an authority, with no per-holder consent step anywhere.** Once you see that shape, the
integration question for all of them is the same question, and it is not a code question.

## The permanent delegate

Start with the extension that has no ERC-20 counterpart at all.

The documentation is unambiguous about the power: with Token-2022 it is possible to specify a
permanent account delegate for a mint, and **this authority has unlimited delegate privileges over any
account for that mint, meaning that it can burn or transfer any amount of
tokens**.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" />

Line that up against the [allowance model](/c/erc20-allowance-model) property by property. Granted by
the holder — no; it is a property of the mint, configured before any holder existed. Granted to a
named spender — the spender is named, but on the token, not by you. Has an amount — no; unlimited.
Revocable by the holder — no. There is no state in your account that records the delegation and
nothing you can call to remove it.

The documentation also does the thing good documentation does and names the reason. In some
jurisdictions a stablecoin issuer **must** be able to seize assets from sanctioned entities, and the
permanent delegate is how the issuer transfers or burns tokens from accounts owned by such entities.
A second use is a Harberger tax on an NFT, where an auction program holds permanent delegate
authority and can move the NFT from the owner to the buyer if the previous owner does not pay the
tax.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> The guide's own framing is that the feature
certainly has room for abuse and also has many important real-world use
cases.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" />

Two operational details worth having.

**Who holds it by default.** The CLI defaults the permanent delegate to the mint authority, and it can
be changed afterwards with the `authorize` command and the `permanent-delegate`
role.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> So "the permanent delegate" and "the mint
authority" are frequently the same key and frequently not, and which it is today is a thing you read
rather than assume.

**It outranks the user's own protection.** CPI Guard is the account extension that stops a token
account being used inside a cross-program invocation — precisely to prevent users implicitly signing
for actions hidden inside opaque programs.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> The guide
notes that if a mint is configured with the permanent delegate extension, **the permanent delegate may
always burn or transfer tokens, bypassing CPI
Guard**.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> A user-side defence does not apply to a
mint-side authority.

### Why a pooled vault makes this much worse

Neodyme's assessment of the consequence is worth reading closely, because it identifies the design
choice that decides how bad this is for you.

> Losing your funds with this kind of extension is the obvious implication, **especially for protocols
> with only one token account acting as a vault to store tokens for their users**. While a mint owner
> may act according to regulations, your swap pool could suddenly be out of funds because the
> authority did not just withdraw the bad actor's share but drained it to
> zero.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" />

Work the mechanism.

**Subgoal: describe the normal shape of a Solana vault.** One program-owned token account per mint,
holding every user's deposits, with per-user amounts tracked in the protocol's own state. That is the
same architecture as an EVM pool with an internal `mapping(address => uint256)`, and for the same
reasons — one account is cheaper in rent, simpler to derive, and avoids per-user account creation.

**Subgoal: describe what the permanent delegate sees.** Not users. It sees token accounts of its mint.
Your pooled vault is one account, holding one balance, owned by a program. The delegate has no way to
seize one depositor's share, because on the chain that share does not exist as a separate object.

**Subgoal: state the outcome.** A seizure aimed at one bad actor removes tokens from the pool, and
your [internal accounting](/c/internal-accounting-vs-balanceof) still shows every depositor's full
claim. The protocol is instantly insolvent by the seized amount and the loss is socialised across
everyone. The user who was targeted may still be able to withdraw, because their entry in your ledger
was never touched.

The alternative — one token account per user — makes seizure precise and your accounting survive it,
at the cost of rent per account and the per-account creation path that lesson two warned you to size
correctly. There is no free option here. What there is, is a decision that should be made knowing
this is one of the things it decides.

Neodyme adds a rider that widens the question and should be quoted at whoever signs off on listings:
**for swap pools, lending, cross-margin and similar protocols, the mint authority has similar power —
checking that you trust both is essential.**<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> A mint authority that
can mint freely against a pool you price from is a different attack with the same outcome. The
permanent delegate is the one with a scary name; it is not the only one.

<Aside kind="note">
One caveat the guide raises, which matters if you are the issuer rather than the integrator: the
permanent delegate extension **does not work with tokens stored in the confidential balance** of a
token account, so users may get around the permanent delegate using confidential balances. If you
plan to use confidential transfers and the permanent delegate together, the guide's remedy is to set
auto-approve new accounts to false, or to freeze
accounts.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> A capability designed for regulatory
compliance has a hole that another extension opens — worth knowing before anyone promises a regulator
otherwise.
</Aside>

## Accounts that arrive frozen, and tokens that never move

Two extensions here, and the reason to teach them together is that they break the same assumption
from opposite ends.

**`DefaultAccountState = Frozen`.** A mint creator may want to restrict who can use their token, and
this extension can force all new token accounts to be frozen, so users must eventually interact with
some service to unfreeze their account and use the tokens. The freeze authority can later sign an
`update_default_account_state` instruction to make accounts unfrozen by
default.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" />

Neodyme names the exact failure and it is the one that will hit you: **frozen newly created vault or
escrow accounts can cause programs to malfunction**, and in certain conditions, combined with a
flawed transfer process implementation, this could even result in a loss of funds. The simple fix is
to check whether the new account is frozen and adapt
accordingly.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" />

Sit with what "adapt accordingly" means in a protocol. Your create-vault-on-demand path produces an
account that cannot receive. If the deposit instruction that follows is in the same transaction, it
fails and nothing is lost. If your flow creates accounts in one transaction and moves value in
another — a common shape for anything that batches or uses a keeper — you have a half-built position
and an operational decision to make while a user waits.

  why="Developers test against permissionless mints they created themselves, where the state is always initialized and the case never arises."
  src="solana-docs-token-extensions"

**`NonTransferable`.** The mint extension that makes soul-bound tokens, which cannot be transferred to
any other entity — the documentation's example is an achievement that can only belong to one person
or account. It is described as very similar to issuing a token and then freezing the account, but with
one difference that matters: it **allows the owner to burn and close the account** if they want
to.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> Neodyme's assessment is that there are no major
security implications.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" />

Which is true for a *holder* and misleading for an *integrator*. There are no major security
implications because there is nothing an attacker gains. There is a total integration implication:
every operation your protocol performs on a token is a transfer, and a soulbound token can be
deposited into nothing, collateralised nowhere, and swapped never. The paired `NonTransferableAccount`
extension marks the accounts of such a mint.<Cite src="spl-token-2022-docsrs-extensiontype" /> This is
a mint to reject at the listing gate, not to handle.

<Aside kind="warn">
Two more members of this family postdate the 2024 hazard catalogues, which means the standard
write-ups do not cover them and you should reason about them yourself. **Pausable**: enabling it on a
mint means the program aborts **all transfers, mints and burns** when the paused flag is flipped, and
the pause authority may pause or unpause at
will.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /><Cite src="spl-token-2022-docsrs-extensiontype" />
**Permissioned burn**: once a burn authority is configured on the mint, every burn must be co-signed
by that authority in addition to the account's owner or delegate, and the regular `Burn` and
`BurnChecked` instructions are **rejected**; the authority can be rotated or cleared with
`SetAuthority` using the `PermissionedBurn` authority type, and clearing it re-enables the standard
burn instructions.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> Read both against your own code:
Pausable is a liveness dependency on a third party for your liquidation path, and permissioned burn
breaks any redemption or share-burning flow that assumes it can burn what it holds.
</Aside>

## The rebasing pair, and a genuine disagreement about how dangerous it is

`InterestBearingConfig` and `ScaledUiAmount` change the relationship between the raw amount stored in
an account and the amount shown to a user. They are the closest thing Token-2022 has to a rebasing
token, and the difference from the EVM version is the whole point.

**What they do.** The interest-bearing extension lets you set an interest rate on a token and fetch
its amount with interest at any time via `amount_to_ui_amount`; interest is continuously compounded
based on the network timestamp, and network timestamp drift can make accumulated interest lower than
expected, though the guide describes that as rare. **No new tokens are ever created — the UI amount
returns the amount of tokens plus accumulated interest, and the feature is entirely
cosmetic.**<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> The rate authority may update the interest
rate at any time.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" />

`ScaledUiAmount` is the same idea with a multiplier rather than a rate, motivated by stock splits —
doubling every holder's share count without minting to every holder — and usable for dividends or
yield distribution. Again: no new tokens are created, the UI amount is the raw amount multiplied by
the current multiplier, and the feature is entirely cosmetic. The multiplier authority may update the
multiplier at any time, **optionally setting a timestamp at which the new multiplier takes
effect**.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" />

Now put that next to [rebasing ERC-20s](/c/rebasing-tokens), because the contrast is exact and it
inverts.

An EVM vault that recorded "user deposited 100 tokens" and later reads `balanceOf` is wrong when the
token rebases, which is why the standard advice is to hold shares rather than balances. Here the raw
ledger is inert, so that failure cannot occur. What can occur is the mirror image: a system that
records a **UI amount** anywhere — a price feed, a risk limit, an off-chain ledger, a display value
written back into on-chain state — has recorded a number that will not match the same quantity
computed later, and the divergence grows monotonically and looks exactly like a rounding bug.

**Sources disagree about how serious this is, and the disagreement is real rather than an error.**
Neodyme's verdict on the interest-bearing extension is that no dangers arise from it because it is
solely cosmetic, and that it is therefore safe for programs to support such
tokens.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> That is correct about the token. It says nothing about the
integrating system, and the concept this lesson teaches is about the integrating system. Hold both:
**the ledger is safe; your representation choice is the exposure.** The rule that follows is one line
— pick one representation at the boundary, convert there, and never store the other — and it is
cheap enough that the disagreement need not be resolved before you apply it.

<Aside kind="note">
There is a second-order effect worth naming for anyone building pricing or collateral logic. The
multiplier authority can change the multiplier at any time and can schedule it for a future
timestamp.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> Any component that samples a UI amount at
one moment and compares it to a UI amount from another — a TWAP, a health factor computed across two
transactions, a reconciliation job — is comparing across a parameter that a third party can move
between the samples. Raw amounts have no such property. That is another reason to keep raw as the
system of record.
</Aside>

A lending protocol lists a regulated stablecoin. The mint carries `PermanentDelegate`,
`DefaultAccountState = Frozen`, and `ScaledUiAmount`. The team's integration plan is: hold deposits in
the existing single pooled vault account, thaw the vault account once at listing, and read balances
through `amount_to_ui_amount` so the UI matches the issuer's app.

Say what breaks, in order of how much it costs, and what the plan should be instead.

<Answer>
**1 — The pooled vault turns a targeted seizure into a protocol-wide insolvency.** The permanent
delegate has unlimited privileges over any account of that mint and can transfer or burn any
amount.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> One pooled account means the delegate cannot
seize one user's share; it can only reduce the pool, and the protocol's internal ledger still owes
every depositor in full. Neodyme's warning is exactly this shape — a protocol with a single token
account as a vault, drained not by the bad actor's share but to
zero.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> The structural answer is per-user token accounts so a seizure
lands where it was aimed; the cheaper partial answer is a solvency check that compares the vault's
measured balance against total liabilities every block and halts on a shortfall rather than
discovering it at the last withdrawal.

**2 — Thawing once is not a plan, because it is per account and the state is default.** The extension
makes *all new* token accounts frozen, and only the freeze authority can change the default for future
accounts via `update_default_account_state`.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> Every
account the protocol ever creates for this mint — a new vault, a per-user account, an escrow, a fee
account, a liquidation destination — arrives frozen. Neodyme's fix is the right one and it belongs in
the code path, not the runbook: check whether the new account is frozen and adapt
accordingly.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> Worse, the freeze authority can also freeze an existing
account, so a vault that worked yesterday is a liveness dependency.

**3 — Reading balances through `amount_to_ui_amount` puts a moving number into the ledger.** The
scaled amount is cosmetic; no tokens are created and the raw amount is
untouched.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> If the protocol stores UI amounts as
positions or collateral values, and the multiplier authority updates the multiplier — which it may do
at any time, optionally scheduled for a future
timestamp<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> — then every stored value silently means
something different from every newly computed one. Use raw amounts everywhere internally and convert
only at the display boundary.

**4 — The plan never asked who holds the authorities.** The CLI defaults the permanent delegate to the
mint authority and it can be reassigned later.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> Neodyme's
point applies with full force to a lending protocol: the mint authority has comparable power, and you
must decide that you trust **both**.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> For a mint like this the listing
decision is a counterparty decision — whether you accept the issuer as a party who can unilaterally
reduce your vault — and no amount of code changes that. What code can do is bound the damage: per-user
accounts, a continuous solvency invariant, and a documented response for the day a seizure lands.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**A listing question that is not about code.** For the permanent delegate, the freeze authority, the
pause authority and the burn authority, the only real question is *who holds this key and do we accept
them as a party who can act on our vault without asking*. That is the same question as
[upgrade-key risk](/c/upgrade-key-risk) in the rollup material, and it has the same answer shape: name
the party, name what they can do alone, and decide.

**An architectural consequence you can act on today.** Pooled vaults and per-user accounts are not
merely a rent-versus-simplicity trade on Solana. They decide whether a mint-level authority's action
lands on one user or on all of them. Any protocol that expects to list regulated assets should make
that choice deliberately and write down why.

**And a sharper reading of what "cosmetic" means.** The rebasing extensions genuinely do not touch the
ledger, and one auditor calls them safe to support for exactly that
reason.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> The exposure moved rather than vanished: it now lives in the
boundary between raw and displayed quantities, which is code you own. That is the general shape of
this whole module — Token-2022 makes token behaviour readable and explicit, which relocates the risk
from "what might this token do" to "what did my integration assume".

last four lessons has been a read of the mint's current state — and a mint with a close authority can
be destroyed and rebuilt at the same address with a different set of rules, while the token accounts
created under the old rules go on existing.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" />
