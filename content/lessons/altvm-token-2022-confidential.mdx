Confidential transfers hide the amount. They do not hide who transacted with whom, and on this
machine they structurally cannot — which is the single most useful thing to know about them and the
thing the name most reliably obscures.

Start from the architecture rather than the cryptography, because the architecture settles it. Every
Solana transaction declares in advance the complete list of accounts it will touch — not an
optimisation you can opt out of, but how the scheduler decides what runs in parallel. A confidential
transfer is still a transaction, so it still names the source and destination token accounts in the
clear. An observer reads the account list and knows exactly which two accounts moved value. What they
cannot read is how much.

Hold that next to what an [anonymity set](/c/anonymity-set) means in a shielded pool, where the whole
design goal is that a spend is indistinguishable among all the notes in the tree. Here the
participants' anonymity set has size one. The extension is confidentiality of *amounts*, and every
design decision below follows from taking that goal seriously and no further.

## What is actually stored

An ordinary token account stores a `u64` amount that anyone can read. The confidential extension
replaces that with ciphertexts and a way to prove things about them.

**Encryption that supports arithmetic.** Balances are encrypted with a linearly homomorphic scheme so
the program can add and subtract without decrypting: for two values and their ciphertexts under the
same public key, ciphertext addition and subtraction produce a ciphertext of the sum and
difference.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-overview" /> Because linear homomorphism holds only
within one public key, a transfer amount must be encrypted under **both** the sender's and the
receiver's keys, and the program then subtracts one ciphertext from the source and adds the other to
the destination.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-overview" />

The scheme is **twisted ElGamal**, chosen to shrink transactions: a ciphertext splits into a Pedersen
commitment to the message, independent of any ElGamal public key, plus a decryption handle encoding
the encryption randomness for a specific public key and independent of the
message.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-overview" /> That split is what lets one commitment
serve several recipients with only a small handle each.

**Proofs, because the program can no longer check anything itself.** With balances hidden, the program
cannot see that a sender has enough funds, so transfers carry zero-knowledge proofs. A **range proof**
certifies both that the transfer amount is a positive value in range and that the source balance minus
the transfer amount remains non-negative; an **equality proof** certifies that the ciphertexts under
the sender's and receiver's keys encrypt the same
value.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-overview" /> A **ciphertext validity proof** certifies
that the twisted-ElGamal ciphertexts were generated properly at
all.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-zkps" />

The proof systems are deliberately small: sigma protocols tailored to these specific statements, plus
Bulletproofs for the ranges, and **none of them requires any trusted setup or sophisticated circuit
design**.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-zkps" /> After the zkVM material in Track 11, that is
worth registering as a design choice rather than a limitation — the statements here are simple enough
that a general proving system would be enormous overkill.

Verification runs in a dedicated core program. The Solana documentation lists **ZK ElGamal Proof** at
`ZkE1Gama1Proof11111111111111111111111111111` among the network's core programs, described as
verifying zero-knowledge proofs for ElGamal-encrypted data.<Cite src="solana-docs-core-programs" />

<Aside kind="warn">
This is the most volatile material in the module and it should be treated that way. The verification
path for these proofs has had its own operational history, and this lesson records only what the
documentation stated on the date in the frontmatter: that the proof program is listed as a core
program at that address.<Cite src="solana-docs-core-programs" /> Before designing anything that
depends on confidential transfers, check the current status of the proof program and the extension
against the program's own documentation rather than against any course, including this one.
</Aside>

## The 48-bit amount, and why a range limit exists at all

Here is a constraint with no analogue in any token system you have used, and it comes from a
cryptographic detail rather than a product decision.

ElGamal encryption is easy to compute and awkward to *decrypt*: recovering the plaintext requires
solving a discrete logarithm, which takes exponential time in general. The extension deals with that
in three ways — transfer amounts are restricted to **48-bit numbers**, transfer amounts and pending
balances are encrypted as two independent ciphertexts, and available balances are additionally
encrypted under a symmetric scheme.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-overview" />

The split is not even. A transfer amount is divided into `amount_lo`, the low **16** bits, and
`amount_hi`, the high **32** bits, each encrypted under all three relevant public keys, with
randomness reuse to keep the ciphertexts small.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-zkps" /> The
range proof then certifies that `amount_lo` and `amount_hi` are positive 16-bit and 32-bit values
respectively.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-zkps" />

That asymmetry looks arbitrary until you see what it buys, which the encryption documentation works
out directly. If the maximum pending-balance credit counter is 2^16, then after 2^16 incoming
transfers the low ciphertext still encrypts at most a 32-bit value, which decrypts efficiently; the
high ciphertext reaches at most a 48-bit value, whose decryption is slow but is only reached by
receiving a large number of very large transfers, and can be avoided by flushing the pending balance
often.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-encryption" />

Note what that means for a product. A 48-bit ceiling on the transfer amount is a ceiling in **raw
units**, so on a nine-decimal mint it bounds a single confidential transfer well below what a
treasury movement might need. Nothing about this is a bug; it is the price of a scheme whose
decryption you have to be able to perform on a phone.

## Pending and available: a defence, not an inconvenience

Every hands-on guide describes the pending balance as a step you have to remember. It is actually a
mitigation, and knowing what it mitigates tells you when it fails.

The problem is front-running. Proofs are verified with respect to an account's **encrypted balance**.
If Alice generates a proof against her current balance and Bob's transfer to her is processed first,
her transaction is rejected because the proof no longer verifies. Normally she reads the new
ciphertext and retries — but an attacker continuously flooding her account with transfers can
theoretically make it unusable.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-overview" />

The fix splits the account's encrypted balance in two. **Outgoing funds are subtracted from the
available balance; incoming funds are added to the pending
balance.**<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-overview" /> Since range proofs are generated and
verified against the *available* balance, nobody else's transfer can invalidate your outgoing proof.
The owner merges the two with `ApplyPendingBalance`, which only the owner can
authorise.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-overview" />

### The counter, and the denial of service it did not quite close

Splitting the balance moved the problem rather than removing it, because the pending balance itself
can overflow past the range where decryption is practical. So the account state tracks the number of
incoming transfers since the last `ApplyPendingBalance`, and stores a
`maximum_pending_balance_credit_counter` limiting how many credits it will accept before the owner
applies them — a bound configurable with `ConfigureAccount` and, per the documentation, one that
**should typically be set to 2^16**.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-encryption" /> The two
fields sit side by side in `ConfidentialTransferAccount` as
`maximum_pending_balance_credit_counter` and
`pending_balance_credit_counter`.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-encryption" /> Applying the
pending balance resets the counter to zero.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" />

Neodyme names the consequence: **an attacker can intentionally exceed your maximum pending balance
credit counter and thereby prevent you from receiving any more confidential
transfers.**<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> The value is controlled by the user and can only be set
during initialisation of the extension, so their recommendation is to set it according to the number
of incoming transfers you expect.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" />

And then they say the thing that most write-ups leave out, which is that the obvious response does not
work: setting the value too high **increases the calculation time required to generate the proofs
needed to apply the pending balance, and if you receive another transfer during that calculation you
have to start over. So higher does not necessarily mean better. It is difficult to say what good
values for this are.**<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" />

<Aside kind="stop">
That is an open question rather than a gap in the write-up, and it should be taught as one. The
counter has no setting that is safe against both failure modes: too low and an attacker locks your
account out of receiving with a stream of dust; too high and the proof work to recover grows until a
single well-timed incoming transfer can make you restart it. The documentation's 2^16
suggestion<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-encryption" /> is a reasonable default derived from
the decryption arithmetic, not a security parameter derived from an attacker model. If your product
depends on an account reliably receiving confidential transfers, this is a thing to measure under
adversarial load yourself.
</Aside>

## What still leaks

Three things, and all three are behavioural rather than cryptographic.

**The participants, always.** The account list is public because the runtime requires it. No amount of
encryption changes that.

**The boundary transactions.** Moving value into the confidential balance is a `Deposit` and moving it
out is a `Withdraw`, and Neodyme notes that these reveal the confidential balance initially, with the
protection accruing only after several transfers with various parties make the balance harder to
track.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> Confidentiality here is a property that builds over usage, not
one that switches on.

**The naive usage pattern, completely.** Neodyme's example is the one to remember: a user who applies
the pending balance and immediately withdraws after receiving only one transaction **leaks the amount
they received**, letting chain analysts determine the transfer value and effectively removing the
privacy feature of the extension.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> Deposit *x*, receive one transfer,
withdraw *y*, and the difference is arithmetic anyone can do.

## The parts an integrator has to plan for

**Enable it at mint creation or never.** The documentation is explicit that the mint must be
configured with confidential transfers at creation and cannot have them added
later.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-balances" /> Two approval policies exist: `auto`, where
any token user can permissionlessly configure their account, and `manual`, where every user must be
approved by the authority — and under either policy anyone can still use the token
non-confidentially.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-balances" />

**Only the owner can configure an account for it.** This breaks a habit. Ordinary associated token
accounts can be created by anyone on someone else's behalf; here only the account owner may configure
confidential transfers, because only they should set their account's encryption
key.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-balances" /> Any flow where your protocol or a keeper
prepares accounts for users stops at this step.

**The encryption key is not the signing key.** The account stores an account-specific encryption key
set by the owner via `ConfigureAccount`; the secret can live client-side or be derived
deterministically from an owner signing key, and direct reuse of a signing key for encryption is
**discouraged** — separate keys also allow the decryption key to be shared with, say, a regulator,
while only the owner's signing key can authorise a
transfer.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-overview" />

**The auditor is a first-class feature, not an afterthought.** A mint can carry a global auditor
ElGamal public key; when set, every confidential transfer must additionally encrypt the amount under
the auditor's key, which lets the holder of the corresponding secret decrypt every transfer amount
for that mint.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-balances" /> A dishonest sender could encrypt an
inconsistent amount under the auditor key, so the program requires an additional proof that the
auditor encryption is consistent with the others.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-overview" />
The confidential transfer authority can set or update the auditor key, or remove it
entirely.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-balances" />

**Closing an account gains preconditions.** `EmptyAccount` sets the confidential balance to zero and
must be called before the account can be closed.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> Offside Labs states
the closure conditions concretely: for an account with the confidential transfer extension both
pending and available balances must be zero, and with the confidential transfer fee extension the
withheld amount must be zero as well — which is what the extensions' `closable()` helpers
check.<Cite src="offside-token-2022-part1" />

**Fees behave differently under confidentiality.** For confidential transfers the fee is **not
deducted from the transferred amount**; instead the fee must be separately included in the proof used
for the transfer.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> And the extension interacts badly with a
capability from the previous lesson: the permanent delegate does not work on tokens held in a
confidential balance, so users can escape it there, and an issuer relying on both must set
auto-approval to false or freeze accounts.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" />

A payments company wants merchant balances confidential. Each merchant gets a Token-2022 account with
the confidential transfer extension, configured once at onboarding by the company's backend, with
`maximum_pending_balance_credit_counter` set to 8 because merchants settle daily and the team wanted

<Answer>
**1 — The backend cannot configure the accounts at all.** Only the account owner may configure
confidential transfers for their account, precisely because only they should set the encryption
key.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-balances" /> The onboarding flow has to put a signing and
key-management step in the merchant's hands, which is a product decision the architecture forces.

**2 — A counter of 8 is a nine-cent denial of service.** An attacker sends nine dust confidential
transfers and the merchant cannot receive anything further until they apply the pending balance —
which is exactly the attack Neodyme describes, an attacker intentionally exceeding the maximum to
prevent further confidential transfers.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> For a merchant taking
payments continuously, "cannot receive until you act" is an outage, and the attacker can repeat it
immediately after every recovery.

**3 — Raising the counter is not a fix, and the team should not expect one.** A higher maximum
increases the proof-generation time needed to apply the pending balance, and receiving another
transfer during that computation means starting over.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> The
documentation's typical value of 2^16 comes from keeping ciphertext decryption
tractable,<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-encryption" /> not from resisting an adversary. The
honest position is that no setting is safe against both failure modes, so a merchant-facing design
needs automated, fast, frequent `ApplyPendingBalance` — which only the owner can
authorise<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-overview" /> — and monitoring for the counter
approaching its maximum.

**4 — Daily settlement leaks the balances the design was meant to hide.** A merchant who receives a
day's transfers and then withdraws the lot to the non-confidential balance publishes the total: the
deposit and withdraw boundaries are visible, and applying then immediately withdrawing after a small
number of transfers is exactly the pattern that lets chain analysts recover the
amount.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> Confidentiality accrues from many transfers with many
counterparties;<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> a clean daily sweep destroys it by construction.

**5 — And the amounts may not fit.** A single confidential transfer amount is limited to 48
bits.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-overview" /> On a mint with nine decimals that bounds one
transfer to a fraction of what a settlement run might carry, so large movements must be split — which
is itself a visible pattern.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**A precise vocabulary for what a privacy feature provides.** Ask three separate questions of any such
system: are the amounts hidden, are the participants hidden, and what does ordinary usage reveal
regardless. Confidential transfers answer yes, no, and *quite a lot* — and the second answer is forced
by declared account access rather than chosen, which is why no future version will change it.

**A read on when small proof systems are the right call.** Sigma protocols plus Bulletproofs, no
trusted setup, no circuit design.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-zkps" /> Statements this
narrow do not need a general-purpose zkVM, and recognising that boundary is worth more than knowing
either technology.

**And an integration checklist that is mostly about who does what.** The owner holds the encryption
key, configures the account, applies the pending balance and empties it before
closure.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-balances" /><Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> A
protocol that expected to do those things for its users has to redesign around a token whose state its
users alone control.

One lesson remains, and it collapses the module into a single decision. The closing question is what
posture an integrator adopts when the answer to "what can a mint do" is a list this long — and why
accepting the *program* is the wrong unit of trust.
