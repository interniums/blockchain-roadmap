You have seen this bug before, on another chain, in another language, and you already know the fix.
That is the reason to spend sixteen minutes on it here rather than four: the defect is one you can
recognise instantly, and the *mechanism* underneath it is different enough that half your instincts
about detecting it are wrong.

Start with the shape. A vault program accepts a deposit. It builds a transfer of 1,000,000 raw units
from the user into its own token account, the transfer succeeds, and it credits the user with
1,000,000. Every accounting identity the program maintains is now off by the fee, in the same
direction, on every deposit, forever — and the deficit is paid by whoever withdraws last.

On Ethereum this is the [fee-on-transfer](/c/fee-on-transfer-tokens) class, and the canonical answer
is the [balance-delta pattern](/c/balance-delta-pattern): read your own balance before and after,
credit the difference. That answer is still right here. What is different is where the missing tokens
go, who can see them, what happens to your account while they sit there, and the fact that the
instruction you have been using for years now **fails outright** on exactly these mints.

## Three destinations, not two

The ERC-20 fee-on-transfer tokens you catalogued in Track 04 implement the fee inside their own
`transfer` function. The mechanism varies but the accounting does not: the sender's balance falls by
the full amount, the recipient's rises by less, and the remainder lands in some other account the
token chose. Two credits. Once the call returns, `balanceOf(recipient)` is the truth, and the delta
pattern reads that truth directly.

Token-2022's transfer fee splits it differently. The extension guide's phrasing is precise: on every
transfer, **some amount is withheld on the recipient account, untouchable by the recipient**, and
those tokens can be withheld by a separate authority on the
mint.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> The money does not leave for a fee wallet. It
stays where it landed, in a compartment of the recipient's own account that the recipient cannot
spend.

That figure uses a 50-basis-point fee, which the guide illustrates as: with 50 basis points, a
transfer of 1,000 tokens yields 5 tokens.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" />

The configuration has four fields, and every one of them is a thing an integrator has to look at: the
**fee in basis points** assessed on each transfer, a **maximum fee** capping it in absolute terms — a
maximum of 5,000 tokens means even a transfer of 10,000,000,000,000 yields only 5,000 — the
**transfer fee authority** that can modify the fees, and the **withdraw withheld authority** that can
move tokens withheld on the mint or on token accounts.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" />

The fee arithmetic is specified exactly, in the same documentation set that describes the
confidential variant. The basis-point parameter `bp` is a percentage rate two decimal places to the
right, so `bp = 1` is 0.01%, `bp = 100` is 1%, and `bp = 10000` is 100%; the computed fee is capped
by `max_fee`; and **the transfer fee is always rounded up to the nearest positive
integer**.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-zkps" /> The worked cases given there are worth
copying into a test file verbatim:

Rounding up matters more than it looks. It means there is no transfer amount small enough to be
fee-free, which removes a whole family of "dust the vault" tricks and also means your minimum deposit
size can never be one raw unit.

## Why the fee sits in the recipient's account

This is the part that has no Ethereum analogue at all, and it is a direct consequence of the machine
you spent the first four modules of this track learning.

The documentation states the reason outright: **the design of pooling transfer fees at the recipient
account is meant to maximise parallelization of transactions. Otherwise, one configured fee recipient
account would be write-locked between parallel transfers, decreasing throughput of the
protocol.**<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" />

Read that against [declared account access](/c/declared-account-access). Every Solana transaction
names the accounts it will touch and whether each is writable, and a write lock on an account is
exclusive. A fee design that credited one central treasury account on every transfer would name that
account as writable in **every transfer of that mint**, which serialises all of them against each
other. The token would have a throughput ceiling set by a single account's write lock, no matter how
many cores were available.

So the fee is not withheld at the recipient because that is a natural place for it. It is withheld
there because the recipient's account is already write-locked by the transfer, and using it costs no
additional contention. The extension's most confusing property for an integrator is a **scheduling
optimisation**, and once you see that, the rest of the lifecycle follows: the fees have to be swept
up later, out of band, by someone willing to pay for it.

<Aside kind="note">
This is a good place to notice what the module's contrast is actually for. An EVM engineer reads
"fee withheld inside the recipient's account" as a baroque choice. It is a forced move on a machine
with pessimistic account-level locking — the same machine property that gives Solana its
parallelism. Nearly every Token-2022 design decision that looks odd has a constraint like this behind
it, and finding the constraint is more useful than cataloguing the oddity.
</Aside>

## The harvest lifecycle, and the part that will break your close path

Because the fees are scattered across every account that ever received a transfer, collecting them is
a search, not a read. The guide is explicit: before withdrawing, the authority must **find which
accounts have withheld tokens by iterating over all accounts for the
mint**.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" />

Three instructions cover the lifecycle. `withdraw_withheld_tokens_from_accounts` lets the withdraw
withheld authority pull directly from token accounts. `harvest_withheld_tokens_to_mint` moves the
withheld amounts from an account into the mint and is **permissionless** — anyone may call it.
`withdraw_withheld_tokens` with the mint included then moves what has accumulated on the mint to a
destination of the authority's choosing.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> A vendor
walkthrough of the whole cycle, mint creation through withdrawal, is worth doing once with a devnet
mint before you integrate.<Cite src="quicknode-transfer-fees" /><Cite src="chainstack-token-2022-hooks" />

Now the consequence that turns up as an incident. **An account cannot be closed while it holds any
tokens, including withheld ones**, which is why the permissionless harvest exists at all: a user who
wants to close an account must first clear the withheld
amount.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> Neodyme states the same in terms of the account
state — fees accumulate in the `withheld_amount` property of the token account extension, and the
account cannot be closed until they are harvested to the
mint.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" />

Offside Labs supplies the exact failure. A program that closes a token account after checking only
`amount == 0` will hit `AccountHasWithheldTransferFees`, whose message reads that an account can only
be closed if its withheld fee balance is zero and that you should harvest fees to the mint and try
again.<Cite src="offside-token-2022-part1" /> Their remedy is to stop hand-rolling the precondition:
the extensions expose `closable()` helpers — `TransferFeeAmount::closable()`,
`ConfidentialTransferAccount::closable()`, `ConfidentialTransferFeeAmount::closable()` — and the CPI
Guard case has its own check.<Cite src="offside-token-2022-part1" />

Take the general lesson from that, because it recurs: **when an extension adds state, it adds
preconditions to operations that previously had none**, and the extension usually ships the predicate
you need. Reconstructing the precondition from the documentation is how you get it 80% right.

## `transfer` does not merely become unfashionable; it fails

This is the difference that most surprises people arriving from ERC-20, where a fee-on-transfer token
still satisfies the standard interface and `transfer` still returns.

The extension guide flags it as an important note: **transferring tokens with a transfer fee requires
using `transfer_checked` or `transfer_checked_with_fee` instead of `transfer`. Otherwise, the transfer
will fail.**<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" />

Offside Labs traced why, in the program's own source. `transfer` in Token-2022 is marked deprecated
since version 4.0.0 with a note recommending `transfer_checked` or `transfer_checked_with_fee`. The
two replacements take extra arguments — `transfer_checked` adds `mint_pubkey` and `decimals`;
`transfer_checked_with_fee` adds `mint`, `decimals` and `fee`.<Cite src="offside-token-2022-part1" />
Inside `process_transfer`, when `expected_decimals` is `None` — which is what calling plain `transfer`
means — the code takes a branch that has no mint account to work with, and in that branch, **if the
source account carries the transfer hook extension or the transfer fee amount extension, it returns
`TokenError::MintRequiredForTransfer`**.<Cite src="offside-token-2022-part1" />

That mechanism is worth reading twice, because it explains both the good news and a subtlety you will
meet again in two lessons.

**The good news.** The failure is structural rather than conventional. The program cannot compute a
fee or resolve a hook's accounts without the mint, so it refuses instead of guessing. Requiring the
mint and the decimals forces the caller to have *looked at the mint*, which is the single most useful
thing an integrator can be forced to do. An interface that fails loudly on the mints whose rules you
did not account for is a gift.

**The subtlety.** The check that triggers it is made **on the source account**, not on the mint. That
is fine when every account of the mint carries the paired `TransferFeeAmount` extension, which is
what happens when accounts are created for a mint that already had
`TransferFeeConfig`.<Cite src="spl-token-2022-docsrs-extensiontype" /> It stops being fine when
accounts exist that were created *before* the fee did — and Neodyme's write-up of the close-and-reinit
hazard names exactly that scenario, noting the fee bypass is possible **because the check for the
Transfer Fee extension only happens on the source account**.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> Park it;
lesson six is about nothing else.

## Two correct ways to credit, and when each one is right

Neodyme names both fixes and states a preference, which is worth reproducing exactly because the
preference runs against the EVM habit.

> **First, precalculate the fee amount and use the new `TransferCheckedWithFee` instruction. Second,
> calculate the difference in your balance before and after the transfer** — with the first
> recommended as the cleaner solution.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" />

Both are correct. They fail differently, and the choice is about what you want to happen when your
model of the mint is stale.

**Precalculation with `transfer_checked_with_fee`.** You read the fee configuration, compute the fee
yourself, and pass it. The instruction **only succeeds if the fee is correctly calculated, in order
to avoid any surprises during the transfer**.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> So a
wrong expectation is not silently absorbed; the transaction fails. That is a strong property: your
accounting can never be quietly wrong, because a disagreement between your model and the program's
becomes an aborted transaction.

It also means you have taken on a live dependency. The transfer fee authority can modify the
fees,<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> so a fee you read at listing time, or cached at
the start of a batch, is a value that can move underneath you. With this approach that shows up as
failed user transactions — visible, annoying, and safe.

**Measuring the delta.** Read your own token account before and after, credit the difference. This
does not require you to model the fee at all, which is precisely its virtue: it is correct for a fee
you misread, a fee that changed between your read and the transfer, a max-fee cap you did not apply,
and the rounding-up rule you forgot.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-zkps" />

Say the rule as a rule, because it is the most reusable sentence in this module:

**Credit what arrived, never what you asked for.** Read the vault's own balance before and after the
transfer and credit the difference. Crediting the amount named in the instruction is correct for
exactly the tokens that take no fee, and it fails in the direction that makes you insolvent rather
than the direction that makes you rich.

The two approaches compose, and in a vault they should. Use `transfer_checked_with_fee` so that a
disagreement fails loudly, and still credit the measured delta so that the number you write into your
ledger comes from the chain rather than from your arithmetic. This is the same argument as
[internal accounting versus `balanceOf`](/c/internal-accounting-vs-balanceof) with the conclusion
inverted for the deposit step specifically: internal accounting is right for what you *owe*, and a
measurement is right for what you *received*.

<Aside kind="warn">
One thing the delta does not solve: the amount you measure is your account's spendable `amount`, and
the withheld tokens are also *in your account*, in a compartment you cannot spend. If your protocol's
solvency check compares liabilities against a figure that includes withheld tokens, it will believe
it is solvent by exactly the amount it cannot pay out. Whichever field you use, use the same one in
the deposit path and the solvency check, and know which one it is.
</Aside>

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="amount-sent-is-not-amount-received">
This deposit path was written for SPL Token, then updated for Token-2022 by someone who had read
about transfer fees. It is closer to right than most, and it is still insolvent. Find the defects.

```rust
pub fn deposit(ctx: Context<Deposit>, amount: u64) -> Result<()> {
    let mint = PodStateWithExtensions::<PodMint>::unpack(&ctx.accounts.mint.data.borrow())?;

    let fee = match mint.get_extension::<TransferFeeConfig>() {
        Ok(cfg) => amount * (cfg.transfer_fee_basis_points as u64) / 10_000,
        Err(_)  => 0,
    };

    transfer_checked(ctx.accounts.transfer_ctx(), amount, ctx.accounts.mint.decimals)?;

    ctx.accounts.position.shares += amount - fee;
    Ok(())
}
```

<Answer>
**Defect 1 — the fee formula is wrong in three ways at once, and every one is in the protocol's
disfavour.** It ignores `max_fee`, so for large transfers it subtracts far more than was actually
withheld and under-credits the depositor. It divides, which truncates, while the program **rounds the
fee up to the nearest positive integer** — so for the documented case of amount 100 at `bp = 110` the
real fee is 2 and this computes 1.<Cite src="solana-program-confidential-zkps" /> And it reads the
basis points at the moment of the deposit while the transfer fee authority can modify
them,<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> so even a correct formula is a snapshot. Note the
direction of the third error: it under-credits some users and over-credits others, so the ledger
drifts in both directions and never reconciles.

**Defect 2 — it computed a fee and then did not tell the program about it.** `transfer_checked` takes
mint and decimals only. The instruction that accepts an expected fee is
`transfer_checked_with_fee`,<Cite src="offside-token-2022-part1" /> and it succeeds only if the fee is
correctly calculated, specifically to avoid surprises during the
transfer.<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> Passing the fee would have converted defect 1
from silent mis-accounting into a failed transaction — which is the entire value of the with-fee
variant.

**Defect 3 — the credit is derived rather than measured.** The vault's own balance before and after
the transfer is the only figure that is true regardless of the fee configuration, the cap, the
rounding, or a change made between reading and
transferring.<Cite src="neodyme-token-2022" /> Replace `amount - fee` with the delta and defects 1
and 3 both disappear.

**Defect 4 — nothing in this path handles the account it is transferring into.** Two problems live
there. If the mint sets `DefaultAccountState = Frozen`, a vault account created on demand is frozen
and cannot receive anything. And once fees start accumulating, the vault's own account carries a
non-zero `withheld_amount`, so any close path that checks `amount == 0` will fail with
`AccountHasWithheldTransferFees` rather than closing — the extensions' `closable()` helpers are the
correct precondition.<Cite src="offside-token-2022-part1" />

**Defect 5 — a `u64` multiplication before division.** `amount * basis_points` overflows for large
`amount`. In release builds Rust wraps rather than panicking unless overflow checks are enabled,
which turns an arithmetic bug into a wrong fee rather than an abort. The correct fee is not computed
this way at all, but the habit is worth naming.

**The version that is actually right** is shorter than the wrong one: unpack the mint, refuse the
deposit if it carries any extension you have not handled, transfer with
`transfer_checked_with_fee` when a fee is configured, and credit `balance_after - balance_before`.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**A defect class you can now recognise in a second ecosystem.** The ERC-20 fee-on-transfer bug and
the Token-2022 transfer fee bug are the same defect — crediting a requested amount instead of a
received one — and the fact that it survived a complete change of virtual machine, language, state
model and token standard is the strongest evidence available that it is a *design* error rather than
an implementation one. When you meet a third ecosystem, this is the first question to ask of its
transfer primitive.

**A concrete reason the extension model is not merely ERC-20's problem in new clothes.** You can read
`TransferFeeConfig` off the mint before you list it, get the basis points and the cap, and know that
a mint without the extension will never grow one.<Cite src="solana-docs-token-extensions" /> That is
strictly more than ERC-20 offers. What you cannot read off the mint is what the fee will be *later*,
because the transfer fee authority can change it,<Cite src="solana-token-2022-extensions-ref" /> and
what you cannot read at all is whether the mint itself will be replaced.

**And the working habit.** Prefer the instruction that fails when your model is wrong over the one
that proceeds, and derive nothing you can measure. Both halves matter: the first turns silent drift
into a visible error, and the second means you were never depending on the model in the first place.

The next lesson replaces the fee with something considerably less predictable. With the transfer hook
extension, the thing that runs inside your transfer is not an arithmetic rule the program applies —
it is **a program of the mint authority's choosing**, executing in your transaction, spending your
compute budget, and able to fail your instruction whenever it likes.
