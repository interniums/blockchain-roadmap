A user installs your app, connects nothing, funds nothing, and completes their first on-chain action
forty seconds later. They have never held ETH. They do not know what ETH is.

Somebody paid for that. In ERC-4337 the somebody is a contract you deployed, holding a deposit you
funded, running validation logic you wrote. The user's experience is "gasless". Your experience is a
contract that spends real money according to rules you specified, at a rate an adversary chooses.

This lesson is that contract: the mechanism first, then the three ways it gets emptied, then the
arithmetic that tells you whether your rules are actually rules.

## The mechanism

A paymaster is named inside the UserOperation's `paymasterAndData` field. The documentation's summary
of the role is two lines: paymasters sponsor gas for accounts under programmable conditions, and must
stake and deposit ETH into the EntryPoint.<Cite src="erc-4337-docs" />

During the verification loop, the EntryPoint asks it a question:

```solidity
function validatePaymasterUserOp(
    PackedUserOperation calldata userOp,
    bytes32 userOpHash,
    uint256 maxCost
) external returns (bytes memory context, uint256 validationData);
```

<Cite src="eip-4337" />

`maxCost` is the number to stare at. It is the most this operation can cost, computed from the gas
limits and fee fields in the UserOperation. Returning without reverting is a commitment to cover it.
`validationData` carries the same packed authoriser-plus-time-window word an account
returns.<Cite src="eip-4337" /> `context` is an optional blob handed back to you after execution.

If you returned a context, the EntryPoint calls you again after the main execution call:

```solidity
function postOp(
    PostOpMode mode,
    bytes calldata context,
    uint256 actualGasCost,
    uint256 actualUserOpFeePerGas
) external;

enum PostOpMode {
    opSucceeded, // UserOperation succeeded
    opReverted   // UserOperation reverted. paymaster still has to pay for gas.
}
```

<Cite src="eip-4337" />

That comment in the enum is the specification telling you the deal in its own words, and it is the
single most important sentence in this lesson. **Paymasters are charged even when UserOperations
revert.**<Cite src="eip-4337" />

You are not sponsoring successful actions. You are sponsoring *attempts*.

## Two gas budgets of its own

The paymaster has its own metered slices, separate from the account's:
`paymasterVerificationGasLimit` for its validation and `paymasterPostOpGasLimit` for its post-operation
hook.<Cite src="eip-4337" /> Both are fields in the UserOperation — which is to say, both are numbers
chosen by the party submitting the operation, not by you.

Hold that next to what you know about
[a transaction's gas limit versus what it uses](/c/tx-gas-limit-vs-used). In ordinary transactions the
person choosing the limit is the person paying, so a bad choice punishes only them. Here those are
different people. Someone else picks the ceiling; your deposit absorbs the consequences.

## Drain one: sponsoring anything

The simplest failure needs no cleverness at all.

A paymaster whose `validatePaymasterUserOp` returns success unconditionally — or on a condition an
attacker satisfies trivially, like "the sender is a smart account" or "the call is to our contract" —
will pay for every operation that names it. An attacker does not need a bug. They submit valid,
expensive, sponsored operations in a loop until the deposit reaches zero.

Nothing in the trace looks like an attack. Every operation is well-formed, correctly signed by an
account the attacker controls, and accepted by your rules. Your deposit goes to bundlers, who did
nothing wrong either.

The rate is calculable, which is what makes this a design question rather than a fear:

```text
loss per hour  =  maxCost you accept
                × operations per block a bundler will carry for one sender
                × blocks per hour
```

The middle term is not zero and not unbounded — ERC-7562 caps a single sender at
`SAME_SENDER_MEMPOOL_COUNT` = 4 operations in the mempool, and an unstaked entity at
`SAME_UNSTAKED_ENTITY_MEMPOOL_COUNT` = 10.<Cite src="erc-7562" /> Those are mempool quotas per
bundler, not a global rate limit, and an attacker with many sender addresses multiplies straight
through the first one. Treat the quotas as friction, never as your control.

<Aside kind="warn">
The reflection prompt for this module asks you to compute the maximum your paymaster can lose in one
hour assuming every rule you wrote is respected and every sponsored user is an attacker. If your
scoping rules do not let you compute that number, you have written preferences, not rules. The
formula above is the whole exercise; the only hard part is knowing your own `maxCost` ceiling, and if
you do not cap `callGasLimit` yourself, you do not have one.
</Aside>

## Drain two: the penalty you did not know you were paying

This one is a genuinely non-obvious mechanic, and it is the first attack OtterSec documents.

Recall the rule from the first lesson: a penalty of 10% (`UNUSED_GAS_PENALTY_PERCENT`) applies to the
amounts of `callGasLimit` and `paymasterPostOpGasLimit` left **unused**, when the unused remainder is
at least 40,000 (`PENALTY_GAS_THRESHOLD`).<Cite src="eip-4337" />

OtterSec's finding is that paymaster implementations frequently do not model it. In their words, the
code does not account for gas penalties: the actual gas charged to the paymaster includes not just the
gas used, but also any penalties incurred from differences between the execution gas limit and actual
execution gas.<Cite src="ottersec-paymasters" /> The exploitation is direct — a malicious actor sets
an artificially high gas limit to trigger the penalty.<Cite src="ottersec-paymasters" />

### Worked example: where the shortfall comes from

An ERC-20 paymaster charges the user tokens equal to the gas the operation consumed, and pays the
EntryPoint in ETH. Trace one operation.

**Subgoal 1 — write down what the paymaster believes it owes.** Its accounting is built on
`actualGasCost`, the figure handed to `postOp`.<Cite src="eip-4337" /> It converts that to tokens at
its own rate and takes them from the user. In its ledger, revenue equals cost.

**Subgoal 2 — write down what the EntryPoint actually debits.** The debit is the gas consumed *plus*
the penalty on unused `callGasLimit` and `paymasterPostOpGasLimit` above the
threshold.<Cite src="eip-4337" /> The penalty is not a separate invoice; it is part of what the
paymaster's deposit funds.

**Subgoal 3 — identify who controls the gap.** The gap is 10% of (limit − used) whenever that
difference is at least 40,000. `callGasLimit` is a UserOperation field. The submitter picks it. A
sponsored operation that genuinely needs 30,000 gas can declare a limit of 1,030,000 and leave
1,000,000 unused, of which 100,000 gas is penalty.

**Subgoal 4 — price it.** At a modest 1 gwei effective fee, 100,000 penalty gas is 0.0001 ETH per
operation that the paymaster pays and does not recover. The user was charged for 30,000. Repeat.

**Subgoal 5 — name the fix and its cost.** Either cap the gas limits your validation will accept —
read `callGasLimit` and `paymasterPostOpGasLimit` out of the operation and reject anything above a
ceiling you chose — or include the worst-case penalty in the amount you collect up front. The first
is better: it makes the exposure a number you set, and a rejected operation costs you nothing at all.

## Drain three: charging after execution

The third failure is the most severe of the three, and it is structural rather than arithmetic.

Two patterns exist for an ERC-20 paymaster. **Pre-payment with refund**: the user pre-approves and
prepays the maximum cost, and gets the difference back. **Post-execution charging**: the user bundles
the token approval into the same UserOperation, which removes the need for a separate pre-approval —
better UX, and the reason the pattern is popular.<Cite src="ottersec-paymasters" />

The second pattern is exploitable. An attacker can approve the required token allowance during
simulation to pass validation, then revoke the allowance just before the UserOperation is
submitted.<Cite src="ottersec-paymasters" /> By the time `postOp` runs and tries to pull tokens, there
is nothing to pull.

Now apply what the previous lesson established about the two loops. The payment guarantee the bundler
relies on was settled in the verification loop. So: even when `postOp()` fails, the EntryPoint will
still complete the execution and pay the bundler who submitted it — and that payment comes from the
paymaster's deposit.<Cite src="ottersec-paymasters" /> The paymaster has paid ETH and collected
nothing.

OtterSec's recommendation is blunt, and it is the one to internalise: always collect full payment
during validation, not after execution; the most secure approach is to require upfront pre-funding,
even though it may temporarily lock more user funds.<Cite src="ottersec-paymasters" />

Note what that costs. The safe pattern is the one with the worse onboarding story — a separate
approval, more locked funds, an extra step. The insecure pattern exists because it is the nicer
product. That tension is the actual decision, and it should be made deliberately rather than by
copying whichever example you found first.

## Why validation cannot check what you want it to check

At this point the obvious instinct is to write better rules. "Sponsor at most three operations per
account per hour." "Only sponsor if this user's on-chain reputation score is above X."

Neither is expressible in the validation phase as written, and the reason is the ERC-7562 fence from
the first lesson.

**You cannot read the clock.** `TIMESTAMP` is on the OP-011 banned opcode list, along with `NUMBER`,
`BASEFEE`, `GASPRICE`, `ORIGIN`, `COINBASE` and the rest.<Cite src="erc-7562" /> "Per hour" has no
in-validation expression. What you have instead is the `validAfter`/`validUntil` window in the
returned `validationData`, which the EntryPoint checks for you.<Cite src="eip-4337" />

**You cannot freely read other contracts.** A staked paymaster may access its own storage, associated
storage in non-entity contracts, and read-only storage of any non-entity contract; an unstaked one may
not.<Cite src="erc-7562" /> Reputation-score lookups against a third-party registry are exactly the
shape the rules restrict.

**You cannot call an address without code.** `EXTCODE*` and `*CALL` against a codeless address are
forbidden during validation by OP-041.<Cite src="erc-7562" />

**And counting costs storage.** A per-account counter is an `SSTORE` in the validation phase. On the
measurements taken for this course, a non-zero to non-zero `SSTORE` is 2,900 gas plus 2,100 when the
slot is cold, and a cold `SLOAD` is 2,100.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /> Every sponsored
operation pays that, on top of everything else, out of the deposit you are trying to protect.

So real paymasters take one of two roads, and both have a cost worth naming.

**Sign off-chain.** A backend service decides whether to sponsor, signs an approval over the
operation's hash, and the paymaster's validation reduces to a signature check against a known key.
This is expressive — your backend can apply any policy at all, including rate limits and reputation —
and it moves the spending decision into a web service. Your signing endpoint is now the thing that
authorises spending, so it inherits every property of a spending endpoint: it needs authentication,
rate limiting, monitoring, and a key that is not in an environment variable on a laptop. You have not
removed the risk; you have moved it somewhere your existing tooling can see it, which is usually the
right trade as long as you say so out loud.

**Stake and use on-chain state.** Accept the stake requirement and the storage rules and enforce what
you can enforce inside them. Narrower, cheaper to reason about, and constrained by what ERC-7562
permits.

## The failure that is not a drain

There is a fourth outcome worth naming, because it takes the paymaster off the air without costing a
single wei.

Bundlers score the entities a UserOperation names. The specification's stated policy is that an entity
causing invalidation of a large number of UserOperations in the mempool is throttled down, or
completely banned for a period.<Cite src="eip-4337" /> A throttled entity is limited to
`THROTTLED_ENTITY_MEMPOOL_COUNT` = 4 operations in the mempool, and an unstaked entity to
`SAME_UNSTAKED_ENTITY_MEMPOOL_COUNT` = 10.<Cite src="erc-7562" /> The rules are careful about
attribution — EREP-015 says a paymaster should not have its `opsSeen` incremented because of a failure
of the factory or the account<Cite src="erc-7562" /> — but careful attribution is not the same as no
attribution.

So a paymaster whose validation reverts often, for any reason, degrades its own standing with each
bundler independently. There is no on-chain record of this, no event, no balance change. Sponsorship
simply starts working less often, for some users, on some bundlers. Build the observability for it
before you need it: track your accepted-versus-submitted ratio per bundler endpoint, because that
ratio is the only view you get of a score you cannot query.

## The scoping list

Every dimension below is one you can constrain, and each one you leave open is a term in the loss
formula. This is the same discipline as
[naming a capability rather than a role](/c/capability-not-role) — the question is not who the user is
but what your money is allowed to do.

- **Sender.** An allowlist, a proof of prior interaction, or a signature from your backend. "Any
  address" means "any number of addresses".
- **Target and selector.** The most valuable single constraint, and the cheapest to check: the target
  contract must be yours and the first four bytes of the inner calldata must be a selector you chose.
- **Calldata shape beyond the selector.** A sponsored `mint(to)` where `to` is unconstrained sponsors
  minting to anyone.
- **Gas ceilings.** Reject `callGasLimit` and `paymasterPostOpGasLimit` above a value you set. Without
  this, drain two is open and your `maxCost` is whatever the submitter felt like.
- **Value.** Sponsor gas, not value transfer, unless value transfer is the product.
- **Rate per account per period.** Expressible as an on-chain counter (with the storage costs above)
  or as backend policy behind a signature.
- **Expiry.** Use the `validUntil` half of `validationData`; it is free and it is enforced by the
  EntryPoint.<Cite src="eip-4337" />
- **Chain.** The `userOpHash` binds to `chainid` already, but your backend's policy should too.

Review this paymaster. It is meant to sponsor the first action of new users of one game contract, and
it is currently deployed with 4 ETH of deposit at the EntryPoint. Name every way its deposit leaves,
ranked by how fast.

```solidity
contract GamePaymaster {
    address public constant GAME = 0x00000000000000000000000000000000000000A1;
    address public immutable entryPoint;
    mapping(address => bool) public sponsored;

    function validatePaymasterUserOp(
        PackedUserOperation calldata userOp,
        bytes32,
        uint256
    ) external returns (bytes memory context, uint256 validationData) {
        require(msg.sender == entryPoint, "not entrypoint");
        address target = address(bytes20(userOp.callData[16:36]));
        require(target == GAME, "wrong target");
        require(!sponsored[userOp.sender], "already sponsored");
        sponsored[userOp.sender] = true;
        return ("", 0);
    }
}
```

<Answer>
**Fastest: unbounded gas limits, so `maxCost` is whatever the attacker declares.** The third parameter
of `validatePaymasterUserOp` is `maxCost` and this implementation does not even name it, let alone cap
it.<Cite src="eip-4337" /> Nothing reads `callGasLimit`. An attacker generates fresh sender addresses
— each one passes the `sponsored` check exactly once, which is the entire intended defence — and
declares an enormous `callGasLimit`. Each operation costs the deposit its real gas *plus* 10% of the
unused remainder above the 40,000 threshold.<Cite src="eip-4337" /><Cite src="ottersec-paymasters" />
Address generation is free. The one-sponsorship-per-address rule bounds nothing.

**Second: the selector is never checked, only the target.** The code extracts a target from a fixed
calldata offset and compares it to `GAME`. Any function on the game contract is sponsored, including
whichever one is most expensive, and including any function added by a future upgrade. Constrain the
selector, not just the address.

**Third: the calldata offset is an assumption, not a parse.** `userOp.callData[16:36]` assumes one
specific account implementation's encoding of an `execute` call. A different account implementation
lays those bytes out differently, so the value read as "target" may be an argument, a length prefix,
or padding — and an attacker who wants `target == GAME` to be true while calling something else only
has to pick an account whose encoding puts the right twenty bytes at offset 16. Decode the call
properly or restrict which account implementations you sponsor.

**Fourth: no expiry.** `validationData` is returned as `0`, meaning valid forever. A campaign that was
supposed to run for a launch weekend is still sponsoring in two years. `validUntil` costs
nothing.<Cite src="eip-4337" />

**Fifth, and structural: the `sponsored` write happens during validation.** That is an `SSTORE` on the
paymaster's own storage, which is an access a **staked** paymaster is permitted — a staked paymaster or
factory may access its own storage, an unstaked one may not.<Cite src="erc-7562" /> It also means every
sponsored operation pays roughly 2,900 to 5,000 gas for the bookkeeping, depending on whether the slot
is cold.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /> That is a cost, not a bug, but it belongs in the loss
arithmetic — and if this paymaster is unstaked, it is not a cost but a rejection.

**What is right about it.** The EntryPoint check on the first line is correct and non-negotiable, and
scoping to a single target contract is the right instinct — it is simply implemented against an
assumption rather than a parse, and left without the other four constraints.

**The number the author could not compute.** With unbounded gas limits there is no finite answer to
"what can this lose in an hour". That is the tell. A scoped paymaster's worst hour is arithmetic; an
unscoped one's is a function of how bored the attacker is.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The practice exercises for this module** are the two halves of what you just read: build a paymaster
scoped to a single contract method and prove sponsorship is refused for anything else, then deploy a
permissive one on Anvil and drain it from an unrelated account. Do the second one first. The drain
takes about ten lines and it changes how the first one feels.

**The next lesson** puts the paymaster in the other architecture. A 7702-delegated EOA can be
sponsored by the same paymaster through the same EntryPoint — the economics you just learned do not
change when the account's address stops changing.

**The session-key module** is the same problem with a different budget. A session key is a scoped
authority over the user's funds; a paymaster is a scoped authority over yours. Both are secured by the
same act: writing down what the rule permits, then computing the worst case it allows.
