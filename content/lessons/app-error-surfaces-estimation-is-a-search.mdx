Then send it for real, twice, from the same starting state:

```text
gas limit 127,400  (what it consumes)   →  receipt status 0x0, reverted, gas burned
gas limit 140,781  (what it needs)      →  receipt status 0x1
```

Consumption and requirement are different quantities, they diverge with call depth, and the gap is
not a rounding error — it is 10.5% at depth forty and it
grows.<Cite src="measured-revert-surfaces-1-7-1" /> This lesson is about what
`eth_estimateGas` is actually computing, the assumption that computation rests on, and the
documented rule that makes an exact estimate insufficient even when the estimate is right.

## Estimation is a search for a limit, not a measurement of consumption

go-ethereum's estimator states its own contract in the doc comment above `Estimate`: it "returns the
lowest possible gas limit that allows the transaction to run successfully with the provided context
options".<Cite src="geth-gasestimator-source" /> Read that as a definition rather than a
description. The output is a *limit*, chosen so that execution completes. It is not the gas the
execution consumed, and in the table above those two numbers differ at depth twenty and diverge
sharply by depth forty.

The implementation is a bisection over real executions. Stripped to its skeleton, and following the
current source:<Cite src="geth-gasestimator-source" />

**Step one — pick the ceiling.** `hi` starts at the block header's gas limit, or at the caller's
own `gas` field when they supplied one at least as large as a plain transfer. Then it is capped
three times over: by `params.MaxTxGas` when the target fork is Osaka and not yet Amsterdam; by what
the sender can afford; and by the node's configured RPC gas cap.

**Step two — the affordability recap, which is not a revert check.** With a non-zero fee cap the
estimator computes `available = balance - value`, then `allowance = available / feeCap`, and lowers
`hi` to that allowance if it is smaller. This is arithmetic on the sender's balance, performed
before any execution. It is the origin of the message every wallet has shown you, and the next
lesson is largely about it.

**Step three — the plain-transfer short circuit.** If the call carries no data and the target holds
no code, the estimator tries `params.TxGas` — 21,000 — once and returns it if it
works.<Cite src="geth-protocol-params" />

**Step four — one execution at the ceiling.** If the call fails at `hi` for a reason other than
running out of gas, the estimator gives up immediately and returns the revert data. If it fails at
`hi` because it ran out of gas, no limit can help and you get `gas required exceeds allowance`.

**Step five — bracket the answer.** On success, `lo` is set to `UsedGas - 1`: consumption is a lower
bound on the limit for almost every transaction. Then it tries an informed guess,
`optimisticGasLimit = (MaxUsedGas + CallStipend) * 64 / 63`, and uses the outcome to move whichever
side of the bracket that result narrows.<Cite src="geth-gasestimator-source" /> The `* 64 / 63` in
that expression is not decoration — it is the same rule that produced the table at the top of this
lesson, applied as a first-order correction. `CallStipend` is 2,300.<Cite src="geth-protocol-params" />

**Step six — bisect.** While `lo + 1 < hi`, execute at the midpoint and move the bracket. The
midpoint is deliberately skewed low, capped at `lo * 2`, because most transactions need a limit
close to their consumption and the search should not waste executions exploring the top of the
range.

**Step seven — stop early if asked.** With a non-zero `ErrorRatio` the loop exits once
`(hi - lo) / hi` falls below it. The comment explains why this is acceptable: a perfect estimate is
pointless because changing network conditions force the caller to bump it anyway, and "wallets tend
to use 20-25% bump".<Cite src="geth-gasestimator-source" />

<Aside kind="note">
That comment is worth keeping in your head for the rest of the module. The people who wrote the
estimator do not expect you to use its output as the gas limit. They expect a client to add
headroom on top, and they tuned the search's precision on that expectation. A client that sends the
estimate unmodified is using the number in a way its authors explicitly did not design for.
</Aside>

## What that costs you

Several executions of the same call against the same state, one after another. Not one. Measured on
a local in-process anvil with no network in the path, for a two-hundred-deep recursion, twenty
samples each:<Cite src="measured-revert-surfaces-1-7-1" />

```text
eth_call          median   3.8 ms
eth_estimateGas   median  16.4 ms
```

Four times slower, with zero network latency and zero provider queueing in the measurement. On a
hosted endpoint, against a heavier contract, over a real connection, this is the difference between
a form that responds instantly and one that visibly stalls before it can enable its submit button.
It is also why providers meter `eth_estimateGas` differently from `eth_call`: it is a genuinely
more expensive request, and the cost scales with how many bisection steps your contract's range
requires.

Two practical consequences. Do not estimate on every keystroke — debounce, and estimate when the
input settles. And do not estimate a call you have not already simulated: simulation is the cheaper
request, it fails faster on a doomed call, and as the next lesson shows it is also the one that
reliably carries the revert data.

## The assumption underneath the search

Bisection is only correct on a monotone predicate. Here the predicate is "does this call succeed at
limit `L`", and bisection requires that succeeding at `L` implies succeeding at every larger limit.

Contracts can break that. The estimator's own source says so, in a comment sitting right above the
line that sets `lo`: consumption lower-bounds the required limit "for almost any transaction", and
"one exception is those that explicitly check gas remaining in order to execute within a given
limit".<Cite src="geth-gasestimator-source" /> Anything that reads `gasleft()` and branches on it,
forwards a computed fraction of gas to a callback, or is written to fit inside a fixed budget can
succeed at a small limit and fail at a larger one.

Here is one, measured. A contract that takes a cheap path when `gasleft()` is at or below 100,000
and an expensive path — forty writes to distinct cold storage slots — when it is
above:<Cite src="measured-revert-surfaces-1-7-1" />

```solidity
function work() external {
    if (gasleft() > 100_000) {
        for (uint256 i = 1; i <= 40; ++i) { slots[i] = i; }
    } else {
        cheap = 1;
    }
}
```

Bisecting real executions over the gas limit gives a success set with a hole in the middle:

`cast estimate` on this contract returns **915,457**.<Cite src="measured-revert-surfaces-1-7-1" />

That number is not wrong. It is a limit at which the transaction succeeds, exactly as the doc
comment promises. It is also twenty-one times the 43,325 that would have done the job, and the
transaction will consume most of it, because at that limit the contract genuinely takes the
expensive branch.<Cite src="measured-revert-surfaces-1-7-1" /> The estimator's first execution runs at the block ceiling, where the expensive
branch is taken, so `lo` is bracketed into the upper region and the entire lower region is invisible
to the search.

Reverse the branch — expensive below the threshold, cheap above — and the same mechanism produces
the failure that actually costs money: the unconstrained execution takes the cheap path and reports
a small consumption, the bisection brackets low, and the returned limit lands in a region where the
contract takes the expensive path and runs out. Nothing in the RPC response distinguishes that case
from a correct estimate. You find out from a reverted receipt.

The defence is not clever client code. It is knowing whether the contracts you call read
`gasleft()`, which is a source-level question you can answer once per integration, and it is why
[bounding gas explicitly](/c/gas-metering-control) rather than branching on what happens to be left
is the pattern that keeps estimation honest.

## Why an exact estimate is still not a safe limit

Now the other half, and this one has no exceptions — it is the rule, applied on every call in every
transaction.

EIP-150 changed gas forwarding so that a call passes on "all but one 64th" of the gas remaining in
the current frame, with the rest retained by the caller.<Cite src="eip-150" /> You met this as
[the 63/64 rule](/c/63-64-rule) when you learned the call semantics. On the client side it has a
consequence that is easy to state and easy to miss: **some of the gas you pay for is structurally
unusable by the deepest frames.**

That is exactly the gap in the opening table. At depth forty the transaction consumes 127,400 gas
and requires a limit of 140,781.<Cite src="measured-revert-surfaces-1-7-1" /> The 13,381
difference was retained a fraction at a time on the way down and never spent by anything. It is not waste in the sense of being burned — an unused limit is
not charged — but it *must be supplied*, because the deepest frame's share is computed from what
remains above it, not from the top-level limit.

Read the progression again with that in mind. At depth zero, ten, the requirement equals the
consumption exactly: with one frame there is nothing to retain. At depth twenty the gap is 0.85%.
At depth forty it is 10.5%.<Cite src="measured-revert-surfaces-1-7-1" /> The compounding is real but it is not the whole story — what makes the
gap appear at all is that the *deepest* frame needs a fixed amount, and delivering that fixed amount
through forty retentions requires more at the top.

<Aside kind="warn">
This is the mechanism behind a specific and common bug: taking `gasUsed` from a receipt, or from a
trace, and reusing it as the gas limit for the same call later. Measured, that produces a reverted
transaction at depth forty — status `0x0`, gas
burned.<Cite src="measured-revert-surfaces-1-7-1" /> `gasUsed` is consumption. A limit is not
consumption. They coincide for shallow calls, which is why the practice survives long enough to
fail in production on the one code path with a deep call tree.
</Aside>

## Estimates are a function of state, and state moves

One more property, because it decides how long an estimate stays valid. The same call, on the same
contract, estimated twice with one successful execution in
between:<Cite src="measured-revert-surfaces-1-7-1" />

```text
estimate, storage slot never written   140,781
after one successful call              112,715
```

A twenty percent drop, with no code change and no configuration change. The first execution wrote a
storage slot that was previously zero; every subsequent execution pays the far lower cost of
updating a non-zero slot.

The direction matters as much as the size. Here it fell, which is harmless — an over-sized limit is
not charged. It rises just as easily: the first interaction of a new user with a contract touches
cold slots and cold accounts, an approval that existed at estimate time can be consumed by another
transaction before yours is included, a pool can cross a tick boundary and take a longer code path.
Any of those makes the estimate you cached too small.

So an estimate has a shelf life measured in blocks, and it is specific to the account, the
arguments, and the state at one moment. Caching one across users is a bug. Caching one across a
user's session is a bug with a longer fuse. Estimating fresh, immediately before the signing prompt,
is the only version that is defensible — and it still leaves the window between the estimate and
inclusion, which is what headroom exists to cover.

## Worked example: choosing a headroom policy you can defend

**Subgoal 1 — separate the three things headroom is compensating for, because they have different
sizes.** The 63/64 retention is structural and depends on call depth: measured, zero at depth ten
and 10.5% at depth forty.<Cite src="measured-revert-surfaces-1-7-1" /><Cite src="eip-150" /> State drift between estimate and inclusion is unbounded in principle and
usually small. Non-monotonic contracts are a correctness problem that headroom does not fix at all
— a multiplier cannot rescue an estimate that landed in the wrong region.

**Subgoal 2 — establish what the ecosystem already does, and treat it as a floor rather than a
rule.** geth's own source records that "wallets tend to use 20-25%
bump".<Cite src="geth-gasestimator-source" /> That is an observation about wallet behaviour written
into the estimator's early-exit logic, not a specification. It is a reasonable default precisely
because it comfortably exceeds the structural component for any realistic call depth.

**Subgoal 3 — measure your own call tree instead of guessing at it.** The number you need is
specific to your contracts: run the call, take the estimate, then bisect the real minimum with
`eth_call` at varying limits. If those two numbers are equal, your call tree is shallow and headroom
is covering only state drift. If they differ by ten percent, you have a deep tree and you now know
the size of the structural part.

**Subgoal 4 — decide what the ceiling costs you, honestly.** An unused gas limit is not charged, so
the direct cost of generosity is zero. The real costs are indirect and worth naming: the limit is
part of the signed transaction and part of what the user is shown, an absurd limit looks alarming
in a wallet prompt, and the limit must fit under the per-transaction cap — EIP-7825 sets that at
16,777,216 gas.<Cite src="eip-7825" /><Cite src="geth-protocol-params" /> A node also applies its
own RPC gas cap to `eth_call` and friends; geth's default is
50,000,000.<Cite src="geth-ethconfig-defaults" />

**Subgoal 5 — write the policy down as code with the reasoning attached.** viem lets you pass an
explicit `gas` to the write action, and `estimateContractGas` gives you the number to compute it
from.<Cite src="viem-estimate-gas" /><Cite src="viem-estimate-contract-gas" />

```ts
const estimate = await client.estimateContractGas(request)

// +25% covers 63/64 retention in a call tree measured at depth ~40 (10.5%)
// plus state drift between estimate and inclusion. It does NOT cover a
// contract that branches on gasleft() — see the deployment notes for those.
const gas = (estimate * 125n) / 100n
```

**Subgoal 6 — say what would make you change it.** A revert with an empty payload whose `gasUsed`
sits at the limit is an out-of-gas, and it means the policy is too tight for that path. That is a
measurable alert, not a judgement call, and it is the same signal the previous lesson taught you to
triage. If it fires, the fix is a larger multiplier on that path specifically, plus a look at
whether the call tree got deeper.

<Check type="predict" concept="estimategas-63-64">
A payments feature works in every test and fails for roughly one user in fifty in production, always
with the same symptom: the transaction is mined, `status` is `0x0`, revert data is empty, and
`gasUsed` equals the gas limit exactly.

The client estimates gas and sends the estimate unmodified. The team's argument for that, recorded
in review, was that the estimate already includes everything the call needs and adding a multiplier
would overcharge users.

Predict what distinguishes the failing users from the succeeding ones, and evaluate the argument.
Then say which of two changes — adding a 25% multiplier, or switching from `estimateGas` to reading
`gasUsed` off a successful simulation — you would make, and why the other is worse.

<Answer>
**What distinguishes the failing users.** Something about their state makes the call take a deeper
or longer path than it took at estimate time. Concretely: a batch that iterates a per-user list and
happens to be longer for them; a first-time interaction touching cold slots the estimator did not
pay for because a prior user had already warmed them in the block it estimated against; a routing
path with an extra hop. All three produce a call tree deeper than the estimate assumed, and a
deeper tree needs proportionally more limit than gas — measured at 10.5% extra by depth
forty.<Cite src="measured-revert-surfaces-1-7-1" /> The one-in-fifty rate is the giveaway: a
systematic bug fails everyone, a state-dependent margin fails the tail.

**Why `gasUsed` equal to the limit is conclusive.** A `revert` returns the unused remainder to the
caller, so a deliberate revert almost never consumes the whole limit. Consumption exactly equal to
the limit, with no revert data, is an out-of-gas. There is no other reading of those two facts
together.

**Evaluating the argument.** It is wrong on the mechanism and wrong on the economics.

On the mechanism: the estimate is the lowest limit found by a bisection against *one* state at
*one* moment, and EIP-150 retains one sixty-fourth of remaining gas at every frame, so the limit a
call needs is structurally above the gas it
consumes.<Cite src="eip-150" /><Cite src="geth-gasestimator-source" /> The estimator's own authors
assume a client bump — their source records wallets using 20-25% and tunes the search's early exit
around it.<Cite src="geth-gasestimator-source" />

On the economics: an unused gas limit is not charged. The user pays for gas consumed, not gas
authorised. The 25% headroom costs nothing in the success case and saves a fully-burned failed
transaction in the failure case, which is the only case where money is actually lost. The argument
protects users from a cost that does not exist, at the price of one they are really paying.

**Which change to make.** Add the multiplier. It addresses the actual mechanism and it is one line.

**Why the other is worse — much worse.** Reading `gasUsed` off a simulation moves in the wrong
direction: `gasUsed` is consumption, and consumption is *below* the required limit for any call
tree with depth. Measured at depth forty, sending with the consumed 127,400 instead of the required
140,781 produces exactly the symptom they are already
reporting.<Cite src="measured-revert-surfaces-1-7-1" /> That change would take the failure rate from
one in fifty to all of them on the deep path. It is a plausible-sounding idea that inverts the very
relationship causing the bug, which is what makes it worth being able to reject on sight.

**One thing neither change fixes.** If any contract in the path branches on `gasleft()`, no
multiplier is a guarantee — a larger limit can select a more expensive branch. That is a source-level
property to check per integration, not a client-side parameter to tune.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

Estimation is a search over executions, its answer is a limit rather than a measurement, its
correctness rests on an assumption your contracts may violate, and its result goes stale with state.
That is enough to size a transaction and to explain most of the gas-related failures you will see.

**The next lesson is about estimation failing rather than succeeding**, and it is the more common
case in an interface. `eth_estimateGas` throws for at least four unrelated reasons: the call
reverts, the sender cannot cover value plus gas at the searched limit — you have already seen that
recap, three steps into the algorithm, computing an allowance before anything executes — the
estimate runs into a cap, or the node's state moved. Measured against one node, two of those four
came back with the *same* error code and near-identical wording while describing completely
different problems. Then the same failure is compared across implementations, because the error you
debug locally is not the error your users generate.
