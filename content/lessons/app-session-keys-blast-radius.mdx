There is a sentence you are allowed to say about a session key and a sentence you are not.

Not allowed: *"the session key is safe."* It is a private key held by software, in a browser or on a
server, with no human in the approval loop, used automatically, possibly thousands of times. Every
property that makes it useful is a property that makes it exposed.

Allowed: *"if this key leaks, the most that can be taken is X, over a window of Y, and here is how we
know."*

That second sentence is the entire security argument, and writing it first — before the policy,
before the SDK, before the grant screen — is what converts a session key from a convenience feature
into a designed risk. This lesson is about producing that sentence honestly, which requires two
things the previous lessons deferred: sizing the loss, and being specific about where the key sits.

## Assume it leaks, then do the arithmetic

The habit to build is the one from [threat scenarios](/c/threat-scenario): stop reasoning about
probability of compromise and start reasoning about the bound on the consequence.

This is not pessimism, it is a modelling choice that makes the design tractable. Probability of
compromise is unknowable and every team estimates it as low. The bound is computable from parameters
you chose, and it is the same number whether the leak came from a dependency, a log line, an
error-reporting service, a stolen laptop or a disgruntled contractor.

**The bound has four terms, and three of them are usually forgotten.**

**Term one, the asset cap.** Whatever your value dimension is — a total allowance, a per-period
amount, a streaming rate with a maximum — this is the term everyone computes. MetaMask's permission
parameters are the concrete version: token address, amounts, periods, an optional `startTime`, and
a `justification`.<Cite src="metamask-permissions-reference" />

**Term two, the number of windows.** A per-period cap is not a loss bound; it is a loss *rate*.
Multiply by how many periods elapse before you stop it, and the previous lesson established that
"before you stop it" is dominated by detection, not by revocation.

**Term three, gas.** A key that can only call a harmless function can still burn the account's native
balance calling it, or cost your [paymaster](/c/paymaster-risk) if you sponsor. A policy with a value
cap of zero still has a non-zero loss bound, and if you are the sponsor,
[gas griefing](/c/gas-griefing) is a bill addressed to you.

**Term four, whatever the target contracts can do.** A session key inherits every capability of the
contracts it may call. Permission to call a router is permission to use every ability the router has
over the user's account, including any standing allowance the user granted it years ago. Your policy
bounds the calls; the callee bounds nothing.

<Aside kind="warn">
There is a fifth term in some designs and it is easy to miss because it is a feature, not a bug.
MetaMask describes sub-delegation as a capability of Advanced Permissions: a grantee can pass a
scoped subset of their permissions to another party, so Alice grants to Bob, Bob delegates to Carol,
and Carol can transact using the intent Alice originally granted without Alice approving each
step.<Cite src="metamask-advanced-permissions" /> That is genuinely useful for solver and agent
architectures. It also means your blast-radius statement is about a set of holders you do not
enumerate. If your product does not need sub-delegation, constrain who may redeem — the `redeemer`
caveat restricts redemption to specific addresses<Cite src="metamask-caveats-reference" /> — and say
so in the incident sentence.
</Aside>

## Where the key actually lives

Now the question that usually matters more than the policy, and that has no ERC to read.

A policy bounds the loss *given* a leak. Where the private key sits decides how likely the leak is,
who can cause it, and — the part almost always missed — **whether what leaks is the key bytes or the
ability to use them.** Those are different, and the difference is the most interesting thing in this
lesson.

Here are the options, in the order teams reach for them.

**A variable in page memory.** The key exists for the tab's lifetime and dies with it. It is
reachable by any script the page executes — which means every dependency in your bundle, every
analytics tag, every browser extension with content-script access. The custody story is "my supply
chain", and the supply chain is measured in hundreds of packages.

**Raw bytes in `localStorage` or IndexedDB.** IndexedDB is origin-scoped client-side
storage,<Cite src="mdn-indexeddb" /> which means it survives reloads and is readable by any
same-origin script. If you generated the key with viem — `generatePrivateKey`, then
`privateKeyToAccount`<Cite src="viem-private-key-to-account" /> — the private key is a hex string
that exists in JavaScript, which means it can be read, serialised and sent anywhere. Persistence is
the feature you wanted and the exposure you bought.

**A non-extractable WebCrypto key.** This is the interesting one, and it is worth understanding
precisely rather than as a slogan.

`generateKey(algorithm, extractable, keyUsages)` takes a boolean that decides whether the key can
later be exported via `exportKey()` or `wrapKey()`; for asymmetric pairs, that flag governs whether
the **private** key can be exported at all.<Cite src="mdn-subtlecrypto-generatekey" /> A `CryptoKey`
exposes `type`, `extractable`, `algorithm` and `usages`, and the interface is available only in a
secure context.<Cite src="mdn-cryptokey" /> And `CryptoKey` objects are serializable objects with
defined serialization and deserialization steps<Cite src="webcrypto-l2" /> — which is what lets you
put one in IndexedDB and get it back after a reload without the bytes ever entering your JavaScript.

So with `extractable: false` you hold a **signing oracle**, not a key. Script can ask it to sign and
cannot read it. That is a real and large improvement over a hex string, and you should prefer it.

Now read what the specification declines to promise, because this is where the slogan breaks.

The Web Cryptography specification states that the only requirement on key storage is that key
material is not exposed to script except through `exportKey` and `wrapKey`; in particular it **does
not guarantee** that the underlying key material will not be persisted to disk, possibly unencrypted,
nor that it will be inaccessible to users or other applications running with the same privileges as
the user agent — any application or user with access to the device storage may be able to recover the
key material, even though scripts may be prohibited.<Cite src="webcrypto-l2" /> It also places no
requirement on zeroization: conforming user agents are not required to zeroize key material, and it
may remain accessible in device storage or memory after all references to the `CryptoKey` are
gone.<Cite src="webcrypto-l2" />

And then the sentence that should change your threat model. Applications may share a `CryptoKey`
across security boundaries such as origins through the structured clone algorithm and APIs such as
`postMessage`; once a key is shared with a destination origin, **the source origin cannot later
restrict or revoke access to it**, and in the event of script injection on the source origin,
attackers may post the key to an origin under their control, after which any visit to the attacker's
origin may direct the user agent to perform cryptographic operations with that
key.<Cite src="webcrypto-l2" />

Read that against the previous lesson and the shape appears: **non-extractability bounds exfiltration
of the bytes, not exfiltration of the capability** — and the capability, once handed to another
origin, has its own revocation problem that your on-chain revocation does not touch.

<Aside kind="note">
A curve constraint hides in that option, and it connects to two other modules. WebCrypto's ECDSA key
generation takes a `namedCurve` of `P-256`, `P-384` or `P-521`.<Cite src="mdn-eckeygenparams" />
There is no secp256k1. A non-exportable browser session key is therefore a P-256 key, so the account
has to verify secp256r1 signatures — which is why the
[`P256VERIFY` precompile](/c/eip-7951-precompile) matters here and not only for
passkeys,<Cite src="eip-7951" /> and why ERC-7562's otherwise-mechanical precompile allowlist carves
out that one address for the validation phase.<Cite src="erc-7562" /> The custody decision and the
verification path are the same decision, made once.
</Aside>

**A platform authenticator credential.** The strongest custody, and usually the wrong tool. WebAuthn
credential private keys stay with the authenticator and the API returns assertions rather than
keys,<Cite src="webauthn-l2" /> so the material never reaches your process at all. But an assertion
requires the authenticator's user-verification or user-presence step — which is a human in the loop
for every signature. That is a better prompt, not a session key: it removes the wallet round-trip
while keeping the per-action gesture. Reach for it when what you actually wanted was
[a cheaper prompt](/c/passkey-definition) rather than no prompt.

**A key on your server.** The clearest custody story and the heaviest one: you hold a key that can
move a user's funds within a policy, which makes you a custodian in practice whatever the paperwork
says, and makes your signing service the most valuable target in your architecture. It also has one
genuine advantage worth naming — a server key can be rotated, rate-limited and audited by systems you
already run. Just record it in the [trust assumption inventory](/c/trust-assumption-inventory) as
what it is: you have added yourself as the party that must not be compromised.

The bottom row is the one to sit with. Client-side custody distributes the failure: one compromised
user is one compromised user. Server-side custody correlates it: one breach is every user in the
same event. Neither is strictly better, and the choice is a claim about which failure you would
rather explain.

Openfort's builder checklist arrives at the same short list of controls from the vendor side — always
set an expiry, cap native and token spend to the session's maximum expected value, allowlist targets
rather than issuing keys with any target, and constrain
selectors.<Cite src="openfort-smart-wallet-security" /> Useful as a prompt list; it is a vendor
document with an interest in the answer being an SDK, and it does not resolve where the key lives,
which is the part it cannot resolve for you.

## An unlimited approval is a session key with the worst policy

Now the reframing that makes the whole module easier to explain to anyone who has been near this
ecosystem for a while.

Read ERC-20's own words for `approve`: it allows the spender to withdraw from your account
**multiple times, up to the `_value` amount**, and calling it again overwrites the current allowance
with the new value.<Cite src="eip-20" /> That is a session key, described in 2015. A third party is
authorised to act on your balance repeatedly, without you signing each time, until you change it.

Two directions of use, and both are worth having.

**Forwards:** this is why unlimited approvals are the standing risk they are. It is not that
`approve` is a bad function; it is that the default usage sets every dimension to its worst value
and then leaves it there for years. MetaMask makes the same comparison in its own product framing —
ERC-20 approvals grant unlimited or fixed-amount spending authority to a contract address with no
time bounds, no per-period limits and no human-readable context.<Cite src="metamask-advanced-permissions" />
It is a vendor drawing a favourable contrast, and it is also correct.

**Backwards, which is the useful direction:** this sets the bar your session key must clear. If your
grant has no expiry, no cap and no target allowlist, you have not built a session key. You have built
an approval with better branding, and you have added a private key held by your software to a risk
the ecosystem already knew how to describe.

<Aside kind="note">
The symmetry runs deeper than the table. ERC-20's specification carries a NOTE recommending clients
set an allowance to zero before setting it to another value for the same spender, to prevent a known
attack vector, while stating the contract itself should not enforce it for backwards
compatibility.<Cite src="eip-20" /> That is [the approve race](/c/erc20-approve-race) — a spender
watching for a change and spending both values — and it is structurally the revocation race from the
previous lesson, found in 2016. It is also worth remembering
[how auditors rank it](/c/erc20-approve-race-is-overrated): low, relative to the thing that actually
drains people, which is the standing unbounded grant. Apply the same ranking here. The race is real
and it is not your biggest number.

One more echo, in the other direction: among the permission types MetaMask lists is token approval
revocation<Cite src="metamask-supported-permissions" /> — a session permission whose whole purpose is
cleaning up the older pattern. The two ideas have started composing.
</Aside>

## Worked example: writing the incident sentence

A DCA product wants to buy ETH with 10 USDC per day for 90 days.

**Subgoal — write the sentence you would publish, before choosing parameters.** *"If our session key
leaks, an attacker can spend up to 10 USDC of this user's balance per day, buying ETH into the user's
own account, until we or the user stop it, and no more."* Every parameter below exists to make each
clause true.

**Subgoal — check each clause against a dimension, and find the one with no backing.** "Up to 10 USDC
per day" is a per-period cap. "Of this user's balance" is the token address. "Until we stop it" is
the expiry. **"Into the user's own account" has nothing behind it** — a swap's recipient is an
argument, and unless the account pins it, the clause is a wish. This is the same defect the
policy-dimensions lesson found in a trading interface, arriving through a different product.

**Subgoal — decide whether the clause is enforceable at your layer, and change the sentence if not.**
The seven shipping permission types describe value movement, not "call this function with these
arguments".<Cite src="metamask-supported-permissions" /> If you are on that path, the recipient
constraint has to come from the `payee` rule<Cite src="metamask-permission-rules-reference" /> or from
a caveat at the delegation layer,<Cite src="metamask-caveats-reference" /> and if neither fits, the
honest sentence is weaker: *"...an attacker can spend up to 10 USDC per day and choose where the
output goes."* Write the weaker sentence and see whether you still want to ship.

**Subgoal — multiply out the worst case and say it in units the business understands.** Ninety days
at 10 USDC is 900 USDC of authority granted at one click. If detection is a day, the realistic bound
is 10 USDC plus whatever the recipient clause failed to constrain. If detection is a month, it is
300. The gap between those two numbers is a monitoring project, not a policy project.

**Subgoal — attach the custody row.** Add one clause to the sentence naming where the key sits:
*"...the key is a non-extractable P-256 key in IndexedDB, so a script injection on our origin can
sign with it but cannot export it, and can hand the key object to another origin we cannot then
revoke."*<Cite src="webcrypto-l2" /><Cite src="mdn-subtlecrypto-generatekey" /> Long, ugly, and the
only version that is true. If that clause is uncomfortable to write, that discomfort is the finding.

<Check type="explain" concept="session-key-blast-radius">
Two teams describe their session-key design in a security review.

**Team A:** "The key is generated in a service worker, never leaves the browser, and expires after
one hour. We rotate hourly and the user can revoke from settings."

**Team B:** "The key can spend at most 25 USDC per rolling 24 hours, only to our vault contract, only
with the deposit selector, only from the user's own account. It is a non-extractable P-256 key in
IndexedDB. If it leaks we lose at most 25 USDC per day per user until we notice, and our alert on
redemption rate fires within ten minutes."

Both descriptions are honest. Explain why only one of them is a security argument, and what Team A
would have to add to convert theirs.

<Answer>
**Team A described custody and lifetime, and never bounded the loss.** Every clause is about how hard
the key is to get and how long it lives. None of them answers *what can the holder do*. "Never leaves
the browser" is a claim about exfiltration difficulty, and the Web Cryptography specification is
explicit that even a non-extractable key can be handed to another origin by injected script, after
which the source origin cannot revoke it.<Cite src="webcrypto-l2" /> "Expires after one hour" bounds
the window, and the first lesson of this module already showed why that is not safety: an hour is
plenty if the policy is unbounded. And "the user can revoke" is the mechanism the previous lesson
showed to be a race that costs gas and needs a funded signer.

**Team B stated a bound, its units, its rate, and its detection term.** 25 USDC per 24 hours to one
contract with one selector into the user's own account, plus a ten-minute alert, is a complete
argument: it names the maximum, the period, the target scope, and the term that converts elapsed time
into loss. It is falsifiable — you can go and check whether the account enforces each clause — which
is what makes it an argument rather than a description.

**What Team A must add is the policy, and where it is enforced.** Specifically: which contracts the
key may call, which selectors, what value cap per call and per period, what happens to the argument
that names a recipient, and — the load-bearing question — whether each of those is checked by the
account or by their own code. A rotation schedule attached to an unbounded policy just means the
attacker has a fresh key every hour.

**The general test.** A security argument for a session key survives being read by someone who
assumes the key is already in the attacker's hands. Read Team A's paragraph that way and every
sentence evaporates. Read Team B's and the number holds. That is the whole difference, and it is why
this module keeps insisting the sentence be written before the code.

**One thing Team B still owes.** Nothing in their paragraph says whether the permission can be
sub-delegated onward,<Cite src="metamask-advanced-permissions" /> and nothing says who pays gas for
the redemptions. Both change the number. A good bound invites exactly this kind of follow-up
question, which is another sign it is the right artifact.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**The last lesson of this module** takes the sentence you just learned to write and asks who reads
it. Everything here is a bound you compute; the user sees one screen, once, rendered by wallet
software you do not control, before any of the actions exist. If the bound is not on that screen in
plain language, the user did not agree to it.

**In the security track**, this is the row your product contributes to a
[trust assumption inventory](/c/trust-assumption-inventory) and the failure it contributes to a
[key-compromise scenario](/c/key-compromise-failure). Both want the same artifact: an actor, a
capability, and a number.

**And practically**, the module's break exercise is this lesson in code — attack your own validator,
find the dimension you did not enforce, and watch your incident sentence get longer.
