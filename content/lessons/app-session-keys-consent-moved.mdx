The metric on the slide is *signature prompts per session*, and it went from fourteen to one.

It is a real improvement and the team earned it. It is also the only metric in this project that
cannot get worse, because the feature is defined as removing prompts. A number that only moves one
way is not measuring the thing you care about; it is measuring that you shipped.

What it does not measure is where the fourteen decisions went. They did not disappear. They were
compressed into the one remaining screen, shown before any of them existed, and read once.

MetaMask's own explanation of why it built ERC-7715 support names the failure mode on the other side
of the same trade: every transaction requiring an explicit signature meant repetitive pop-ups and
approval fatigue, and over time users started habitually approving requests without reading them —
**the opposite of informed consent**.<Cite src="metamask-advanced-permissions" /> That diagnosis is
correct, and it is worth noticing that it describes a problem consent screens already had before
anyone removed any of them.

## What the per-transaction prompt was doing besides annoying people

The prompt is easy to characterise as pure friction because friction is the part users complain
about. It was also doing three jobs, and only the first one is friction.

**It was the authorisation.** Nothing happened without it. That job moves cleanly to the grant: the
user still authorises, once, in advance.

**It was the disclosure.** Whatever the wallet could render about *this specific call* — the
contract, the value, the decoded arguments — appeared in front of the user at the moment the action
was about to happen, with the action's context on screen behind it. That job cannot move cleanly,
because at grant time the actions do not exist yet.

**It was the interrupt.** A prompt is how a user notices that something they did not intend is about
to happen. Fourteen prompts are fourteen chances to notice. One grant is one chance, taken before the
thing to notice has occurred.

## The screen is the wallet's, and you get one field

Here is the part that surprises product teams: the consent screen for a standardised permission is
not yours to design.

ERC-7715's security considerations put the obligation on the wallet in normative language. Wallets
MUST correctly enforce permissions; ultimately users must trust that their wallet software is
implemented correctly, and **permissions should be considered a part of the wallet
implementation**.<Cite src="erc-7715" /> On the display side: malicious dapps could pose as legitimate
applications and trick users into granting broad permissions, so wallets MUST clearly display the
permissions to users and warn them against granting dangerous
permissions.<Cite src="erc-7715" /> Your side of the contract is one sentence long: dapps should only
request the permissions they need, with a reasonable expiration time.<Cite src="erc-7715" />

In practice that means the wallet renders the structured fields it understands. MetaMask describes
showing a human-readable approval screen with exactly what is being requested — the asset, the
amount, the duration, and any constraints — after which the user reviews, optionally adjusts the
parameters if your request allows it, and approves.<Cite src="metamask-advanced-permissions" />

The one field that is yours is `justification`: a human-readable explanation of **why** the permission
is being requested.<Cite src="metamask-permissions-reference" /> The wallet says what. You say why.
That is the entire copy budget for the most consequential screen in your product, and most teams fill
it with the product name.

<Aside kind="note">
`isAdjustmentAllowed` is where the user gets agency rather than a binary. Set to true, the wallet MAY
attenuate — reduce **or increase** — the permission to meet the user's terms for
approval,<Cite src="erc-7715" /> and MetaMask's UI lets users adjust amount, duration, expiry and
start time before approving.<Cite src="metamask-advanced-permissions" /> Treat that as a consent
feature, not a plumbing flag: a screen where the user can move the number is a screen they have to
read. And it is the reason the asking-the-wallet lesson insisted you store the response rather than
the request — a user who tightens the cap has consented to the tighter one, and your product should
be describing that number back to them everywhere.
</Aside>

There is a corollary that explains the shape of the whole ecosystem. MetaMask's documentation is
direct about why arbitrary delegations do not get this screen: regular delegations are not
human-readable, so it is the dapp's responsibility to provide context, and they cannot be signed
through the extension, because a dapp requesting a delegation without constraints could expose the
whole wallet.<Cite src="metamask-advanced-permissions-concept" /> The seven permission types that do
get a screen are all descriptions of value movement — allowances, per-period budgets, streams, and
approval revocation.<Cite src="metamask-supported-permissions" />

**A permission gets a first-class consent screen exactly when the wallet can state it in a sentence.**
That is not a limitation of today's implementations to be routed around. It is the design constraint,
and it applies to your own screens too: if you cannot say what you are asking for in a sentence a
user can evaluate, the problem is the request, not the rendering.

The reflection prompt for this module asks for the grant sentence in under sixty words. Here is what
has to be in it, and what a user loses when it is not.

A concrete pair. The weak version: *"Approve Yield Vault to manage your USDC."* Manage is not a
capability, there is no number and no date, and the user's mental model afterwards is "I connected to
the app".

The version that carries the same grant honestly: *"Yield Vault can move up to 25 USDC per day from
your account into the Yield Vault contract, until 3 December. Nothing else, and no other destination.
Stop it any time in Settings → Sessions."* Forty-one words, five clauses, no adjectives. MetaMask's
own worked example has the same shape — an agent that may spend up to 10 USDC per day to buy ETH for
30 days, with the interface stating the asset, the amount and the duration before
approval.<Cite src="metamask-advanced-permissions" />

Notice that the second version is only writable if the underlying policy is tight. **A good consent
sentence is a byproduct of a narrow grant, not a copywriting exercise.** When the sentence comes out
vague, that is diagnostic: go back and constrain a dimension until it is not.

## The window you choose is a consent decision

The previous lesson left an unresolved tension, and this is where it resolves.

Expiry is free and revocation is a race, so the security-optimal move is a short window, re-granted.
Every re-grant is another grant screen. Enough grant screens and you have rebuilt approval fatigue in
a more dangerous shape — because now each click authorises a class of future actions rather than one
action, and the habituation MetaMask describes<Cite src="metamask-advanced-permissions" /> is
operating on a much larger unit of authority.

So the window is not a security parameter with a product side effect. It is a joint decision:

- **Anchor re-grants to moments the user is already deciding something.** A renewal, a top-up, a
  session start. A grant screen the user came for is read; one that interrupts is dismissed.
- **Prefer a tighter cap over a shorter window when you have to choose.** The cap bounds the loss on
  every path and costs the user nothing at grant time; the window only bounds it if nothing else
  fails, and costs a screen.
- **Say the expiry as a date, not a duration.** "For 30 days" is a number people do not convert.
  "Until 3 December" is a thing they can picture.

ERC-7715 asks for *a reasonable expiration time* and declines to define reasonable.<Cite src="erc-7715" />
That is the right call by a specification, and it is the decision that lands on you.

## The same problem, one rung up

You have met this shape before. Under EIP-7702, a user signs one authorization that points their
existing address at code, and everything that address can subsequently do is decided by that
code.<Cite src="eip-7702" /> One signature, no visible action, an enormous change in what is
possible — [exactly the consent problem](/c/7702-upgrade-consent) this module has been circling, at
the level of the account rather than a slice of it.

A session key is the same trade at a smaller scale, and MetaMask's implementation stacks them: its
Advanced Permissions flow requires the user's account to be a smart account and will prompt an
upgrade if it is not.<Cite src="metamask-advanced-permissions-concept" /><Cite src="metamask-advanced-permissions" />
A first-time grant is therefore two consent decisions in one flow — *become a smart account* and
*grant this permission* — collapsed into what the user experiences as approving your feature. Both
are worth explaining. Neither is explained by a spinner.

<Check type="explain" concept="session-key-consent-moves-not-disappears">
A team ships a grant flow with a 24-hour expiry, a 5 USDC daily cap, a target allowlist of one
contract, and a `justification` reading *"Enable one-click trading"*. Their metrics after two weeks:
prompts per session down 93%, grant-screen approval rate 97%, median time on the grant screen 1.4
seconds, and support tickets asking "why is this app taking my money" up from zero to eleven.

The security review passed — the policy is genuinely tight. Explain what went wrong and which number
predicted it.

<Answer>
**1.4 seconds predicted the eleven tickets.** Nobody reads a screen in 1.4 seconds; they recognise a
shape and click the primary button. Combined with a 97% approval rate, the pair says the screen is
functioning as a dismissal, not a decision. The policy being tight is what kept this at eleven
confused tickets instead of a loss event — which is the argument for tight policies, and not an
argument that the consent worked.

**The justification did the opposite of its job.** *"Enable one-click trading"* describes the benefit
to the user, not the authority being granted. The field is specified as a human-readable explanation
of why the permission is being requested,<Cite src="metamask-permissions-reference" /> and the wallet
is already rendering the asset, amount and duration.<Cite src="metamask-advanced-permissions" /> So
the one field the team controlled was spent restating the feature the user had just clicked, which is
the one thing they already knew.

**The tickets are the disclosure job failing.** Users approved a *capability* and expected a
*feature*. The first time an automated charge appears without a prompt, it reads as theft, because
nothing in their memory of that 1.4 seconds says otherwise. The old prompts did this work badly and
continuously; one screen now does it well and once, and once was not enough for eleven people.

**What to change, in order.** Put the bound in the justification in the user's units and in the same
sentence as the reason: *"So we can place your trades without asking each time — up to 5 USDC a day,
only on this contract, until tomorrow."* Then show the same sentence again in the product after the
grant, where the user has context and no urgency, and again on the first automated action. And set
`isAdjustmentAllowed` so the cap is a control rather than a fact,<Cite src="erc-7715" /> because a
number a user can move is a number they have to look at.

**And add the metric the slide was missing.** Prompts removed only goes one way. Pair it with
something that can get worse: post-grant comprehension, unexpected-charge tickets, or the rate at
which users revoke within an hour of granting. A metric that can fail is the only kind that tells you
anything.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**This module ends where the security track starts.** You now have an actor with a named, bounded,
time-limited [capability](/c/capability-not-role) over a user's account, a number for what it can
take, and a sentence describing both. That is the artifact — not the SDK integration.

**The standards will move under you.** ERC-7715 and ERC-7710 are drafts,<Cite src="erc-7715" /><Cite src="erc-7710" />
the ERC-4337 documentation still says session-key patterns are not standardised and are implemented
wallet-by-wallet,<Cite src="erc-4337-session-keys-docs" /> and the shipping permission types will
grow. What will not move is the structure: an authority, a set of dimensions, an enforcement point, a
window, a race, a custody decision, and a screen. Learn that shape and each new specification is a
morning's reading.

**And the reflection prompt is the exam.** Write the grant sentence for the most useful session key
your product could issue, in under sixty words. Then write the sentence you would have to publish if
that key leaked. If the second sentence is worse than "you lost X", go back and constrain a dimension
until it is not.
