Product wants a Cancel button. It is a reasonable request: the user has a pending transaction, they
have changed their mind, and every other interface in their life lets them take it back.

The button can be built. What it cannot be is a cancel. There is no operation in the protocol that
removes a broadcast transaction, and there is no party you could ask — the transaction is sitting in an
unknown number of independent pools belonging to people who have never heard of you.

What exists instead is a race, entered by paying an entry fee, which you can lose. This lesson is about
building that honestly: the mechanism, the threshold that governs whether anyone will even accept your
entry, the three genuinely different things a replacement can mean, and the copy that stops the button
from being a lie.

## Replacement is the only edit

The mechanism is one sentence. **You broadcast a different transaction using the same nonce, offering a
higher fee, and hope that nodes swap it for the old one in their pools and that it is the one a
proposer includes.**

Everything about that sentence is worth slowing down on.

*A different transaction* — not an amendment. It is separately constructed, separately signed, and has
its own hash. The old one is not modified; it is competed with.

*Using the same nonce* — this is the entire enforcement mechanism. Because
[nonce order is strict](/c/nonce-ordering), exactly one transaction at a given nonce can ever execute
for an account. Two transactions at nonce N are two candidates for one slot, and the chain will consume
exactly one of them.

*Offering a higher fee* — because nodes will not otherwise take the swap, for a reason that has nothing
to do with fairness. A pool that accepted free replacements would accept unlimited free replacements,
and would spend its bandwidth relaying churn. The fee bump is anti-spam, which is why it is set by node
operators rather than by the protocol.

*And hope* — because you are not talking to the entity that decides. You are talking to a node. The
transaction that gets included is the one held by whoever builds the block, and their pool may have
been updated or may not.

## The bump threshold is client policy, and sources disagree about the number

This is the point in the topic where almost every article states a constant, and where a constant is
not available.

What is verifiable: go-ethereum exposes `--txpool.pricebump` with a **default of 10**, documented as
the "Price bump percentage to replace an already existing transaction".<Cite src="geth-cli-options" />
It sits in the same list as the rest of the pool's tunables, all of which are operator settings.

What is not verifiable as a general rule:

- **It is not in an EIP.** [EIP-1559](/c/eip-1559-fee-fields) defines the fee fields and the validity
  condition against the base fee; it does not define a replacement threshold.<Cite src="eip-1559" />
  Nothing about replacement acceptance is consensus.
- **The commonly quoted figures disagree.** Both 10% and 12.5% circulate widely. The research register
  for this curriculum records the threshold as client mempool policy rather than a protocol rule, with
  the current cross-client values not established.<Cite src="chainpath-conflict-register" /> This
  module does not publish one number as *the* threshold, because there is not one.
- **It is not even uniform within one client.** `--blobpool.pricebump` defaults to **100** — a
  hundred per cent, meaning a blob transaction replacement must double the fee, ten times the legacy
  pool's default.<Cite src="geth-cli-options" /> Same client, same machine, same operator, different
  pool, different rule.
- **Operators change it.** It is a flag. Whoever runs the node you are broadcasting to, and whoever
  runs the node that builds the block, each chose a value independently.

<Aside kind="warn">
The engineering conclusion is not "use 12.5% to be safe". It is that the threshold is a property of
infrastructure you do not control, so a replacement must be built to **fail visibly and be retried at a
higher bump**, not to succeed on the first attempt because you picked the magic number. A rejection at
broadcast time is the cheap failure here: nothing was signed onto the chain and nothing was charged.
The expensive failure is a replacement that is accepted by your node, never propagates far enough, and
loses the race silently.
</Aside>

There is a second reason a generous bump is the right instinct, and it is not about the threshold at
all. The replacement has to beat the original **in the block builder's ordering**, not merely be
accepted into a pool. Being one notch above the minimum accepted bump gets you swapped in; being
meaningfully more attractive gets you built.

## Cancel is a replacement whose payload does nothing

Now the button.

A cancel is a replacement at the same nonce whose content is deliberately inert: a zero-value transfer
from the account to itself, with no calldata, at a higher fee. If it wins the race, the nonce is
consumed by a transaction that does nothing, and the original can never execute — its slot is gone.

Three properties follow, and a UI that omits any of them is misleading its user.

**It costs gas.** A self-transfer is a real transaction that occupies a block and is charged. If the
cancel wins, the user paid for a transaction that did nothing, on purpose. If it loses, they paid for
one that did nothing *and* the original went through anyway. There is no outcome in which cancelling is
free, and the fee involved is higher than the original's by construction.

**It can lose.** If the original is already in a block being assembled, or is held by a builder whose
pool never saw your replacement, it lands and the cancel does not. The user's action happens. From
their point of view they pressed Cancel and the thing happened anyway, which is the worst possible
outcome for trust and the one your copy has to prepare them for *before* they press it.

**It only cancels one nonce.** This is the property teams miss most often. Cancelling nonce N does not
touch N+1 or N+2. It makes N execute as a no-op, which *unblocks* everything behind it — so a user with
three queued transactions who cancels the first one will watch the other two execute immediately, at
speed, which is the exact opposite of what "cancel" led them to expect.

## Three replacements, three different things to say

When `waitForTransactionReceipt` finds a transaction from the same sender at the same nonce — it scans
new blocks for exactly that, because "Transactions are replaced when they are sent from the same
nonce" — it invokes `onReplaced` with a `reason` of `'repriced'`, `'cancelled'` or `'replaced'`, along
with the replacing transaction and its receipt.<Cite src="viem-wait-for-transaction-receipt" /> wagmi
surfaces the same callback on `useWaitForTransactionReceipt`.<Cite src="wagmi-use-wait-for-transaction-receipt" />

These are three distinct events in the user's life and they deserve three distinct sentences.

The third row is the interesting one, and the one that most codebases collapse into the second. A
`replaced` reason means the account signed a *different action* at that nonce. On a user's own wallet
that is usually them doing something unrelated after a stuck transaction. On a shared operational
account it can mean two processes are writing to the same key, which is an incident, not a UI state.

Collapsing all three into "your transaction changed" throws away the only information that distinguishes
"it is still happening" from "it is definitely not happening" — and those two produce opposite
behaviour in anything downstream that was waiting.

<Aside kind="note">
The classification is viem's, derived from comparing value and data between the two transactions, and
it is a heuristic about intent rather than a protocol-level label.<Cite src="viem-wait-for-transaction-receipt" />
A user who "cancels" by sending a self-transfer of a non-zero amount would be classified `replaced`, not
`cancelled`. Use the reason to choose your wording, and use the receipt's actual contents to decide
anything that moves money.
</Aside>

## The button most products should build first

Cancel is the request that arrives. Speed Up is usually the one that should ship.

They are the same mechanism with a different payload — same nonce, higher fee, and in the speed-up case
identical `value` and `data` so that the user's action still happens. It resolves the same ambiguity a
cancel does, because either way exactly one transaction consumes the nonce and the account is
unblocked. The difference is which outcome the user gets when they win.

Two arguments for reaching for it first. The first is that most users pressing Cancel do not want the
action undone; they want it *finished*, and they have reached for the only button that seemed to admit
something was wrong. A flow that says "this is parked because the network got more expensive — pay more
and it goes through now" answers the actual complaint, and it does not risk the outcome where they paid
for nothing.

The second is that speeding up is the recovery your own infrastructure needs. A settlement account with
a parked transaction does not want that work abandoned; it wants it included so the queue moves. Build
the reprice path, expose the cancel as the narrower case on top of it, and both your users and your
operators are served by one piece of code — with a `'repriced'` reason arriving through the same
callback that reports the other two.<Cite src="viem-wait-for-transaction-receipt" />

## Worked example: building the button so it cannot lie

**Subgoal: decide what the button is allowed to promise, before writing any of it.** The promise is
"we will try to make this not happen, it will cost you gas, and it may not work". Everything else in
the implementation follows from having written that sentence down, and if product will not accept the
sentence, the honest response is that the feature does not exist rather than that it is hard.

**Subgoal: construct the replacement so the only difference is the one you intend.** Same `from`, same
`nonce`, `to` set to the sender's own address, `value: 0n`, no data, and a fee raised well clear of any
plausible threshold. Reading the nonce is the step people skip: it must be the nonce of *the
transaction being cancelled*, taken from your own record of it, not a fresh call to
`eth_getTransactionCount`, because the pending count on a node that has forgotten the original will
hand you the wrong number.<Cite src="ethereum-org-json-rpc" />

```ts
const cancelHash = await walletClient.sendTransaction({
  account,
  to: account.address,          // the point is that this does nothing
  value: 0n,
  nonce: original.nonce,        // from your record, not from the node
  maxFeePerGas: bumped(original.maxFeePerGas),
  maxPriorityFeePerGas: bumped(original.maxPriorityFeePerGas),
})
```

Both fee fields have to rise. A pool comparing only one of them will still reject the pair, and
[the effective price](/c/eip-1559-fee-fields) is derived from both:
`priority_fee_per_gas = min(transaction.max_priority_fee_per_gas, transaction.max_fee_per_gas - block.base_fee_per_gas)`.<Cite src="eip-1559" />

**Subgoal: keep watching both, because you do not know which one wins.** You now have two hashes
competing for one nonce, and the resolution is a single fact: which transaction ends up at that nonce.
Watch the original with `onReplaced` — if the cancel wins, that callback fires with
`'cancelled'`.<Cite src="viem-wait-for-transaction-receipt" /> Watch the cancel too, because if it is
the one that gets dropped you need to know you are back to watching only the original.

**Subgoal: render the intermediate state, which is the one everyone forgets.** Between broadcasting the
cancel and learning the outcome there is a window where *both* are live. The user pressed Cancel and
the correct interface state is not "cancelled" — it is "cancelling", with the original still shown and
still capable of succeeding. Marking it cancelled optimistically is precisely the
[optimistic-UI mistake](/c/tx-optimistic-ui-boundary) this module ends on: the display can be undone,
but if that display drove an off-chain action, that cannot.

**Subgoal: handle the rejection at broadcast separately from the loss at inclusion.** A node refusing
the replacement outright — bump too small — is recoverable and cheap: raise the bump and try again,
having spent nothing. Losing the race after acceptance is terminal and paid for. These are different
error paths and the second one needs the user to be told what actually executed.

## Reproducing the race deterministically

You cannot reason your way to confidence about a race. You can run it.

Turn off automatic mining with `evm_setAutomine`, whose parameter is `enabled` — "true for instant
mining, false to stop instant mining".<Cite src="anvil-rpc-methods" /> Send the original at a low fee.
Send a replacement at the same nonce with a higher fee. Then mine with `anvil_mine`, which "Mines a
series of blocks" with an optional block count and interval.<Cite src="anvil-rpc-methods" />

What to assert, in order of how much each one teaches:

1. **Exactly one receipt exists.** Two hashes, one nonce, one execution.
2. **The account's transaction count advanced by one, not two.** This is the assertion that makes the
   "one slot" model concrete, and it is the one that surprises people who expected the loser to be
   "queued for later".
3. **Your UI reached the `replaced` state with the right reason** — and, if you built the cancel,
   `'cancelled'` rather than `'repriced'`.
4. **The loser's hash resolves to nothing, forever.** Poll it. It stays `null`, exactly as a dropped
   transaction does,<Cite src="ethereum-org-json-rpc" /> which is why the losing side of a race and a
   drop are indistinguishable by hash alone and distinguishable instantly by nonce.

Then run it the other way — replacement first, original second — and observe that the pool refuses the
lower-fee one. That refusal is the anti-spam rule doing its job, and seeing the error text once is
worth more than any amount of reading about the threshold.

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="tx-cancel-is-a-replacement">
A team ships this cancel flow. It works in their manual testing.

```ts
async function cancel(tx: TrackedTx) {
  const nonce = await publicClient.getTransactionCount({
    address: account.address,
    blockTag: 'pending',
  })
  const hash = await walletClient.sendTransaction({
    account,
    to: account.address,
    value: 0n,
    nonce,
    maxFeePerGas: tx.maxFeePerGas + 1n,
    maxPriorityFeePerGas: tx.maxPriorityFeePerGas,
  })
  markCancelled(tx.id)
  return hash
}
```

Find every defect. For each, say what the user experiences and whether it is a display problem or a
money problem.

<Answer>
**1. The nonce is wrong, and this is the money bug.** `getTransactionCount` at `pending` returns the
count *including* the node's pending transactions, so for an account with one live transaction it
returns the nonce *after* the one being cancelled.<Cite src="ethereum-org-json-rpc" /> This
transaction therefore does not compete with `tx` at all — it is a brand-new zero-value self-send at the
next nonce. The original executes normally. The user pressed Cancel, was told it worked, paid for an
extra transaction, and their action went through. The nonce must come from the tracked transaction's
own record.

Worse, this is the version that *passes* manual testing on a fast local chain, because the original is
usually already mined by the time anyone clicks, at which point the pending count happens to equal the
right number for a different reason.

**2. `maxPriorityFeePerGas` is not raised.** Only the total cap moves, by one wei. Any pool applying a
percentage bump to both fee fields rejects this, and a bump of one wei fails every plausible threshold
anyway — go-ethereum's documented default for `--txpool.pricebump` is a percentage, not an absolute
value.<Cite src="geth-cli-options" /> The user sees an opaque broadcast error. Display problem, and a
cheap one: nothing was signed onto the chain.

**3. `markCancelled` fires before anything is resolved.** The cancel has been broadcast, not won. The
correct state is "cancelling", and the terminal state is only known when one of the two transactions is
included. If `markCancelled` merely greys out a row, this is a display problem. If it releases a hold,
un-reserves inventory, or tells another system the user backed out, it is a money problem — the original
can still land and the two systems now disagree permanently.

**4. There is no watcher on the outcome.** Nothing calls `waitForTransactionReceipt` with `onReplaced`
on the original, so even when the cancel does win, the app never learns the reason and cannot tell
`'cancelled'` from `'repriced'` from `'replaced'`.<Cite src="viem-wait-for-transaction-receipt" /> A
user who instead pressed "Speed Up" in their wallet gets treated identically to one who cancelled.

**5. There is no failure path for losing the race.** The function returns a hash and the flow ends.
Nothing in the code can ever reach "the cancel lost and the original executed", which is the single
state the user most needs to be told about, because it is the one where something happened that they
believe they prevented.

**6. Nothing is said about cost.** No confirmation step, no mention that the cancel is itself a paid
transaction, no mention that it may not work. The button reads as a free undo. This is a copy problem
that becomes a trust problem the first time it loses.

**The corrected shape.** Take the nonce from the record; raise both fee fields by a comfortable
percentage with retry on rejection; set state to `cancelling`; watch the original with `onReplaced` and
watch the cancel's own receipt; resolve to `cancelled` or to `confirmed`/`reverted` for the original
depending on which one lands; and put the cost and the uncertainty in the confirmation dialog, in that
order, before the user commits to paying for it.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**Immediately next: the hash you were tracking is now the wrong hash.** Every replacement in this
lesson — repriced, cancelled or superseded — produces a new transaction hash for the same user action.
Anything you keyed on the old one has just been orphaned, including database rows and webhook
subscriptions you may not own. That is the next lesson, and it is short because the fix is one decision.

**In your product, today:** find every "Cancel", "Undo" or "Stop" in a transaction flow and check
whether its confirmation copy states that it costs gas and may fail. If it does not, you are shipping a
promise the protocol does not make.

**And in your operational tooling:** the replacement path is the only recovery mechanism for a blocked
account. If it exists only as a button in a wallet UI that one engineer has open, it does not exist at
3am. Build it as a script, with the nonce as an argument, and test it against a local chain the way this
lesson describes — before it is the thing standing between you and a settlement queue that has been
stopped for an hour.
