The pull request changed two characters. `confirmations: 1` became `confirmations: 12`, the title was
*wait longer before crediting*, and the description said *see incident #414*.

The review comment was one line: **why twelve?**

The thread that followed ran to thirty messages and never answered it. Someone linked a tutorial.
Someone else said twelve was "what exchanges use". A third person pointed out that the same codebase
waited for one confirmation before sending the customer an email and nobody had ever filed a bug about
that. The PR merged. The number is still there.

That thread is the subject of this lesson, because the interesting thing about it is not that twelve
was wrong. It is that the team had no procedure that could have produced a right answer — no statement
of what the wait was protecting, and therefore no way to tell whether any number was enough.

## Two different guarantees are wearing the same word

Everything before this lesson treated "confirmed" as a single event: a receipt arrived, its status was
success, the transaction happened. Under that reading, waiting longer is just being more careful about
the same thing.

It is not. Two separate properties are in play and they do not come from the same mechanism.

**Confirmation depth** is a count. Your transaction is in block N, the chain head is at block N+k, so
you have k confirmations. It is a proxy: it assumes that undoing your transaction means displacing k
blocks, and that displacing k blocks gets harder as k grows. On a chain where the only thing standing
behind a block is accumulated work, depth is the *whole* security argument, which is why depth-counting
is the habit every engineer arrives with.

**Finality** is not a count and not a probability. On Ethereum it is a property conferred by the
consensus layer on checkpoint blocks: the first block of each epoch is a checkpoint, validators vote
for pairs of checkpoints, and when a pair attracts votes representing at least two-thirds of total
staked ETH the more recent one becomes justified and the earlier one is upgraded to finalized — the
process handled by Casper FFG.<Cite src="eth-pos-finality" /> Reverting a finalized block requires an
attacker to commit to losing at least one-third of the total supply of staked ETH.<Cite src="eth-pos-finality" />
ethereum.org's own framing is that finality means a block "cannot be altered or removed from the
blockchain without burning at least 33% of the total staked ETH".<Cite src="ethereum-org-ssf" />

You already have the mechanism from [Casper FFG](/c/casper-ffg) and
[finality as a spectrum](/c/finality-is-a-spectrum), and you already know from
[the reversal cost model](/c/finality-reversal-cost-model) to quote that cost in ETH and never in
fiat. What is new here is the interface consequence: **these two things do not convert into each
other.** There is no depth k that equals finality, and there is no finality threshold expressible as a
number of blocks, because finality is a vote outcome and depth is a subtraction.

That has a very practical upside, and it is the single most useful thing in this lesson: you do not
have to count. The JSON-RPC default block parameter accepts `safe` and `finalized` alongside `latest`,
`earliest` and `pending`.<Cite src="ethereum-org-json-rpc" /> You can ask the chain whether your
transaction's block has been finalized rather than estimating it from arithmetic that was never a
guarantee in the first place.

## The wall-clock number is genuinely disputed, and your code should not contain it

Here is where curricula usually hand you a minute count. This one will not, because the published
figures disagree and the disagreement is not a rounding difference.

The mechanism is the fact. Time is divided into slots of twelve seconds and epochs of thirty-two
slots, checkpoints sit at epoch boundaries, and finalization requires the justification of a pair of
them — so finality trails the head by about two epochs.<Cite src="eth-pos-finality" /> That is what
[epoch and finality timing](/c/epoch-and-finality-timing) established, and it is not in dispute.

The wall-clock translation is. The arithmetic — 64 slots at 12 seconds — gives 12.8
minutes.<Cite src="eth-pos-finality" /> ethereum.org's single-slot-finality page states in prose that
"It takes about 15 minutes for an Ethereum block to finalize" and describes the current design as
having set time to finality at ~15 minutes.<Cite src="ethereum-org-ssf" /> A vendor reference
summarises Ethereum's threshold as two epochs at roughly 12 to 13 minutes.<Cite src="quicknode-reorg" />
And the research register behind this curriculum carries a benchmark source reporting 15.9 minutes at
the median over a 24-hour window, recorded as unreconciled with the two-epoch
arithmetic.<Cite src="chainpath-conflict-register" />

Four numbers, none of them wrong exactly, none of them the same. Treat the two-epoch mechanism as the
fact and any minute figure as an observation with a measurement date attached.

<Aside kind="warn">
The stronger reason not to encode a duration: **finality is not a timer, and it can stop.** If the
chain fails to finalize for more than four epochs, the inactivity leak activates and bleeds stake from
validators voting against the majority until a two-thirds majority can be recovered.<Cite src="eth-pos-finality" />
That is the protocol working as designed, and during it, the finalized checkpoint does not advance. A
handler written as `setTimeout(credit, 13 * 60_000)` credits during a liveness failure. A handler
written as "wait until the `finalized` block number is at least my block number" does not.<Cite src="ethereum-org-json-rpc" />
</Aside>

Single-slot finality would collapse this distance, and it is worth knowing where it stands so you do
not design around it arriving: ethereum.org describes SSF as in the research phase, not expected to
ship for several years, and likely after other substantial upgrades.<Cite src="ethereum-org-ssf" />

## There is no standard number, and the one you have heard is not sourced

The other half of the PR thread was an appeal to a convention. It is worth being precise about what
that convention is.

The research sweep behind this curriculum tried to corroborate the widely circulated claim that dApps
standardly wait somewhere in the region of twelve to thirty-two confirmations. It came from a single
low-tier secondary source, was not corroborated anywhere else, and was explicitly recorded as not
publishable as guidance.<Cite src="chainpath-conflict-register" /> That is why you will not find a
number in this lesson to copy: not because the answer is hard, but because the honest answer is that a
number without a stated exposure behind it is somebody else's risk model wearing your product's
clothes.

Parties with genuine value at risk do treat this as a design problem rather than a constant; Circle
published an exploration of confirmation rules for Ethereum as exactly that kind of
analysis.<Cite src="circle-confirmation-rules" /> The research direction there is worth knowing about
because it is not a depth count either: a [confirmation rule](/c/confirmation-rule) derives, from the
fork-choice state, the condition under which a block cannot be reorged unless more than a stated
fraction of stake is adversarial — strong confidence in seconds rather than minutes, expressed as an
assumption you can state rather than a number you inherited.

## The policy is derived from the action, not from the transaction

Here is the reframe that makes the question answerable. Stop asking *how many confirmations should we
wait for*. That question has no answer because it has no object. Ask instead, for each thing your
system does when a transaction succeeds: **what does it cost us to have done this and be wrong?**

That is a per-action question, and the same transaction can trigger three actions with three different
answers.

### Worked example: the policy table for one deposit

Take the deposit flow that produced the PR. A user deposits USDC; four things happen downstream.

**Subgoal: enumerate the consequences, not the transactions, because the transaction is one and the
consequences are several.** Writing them out is most of the work, and teams routinely discover at this
step that they have no idea what fires on receipt.

**Subgoal: price the false positive for each row, because that price — not the transaction's value — is
what sets the wait.** Notice that rows two and three carry the same amount of money and completely
different exposure. Crediting a balance nobody can move is a database write you can undo. Making it
withdrawable hands the money to someone. That difference, not the deposit size, is the reason those
rows get different policies.

**Subgoal: map each price onto an observation the chain can actually give you, so the policy is
checkable rather than aspirational.** There are exactly four useful observations, and the last two do
not require you to count anything.

**Subgoal: write it down as data, so the policy is reviewable by someone who is not reading the call
site.** This is the artefact the PR thread was missing. A reviewer can argue with a table. Nobody can
argue with a magic number buried three files deep.

```ts
type Tier =
  | { kind: 'included' }
  | { kind: 'depth'; blocks: number }
  | { kind: 'safe' }
  | { kind: 'finalized' }

export const policy = {
  renderConfirmed:   { kind: 'included' },
  creditInternal:    { kind: 'depth', blocks: 5 },
  enableWithdrawal:  { kind: 'finalized' },
  sendReceiptEmail:  { kind: 'finalized' },
} satisfies Record<string, Tier>
```

The `blocks: 5` on the middle row is not smuggling a standard back in. It is a number this team owns:
the credit is reversible, they measured that reversing it costs one operator ten minutes, and they are
willing to accept that ten minutes at a frequency of roughly however often a five-block rewrite
happens. State that reasoning next to the row and it stops being folklore.

**Subgoal: implement each tier against the client library, so that "the policy" and "the code" are the
same object.**

```ts
async function hasCleared(receipt: TransactionReceipt, tier: Tier) {
  switch (tier.kind) {
    case 'included':
      return receipt.status === 'success'

    case 'depth': {
      const n = await publicClient.getTransactionConfirmations({ transactionReceipt: receipt })
      return receipt.status === 'success' && n >= BigInt(tier.blocks)
    }

    case 'safe':
    case 'finalized': {
      const block = await publicClient.getBlock({ blockTag: tier.kind })
      return receipt.status === 'success' && block.number >= receipt.blockNumber
    }
  }
}
```

Three details in there are load-bearing.

`getTransactionConfirmations` returns the number of blocks passed since the transaction was processed,
as a `bigint`, and returns `0` when the transaction has not been confirmed and processed
yet.<Cite src="viem-get-transaction-confirmations" /> It takes either the receipt or the hash — pass
the receipt when you already have it and save a round trip. wagmi exposes the same thing as
`useTransactionConfirmations`, with the same "`0` means not yet processed"
semantics.<Cite src="wagmi-use-transaction-confirmations" />

The `safe` and `finalized` branches are a block-number comparison, not a wait loop and not a duration.
They ask the chain a question the chain can answer.<Cite src="ethereum-org-json-rpc" />

And every branch re-checks `receipt.status === 'success'`, because none of this tiering means anything
for a transaction that reverted. Depth on a reverted transaction is a well-confirmed nothing.

<Aside kind="note">
`waitForTransactionReceipt` takes a `confirmations` option and defaults it to
`1`.<Cite src="viem-wait-for-transaction-receipt" /> wagmi's `useWaitForTransactionReceipt` exposes the
same option.<Cite src="wagmi-use-wait-for-transaction-receipt" /> That default is the right one for the
`included` tier and it is a policy you inherited by not writing one down. It is also worth knowing that
raising `confirmations` interacts with that action's `timeout`, which defaults to 180 000
milliseconds<Cite src="viem-wait-for-transaction-receipt" /> — asking one call to wait for many blocks
means asking it to outlive a timeout that was not designed for the job. Beyond a couple of blocks, the
tier check above belongs in a job, not in the call that sent the transaction.
</Aside>

## The waiting cannot live in the browser

There is a structural consequence that falls straight out of the table, and it is where most teams
discover their architecture is wrong.

Everything above `depth` takes minutes. A browser tab does not reliably exist for minutes. The user
closes it, the laptop sleeps, the app redeploys, the service worker is evicted. If the code that
credits a balance or enables a withdrawal is the same code that renders the spinner, then your
confirmation policy is really "whichever of the policy and the user's attention span expires first".

So the policy splits along the same line the table splits along:

- **The client owns the `included` tier.** It renders progress, and it is allowed to be optimistic,
  because everything it does is a re-render.
- **A server-side watcher owns everything above it.** It holds the durable record keyed on
  `(address, chainId, nonce)` — the key [lesson four](/c/tx-replacement-changes-the-hash) argued for —
  and it advances that record through the tiers on its own schedule, whether or not anyone is looking.
- **Nothing irreversible fires from the client.** Not because the client is untrusted in a security
  sense — that is a separate argument, and a real one — but because it is a process that can stop
  halfway through a policy.

This is also the resolution of the disagreement that opened the module: the front end optimising for a
fast spinner and the backend optimising for never crediting twice are not in conflict, they are
implementing two different rows of one table.

<Check type="explain" concept="tx-confirmation-policy-is-a-product-decision">
You are building a marketplace. A buyer pays on-chain and four things happen: (1) the order card in the
buyer's UI flips to "paid"; (2) the seller's dashboard shows a new order; (3) your warehouse API is
called to pick and dispatch the physical item; (4) the seller's payout balance increases, and payouts
run nightly.

Assign a tier to each of the four, and justify each one in the form "we act after X because the worst
case if we are wrong is Y". Then say what the reviewer should ask about any tier you assigned that is
not `included` or `finalized`.

<Answer>
**(1) Order card flips to "paid" — `included`.** We act on a successful receipt because the worst case
if we are wrong is that a card re-renders back to "pending" and the buyer is briefly confused. Nothing
outside our own render tree changed. This is precisely the region where optimism is good interface
design.

**(2) Seller dashboard shows the order — `included`, or `depth` with a small number if sellers act on
it.** The reasoning depends on a product fact, not a chain fact: if the dashboard is informational,
being wrong costs a disappearing row. If sellers start packing boxes when the row appears, then the row
is a trigger for an irreversible physical action and it inherits row three's policy through the human
in the loop. This is the row teams get wrong, because the consequence lives outside the software.

**(3) Warehouse dispatch — `finalized`.** We act after the `finalized` block tag has passed our block
because the worst case if we are wrong is that we shipped a physical object to someone who did not pay,
and there is no rollback for a courier. Reversal of a finalized block would require an attacker to
commit to losing at least one-third of total staked ETH,<Cite src="eth-pos-finality" /> which is the
strongest guarantee the chain offers and the appropriate one for the strongest consequence we have.
Note the shape of the argument: it is about *courier irreversibility*, not about the order's value. A
five-dollar item is just as unrecoverable as a five-hundred-dollar one.

**(4) Payout balance — `included` or a small `depth`, and the payout run itself gates on
`finalized`.** Incrementing a number in our database is fully reversible until money leaves, so the
increment can be fast. The nightly payout is the irreversible step, and it should refuse to include any
order that has not reached `finalized`. This is the general trick: when an action is far enough in the
future, the wait is free, and the check belongs at the moment of the irreversible step rather than at
the moment of the increment.

**What the reviewer should ask about any middle tier:** *what does this number buy, and what does it
cost us when it is not enough?* A `depth: 5` is defensible if the answer is "five blocks would have to
be rewritten, we can undo this credit with one operator action, and we have decided that trade is
acceptable". It is not defensible if the answer is "that is the standard" — the circulating standard
figures come from sources that could not be corroborated, and were flagged as not publishable as
guidance for exactly this reason.<Cite src="chainpath-conflict-register" /> If nobody can state the
worst case, the correct move is not to pick a larger number; it is to move the row to `finalized` until
somebody can.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson shows you the failure this policy is protecting against, in the shape your code will
actually see it.** A reorg is not an abstraction about competing branches — it arrives as a receipt
that stops existing, a log re-delivered with a flag set, and a screen still showing a green check for
something that is no longer anywhere. Having a policy is half the work; being able to walk back the
state when the policy was not enough is the other half.

**And it makes the L2 question askable.** Everything above assumed one chain with one notion of
finalized. On a rollup that word splits into several guarantees that arrive at very different times,
and the table you just built is exactly the instrument for deciding which of your rows needs which one.

**One thing to do this week, before either of those:** find every place in your codebase that fires on
a receipt, and write the table. Not the policy — just the inventory of consequences and whether you can
take each one back. Most teams find at least one row where the answer is no and the current wait is
one block.
