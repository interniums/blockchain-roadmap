Four support tickets arrive within ten minutes, all saying the same thing: *"all my transactions are
stuck"*.

Three of them are one user with one stuck transaction and three later ones queued behind it. The
fourth is a user whose transaction was quietly evicted from every pool that held it an hour ago and is
never coming back, and whose interface is showing exactly the same spinner as the other three.

Those two situations need opposite advice. "Wait, or pay more" is correct for the first and cruel for
the second. "Send it again" is correct for the second and actively harmful for the first. Your UI
cannot currently tell them apart, and neither can the user, because from the outside both look like
`null` returned from `eth_getTransactionReceipt` forever.

This lesson makes them distinguishable. The instrument, throughout, is the nonce.

## One queue per account, and it is strictly ordered

You have [nonce ordering](/c/nonce-ordering) already: an account's transactions execute in strict
increasing nonce order, and a [gap](/c/nonce-gap) is not executable.

The product consequence is head-of-line blocking, and it is worth stating in the language of the
systems you have built before. **An externally-owned account is a single-consumer FIFO queue with no
skip and no timeout.** Not a thread pool. Not a work queue with retries. One slot at a time, in order,
forever.

Two things follow immediately, and both surprise teams.

**For a user**, one transaction at a low fee cap parks everything they do afterwards. They approve a
token at a moment of low fees, walk away, come back during a spike, and their swap — sent at a
perfectly sensible fee — cannot be mined, because the approval in front of it cannot. The swap's own
fee is irrelevant. There is no bypass.

**For your application**, any account you control that sends more than one transaction inherits the
same constraint. A relayer, a keeper, a batch settler, a faucet, an automated market operation: all of
them are one queue. If you have ever built a system where "just add another worker" was the answer to
throughput, this is the case where it is not, and where the number of parallel senders you can have is
exactly the number of funded addresses you are willing to operate.

## Parked: the fee cap is a ceiling, not a bid

The first stuck case is not stuck at all in the sense of being broken. It is waiting for the market to
come to it.

[EIP-1559](/c/eip-1559-fee-fields) gave the transaction two fee fields, `max_priority_fee_per_gas` and
`max_fee_per_gas`, and imposed a validity condition:
`transaction.max_fee_per_gas >= block.base_fee_per_gas`, so "the signer is willing to pay the current
base fee".<Cite src="eip-1559" /> The effective price actually charged is
`priority_fee_per_gas = min(transaction.max_priority_fee_per_gas, transaction.max_fee_per_gas - block.base_fee_per_gas)`
added to the base fee, and the base fee itself "is not given to anyone (it is
burned)".<Cite src="eip-1559" />

Read the validity condition as a UI fact rather than an equation. **While the base fee sits above the
cap the sender signed, the transaction is not merely unattractive — it is invalid for inclusion in that
block.** No amount of proposer greed can include it. And the moment the base fee falls back under the
cap, it becomes eligible again, with nothing having changed on the sender's side.

That is why "pending for an hour" is not a synonym for "failed". A transaction signed during a quiet
hour with a modest cap can sit through a busy afternoon and be mined that evening, exactly as
authorised, at whatever the base fee is then. Nothing decayed. Nothing expired at the protocol level.

The interface consequence is that this state has two honest sentences and one dishonest one:

- Honest: "the network is currently more expensive than the maximum you approved."
- Honest: "this will be included if fees fall, or you can replace it with a higher cap."
- Dishonest: "your transaction failed."

<Aside kind="note">
This also explains a class of bug report that looks supernatural: *the transaction went through by
itself in the middle of the night.* It did. The user, or your app, had already authorised it. The base
fee came down. Nobody did anything. If your product treated the earlier timeout as terminal and undid
its own optimistic state, you now have a confirmed on-chain action and an application that believes it
never happened — the failure mode
[the optimistic-UI boundary lesson](/c/tx-optimistic-ui-boundary) is built to prevent.
</Aside>

## Dropped: eviction, and the absence of any announcement

The second case is genuinely terminal for that hash, and nothing tells you.

A mempool is not part of consensus. It is a node's local cache, bounded, with its own admission and
eviction policy — which you have from [mempool as a public broadcast](/c/mempool-is-a-public-broadcast)
and [the node-local view](/c/mempool-view-is-node-local). What is worth making concrete here is how
small and how *policy-shaped* those bounds are. go-ethereum's command-line defaults:

| Flag | Default | What it governs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `--txpool.globalslots` | 5120 | "Maximum number of executable transaction slots for all accounts" |
| `--txpool.accountslots` | 16 | "Minimum number of executable transaction slots guaranteed per account" |
| `--txpool.globalqueue` | 1024 | "Maximum number of non-executable transaction slots for all accounts" |
| `--txpool.accountqueue` | 64 | "Maximum number of non-executable transaction slots permitted per account" |
| `--txpool.pricelimit` | 1 | "Minimum gas price tip to enforce for acceptance into the pool" |
| `--txpool.lifetime` | `3h0m0s` | "Maximum amount of time non-executable transaction are queued" |

Every one of those is a default on one client, settable by whoever runs the node, and none of them is
in any EIP.<Cite src="geth-cli-options" /> That matters twice over: it means the numbers above are not
a specification you can rely on, and it means the behaviour differs between the node your wallet talks
to, the node your backend talks to, and the nodes that actually build blocks.

Read the table for its shape rather than its values. There is a bounded set of *executable* slots, a
separately bounded set of *non-executable* ones — the queued, nonce-gapped transactions — and a clock
on the second set but not the first. A transaction can therefore leave a pool because it lost a
competition for space, because it sat too long in the queued category, or because the node restarted
and its pool started empty.

**None of these produces a notification.** There is no `eth_transactionDropped`. There is no receipt
saying "discarded". The transaction simply stops being anywhere, and every subsequent
`eth_getTransactionReceipt` returns the same `null` it was returning while everything was
fine.<Cite src="ethereum-org-json-rpc" />

  belief="If a transaction were dropped, the node would tell me, or at least the library would."
  reality="Nothing emits an event. Dropping is an omission, and omissions are only visible by comparison with something else — here, the account's nonce."
  why="Message brokers publish dead-letter events, and HTTP has a status code for everything. Both trained the expectation that failure is announced."
  src="ethereum-org-json-rpc"

## Telling them apart: the nonce comparison

The whole distinction reduces to one question. **Is the slot this transaction was using still open?**

`eth_getTransactionCount` returns the number of transactions sent from an address, and takes a block
parameter that accepts a block number or the tags `latest`, `earliest`, `pending`, `safe` and
`finalized`.<Cite src="ethereum-org-json-rpc" /> Two of those tags are the ones that matter here, and
the difference between them is the entire diagnostic.

- **`latest`** — the count as of the last mined block. This is *how many of this account's
  transactions have actually executed*, and therefore the nonce the account's next executed
  transaction must carry.
- **`pending`** — the count including transactions the node is currently holding in its own pool.
  This is *that node's opinion* about what is queued, and it is exactly as authoritative as that one
  node's view of the world.

### The procedure

**Subgoal: establish whether the slot has been consumed, because that is a fact about the chain rather
than about anyone's pool.** Read the count at `latest` and compare it with the nonce of the transaction
you are tracking.

- `latestCount > txNonce` — the slot is gone. Something at that nonce executed. If it was not your
  hash, it was a replacement or another transaction from the same account, and your hash will never
  appear. This is terminal, and you should go and find out what *did* land at that nonce
  ([lesson four](/c/tx-replacement-changes-the-hash)).
- `latestCount == txNonce` — the slot is still open. Your transaction has not executed and nothing
  else has taken its place. It is either still alive somewhere or it was dropped, and these two are
  what you have to separate next.
- `latestCount < txNonce` — there is a gap in front of you. An earlier transaction from this account
  has not executed, so yours is not even eligible. This is the head-of-line case, and the thing to fix
  is the *earlier* nonce, not this one.

**Subgoal: separate "alive somewhere" from "dropped", knowing that you cannot do it conclusively.**
With the slot open, read the count at `pending` from as many independent endpoints as you have.

- `pendingCount > txNonce` on a node — that node is still holding it. It is alive there. It may not be
  alive anywhere else.
- `pendingCount == latestCount` everywhere you asked — none of the nodes you can see is holding it.
  That is evidence, not proof. Some other node may still have it, and a proposer only needs one.

**Subgoal: turn the ambiguity into a UI decision instead of pretending it away.** The honest state is
"we can no longer find this transaction". The honest options offered alongside it are: keep watching
(cheap, might resolve itself), or replace at the same nonce with a higher fee (costs gas, resolves it
either way — the subject of the next lesson). What you must not do is offer "send it again" as a fresh
transaction, because if the original is alive on a node you cannot see, a second transaction at the
*next* nonce means the user has now authorised the action twice.

<Aside kind="warn">
That last hazard is the one that turns a display bug into a money bug. "Retry" in most software means
"do the same thing again". Here, doing the same thing again at a new nonce means doing it *additionally*,
and the two transactions can both be included in the same block. A retry in this domain is a
**replacement at the same nonce**, never a new one — which is why the button in the next lesson is
wired the way it is.
</Aside>

### Why the same comparison explains the wallet's nonce errors

The two tags are also how the next nonce gets chosen in the first place. A wallet or library that is
about to sign asks for the count at `pending`, so that a transaction you sent thirty seconds ago is
counted and the new one goes behind it rather than colliding with it.<Cite src="ethereum-org-json-rpc" />
That is the right default and it inherits the same weakness as everything else here: the answer is one
node's opinion.

Which is why the two failure shapes you see in the wild are mirror images of a disagreement about that
opinion. Sign against a node that has *forgotten* a live transaction and you reuse a nonce that is
already spoken for. Sign against a node that is *behind* and you leave a hole, so your transaction is
queued rather than executable and sits in the non-executable category with a clock on it. viem gives
both their own error classes, `NonceTooLowError` among them, rather than one generic nonce
failure.<Cite src="viem-errors-glossary" />

### What the client library gives you, and what it does not

viem exports `TransactionNotFoundError`, `TransactionReceiptNotFoundError` and
`WaitForTransactionReceiptTimeoutError` as separate classes, all extending
`BaseError`.<Cite src="viem-errors-glossary" /> That distinction is useful and it is not the same as
the diagnosis above: `TransactionNotFoundError` tells you a node could not find the transaction, which
is one input to the nonce comparison, not a substitute for it.

`waitForTransactionReceipt` will detect a *replacement*, because it scans new blocks for a transaction
sent from the same nonce and reports it through `onReplaced`.<Cite src="viem-wait-for-transaction-receipt" />
It has no equivalent for a drop, and it could not have one: there is no observation that establishes a
drop, only an accumulating absence.

<Aside kind="note">
The research register for this curriculum records that current mempool eviction behaviour — how long a
transaction survives in a given client's pool before being discarded — was not established from
primary sources, and the widely repeated figures for the minimum fee bump required to replace one are
client policy rather than protocol.<Cite src="chainpath-conflict-register" /> Treat everything in this
section as *shape*, verified from one client's documented defaults, and treat any specific duration as
something to measure against the nodes you actually use.
</Aside>

## Reproducing both states locally

Neither of these is a state you want to first encounter in production, and both are two commands on a
local node.

**Parked.** Turn off automatic mining with `evm_setAutomine` (parameter `enabled`, "true for instant
mining, false to stop instant mining"), then set the next block's base fee above your transaction's cap
with `anvil_setNextBlockBaseFeePerGas`, which "Sets the base fee of the next block. Only supported when
EIP-1559 is active".<Cite src="anvil-rpc-methods" /> Send the transaction, mine with `anvil_mine`, and
watch it not be included. Lower the base fee, mine again, and watch it appear — with no action from the
sender in between. That last step is the one worth showing a product manager.

**Dropped.** `anvil_dropTransaction` "Removes a transaction from the pool. Returns the hash if the
transaction was pending, otherwise `null`", taking the transaction
hash.<Cite src="anvil-rpc-methods" /> This is the only way most engineers ever see the dropped state
deliberately, and the point of the exercise is to observe what your UI does *without* being told: the
receipt stays `null`, no error is raised, and the nonce at `latest` never advances.

**Head-of-line blocking.** Automine off, send three transactions at nonces N, N+1, N+2 where the first
has an unusably low fee cap, then mine. Exactly none of them is included. Raise the base fee, mine
again, still none. This is the demo that ends the argument about whether the queue is really strict.

Your backend runs a settlement account that submits one transaction every few minutes. At 14:02 it
submits nonce 4,181 with a `maxFeePerGas` set from a fee estimate taken a minute earlier. Base fee
climbs sharply from 14:03. Your monitor polls every 30 seconds and finds:

- 14:05 — `getTransactionCount(latest) = 4181`, `getTransactionCount(pending) = 4182`, no receipt
- 14:40 — `getTransactionCount(latest) = 4181`, `getTransactionCount(pending) = 4181`, no receipt
- 15:20 — `getTransactionCount(latest) = 4182`, and a receipt exists for a hash that is **not** the one
  you sent

Say what happened in each window, what your monitor should have alerted on and when, and what the
settlement job should have done at 14:40 — including what it must not do.

<Answer>
**14:05 — alive and parked.** `pending` is one ahead of `latest`, so the node you are asking is holding
your transaction and considers it executable-in-waiting. `latest` has not moved, so it has not been
mined. Combined with the fee history, the diagnosis is that the base fee has risen above the
`maxFeePerGas` you signed, which makes it invalid for inclusion until the base fee comes back
down.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> Nothing is broken. No alert beyond an informational "submission parked"
is warranted yet.

**14:40 — the node no longer has it.** `pending` has fallen back to equal `latest`. Your transaction is
not in that node's pool any more. The slot at 4,181 is still open, so it was not replaced and it was
not mined; it was evicted, or the node restarted, or you are talking to a different node behind a load
balancer than the one that accepted it. That last possibility is why this is evidence and not proof,
and why the check is worth repeating against a second endpoint before acting.

The alert belongs here, and its text matters: **"submission no longer visible in mempool; nonce 4,181
still open"** — not "transaction failed". The alert is about your loss of visibility, which is exactly
what you observed.

**15:20 — something else took the slot.** `latest` advanced to 4,182, so a transaction at nonce 4,181
executed, and it was not yours. Either an operator or another process sharing this key sent one, or a
replacement was broadcast at that nonce. Your hash is now permanently unresolvable — no future poll
will ever return a receipt for it. The correct move is to look up what *did* land at that nonce and
reconcile against it, which is the case for keying your records on `(address, chainId, nonce)` rather
than on the hash.

**What the job should have done at 14:40.** Broadcast a **replacement at nonce 4,181** with a higher
fee cap — same intent, same nonce, new fee — and start tracking the new hash while keeping the same
record. This resolves the ambiguity in one move: whichever of the two lands, exactly one transaction at
nonce 4,181 executes, and the account is unblocked either way.

**What it must not do.** Submit the settlement again as a *new* transaction at nonce 4,182. If the
original is still alive on a node you cannot see — which is precisely the situation you failed to rule
out — both can be included, and you have settled twice. The absence of a transaction from your view of
the mempool is not the absence of a transaction from the network, and the retry semantics of every
other system you have built are wrong here for exactly that reason.

**One further thing to fix upstream.** A fee estimate taken a minute before signing became invalid
during a climb. That is not an argument for enormous caps on everything; it is an argument for the fee
cap being a deliberate parameter of a settlement job — chosen against how long you are willing to be
parked — and for the job owning a replacement path rather than treating each submission as fire-and-
forget.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson is the only lever this one identified.** Replacement is the sole mechanism that
resolves both states: it un-parks a transaction whose cap was too low, and it settles the ambiguity of
a possibly-dropped one. It is also the mechanism behind the Cancel button, which turns out not to
cancel anything.

**In your own systems, the audit is short.** Find every account your infrastructure signs with, and
ask what happens to the *second* transaction when the first parks. If the answer is "we have never
thought about it", the answer in production is "everything behind it stops, silently, and the alert
that fires is about something else".

**And one wording change pays for the lesson.** Anywhere your interface currently says a transaction
failed after a timeout, it is making a claim it cannot support. "We have stopped watching" is both true
and more useful, and it leaves the user's next action available instead of pushing them toward the one
that duplicates their spend.
