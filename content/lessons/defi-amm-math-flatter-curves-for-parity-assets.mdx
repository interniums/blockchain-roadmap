A pool holds 5,000,000 of one dollar stablecoin and 5,000,000 of another. You want to swap 1,000,000
of the first for the second. Both are supposed to be worth a dollar.

On a constant-product curve you receive 833,333. You have paid a sixth of your money to move between
two assets that a bank would exchange at par, and the pool is behaving correctly: `α = dx/x = 20%`,
so by the previous lesson the shortfall is `α/(1+α) = 16.67%` on a curve that keeps the product of
the reserves from decreasing.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> Nothing is broken. The curve simply
does not know that these two numbers are supposed to be the same.

On a StableSwap curve with the same reserves and an amplification coefficient of 100, you receive
998,965 — a shortfall of 0.104%, before fees.

A factor of 160 on the same trade against the same capital, entirely from choosing a different
invariant. This lesson derives that invariant from the two curves you already know, shows what
assumption the amplification coefficient encodes, and prices what happens when the assumption is
false.

## The two curves it is built from

Michael Egorov's 2019 paper sets the problem up as a search between two known
invariants.<Cite src="curve-stableswap-paper" />

**Constant sum.** Keep the total number of coins fixed:

```text
Σ xᵢ = D
```

The paper's framing: selling `dx` of coin X buys exactly `dx` of coin Y, and the price — the
derivative — is always exactly 1.<Cite src="curve-stableswap-paper" /> Zero price impact at any size.
It is also, as the paper notes, unusable in a fluctuating market unless the price is adjusted all the
time.<Cite src="curve-stableswap-paper" />

Derive the failure mode yourself, because it is the reason the rest of the design exists. If the pool
always trades one X for one Y, and the market values X at 0.99, then buying X on the market and
selling it to the pool at 1.00 is profitable **for every unit until the pool has no Y left**. There
is no size at which the pool begins defending itself, because the price never moves. A constant-sum
pool holding a de-pegging asset is not a market maker; it is an unlimited redemption window.

**Constant product.** The generalised form:

```text
Π xᵢ = (D / n)ⁿ
```

The constant `D` has the meaning of the total amount of coins when they all have equal
price.<Cite src="curve-stableswap-paper" /> This one can never be drained — as you saw, one reserve
approaches zero only asymptotically — but the price impact is, in the paper's word, enormous, so
providing meaningful liquidity for assets that should trade at par requires enormous
funds.<Cite src="curve-stableswap-paper" />

**The requirement, stated precisely.** An invariant that is relatively flat near balance — the graph
close to the straight line, like a zoomed-in hyperbola — and that shifts towards the constant product
as the portfolio becomes imbalanced, so there is liquidity at any
price.<Cite src="curve-stableswap-paper" />

## Deriving the blend

The construction takes four steps, and every one of them is a choice you can interrogate.

**Subgoal 1 — treat flatness as leverage.** The zero-slippage invariant is the constant-sum one, so
constant sum behaves like infinite leverage and constant product like zero. Call the leverage `χ`,
scale the constant-sum equation by it and add the constant-product one: at `χ = 0` you recover
constant product, at `χ = ∞` constant sum.<Cite src="curve-stableswap-paper" />

**Subgoal 2 — make the parameter dimensionless.** The two invariants have different units, so a raw
sum cannot be added to a raw product. Multiplying the constant-sum side by `χDⁿ⁻¹` fixes the
dimensions and leaves `χ` a pure number that does not depend on how many coins the pool
holds:<Cite src="curve-stableswap-paper" />

```text
χ·Dⁿ⁻¹·Σ xᵢ + Π xᵢ = χ·Dⁿ + (D/n)ⁿ
```

**Subgoal 3 — make the leverage fall away when the pool is skewed.** A fixed `χ` would give constant
leverage everywhere, which reintroduces the constant-sum drainage problem far from parity. So `χ` is
made dynamic: equal to a constant `A` at perfect balance, falling to 0 as the portfolio goes out of
balance. The paper's choice is<Cite src="curve-stableswap-paper" />

```text
χ = A · Π xᵢ / (D/n)ⁿ
```

Read that expression on its own for a moment. The numerator is the constant-product term and the
denominator is what that term would be at perfect balance, so **`χ/A` is a measure of how balanced
the pool is**, equal to 1 when every reserve is `D/n` and heading to 0 as any reserve heads to 0. The
leverage is not a setting; it is a setting multiplied by the pool's own health.

**Subgoal 4 — substitute and collect.** Putting `χ` back into the blended equation gives the
StableSwap invariant:<Cite src="curve-stableswap-paper" />

```text
A·nⁿ·Σ xᵢ + D = A·D·nⁿ + Dⁿ⁺¹ / (nⁿ · Π xᵢ)
```

For a two-coin pool, `n = 2` and `nⁿ = 4`:

```text
4A·(x + y) + D = 4A·D + D³ / (4xy)
```

Sanity-check it at balance. Put `x = y = D/2`: the right-hand side becomes `4AD + D³/D² = 4AD + D`,
and the left becomes `4AD + D`. The invariant is satisfied, which confirms that `D` really is the
total holding when the pool is balanced.

The paper's summary of the tuning knob is one sentence worth memorising: the lower the amplification
coefficient, the closer the invariant is to the constant product.<Cite src="curve-stableswap-paper" />
For its slippage comparison it uses a practical value of `A = 100`, describing that as comparable to
using Uniswap with 100× leverage;<Cite src="curve-stableswap-paper" /> its six-month simulation over
DAI, USDC and USDT found an optimal amplification coefficient of 85, an optimal fee of 0.06% per
trade, and a simulated liquidity-provider profit of 312% APR at those
parameters.<Cite src="curve-stableswap-paper" />

<Aside kind="warn">
Do not compare a pool's on-chain `A` with the paper's `A` without checking the implementation's
normalisation. Curve's 3pool computes `Ann = amp * N_COINS` inside its `get_D` iteration, whereas the
paper's invariant carries `A·nⁿ` — so the contract's `amp` and the paper's `A` are not the same
quantity.<Cite src="curve-3pool-source" /><Cite src="curve-stableswap-paper" /> A published "A" is a
number in some contract's convention, and reading that contract is the only way to know which.
</Aside>

closer to parity; moving 40% of one side it is a factor of 40, with the shortfall past 1%. The
paper's claim that slippage is typically 100 times smaller than constant
product<Cite src="curve-stableswap-paper" /> is a statement about the regime the design targets, not
a property holding at every size.

The curve holds parity to within 1.5% until one asset is 80% of the pool, and then collapses. That
shape — a long flat region ending in a cliff — is the design working exactly as specified. It is also
the entire risk profile in one table.

## The amplification coefficient is an assumption, written as a number

Everything above is mechanism. Here is the part that decides whether the mechanism is a good idea.

A constant-product pool makes no claim about what its assets are worth. StableSwap does. The flat
region is only *correct* if the assets in it are genuinely interchangeable, and `A` is how strongly
that belief is asserted: a high `A` means the pool trades near parity across a wide range of
imbalance, which is another way of saying it keeps quoting near 1.00 after the market has stopped
believing it.

Price it. Suppose one asset is genuinely worth 0.90 and the market knows. Arbitrageurs buy it at 0.90
and sell it to the pool until the pool's marginal price reaches 0.90 too — the previous lesson's
mechanism on a different curve. Run that to completion on the `A = 100` pool, fee-free:

**Subgoal 1 — how much of the bad asset does the pool absorb?** It takes 4,323,784 units of the
de-pegging asset to drag the marginal price down to 0.90 — 86% of the starting balance of that side.
The constant-product pool with the same reserves stops at 270,463, about 5%.

**Subgoal 2 — what does the pool hold afterwards?** StableSwap: 9,323,784 of the asset worth 0.90 and
741,605 of the sound one. Constant product: 5,270,463 and 4,743,416.

**Subgoal 3 — value both against the honest alternative.** Simply holding 5,000,000 of each through
the repricing is worth 9,500,000. The StableSwap position is worth 9,133,011, down 3.86%. The
constant-product position is worth 9,486,833, down 0.14%.

**Subgoal 4 — state the ratio.** The StableSwap liquidity provider loses about **28 times** what the
constant-product provider loses to the same event. The flatness that made the pool 160× better at
routine trades makes it 28× worse at this one, and the two facts are the same fact: a curve that
resists repricing will keep selling the sound asset cheaply for longer.

Note also what the design does *not* do, because the paper is explicit about it: away from the
equilibrium point the invariant operates suboptimally but still provides some liquidity, and at any
price it provides some, unlike the constant-sum invariant.<Cite src="curve-stableswap-paper" /> That
is the guarantee the blend was built to preserve. The pool is drained slowly rather than instantly.
It is still drained.

## Solving it on-chain

One practical difference from constant product deserves a paragraph, because it shows up in gas costs
and in bug reports.

`x · y = k` can be inverted in closed form — that is why `getAmountOut` is four lines of integer
arithmetic.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-library-source" /> The StableSwap invariant cannot. The paper says
so directly: `D`, and the output amount `xⱼ`, are obtained by finding an iterative, converging
solution.<Cite src="curve-stableswap-paper" />

In Curve's 3pool that is a Newton iteration in Vyper, capped at 255 rounds and exiting as soon as
successive estimates agree to within 1 wei, with a comment in the source noting that a division by
zero here would leave only withdrawals working.<Cite src="curve-3pool-source" /> The same file ramps
`A` over time rather than setting it instantly, bounded by a minimum ramp time and a maximum change
factor, so that a governance action cannot reshape the curve underneath open
positions in one block.<Cite src="curve-3pool-source" />

Three consequences worth carrying. A swap costs more gas than a constant-product swap, and the cost
is state-dependent because the iteration count is. The arithmetic is integer throughout, so rounding
direction is load-bearing in the same way as `getAmountIn`'s `+ 1`. And the invariant is solved
rather than evaluated, so every quoter, router and integration test reimplements a numerical method —
a category of code where two implementations agreeing to within 1 wei is a property worth
[testing](/c/differential-fuzzing) rather than assuming.

<Check type="predict" concept="stableswap-invariant">
A protocol lists a new yield-bearing dollar token, `yUSD`, and creates a two-coin StableSwap pool
against USDC with `A = 500` — chosen because the higher `A`, the tighter the quotes, and tight quotes
attract volume. The risk memo says: "Downside is bounded, because the pool holds only dollar assets."

You are reviewing. Name the specific thing `A = 500` buys, the specific thing it costs, and the one
question the memo has not asked.

<Answer>
**What `A = 500` buys.** A wider flat region. The pool quotes closer to parity at larger sizes, so it
wins routing against pools with lower `A` and against constant-product pools, and it earns fees on
that volume. This is real and it is the reason to do it.

**What it costs, stated as a mechanism.** A higher `A` means the curve resists repricing over a wider
range of imbalance.<Cite src="curve-stableswap-paper" /> If `yUSD` becomes worth less than a dollar,
the pool goes on quoting near 1.00 while it accumulates `yUSD` and pays out USDC, and it stops only
when its own reserves are extremely skewed. The pool has no oracle — its only evidence that something
has changed is the imbalance that the draining trades themselves create, which arrives after the
value has left.

**The comparison worth putting in the memo.** On the two-coin numbers in this lesson, `A = 100` costs
its liquidity providers roughly 28 times what a constant-product pool with identical reserves would
lose to the same 10% depeg, because it absorbs roughly 16 times as much of the failing asset before
its price moves. `A = 500` is a stronger version of the same bet.

**"Both sides are dollar assets" is the error, not the reassurance.** It is an assumption about the
inputs presented as a property of the pool. The right restatement is: *this pool is a standing offer
to buy `yUSD` at approximately one dollar, in size, with the liquidity providers' USDC, and `A` sets
how much size.* Whether that offer is safe is entirely a question about `yUSD`.

**The question the memo has not asked: what is `yUSD` actually worth, and who can change it?** A
yield-bearing dollar token's value comes from an exchange rate computed by another
protocol — which, as the security track established, is
[an oracle](/c/orac-lp-token-and-rate-oracles) whether or not anyone calls it one, and belongs in the
[trust-assumption inventory](/c/trust-assumption-inventory) with an owner and a failure mode. The
pool's parameters are a second-order question until that one is answered.

**What to ask for.** The redemption path and its constraints; a lower `A` while the asset is young,
raised by governance ramp rather than by jump if it earns one;<Cite src="curve-3pool-source" /> and a
monitoring rule on reserve imbalance, because imbalance is the pool's only sensor and it leads the
loss.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The last lesson in this module** removes the final barrier to every scenario above. Each argument
about draining a pool has quietly assumed the attacker holds the capital to do it. Flash loans make
that assumption false, and change which of these calculations are worth worrying about.

**crvUSD and the lending track.** Curve's stablecoin puts a purpose-built AMM at the centre of
borrowing rather than of swapping: LLAMMA is the market-making contract that rebalances and
liquidates collateral, and every market has its own AMM holding the collateral asset and
crvUSD.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-overview" /> Same instinct — shape the curve around what the assets
are expected to do — applied to liquidation. The stablecoin module works through what happens when a
pool's parity assumption meets a real redemption.

**Choosing an invariant.** The transferable lesson generalises past AMMs. Every curve is a claim about
what the pooled assets are worth relative to each other, and the parameter that makes it
capital-efficient is the same parameter that makes it wrong more expensively. When you meet a new
invariant — a weighted pool, a dynamic-peg pool, a concentrated range — ask what it assumes and what
happens on the day the assumption fails. The answer is usually visible in the flat part of the graph.
