A pool holds 1,000 WETH and 3,000,000 USDC. The marginal price is 3,000 USDC per WETH — the reserve
ratio, derived last lesson.

Two people sell WETH into it, each against exactly these reserves. The first sells 10 WETH and
receives 29,614.74 USDC, an average of 2,961.47 each. The second sells 100 WETH and receives
271,983.27 USDC, an average of 2,719.83 each. Ten times the size, and the average price is 8% worse.

Nobody traded against them. No fee changed. No attacker was involved. The second trader simply moved
further along a curve whose slope is not constant, and this lesson derives exactly how much — closely
enough that you can predict the number before you send the transaction, and then separates that
effect from the entirely different thing wallets file under the same heading.

## Deriving the output

You know the fee-free invariant `x · y = k` and the marginal price `p = y / x`. Build the trade
formula from those in four steps.

**Subgoal 1 — write the constraint after the trade.** A trader deposits `dx` of token X and withdraws
`dy` of token Y. If no fee were charged, the invariant holds with equality:

```text
(x + dx) · (y - dy) = x · y
```

**Subgoal 2 — solve for the output.** Expand and cancel `x · y` from both sides:

```text
x·y - x·dy + y·dx - dx·dy = x·y
y·dx = x·dy + dx·dy = dy·(x + dx)
dy = y · dx / (x + dx)
```

**Subgoal 3 — charge the fee on the way in.** Uniswap applies its trading fee by reducing the amount
paid in by 0.3% before enforcing the invariant.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> So substitute
`dx · (1 - f)` for `dx` wherever the *invariant* sees it, while the trader still pays the full `dx`:

```text
dy = y · dx · (1 - f) / (x + dx · (1 - f))
```

**Subgoal 4 — check it against the shipped code.** With `f = 0.003`, `1 - f = 997/1000`. Clearing
denominators gives exactly what `UniswapV2Library.getAmountOut` computes: multiply the input by 997,
multiply by the output reserve, and divide by the input reserve times 1000 plus that same 997-scaled
input.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-library-source" />

```solidity
uint amountInWithFee = amountIn.mul(997);
uint numerator = amountInWithFee.mul(reserveOut);
uint denominator = reserveIn.mul(1000).add(amountInWithFee);
amountOut = numerator / denominator;
```

The reverse direction, `getAmountIn`, is the same algebra solved the other way — and it adds 1 to the
result, so integer truncation can never round in the trader's favour at the pool's
expense.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-library-source" /> That `+ 1` is the
[rounding-direction](/c/rounding-direction) discipline you met in the security track, in its most
compact possible form.

## One variable, not three

The formula above appears to depend on `x`, `y` and `dx` separately. It does not. Divide numerator
and denominator by `x` and write `α = dx / x` — the trade as a fraction of the input reserve:

```text
dy / y = α(1 - f) / (1 + α(1 - f))
```

**The fraction of the output reserve you receive depends only on the fraction of the input reserve
you deposit.** Pool size cancels. A trade of 1% of reserves gets the same proportional treatment in a
$10,000 pool and a $10 billion one.

This is the single most useful consequence of the constant-product form, because it converts every
question about execution quality into a question about one number: your size relative to the pool.
Not the pool's TVL, not the quoted price, not the fee tier. **Depth is the security parameter**, and
`α` is how you read it.

## Three prices, all different

Set `f = 0` for a moment and take the ratios that traders actually care about.

**The marginal price before.** `p₀ = y / x`.

**The average execution price.** Divide the output by the input: `dy / dx = y / (x + dx)`. Relative to
the marginal price, that is

```text
(dy/dx) / p₀ = x / (x + dx) = 1 / (1 + α)
```

So the *execution shortfall* — how much worse your average fill is than the price you were quoted —
is `α / (1 + α)`.

**The marginal price after.** The new reserves are `x + dx` and `y·x/(x + dx)`, so

```text
p₁ = p₀ / (1 + α)²
```

and the *marginal price impact* is `1 - 1/(1 + α)²`.

Three numbers, all commonly called "the price impact", and the ordering never changes: the average
you paid sits strictly between the price before and the price after. For small `α`, expanding both
expressions gives shortfall ≈ `α` and marginal impact ≈ `2α`. **The average fill is about half as bad
as the post-trade quote suggests.** A UI reporting the larger number is not lying, but it is not
reporting what you paid.

<Aside kind="note">
Check it against the opening numbers. The 10 WETH trade is `α = 1%`. Predicted shortfall
`0.01/1.01 = 0.990%`; measured, with the 0.3% fee included, 1.284% — the difference is the fee,
almost exactly. Predicted marginal impact `1 - 1/1.01² = 1.970%`; measured 1.967%. The 100 WETH
trade is `α = 10%`: predicted shortfall 9.091% against a measured 9.339%, predicted marginal impact
17.355% against a measured 17.333%. The derivation is not an approximation of the contract; it *is*

</Aside>

## What grows superlinearly, and what does not

"Price impact grows superlinearly with size" is repeated everywhere and is half right. Being precise
about which half is worth two minutes, because the two halves lead to opposite trading decisions.

**The per-unit shortfall is not superlinear.** `α / (1 + α)` is concave and bounded above by 1. Double
`α` from 1% to 2% and the shortfall goes from 0.990% to 1.961% — slightly *less* than double. Push
`α` to 10 and the shortfall is 91%, not 1000%. The percentage you give up flattens out, because a
constant-product pool can never be drained.

**The value destroyed is superlinear.** Compare what you received against what you would have
received at the unmoved marginal price:

```text
p₀ · dx - dy  =  y·dx/x - y·dx/(x + dx)  =  y · α² / (1 + α)
```

Quadratic in `α` for small trades. Halve your size and you lose a quarter as much value. That is the
sentence with operational content.

Run it on the opening pool. Selling 100 WETH in one transaction returns 271,983.27 USDC. Selling it
as 100 separate 1 WETH transactions against the same untouched pool returns 271,947.72 — worse by
35.55 USDC, which is the extra fee incurred by paying 0.3% on a hundred separate inputs. Fee-free,
both routes return exactly 272,727.27. Splitting is a routing decision, not a price-impact decision.

## Slippage is the other thing

Everything above is deterministic. Given the reserves at the moment your transaction executes, the
output is a pure function with no randomness in it. You can compute it, and the pool will honour it.

The catch is in the clause: *at the moment your transaction executes*. You computed the quote against
reserves you read some seconds earlier. Between the read and the execution, other transactions may
be ordered into the same pool. Slippage is the difference between the execution you predicted and
the execution you got, caused by those other transactions.

**Price impact is what the curve charges you for your own size. Slippage is what the ordering
charges you for everyone else's.** They are measured in the same units and displayed in the same
box, and confusing them makes it impossible to reason about either.

## What the tolerance dial actually authorises

The pool cannot protect you from ordering, because the pool has no memory of what you were quoted.
Protection lives in the router, and it is one comparison.

`swapExactTokensForTokens` computes the amounts along the path and then requires the final output to
be at least `amountOutMin`, reverting with `INSUFFICIENT_OUTPUT_AMOUNT` otherwise. The exact-output
functions carry the mirror check against `amountInMax`, reverting with `EXCESSIVE_INPUT_AMOUNT`. All
of them sit behind an `ensure(deadline)` modifier that requires `deadline >= block.timestamp`, so a
transaction that sits in the mempool for an hour cannot execute against an hour-old
assumption.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-router02-source" />

That is the entire mechanism. Note what it is *not*: it is not a comparison against your quote, which
the chain never saw. It is a floor. Any execution at or above the floor is accepted, and the
contract has no way to distinguish "the market moved" from "someone moved the market on purpose".

**So the gap between your quote and your floor is a window of value that anyone who controls ordering
may take, without ever causing a revert.** Widening the tolerance does not make the trade more likely
to succeed in any benign sense. It enlarges the set of executions the contract will accept, and
therefore enlarges the amount extractable while your check still passes.

### Pricing the window

Work the 10 WETH trade with a 1% tolerance. The quote is 29,614.74 USDC, so `amountOutMin` is
29,318.59 and the window is 296.15 USDC. What can a party who controls ordering do with it?

**Subgoal 1 — establish the attacker's constraint.** They may push your fill down to your floor and
no further, because one wei below it your transaction reverts and their position is stranded. So the
optimisation is: maximise their profit subject to your output landing exactly on `amountOutMin`.

**Subgoal 2 — find the front-run size.** Solving numerically against `getAmountOut`, selling 5.0704
WETH into the pool ahead of you leaves reserves at which your 10 WETH returns precisely
29,318.59.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-library-source" />

**Subgoal 3 — close the position.** They sold that 5.0704 WETH for 15,089.43 USDC and buy it back
after your trade for 14,882.44, netting **206.98 USDC**.

**Subgoal 4 — account for the difference.** You lost the full 296.15. They gained 206.98. The missing
89.17 is the fee their two trades paid the pool. The window is not transferred to the attacker; about
70% of it is, and the rest is destroyed on your behalf. Across every tolerance setting from 0.1% to
5% that ratio stays near 70%, because it is the fee structure, not the size.

<Aside kind="warn">
This arithmetic assumes the extractor already holds WETH and pays nothing for gas or for ordering.
Neither holds in practice: the inventory can be borrowed for the duration of the transaction (the
last lesson in this module), gas is real, and the right to be ordered first is auctioned, so most of
the 206.98 ends up bid away. **That changes who receives the value, not whether you lose it.** The
victim's loss is set by the tolerance, not by the competitiveness of the market for taking it — which
is why "sandwiching has declined" is never an argument for a wider tolerance.
</Aside>

The correct mental model is that the tolerance is a **security parameter denominated in your own
money**, and its right value is a function of two things you can actually estimate: how volatile the
pair is over the time your transaction is pending, and how much of your quote you are willing to
lose to guarantee inclusion. The [MEV module](/c/mev-slippage-is-the-attack-surface) develops what
changes when you route through a venue that does not expose the window at all.

A quoter service prices a swap on a pool holding 40,000,000 USDC and 10,000 WETH. A user wants to
sell 500 WETH. The service returns two numbers: "price impact 4.76%" and "you receive 1,904,761

Decide who is right, and say what the reviewer should ask for instead. Ignore the 0.3% fee.

<Answer>
**The reviewer is wrong on the arithmetic and right on the substance.**

**Both numbers are correct, and they measure different things.** Here `α = 500 / 10,000 = 5%`. The
average execution shortfall is `α/(1+α) = 0.05/1.05 = 4.762%`, and the marginal price after the
trade is `1/1.05² = 90.70%` of the price before — a 9.30% marginal impact. The service is reporting
the *average* shortfall. Check the output: `dy = y·α/(1+α) = 40,000,000 × 0.047619 = 1,904,762`,
which is the quoted figure to the last digit. The two numbers are the same fact stated twice.

**Which is why the reviewer's instinct is sound.** A single field labelled "price impact" is
ambiguous between two quantities that differ by roughly a factor of two, and the ambiguity is
invisible at the small sizes where UIs are usually tested — at `α = 0.1%` the two differ by one
tenth of a basis point. It only becomes material at exactly the size where a user needs it.

**What to ask for instead.** Two fields with unambiguous names: the average execution price, in units
of the quote asset per unit of the base asset, and the post-trade marginal price. Anyone reasoning
about arbitrage or writing a follow-on trade needs the second; the user needs the first.

**The third number, which neither of them mentioned.** The user is being told about percentages when
the decision requires an amount. The value given up against an unmoved price is `y · α²/(1+α)` =
`40,000,000 × 0.0025/1.05` = **95,238 USDC**. That is the number that determines whether to route
across venues, wait, or accept — and it is quadratic in size, so a user who halves the trade gives
up about 24,000 rather than about 48,000.

**A fourth, if the quoter is used by contracts.** The manipulation-cost literature defines the cost
of moving an AMM price as the attacker's minimum mark-to-market loss, not the capital they
deploy.<Cite src="arxiv-manipulation-cost-2026" /> `y · α²/(1+α)` is that loss for a
constant-product pool, computed from this pool's depth — the same formula, read as a defence budget
instead of a trading cost.
</Answer>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** takes the same curve from the liquidity provider's side. If every trade moves the
price, and arbitrageurs trade whenever the pool disagrees with the world, then the pool is
continuously selling whichever asset is appreciating. That is derivable from what is now on the page,
and it has a name that misleads people about what it is.

**Concentrated liquidity** exists to attack the `α` term directly. Everything in this lesson depends
on your size relative to the reserves *that are actually available at the current price*; a v3
position confines the same capital to a price interval, raising effective depth there and dropping it
to zero outside.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-math-primer-2" /> The formulas change shape; the reasoning does
not.

**Reviewing a protocol that swaps.** Any contract that performs a swap on behalf of users has to
choose a `minAmountOut`. Find that line. If it is hardcoded to zero, or computed on-chain from the
same pool's current reserves, the protocol has no slippage protection at all — the second case looks
like a bound and is a tautology, since the pool's own state cannot testify against itself.

**Sizing your own trades.** The habit worth keeping: before asking what price a venue quotes, ask
what `α` your trade represents there. The quote follows from the depth, and the depth is the thing
that survives a change in market conditions.
