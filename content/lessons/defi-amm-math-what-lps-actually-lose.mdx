Deposit 1,000 WETH and 3,000,000 USDC into a constant-product pool when WETH trades at 3,000. Come
back when WETH trades at 6,000.

You now hold a claim on 707.106781 WETH and 4,242,640.69 USDC. You never placed a sell order. You
never approved a trade. Yet 292.893219 WETH left your position, and the USDC that arrived in exchange
came in at an average of 4,242.64 each, into a market that ended at 6,000.

Your position is worth 8,485,281.37. Holding the original two amounts would have been worth
9,000,000. The gap is 514,718.63, and it is exactly the 292.893219 WETH you sold multiplied by the
1,757.36 gap between what you got for them and what they ended up worth.

The dashboard calls that *impermanent loss*. This lesson derives it from the invariant, shows that
the reconciliation above is an identity rather than a coincidence, and argues that the name has cost
people real money.

## The composition is a function of price alone

Everything follows from one observation. A constant-product position has two state variables, `x` and
`y`, but they are pinned by two equations: the invariant and the price.

**Subgoal 1 — solve the two constraints together.** From `p = y / x` and `x · y = k`, substitute
`y = p·x` into the invariant to get `p·x² = k`, so:

```text
x = sqrt(k / p)        y = sqrt(k · p)
```

**The pool's composition is determined by the price.** Not by the price path, not by volume, not by
who traded. If two pools with the same `k` are at the same price, they hold identical reserves, no
matter what happened in between. Fees make `k` grow, which is why this is stated for a fee-free pool
and why fee income is bookkept separately below.

**Subgoal 2 — value the position.** Denominate in the quote asset, so a unit of X is worth `p`:

```text
V_pool(p) = y + p·x = sqrt(k·p) + p·sqrt(k/p) = 2·sqrt(k·p)
```

**Subgoal 3 — value the alternative.** Someone who deposited at `p₀` and instead simply held the two
amounts `x₀` and `y₀` has:

```text
V_hold(p) = y₀ + p·x₀ = sqrt(k·p₀) + p·sqrt(k/p₀)
```

**Subgoal 4 — take the ratio and let scale cancel.** Divide, factor `sqrt(k)` out of both, and write
`r = p / p₀`:

```text
V_pool / V_hold = 2·sqrt(p·p₀) / (p₀ + p) = 2·sqrt(r) / (1 + r)
```

generates.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-understanding-returns" />

Two properties of that function are worth reading off directly. It equals 1 at `r = 1` and is
strictly below 1 everywhere else, so **divergence loss is never positive**, in either direction. And
it is symmetric under `r → 1/r`: the documentation states that the loss is the same whichever
direction the price moves, so a doubling costs the same as a
halving.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-understanding-returns" /> That symmetry is a consequence of the
geometric mean sitting in the formula, and it surprises people who expect to be hurt only by moves
against them.

## Where the money physically went

The formula is a ratio of two portfolio values, which makes it feel like an accounting artefact. It
is not. Trace the actual transfers.

**Subgoal 1 — who did the trading.** Nobody rebalanced your position on purpose. When the external
price of WETH rose above the pool's, buying WETH from the pool became profitable, so arbitrageurs
bought until the pool's marginal price matched the market. Each such trade took WETH out and put USDC
in. That is the previous lesson's mechanism running in reverse: they moved along your curve, and the
price impact they paid is the price you received.

**Subgoal 2 — compute the average price you sold at.** The quantity sold is `x₀ - x₁` and the proceeds
are `y₁ - y₀`. Substituting the composition formulas and cancelling:

```text
(y₁ - y₀) / (x₀ - x₁)
  = sqrt(k)·(sqrt(p) - sqrt(p₀)) / [ sqrt(k)·(1/sqrt(p₀) - 1/sqrt(p)) ]
  = sqrt(p₀ · p)
```

**The average price at which a constant-product position sells the appreciating asset is exactly the
geometric mean of the starting and ending price.** Not approximately. Exactly, and independently of
the path taken between them, because composition depends only on price.

**Subgoal 3 — reconcile.** You sold `x₀ - x₁` units at `sqrt(p₀·p)` when they finished worth `p`, so
the shortfall against holding is `(x₀ - x₁) · (p - sqrt(p₀·p))`. In the opening numbers,
`292.893219 × (6000 - 4242.640687) = 514,718.63` — the same figure the portfolio comparison gave, to
the cent.

This is what the ratio formula is *about*. It is not a valuation quirk. It is a completed sequence of
sales at prices that turned out to be too low, executed by counterparties who kept the difference.

<Aside kind="note">
Run it downward for the symmetry. At `p = 1,500` the position holds 1,414.2136 WETH and 2,121,320.34
USDC: it *bought* 414.2136 WETH, paying an average of 2,121.32 each, in a market that ended at 1,500.
Same identity, same 5.72% shortfall, opposite direction. A constant-product position is a standing
instruction to sell into strength and buy into weakness, with the fill price fixed by the curve.
</Aside>

### The fee is also a no-trade band

One refinement, because it is the only thing standing between an LP and continuous extraction. The
whitepaper's phrasing is that arbitrageurs will trade with Uniswap if the price is incorrect *by a
sufficient amount to make up for the fee*, which is why the pool's price tends to track the market
price.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" />

Unpack the qualifier. An arbitrageur pays 0.3% on their input, so a divergence smaller than roughly
that is not worth acting on. The pool's price therefore does not track the market continuously; it
sits inside a band around it, and only gets dragged when the market leaves the band. Every drag is
one of the sales in the reconciliation above.

Two consequences follow. **The LP is not rebalanced on every tick** — they are rebalanced once per
excursion large enough to clear the fee, which is why realised volatility at the pool's own time
scale, not headline volatility, is the quantity that matters. And **the fee tier is doing two jobs at
once**: it sets fee income per unit of volume, and it sets the width of the band inside which no
value leaves. Raising it increases both, and reduces the volume that arrives. That three-way tension
is exactly what choosing a fee tier is trading off, and it is why the right tier for a volatile pair
is not the right tier for a correlated one.

## Why "impermanent" is the wrong word

Here is the precise sense in which the name is defensible, and the much larger sense in which it is
not.

**Defensible:** the divergence factor `2·sqrt(r)/(1 + r)` depends only on the *current* price ratio.
If the price returns to `p₀`, the factor returns to 1 and the shortfall against holding vanishes. In
that narrow mark-to-market sense the loss can un-happen.

**Not defensible:** the trades that produced it have already settled. Your 292.893219 WETH is gone,
transferred to arbitrageurs, paid for in USDC that is now in the pool. Nothing about continuing to
hold the LP position preserves your original composition — the position *is* its current composition,
and the current composition is whatever the price says it is. The only way the WETH comes back is
for the pool to buy it back, which requires the price to actually fall, from someone who will only
sell at the price the curve then quotes.

<Misconception
  belief="Impermanent loss becomes permanent only when you withdraw."
  reality="The rebalancing has already happened inside the pool. Holding the LP position does not preserve the original composition; it preserves only the option that the price path returns. Withdrawing does not cause the loss — it forfeits that option."
  why="The word implies a paper loss that a later price move can undo, and a later price move genuinely can undo it. What the word hides is that the position's composition has already changed, so 'holding on' is a directional bet on mean reversion, not a decision to wait."
  src="elsts-v3-liquidity-math"
/>

That reframing has an operational consequence. "I will wait for it to come back" is a coherent
sentence about an unrealised stock position, because the stock is still in the account. It is a
different sentence about an LP position, because what you are waiting for is not the recovery of an
asset you still own — it is the reversal of a price move so that the pool buys back, cheaply,
something it already sold. An LP holding through a permanent revaluation is not waiting; they are
short volatility with no expiry.

This is why the research literature prefers other names. The 2022 loss-versus-rebalancing work
decomposes LP returns into a beta-like component reflecting market-risk exposure and an alpha-like
component that is accrued fees minus losses to arbitrageurs, and reports that for the Uniswap v2
ETH-USDC pool over 99.991% of LP return *variance* is driven by the beta
exposure.<Cite src="arxiv-lvr" /> Read that second figure carefully: it is a statement about
variance, not about profit. Almost all of the *movement* in an LP's position is just holding the
assets. The part that decides whether providing liquidity beat holding them is the small alpha term,
and it has a minus sign in it.

## Fees are compared against this, not against zero

Every LP interface reports fee income. Almost none report the divergence term beside it, which makes
the reported number an answer to a question nobody asked.

The condition for an LP position to have beaten simply holding the two deposited amounts is:

```text
fees earned  >  divergence loss over the same period
```

Both terms are needed and neither is informative alone. A pool advertising a high fee APR is
advertising the left-hand side of an inequality whose right-hand side is set by how far the pair's
price moved while you were in it.

<Misconception
  belief="The position earned fees, so providing liquidity was profitable."
  reality="Fees are measured against divergence loss, not against zero. A high-fee pool on a pair whose price diverged can underperform simply holding the two assets, and frequently does."
  why="Dashboards report fee APR because fees are an unambiguous inflow that can be attributed to your position. Divergence loss has no transaction to point at — it is the difference between your position and a counterfactual — so it does not appear in a ledger and does not get a row."
  src="elsts-v3-liquidity-math"
/>

Uniswap's own returns page is honest about how hard the comparison is in advance: it says you cannot
know the trade-off between fee revenue and losses from directional movement without knowing the
amount of in-between trades, and summarises the LP's preference as "the more chop and back and forth,
the better".<Cite src="uniswap-v2-understanding-returns" /> That sentence is the entire strategy in
nine words. Volume without net price movement is fee income at no divergence cost. Net price movement
without volume is divergence cost with no fee income.

<Aside kind="note">
A precision note on combining the two terms. The derivation above holds `k` fixed, but fees make `k`
ratchet upward on every swap, so a real position is the divergence factor applied to a *grown* `k`
rather than the original one.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> The two effects are strictly
multiplicative; treating them as an additive fee term minus a divergence term is an approximation
that is excellent while both are small percentages and degrades when they are not. It is the standard
treatment, and it is worth knowing that it is a treatment.
</Aside>

<Check type="explain" concept="impermanent-loss">
An LP deposits into a WETH/USDC pool at WETH = 3,000. Six months later WETH is at 3,000 again, having
gone to 5,000 and back. The position has earned fees throughout.

They conclude: "Impermanent loss was zero, because the price came back. My return is exactly the fees
I earned." A colleague says the return is higher than that, and a second colleague says it is lower.

Say which parts of each claim are right, and what the LP would have needed to measure.

<Answer>
**The LP's headline is right, one colleague is right for a real reason, and the other is right for a
different real reason. All three can hold at once.**

**The divergence term genuinely is zero.** `2·sqrt(r)/(1 + r)` depends only on the current ratio, and
`r = 1`, so the factor is exactly 1.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-understanding-returns" /> The excursion to
5,000 and back leaves no residue in that term. This is the one case where "impermanent" describes
what happened.

**The first colleague is right: the return is more than the fee total, slightly.** Fees compound. Each
fee-paying swap raises `k`, and every later fee is charged against a pool that is already
larger.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> Summing nominal fee income understates the position by the
compounding, which over six months of continuous volume is not nothing.

**The second colleague is right: measured against the alternative, the return may be less.** The
comparison the LP made is against holding WETH and USDC, and that comparison is clean. But the capital
was committed for six months, and the round trip through 5,000 was traded against them the whole way
— arbitrageurs bought WETH out on the way up at prices below 5,000 and sold it back on the way down
at prices above 3,000. Every one of those trades was a completed transfer. The reason the divergence
term nets to zero is not that those transfers did not happen; it is that the up-leg and down-leg
shortfalls exactly cancel *when measured against holding*. Against any other use of the capital, they
do not cancel, and the fee income is the only thing paying for the risk that the price had not come
back.

**What the LP needed to measure.** Three quantities, none of which the fee counter supplies. First,
realised volatility of the pair over the period, which is what determines how much was handed to
arbitrageurs — the loss-versus-rebalancing framing exists to make that term explicit rather than
implicit.<Cite src="arxiv-lvr" /> Second, fee income as a rate on committed capital, not as a
cumulative number. Third, the divergence loss at the *worst* point of the path, not just at the
endpoints, because that is what determined whether they could have exited early without a loss and
what they were exposed to if the price had stayed at 5,000.

**The general form of the error.** "The price came back, so nothing happened" is true of the
divergence factor and false of everything else. The position was rebalanced against the move twice,
paid for it twice, and was compensated in fees. Whether that was a good trade is a question about the
size of the fees relative to the realised volatility — which is a question about the pair, not about
the pool.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** changes the curve instead of the position. If divergence loss is driven by how far
the price moves, a pool whose assets are *supposed* not to move relative to each other should suffer
almost none of it — and the StableSwap invariant is what you get when you design for that assumption
explicitly, along with everything that happens when the assumption fails.

**Concentrated liquidity** intensifies every term in this lesson. Confining the same capital to a
price range raises fee income per unit of capital and raises divergence loss in the same proportion,
and adds a new state — the price leaving the range — in which the position is entirely in one
asset and earning nothing.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-math-primer-2" /><Cite src="elsts-v3-liquidity-math" />
The formulas are longer; the inequality is the same one.

**The MEV module** picks up the counterparty side. The arbitrageurs in this lesson are not villains
and not optional — they are the mechanism that keeps the pool's price honest, and what they take is
the price of that service. [Loss-versus-rebalancing](/c/mev-arbitrage-is-lvr) is the modern way of
measuring it, and where the ongoing design work on reducing it lives.

**Evaluating any yield-bearing position.** The transferable habit is to insist on the counterfactual.
"This position earned 38%" is not a claim until someone names what it is being compared against and
prices that alternative over the same window. Divergence loss is only unusual in that the
counterfactual is easy to state precisely and still gets left out.
