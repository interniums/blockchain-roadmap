Open `UniswapV2Pair.sol` and search it for the word `price`.

It is not there. Not as a variable, not as a function, not as an argument. The contract that defined
how on-chain markets work has no field holding a price and no code path that sets
one.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-pair-source" />

What it has instead is one inequality, at the bottom of `swap`, after the tokens have already left:

```solidity
uint balance0Adjusted = balance0.mul(1000).sub(amount0In.mul(3));
uint balance1Adjusted = balance1.mul(1000).sub(amount1In.mul(3));
require(
    balance0Adjusted.mul(balance1Adjusted) >= uint(_reserve0).mul(_reserve1).mul(1000**2),
    'UniswapV2: K'
);
```

That is the whole pricing engine.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-pair-source" /> This lesson takes it apart:
where the price comes from if nobody stores it, why the contract can fill a trade with nobody on the
other side, and what the fee is doing to the invariant it appears to be part of.

## Why the order book did not survive contact with the EVM

A limit order is a stored commitment. Placing one writes storage; cancelling one writes storage;
matching one reads and writes slots belonging to strangers. On the configuration this curriculum
measures against — forge 1.7.1, solc 0.8.36, optimizer off — a cold `SLOAD` costs 2100 gas and a
zero-to-non-zero `SSTORE` costs 20000 plus the 2100 cold-slot
surcharge.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /> A book worth trading against holds hundreds of live
orders, each paid for twice.

The gas is not the real cost, though. Maintenance is. A market maker on a book must cancel and
repost continuously or be picked off at a stale quote, and on a chain where the finest available
granularity is a block and ordering inside it is public, "continuously" is not available — the
requote is itself a transaction anyone can see and front-run.

The constant-product market maker removes requoting entirely. Liquidity providers post capital once.
The price is not something they set and defend; it is a function of where the capital sits.

## The invariant is a constraint, not a quote

A constant-product pool holds two reserves. Call them `x` and `y`. The rule the contract enforces is
that after any swap the product of the reserves may not decrease:

```text
x · y >= k
```

Uniswap v1 was described in exactly those terms — a "constant product formula" maintaining "the
invariant that the product of the reserves cannot decrease" — and v2 is a new implementation of the
same formula.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" />

Read the direction of that inequality carefully; it is the source of most confusion about what a
pool is. The contract is not computing what you should receive. It is *checking whether what you
took was small enough*. Any withdrawal is legal if the reserves left behind still satisfy the
constraint. The pool never refuses a trade because the price is wrong — it has no opinion about the
price. It refuses trades that broke the inequality.

This is why the tokens move *before* the check. `swap` transfers the requested output optimistically,
optionally calls back into the recipient, and only then reads its own balances and tests the
invariant.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-pair-source" /> A contract that computed a price would have to
compute it first. A contract that enforces a constraint can act first and verify afterwards.

## Deriving the price from the constraint

The price has to fall out of the invariant, since there is nothing else in the contract. Work it in
four steps.

**Subgoal 1 — express one reserve as a function of the other.** Holding `k` fixed, `x · y = k` says
`y = k / x`. Reserves are not two free numbers; they are one number and a curve. In the `x`-`y`
plane, the legal states form a hyperbola.

**Subgoal 2 — decide what "price" means on a curve.** A price is an exchange rate: how much `y` per
unit of `x`. On a curve that rate depends on how far you move, so the *marginal* price is the limit
as trade size goes to zero — the slope at the current point.

**Subgoal 3 — take the slope.** Differentiate `y = k / x`:

```text
dy/dx = -k / x²
```

Substitute `k = x · y` back in:

```text
dy/dx = -(x · y) / x² = -y / x
```

The minus sign says the reserves move in opposite directions, which is what a swap is. The magnitude
is `y / x`.

**Subgoal 4 — state the result in the units traders use.** The marginal price of token X, denominated
in token Y, is the ratio of the reserves:

```text
p = y / x
```

The v2 whitepaper states this as its first equation: the marginal price offered by Uniswap, not
including fees, is computed by dividing the reserves of one asset by the reserves of the
other.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" />

Nothing was stored, consulted, or agreed. The price is a *reading* of the pool's state, the way a
water level is a reading of how much water is in a tank. Move the water and the level changes; that
is not the tank quoting you a level.

<Aside kind="note">
This is also why the reserve ratio makes such a tempting and such a terrible oracle. `getReserves()`
returns a real number that genuinely is the pool's marginal price — and it is whatever the previous
transaction in the same block left it as. Uniswap's own v2 documentation routes oracle consumers to
the cumulative-price accumulator rather than the instantaneous ratio for exactly this
reason.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-oracles-docs" /> You met the consequence in the security track as
[oracle manipulation](/c/oracle-manipulation).
</Aside>

## Where the counterparty went

"No counterparty" is doing something specific, because it is not true that value came from nowhere.

On a book, your counterparty is a particular order at a particular price, and the trade happens only
if that order exists. Discovery of the price and discovery of the counterparty are the same event.

In a pool, the counterparty is the whole set of liquidity providers in proportion to their share,
and their consent was given once, at deposit. The price they will trade at was not negotiated — the
curve fixed it in advance for every possible size. So the pool can always fill you, at *some* price,
for any size strictly below its reserves. It cannot run out of willingness, only of tokens, and even
then only asymptotically: as `x` grows without bound, `y = k / x` approaches zero without reaching
it, which is why a constant-product pool cannot be fully drained by trading.

That property is why the curve was chosen. It is a strictly weaker guarantee than "the price is
good", and the next lesson is about how much worse the price gets.

## The fee, and why k is not constant

Now go back to the `require` at the top of this lesson, because it is not testing `x · y >= k`. It is
testing something with a 997 hidden inside it.

Uniswap v1 applied its trading fee by reducing the amount paid into the contract by 0.3% before
enforcing the constant-product invariant, which the whitepaper writes as `(x₁ - 0.003 · xᵢₙ) · y₁ >=
x₀ · y₀`.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> v2 generalises it to both sides, because flash swaps
make it possible for both input amounts to be non-zero at once, and then multiplies through by
1,000,000 to keep the on-chain arithmetic in integers:<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" />

```text
(1000 · x₁ - 3 · xᵢₙ) · (1000 · y₁ - 3 · yᵢₙ) >= 1000000 · x₀ · y₀
```

That is the line of Solidity you read at the start, character for character.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-pair-source" />

Trace what it does. The trader deposits `xᵢₙ`. The check discounts 0.3% of that deposit before
comparing, so the trade is sized as if only 99.7% of the input had arrived — but all 100% of it
stays in the contract. The reserves after the swap therefore satisfy the *un-discounted* product
strictly *above* the old one. **The fee is not a transfer to anybody. It is the gap between the
product the contract checked and the product it actually holds.**

Two consequences, both load-bearing later.

**k ratchets upward, permanently.** Every fee-paying swap leaves the pool on a higher hyperbola.
Since pool shares are claims on a fraction of the reserves, that ratchet is the entire mechanism by
which liquidity providers are paid: no fee balance, no claim function, no accrual variable — only a
bigger `k` divided among the same number of shares.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" />

**The advertised fee is not the fee you pay.** Charged on the input rather than the output, the fee
relative to the withdrawn amount is slightly higher. The whitepaper does it in a footnote:
`1/(1 - 0.003) - 1 = 3/997`, approximately 0.3009203%.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> Exactly
the kind of small that becomes a finding when a fee-sharing contract assumes the two are the same
number.

A third piece of the fee story is the one people get wrong from memory. The v2 contracts contain a
protocol fee that was written but not switched on: a private key can update a variable on the
factory to enable an on-chain 5-basis-point fee, after which liquidity providers would earn 25 basis
points on every trade rather than 30.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> Traders pay 30 either way;
the switch decides how the 30 is split.

## Who does the quoting, then

If the pool has no price, the number in the wallet came from a reimplementation of the arithmetic —
off-chain, or in a periphery contract.

`UniswapV2Library` holds the canonical version. `quote()` converts an amount at the current reserve
ratio with no curve effect at all — pure `amountA · reserveB / reserveA`, used for sizing liquidity
deposits, never for pricing a swap. `getAmountOut()` prices trades, and carries the fee explicitly:
multiply the input by 997, divide by the reserve scaled by 1000 plus that same fee-adjusted
input.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-library-source" /> The router then wraps the result in a bound and a
deadline before it will send anything.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-router02-source" />

Internalise the separation as an architectural fact, not a Uniswap trivium. **The component that
holds the money enforces a constraint; the component that talks to users computes a prediction.**
The prediction can be wrong — state may change before your transaction lands — and the constraint
cannot. Everything in the next lesson about slippage tolerance follows from that split.

## Where the first price comes from

If price is the reserve ratio, and the reserves start at zero, what sets the initial price?

The first depositor does, unilaterally, by choosing the ratio of the two amounts deposited. Nothing
checks it, because there is nothing to check against. They receive `sqrt(amount0 · amount1)` shares,
minus a `MINIMUM_LIQUIDITY` of 1000 units minted to the zero address and permanently
locked.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-pair-source" /> Every subsequent depositor gets the *minimum* of the two
proportional claims, which is what forces later deposits to arrive at the current
ratio.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-pair-source" />

That 1000-unit burn is a defence you have studied from the other end. The whitepaper's reasoning is
that share value can be inflated by donating to the pool, and locking the first 1e-15 pool shares
means raising one share to $100 would require donating $100,000 that is then permanently
stuck.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> It is the direct ancestor of the dead-shares mitigation
you met as the [ERC-4626 inflation attack](/c/inflation-first-depositor-attack) — same attack, same
fix, four years earlier.

Whoever sets that first ratio has set a price that disagrees with the rest of the world, and the
first arbitrageur to notice corrects it by taking the difference. That is not a launch bug; it is
how every pool tracks the market, and it is what lesson three charges to liquidity providers.

<Check type="explain" concept="constant-product">
A team is writing a contract that reads a Uniswap-v2-style pair. They want the pool's price and they
want to be careful, so instead of calling `getReserves()` they write:

```solidity
uint256 k = pair.kLast();
uint256 price = k / (reserve0 * reserve0);
```

reasoning that `k` is the invariant, so a price derived from `k` is more stable than one derived
from raw reserves. Say what is wrong with this, in terms of what `k` actually is.

<Answer>
**Three errors, and the deepest one is the premise.**

**`k` is not stable.** It ratchets upward on every fee-paying swap, because the contract checks a
fee-discounted product and keeps the undiscounted one.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> It also
jumps on every mint and burn, since adding liquidity scales both reserves. A price derived from `k`
inherits all of that movement from a quantity that has nothing to do with price.

**`kLast` is not even the current `k`.** In the pair contract it is a checkpoint used only for
protocol-fee accounting, written at the end of `mint` and `burn` when the fee is switched
on.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-pair-source" /> Between liquidity events it is a stale snapshot.

**The algebra does not produce a price anyway.** If `k = x · y` then `k / x² = y / x` — the same
reserve ratio they were trying to avoid, computed from a stale numerator, so it is the ratio *as of
the last liquidity event* scaled by a current reserve. It has no interpretation.

**What is actually true.** `k` is the *scale* parameter: for a given ratio, larger `k` means deeper
reserves and less price impact per unit traded. Price and depth are the two independent facts about
a pool, and `k` is the depth one — which is why shares are minted proportional to
`sqrt(x · y)`.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-pair-source" />

**What to do instead.** No single instantaneous read of this pool is safe as a price, however it is
computed, because any transaction ordered before yours sets it —
[spot price is not an oracle](/c/orac-spot-price-is-not-an-oracle). The real question is which
source and what manipulation cost, not which arithmetic.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** turns this derivation into the number that decides whether a trade is sized
correctly. If the marginal price is `y / x` and a trade moves `x`, the marginal price differs at the
end of your trade from the start — and the price you actually paid is neither. That gap is derivable
in three lines from what is now on the page.

**Reviewing an AMM integration.** When a contract holds funds and you are hunting for its pricing
logic, look for the *check*, not the calculation. In a well-built pool the calculation lives in a
component that cannot lose money if it is wrong. Pricing arithmetic inside the vault is either an
unusual design or a finding.

**The rest of this track** assumes this framing. Concentrated liquidity is this same curve with a
liquidity coefficient that varies by price range;<Cite src="uniswap-v3-math-primer-2" /> StableSwap,
three lessons from here, is a different invariant chosen so the slope stays near one over a wide
range. Both answer "what shape should the constraint be", and both are only legible once you stop
looking for the price.
