An LP asks a straightforward question. They have two positions in the same ETH/USDC pool. The front
end says both hold about 4,000 dollars. But when they read the contract, one reports a `liquidity` of
450 and the other reports 2,270. Same pool, same money, a factor of five apart in the number the
protocol actually uses.

Neither number is wrong, and neither is a dollar amount. `L` is a **curve parameter**, and this
lesson is about what it parameterises, why the protocol stores that rather than the two reserves you
would expect, and what changes when you start seeing a position as a bar instead of a curve.

## The definition, and its units

From the previous lessons: a position behaves within its range like a constant-product pool over
virtual reserves, and those reserves satisfy `x_virtual * y_virtual = L^2`. So the definition is
simply the root of the constant:

```text
L = sqrt( x_virtual * y_virtual )
```

The whitepaper puts it as `L = sqrt(x*y)` and notes that the amount of liquidity provided can be
measured by `L`, which equals the square root of `k`.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" />

**Start with the units, because they settle most of the confusion in one step.** `x` is measured in
token0 and `y` in token1, so `L` has units of `sqrt(token0 * token1)`. That is not a currency. It is
not comparable across pools with different token pairs. There is no exchange rate that turns it into
dollars, and the fact that a front end prints a dollar figure beside the word "liquidity" is a
presentation choice about the position's *contents*, not a rendering of `L`.

## Why the root, and not `k`

The obvious alternative is to store `k` directly. `k = L^2` carries exactly the same information, so
the choice looks like taste. It is not, and the reason is one property.

**Subgoal 1 — write down what the pool has to do when two LPs share a range.** Two positions with the
same bounds are, from the pool's point of view, one bigger position. The pool needs a single number
describing the combined depth so the swap loop can use it without iterating over positions.

**Subgoal 2 — test whether `L` combines by addition.** The virtual reserves are `x = L / sqrt(P)` and
`y = L * sqrt(P)`, both **linear in `L`**.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" /> Adding two positions
adds their reserves, so it adds their `L`. Combined depth is `L1 + L2`, with no reference to price.

**Subgoal 3 — test whether `k` combines by addition.** Take `L1 = 3` and `L2 = 4`.

```text
combined L = 3 + 4 = 7        combined k = 7^2 = 49
but k1 + k2 = 9 + 16 = 25     which is not 49
```

`k` is quadratic in the reserves, so it does not add. Storing `k` would force the pool to take a
square root every time a position was added or removed — the exact runtime operation the square-root
price was chosen to avoid.

**Subgoal 4 — check it against the contract.** The pool stores `liquidity` as a single top-level
`uint128`, and each entry in the `ticks` mapping carries a net liquidity delta applied on
crossing.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-pool" /> The whitepaper describes the same thing: each tick tracks
the total amount of liquidity that should be added or removed when it is crossed, as a **net**
value.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" /> Additivity is not a nice property `L` happens to have; it
is the property the entire bookkeeping layer is built on.

<Aside kind="note">
This is a good instance of a general principle in on-chain data design. Between two equivalent
encodings of the same quantity, pick the one under which the operation you perform most often —
here, aggregating positions — is the cheapest operation available. The v3 codebase pays a small,
one-off cost at the interface boundary (users think in token amounts) to make the inner loop free.
</Aside>

## A position is a bar

Now the framing the concept's title promises. Plot liquidity on the vertical axis and price on the
horizontal.

A **v2 pool** is a horizontal line: `L` is the same everywhere from zero to infinity. A **v3
position** is a rectangle: height `L` between `Pa` and `Pb`, and exactly zero
outside.<Cite src="rareskills-v3-concentrated-liquidity" /> Not a curve, not a bell, not a decaying
tail — a bar with vertical sides.

Three consequences follow immediately, and each one is a thing people get wrong.

**The pool's depth is the sum of the bars, so it is a histogram over price.** Two overlapping
positions add where they overlap. A pool with hundreds of positions has a jagged profile that can be
enormously deep at the current price and thin two per cent away.<Cite src="mixbytes-v3-ticks" /> The
single TVL figure an aggregator publishes is the *area* under that histogram, and area tells you
nothing about the height at the price you are about to trade at.

**Depth changes discontinuously, and only at bar edges.** Inside a bar the pool behaves exactly like
a constant-product pool with that `L`. At an edge, `L` jumps. This is why the fourth lesson can
describe the whole protocol as a sequence of v2 segments.

**A position's `L` is constant while the price is inside it, and its token *composition* is not.**
This pairing is the whole point of the parameterisation. One number that does not move, plus a
price, generates both amounts:<Cite src="elsts-v3-liquidity-math" />

```text
x = L * ( 1/sqrt(P) - 1/sqrt(Pb) )
y = L * ( sqrt(P)   - sqrt(Pa)   )
```

Store `L` and the two bounds and you are done. Store `x` and `y` and you would have to rewrite both
on every swap.

## Solving for L from a deposit

An LP does not choose `L`; they choose a range and hand over tokens. Getting from one to the other is
where the interesting case analysis lives.

There are three cases, and they follow from the three positions the price can occupy relative to the
range.<Cite src="elsts-v3-liquidity-math" />

The in-range rule is worth deriving rather than accepting, because "the smaller one binds" sounds
arbitrary until you see the geometry.

**Subgoal 1 — split the range at the current price.** Think of `(P, Pb)` as the sub-range where
token0 supplies liquidity, and `(Pa, P)` as the sub-range where token1 does. Each sub-range is a
single-asset range, so each is covered by one of the two closed-form cases
above.<Cite src="elsts-v3-liquidity-math" />

**Subgoal 2 — require the two halves to agree.** A position has one `L`; it cannot be 450 above the
price and 1,000 below it. So the honoured `L` is whichever the *scarcer* deposit can support, and the
surplus of the other token is simply not used.

**Subgoal 3 — read that back off the reference implementation.** `getLiquidityForAmounts` does
precisely this. When the current price is inside the range it computes `liquidity0` from `amount0`
over `(P, Pb)`, computes `liquidity1` from `amount1` over `(Pa, P)`, and returns
whichever is smaller.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-periphery-liquidityamounts" /> When the price is at or
below the lower bound it uses `amount0` alone; at or above the upper bound, `amount1`
alone.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-periphery-liquidityamounts" />

### Worked: an LP with the wrong ratio

Keep the pool from the first lesson: `P = 2025`, `Pa = 1600`, `Pb = 2500`, so `sqrt(P) = 45`,
`sqrt(Pa) = 40`, `sqrt(Pb) = 50`. The LP brings **1 ETH and 5,000 USDC**, with token0 = ETH and
token1 = USDC.

**Subgoal 1 — the `L` that the ETH can support**, over the sub-range from `P` to `Pb`:

```text
L0 = x * ( sqrt(P) * sqrt(Pb) ) / ( sqrt(Pb) - sqrt(P) )
   = 1 * ( 45 * 50 ) / ( 50 - 45 )
   = 2250 / 5
   = 450
```

**Subgoal 2 — the `L` that the USDC can support**, over the sub-range from `Pa` to `P`:

```text
L1 = y / ( sqrt(P) - sqrt(Pa) ) = 5000 / (45 - 40) = 1000
```

**Subgoal 3 — take the minimum, and see what it costs.** `L = min(450, 1000) = 450`. The ETH binds.
Recomputing the amounts at `L = 450` gives 1 ETH and 2,250 USDC — so **2,750 USDC is left over** and
must be refunded by the periphery contract or it sits in the router.

**Subgoal 4 — state the general lesson.** The ratio of the two tokens a position needs is fixed by
the range and the current price, and it is not the ratio you happen to hold. Ratio comes from
dividing the two amount formulas, and note that `L` cancels:

```text
y / x  =  ( sqrt(P) - sqrt(Pa) ) / ( 1/sqrt(P) - 1/sqrt(Pb) )
```

For this range at this price that ratio is `5 / (1/450)` per unit `L`, or 2,250 USDC per ETH. **Every
position in this range needs USDC and ETH in exactly that proportion, whatever its size**, and the
proportion drifts as the price moves through the range — which is why a "rebalance" is a real
operation with real cost and not a UI refresh.

## Same L, different money

Back to the LP from the opening, with the arithmetic that resolves it.

**Position A** — range 1,600 to 2,500, `L = 450`. Amounts at `P = 2025`: 1 ETH and 2,250 USDC, worth
`2025 + 2250 = 4275` USDC.

**Position B** — same `L = 450`, but a much narrower range: 1,936 to 2,116, chosen because
`sqrt(1936) = 44` and `sqrt(2116) = 46`.

```text
x = 450 * ( 1/45 - 1/46 ) = 450 / 2070 = 0.2174 ETH
y = 450 * ( 45   - 44   ) = 450        USDC
value = 0.2174 * 2025 + 450 = 890 USDC
```

**Identical `L`. Identical depth at the current price. One-fifth the capital.** That is not a
loophole; it is the entire product. The narrow position buys the same depth for a fifth of the money
and, in exchange, leaves the range on a nine per cent move instead of a twenty-three per cent one,
after which it earns nothing until the price comes back.

And this is where the payoff for holding `L` correctly appears. The pool tracks
`feeGrowthGlobal0X128` and `feeGrowthGlobal1X128`, described in the whitepaper as the total fees
earned **per unit of virtual liquidity** over the contract's entire history, stored as unsigned
128x128 fixed point.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" /><Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-pool" /> Fees are
distributed in proportion to `L` while in range — **not in proportion to capital**. So A and B earn
at the same rate while both are in range, on a fifth of the money for B.

That sentence is the whole economic argument for concentrated liquidity, and its second half is the
whole risk argument: B is in range far less of the time, and when it exits it holds one token at the
end of a traversal. What you are choosing when you pick a range is how much of your fee income you
are willing to bet on realised volatility staying below a threshold.

A pool has `P = 2025` (so `sqrt(P) = 45`), and two LPs are already in it:

- **C**: range 1,600 to 2,500, `L = 450`.
- **D**: range 1,936 to 2,500, `L = 300`. (`sqrt(1936) = 44`.)

A third LP, **E**, arrives with 3 ETH and no USDC at all, and wants the range 2,116 to 2,500
(`sqrt(2116) = 46`).

1. What is the pool's active `liquidity` before E arrives, and what will it be after?
2. What `L` does E get, and is any of the 3 ETH refunded?
3. The price now rises to 2,116. What happens to the active `liquidity` at that moment, and what does
   E hold?

<Answer>
**1 — active liquidity.** "Active" means the sum of `L` over positions whose range contains the
current price. At 2,025 both C and D are in range, so active `L` is `450 + 300 = 750`. E's range
starts at 2,116, which is *above* the current price, so E's position is out of range on the upper
side and contributes nothing. **Active liquidity is 750 before and 750 after.** E's `L` is recorded
at the tick boundaries and applied only when the price crosses into the range.

**2 — E's liquidity, and the refund.** Because `P` is at or below `Pa`, the position is entirely in
token0 and only the single-asset formula applies — no minimum is taken, because there is no second
token to compete:<Cite src="uniswap-v3-periphery-liquidityamounts" />

```text
L = x * ( sqrt(Pa)*sqrt(Pb) ) / ( sqrt(Pb) - sqrt(Pa) )
  = 3 * ( 46 * 50 ) / ( 50 - 46 )
  = 3 * 2300 / 4
  = 1725
```

**Nothing is refunded**, and no USDC was ever needed. This is the mechanism behind a range order: a
position placed entirely above the price is a standing offer to sell token0 into the range, and it
converts to token1 as the price passes through.

**3 — at `P = 2116`.** Three things happen at once, and the order matters.

- E's lower tick is crossed going up, so E's 1,725 is **added** to active liquidity.
- D's lower bound was 1,936, below the current price, so D stays in range. C stays in range too.
- Active liquidity becomes `450 + 300 + 1725 = 2475`, a jump of more than three times, at a single
  tick.

E now holds, at `sqrt(P) = 46` exactly at its own lower bound, still 3 ETH and no USDC — the position
has only just become active and no trading has happened inside it yet.

**The instructive part** is the size of that jump. A swapper pushing the price from 2,115 to 2,120
experiences one price impact below 2,116 and a completely different one above it, and no continuous
model of the pool predicts the change. **Depth is a step function, and the steps are wherever LPs put
their bounds.** This is exactly why a quote fetched a block ago can be wrong in a direction that no
slippage estimate derived from "the pool's liquidity" would anticipate.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Two implementation facts worth carrying

**`L` is a `uint128`, while the square-root price is a `uint160`.**<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-pool" />
Different widths for different jobs: the price needs its 64 integer bits plus 96 fractional bits to
span twelve orders of magnitude, whereas `L` is a plain unsigned quantity in `sqrt(token0*token1)`
units and 128 bits is comfortably enough. When you write code that touches both, the casts are not
interchangeable and the compiler will not always tell you.

**Every conversion between `L` and token amounts has a rounding direction, chosen deliberately.**
`getAmount0Delta` and `getAmount1Delta` each take an explicit `roundUp`
boolean.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-sqrtpricemath" /> The invariant the choices maintain is the one you
met on the security track as the [rounding-direction invariant](/c/rounding-direction-invariant):
amounts owed *to* the pool round up, amounts owed *by* it round down, so repetition can never mint
value out of arithmetic. Recomputing in floating point rounds to nearest instead, which is why your
numbers will disagree with the contract's by a few wei in a consistent
direction.<Cite src="elsts-v3-liquidity-math" />

Treat a persistent one-directional discrepancy as confirmation you have the formula right, and treat
a discrepancy that changes sign as a genuine bug.

## What this unlocks

You now have the compression that makes v3 tractable: **one number and two bounds fully determine a
position at every price, and those numbers add across positions.**

- **The next lesson** puts many bars on the same axis and walks a swap across them. Everything it
  describes — the swap loop, the tick bitmap, the net-liquidity deltas at ticks — is bookkeeping over
  the sums you just learned to take.
- **Fee accounting** is the same trick applied to a second quantity: fees per unit of `L`, tracked
  globally and differenced per position, so nobody has to iterate over
  positions.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" /> Once you see that both depth and fees are
  "per unit `L`, accumulated globally, differenced locally", the contract stops having surprises in
  it.
- **The v4 module** carries all of this forward unchanged. v4's concentrated-liquidity maths is the
  same maths; what changes is where the pool lives and who gets to run code inside a
  swap.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-math-primer-2" />

If you want one exercise: take a real position from a block explorer, read its `liquidity` and its
two ticks, and reconstruct both token amounts at the pool's current `sqrtPriceX96`. When your numbers
match the position manager's to within a couple of wei, you are holding `L` correctly — and the wei
that remain are the pool's, on purpose.
