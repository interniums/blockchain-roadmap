You call `slot0()` on a live ETH/USDC pool and the first return value is
`1760625833650318613189865563022222`. Thirty-four digits. The pool is trading at about two thousand
dollars an ETH, and there is no arrangement of a decimal point that turns one of those numbers into
the other.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to derive that integer by hand, and — more usefully — say
why every one of its design choices was forced. The square root is not an optimisation. The 96 is not
arbitrary. The 1.0001 is not a round number someone liked. Each falls out of a constraint you already
know from the EVM track, and the constraint is always the same one: **there is no floating point, and
there never will be.**

## The constraint, restated precisely

The EVM has 256-bit integer arithmetic and nothing else. There is no float type, no exponent field,
no hardware square root. Anything with a fractional part has to be a fixed-point integer — a plain
integer carrying an implied scaling factor that lives in your head and in the variable's name.

For a price, that is immediately awkward. Prices are multiplicative. Two per cent up then two per
cent down is not where you started, and a price of `0.000001` and a price of `1000000` want wildly
different absolute precision. A single fixed-point scale that gives you enough resolution at the
bottom overflows at the top.

v3 solves this with two representations that coexist, and the split confuses people because it looks
redundant:

- **A tick index** — a small signed integer, used wherever a price needs to be a *label*: position
  bounds, the tick bitmap, oracle accumulators.
- **A square-root price in Q64.96** — a large unsigned integer, used wherever a price needs to
  participate in *arithmetic*: swap steps, amount computations.

They are the same quantity in two encodings, and the two encodings exist because indexing and
computing want opposite things.

## The tick: making multiplication into addition

The definition is one line. Conceptually there is a tick at every price that is an integer power of
1.0001, and identifying ticks by an integer index `i`, the price at that tick is
`p(i) = 1.0001^i`.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" />

**Subgoal 1 — check what one tick is worth.** `1.0001^1 = 1.0001`, so consecutive ticks differ by
one hundredth of a percent. The whitepaper states the design property directly: each tick is a
0.01 per cent — one basis point — price movement away from each of its
neighbours.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" /> A basis point is the natural quantum for a market
price, which is why the base is 1.0001 and not 2 or 1.01.

**Subgoal 2 — notice what the index actually is.** Take logs of the definition:
`i = log(P) / log(1.0001)`. **The tick index is the logarithm of price.** That single fact is what
makes the whole scheme work, because logarithms convert multiplication into addition. A price that
doubles adds a fixed number of ticks *wherever it started*:
`log(2)/log(1.0001) = 6931.8`, so a doubling is about 6,932 ticks from 20 dollars and about 6,932
ticks from 20,000 dollars.

That is the property an integer index has to have. Absolute price gaps between adjacent ticks are
useless as labels because they vary by twelve orders of magnitude across the supported range.
Relative gaps are constant, and a log index is exactly the encoding under which relative becomes
absolute.

### Where the range limits come from

`TickMath` declares `MIN_TICK = -887272` and `MAX_TICK = -MIN_TICK`, and its comment says where the
number came from: computed from log base 1.0001 of `2**-128`.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-tickmath" />
That is a derivation you can run.

**Subgoal 1 — decide the price range to support.** The library's own header states the target: it
supports prices between `2**-128` and `2**128`.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-tickmath" /> That is a
symmetric range in log space, which it has to be, since swapping which token you call token0 inverts
the price.

**Subgoal 2 — convert the bound into a tick index.**

```text
log_1.0001(2^-128) = -128 * ln(2) / ln(1.0001)
                   = -128 * 0.6931472 / 0.000099995
                   = -887272.75...
```

**Subgoal 3 — take the integer part, and notice which way.** Truncating toward zero gives
`-887272`, not `-887273`. Rounding the other way would put the minimum tick's price *below* `2**-128`
and outside the range the fixed-point encoding can hold. The direction of that single rounding is a
safety property, not a style choice.

**Subgoal 4 — check the type.** A tick fits in `int24`, and the pool's `Slot0` struct stores it as
one.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-pool" /> `int24` spans roughly plus or minus 8.39 million, so
887,272 leaves plenty of headroom while packing into `slot0` alongside the price and the oracle
counters. The whitepaper makes the same point about the oracle accumulator: storing the tick index
rather than the price lets it detect price movements of one tick while staying
small.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" />

## Why the square root, and not the price

Now the choice that looks like premature optimisation and is not.

Recall from the previous lesson that a position's token amounts are:<Cite src="elsts-v3-liquidity-math" />

```text
x = L * ( 1/sqrt(P) - 1/sqrt(Pb) )
y = L * ( sqrt(P)   - sqrt(Pa)   )
```

and that the virtual reserves are `x = L / sqrt(P)` and `y = L *
sqrt(P)`.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" /> Look at what appears in those expressions. Not `P`.
Only `sqrt(P)` and `1/sqrt(P)`, each to the first power.

The whitepaper's swap-step formulas are the same shape. The change in token1 for a step is
`Δy = Δsqrt(P) * L`, and the change in token0 is `Δx = Δ(1/sqrt(P)) * L`; the procedure is to compute
the new square-root price first and then the amount from
it.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" /><Cite src="uniswap-v3-math-primer-2" />

**Every relationship the contract needs is linear in `sqrt(P)`.** So:

- **If you stored `P`**, every swap step would need a square root to get `sqrt(P)`, and an integer
  square root over 256-bit values is an iterative routine — expensive, and a source of rounding error
  at every step.
- **If you store `sqrt(P)`**, the same steps are multiplications, divisions and subtractions on
  integers. No roots at runtime. Recovering `P` is a squaring, which is cheap, and is needed far less
  often than people assume — mostly at the display layer.

The whitepaper says as much when it explains why the pool tracks `L` and the square-root price
instead of reserves: using them is convenient because only one of them changes at a time — the price
changes when swapping within a tick, and liquidity changes when crossing a tick or minting and
burning.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" />

There is a second consequence, and it is why ticks are also defined in root space. The whitepaper
notes that for technical reasons pools actually track ticks at every square root price that is an
integer power of the square root of 1.0001, giving `sqrt(p(i)) = 1.0001^(i/2)`.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" />
The tick ladder is uniform in log space either way — halving every exponent is still a uniform
ladder — so nothing about the one-basis-point property is lost.

## Q64.96, and why 96

`sqrtPriceX96` is `sqrt(P)` multiplied by `2^96` and truncated to an integer, held in a `uint160`.
`TickMath.getSqrtRatioAtTick` is documented as calculating `sqrt(1.0001^tick) * 2^96` and returning a
Q64.96 fixed-point number.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-tickmath" />

The name says the layout: 64 bits of integer part, 96 bits of fraction, 160 bits total. Here is why
each half is that size.

**Subgoal 1 — size the integer part from the price range.** Prices run to `2^128`, so square-root
prices run to `2^64`. Sixty-four bits of integer part is therefore exactly enough, and one fewer
would not be.

**Subgoal 2 — size the fraction from the smallest price.** Prices go down to `2^-128`, so
square-root prices go down to `2^-64`. To represent `2^-64` as a non-zero integer you need at least
64 fractional bits. Ninety-six gives 32 bits of headroom below the smallest supported price, which is
what stops the *relative* precision from collapsing at the bottom of the range.

**Subgoal 3 — check the total.** 64 plus 96 is 160, which is `uint160` — the same width as an
address, and a type the EVM already handles naturally.

**Subgoal 4 — verify against the two constants the library publishes.** This is the satisfying part,
because the prediction is exact. `MIN_SQRT_RATIO` is documented as
`getSqrtRatioAtTick(MIN_TICK)` and equals `4295128739`; `MAX_SQRT_RATIO` equals
`1461446703485210103287273052203988822378723970342`.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-tickmath" />

```text
predicted min = sqrt(2^-128) * 2^96 = 2^-64 * 2^96 = 2^32 = 4294967296
actual    min =                                            4295128739
ratio                                                    = 1.0000376

predicted max = sqrt(2^128)  * 2^96 = 2^64  * 2^96 = 2^160
              = 1461501637330902918203684832716283019655932542976
actual    max = 1461446703485210103287273052203988822378723970342
ratio                                                    = 0.9999624
```

Both land within four parts in a hundred thousand of the top and bottom of a `uint160`, and the gap
is not noise — **it is the tick truncation from earlier, propagated**. The true bound was tick
-887272.75; the library uses -887272, which is 0.75 of a tick *inside* the range. In root space that
is `1.0001^(0.7518/2) = 1.0000376`. The min ratio is high by that factor and the max ratio is low by
its reciprocal, exactly as observed.

If you want one sentence for why the encoding is trustworthy: the maximum square-root price the
protocol admits is, by construction, one truncated tick below the largest value a `uint160` can hold.

<Aside kind="note">
Notice the discipline this shows. Two constants that most codebases would carry as magic hex are
each derivable from one stated design range, and the small deviation between prediction and constant
is itself explained by a rounding decision made three definitions earlier. When you are auditing
fixed-point code, this is the property to look for: **can the constants be re-derived, and does the
error term have a name?**
</Aside>

## Converting a real price, decimals and all

Time to produce the thirty-four-digit number from the opening.

**Subgoal 1 — get the raw price, which is not the human price.** The pool knows nothing about
decimals. Its price is `amount1 / amount0` in *raw integer units*. Take a pool where token0 is USDC
with 6 decimals and token1 is WETH with 18 decimals, trading at 2,025 USDC per ETH:

```text
one ETH   = 1e18            raw token1
2025 USDC = 2025 * 1e6      raw token0

P_raw = 1e18 / (2025 * 1e6) = 1e12 / 2025 = 493827160.4938...
```

The raw price is 493 million, for a pool a human would describe as trading at two thousand. That
factor of `10^(decimals1 - decimals0)` is the single most common source of "my tick is wrong by about
276,324" — which is, not coincidentally, `log_1.0001(10^12)`.

**Subgoal 2 — take the square root.** `sqrt(1e12/2025) = 1e6/45 = 22222.2222...`, exact to as many
places as you like, because 2025 is 45 squared.

**Subgoal 3 — scale by `2^96`.**

```text
2^96 = 79228162514264337593543950336

sqrtPriceX96 = 22222.2222... * 79228162514264337593543950336
             = 1760625833650318613189865563022222.22...
             -> 1760625833650318613189865563022222   (truncated)
```

There is the opening number, and it occupies 111 bits — comfortably inside the 160 available.

**Subgoal 4 — get the tick, and see it disagree.**

```text
tick = ln(493827160.4938) / ln(1.0001) = 200186.97
     -> 200186   (truncated toward negative infinity)
```

Feed 200186 back through `1.0001^i` and you get 493,779,261 rather than 493,827,160 — low by
0.97 basis points. **The tick is a coarser representation than the square-root price, by design**,
which is exactly why the pool stores both. The tick tells you which segment you are in; the
square-root price tells you where in it.

**Subgoal 5 — snap to the tick spacing.** If this is the 0.05 per cent pool, tick spacing is 10 and
the nearest initialisable tick at or below is 200180. A position's bounds must be multiples of the
spacing; the *current price* is not so constrained and sits wherever the last swap left it.

An integration you inherited compares pool prices to an off-chain oracle. It reads `sqrtPriceX96`,
computes `price = (sqrtPriceX96 / 2**96) ** 2` in Python, and compares to the oracle's USD price for

price is out by a factor of about a trillion, and for another it reports a discrepancy of about

Diagnose both, and say which one is a bug.

<Answer>
**The factor of a trillion is the decimals bug.** `(sqrtPriceX96 / 2**96)**2` recovers `P_raw`,
the ratio of raw integer amounts, not the human price. To get a human price you must multiply by
`10 ** (decimals0 - decimals1)`. For a 6-decimal token paired with an 18-decimal token that is
`10^-12` or `10^12` depending on the sort order — a factor of a trillion, which is what the code
reported. The pool that "worked" almost certainly pairs two 18-decimal tokens, where the correction
is `10^0` and the omission is invisible. **This is a real bug**, and one that hides in exactly the
pools most people test against first.

**The persistent 0.5 basis points is not a bug.** Three separate truncations are at work and all of
them are deliberate:

1. `sqrtPriceX96` is the *truncated* integer part of `sqrt(P) * 2^96`, so recovering `P` from it is
   low by up to one unit in the last place.
2. The pool's price only moves to values reachable by its own swap arithmetic, which computes each
   step with a chosen rounding direction rather than to nearest — `getAmount0Delta` and
   `getAmount1Delta` both take an explicit `roundUp` argument, and the call site picks
   it.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-sqrtpricemath" />
3. Your Python is doing binary floating point, which rounds to nearest — a *different* policy from
   the contract's, so the two disagree in a consistent direction rather than randomly.

The tell is the phrase "never goes away". A rounding disagreement is stable and one-directional; a
real pricing bug moves with the market. If you need exactness, do the comparison in integers against
the same libraries the contract uses<Cite src="uniswap-v3-periphery-liquidityamounts" /> rather than
recomputing in floating point and setting a tolerance.

**The general rule:** when your number differs from the chain's, first ask whether the difference has
the shape of a scale error (orders of magnitude, constant) or a rounding error (last few digits,
one-directional). The two have completely different fixes and the second is usually not a fix at all.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Tick spacing: why you cannot use every tick

Not every tick can be initialised. The pool carries a `tickSpacing` parameter, and only ticks whose
index is divisible by it may be used as position bounds. Small spacings allow tighter ranges but make
swaps more gas-intensive, because each initialised tick a swap crosses imposes a cost on the
swapper.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" />

Spacing is bound to the fee tier at the factory. The deployed `UniswapV3Factory` constructor enables
exactly three in its `feeAmountTickSpacing` map: fee 500 with spacing 10, fee 3000 with spacing 60,
and fee 10000 with spacing 200.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-factory" /> The whitepaper describes those
as approximately 0.10 per cent, 0.60 per cent and 2.02 per cent between initialisable
ticks<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" />, and you can confirm each from the tick definition alone:

The pairing is a deliberate coupling of two costs. A one per cent fee tier exists for volatile or
illiquid pairs, where nobody wants a range narrower than two per cent anyway — so the spacing is
coarse and swaps cross few initialised ticks. A five basis point tier exists for stable pairs where
ranges are tight, and there the fine spacing is worth paying for. **Fee tier and tick spacing are one
decision, not two.**

### The gas consequence, stated carefully

Here is the part that changes how you think about swap cost. Crossing an initialised tick is the only
moment the pool's active liquidity changes; the whitepaper states that whenever the price crosses an
initialised tick, virtual liquidity is kicked in or out, and — importantly — that the gas cost of an
initialised tick crossing is constant and does not depend on how many positions are kicked in or out
at that tick.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" />

So swap gas is driven by **how far the price moves**, not by notional size. A ten-million-dollar
trade in a deep stable pool may cross no initialised ticks and cost about what a hundred-dollar trade
costs. A small trade in a thin pool whose liquidity is scattered across dozens of narrow positions
can cross many and cost several times more.

Finding the next initialised tick is made cheap by a packed bitmap: `TickBitmap` stores one bit per
usable tick, keyed by `int16` because ticks are `int24` and there are 256 values per
word.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-tickbitmap" /> Each word therefore answers "is there an initialised
tick in the next 256 spacings?" in a single storage read.

That is as far as this lesson will go on cost, deliberately. Quoting a gas figure per tick crossing
would mean quoting a number that depends on which slots are already warm within the transaction. What
is safe to say is the shape: each crossing touches at least one storage slot that the transaction has
not touched before, and the first touch of a slot costs 2,100 gas against 100 for a warm one on the
configuration this curriculum measures against.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /> Combine that with
[cold versus warm access](/c/cold-vs-warm-access) and the pattern is clear: the *first* swap through a
region of the tick space is the expensive one, and a router splitting a large order into several
transactions pays that cost again each time.

## What this unlocks

You can now read a pool's state and reconstruct its price by hand, in both encodings, including the
decimals correction that breaks most first attempts.

- **`L` next.** The third lesson defines the liquidity parameter properly and shows that with
  `sqrt(P)` in hand, a position collapses to a single number plus two bounds — a bar, not a curve.
- **The swap loop after that.** The fourth lesson walks the price across ticks and shows that the
  whole of v3 is the previous lesson's curve, re-parameterised by this lesson's integers, restarted
  at every initialised tick.
- **Oracles.** The tick-index accumulator you met in passing here is what makes a v3 TWAP a
  *geometric* mean rather than an arithmetic one, because averaging a logarithm and exponentiating is
  a geometric mean by construction.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" /> If you took the security
  track, that connects directly to [oracle manipulation](/c/oracle-manipulation) — the cost of moving
  a TWAP is the cost of holding the price away from equilibrium across the whole window, and the
  window is measured in ticks-seconds rather than dollars.

The concrete exercise worth doing before the next lesson: pick any v3 pool, read `slot0`, and
reproduce both the tick and the square-root price from the token decimals and a price you get from an
exchange. When the two agree to within a tick, the representation has stopped being a wall of digits.
