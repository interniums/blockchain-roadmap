An LP deposits 1 ETH and 2,250 USDC into a narrow range. Two days later the position shows 4,500
USDC and no ETH. Nothing was liquidated, no keeper touched it, and the LP never approved a sale. The
tokens were traded away one swap at a time by strangers, and the position let it happen because that
is what it was built to do.

You already know an AMM sells the appreciating asset. What this lesson answers is why the arithmetic
that produced 4,500 is the *same* arithmetic as `x * y = k`, applied to a pair of numbers the
contract does not hold and never held.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" /> Build that pair once and
the rest of the module is substitution rather than new ideas.<Cite src="rareskills-v3-concentrated-liquidity" />

## The waste that concentrated liquidity is a response to

Reserves `x` and `y` sit on the curve `x * y = k` and every trade slides along
it.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> You have [constant product](/c/constant-product) and
[price impact](/c/price-impact) already; what matters here is the *domain*.

The curve is defined on the open interval from zero to infinity. The v3 whitepaper names the
consequence directly: earlier versions were designed to provide liquidity across the entire price
range, which is simple to implement and allows liquidity to be efficiently aggregated, but means that
much of the assets held in a pool are never touched.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" /> For an
ETH/USDC pool to serve a trade at 20 USDC per ETH it must hold reserves in that ratio at that moment,
which it will only do if the market actually goes there. Until then those reserves are parked against
a price nobody trades at.

The response is one sentence in the whitepaper, and it is the whole design: it seems reasonable to
allow LPs to concentrate their liquidity to smaller price ranges, and liquidity concentrated to a
finite range is called a **position**.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" /> The interesting half is
the next sentence — a position only needs to maintain enough reserves to support trading within its
range, and therefore can act like a constant product pool with larger reserves, **the virtual
reserves**, within that range.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" />

That is not describing a new kind of market maker. It is describing an *impersonation*: within its
range, the position behaves as if it were a bigger constant-product pool than it is.

## Deriving the impersonation

The trick is a translation of the curve, and the two offsets that fall out are the entire content of
the concept. Fix a position with a lower price bound `Pa`, an upper bound `Pb`, and a liquidity parameter `L`
whose definition arrives properly in the third lesson. For now take two facts from the whitepaper's
own notation, both of which you can check by multiplying them together:<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" />

```text
x_virtual = L / sqrt(P)      y_virtual = L * sqrt(P)
so  x_virtual * y_virtual = L * L
```

The product is constant. This *is* `x * y = k` with `k = L^2`. Nothing new so far.

**Subgoal 1 — say what "out of range" must mean, in reserve terms.** A position serving only prices
between `Pa` and `Pb` must be empty of token X at the top of its range: if it still held X at `Pb` it
would go on selling X above `Pb`, which is the thing being forbidden. Symmetrically, empty of Y at
the bottom.

**Subgoal 2 — read off the two offsets.** Evaluate the virtual reserve of X at the upper bound:
`x_virtual(Pb) = L / sqrt(Pb)`. That is the quantity of X the impersonated pool would still hold at
`Pb`. The real position must hold zero there. So the real amount is the virtual amount minus a
constant offset, and the offset is exactly that value:

```text
x_real = x_virtual - L / sqrt(Pb)
y_real = y_virtual - L * sqrt(Pa)
```

**Subgoal 3 — substitute back and get the position's own curve.** Put those into
`x_virtual * y_virtual = L^2`:

```text
( x_real + L / sqrt(Pb) ) * ( y_real + L * sqrt(Pa) ) = L * L
```

That is equation 2.2 of the whitepaper, described there as a translation of the constant-product
formula such that the position is solvent exactly within its range.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" />
**Solvent exactly within its range** is the design goal; the translation is the mechanism.

**Subgoal 4 — notice what the offsets are made of.** Both depend only on `L` and the range
endpoints, never on the current price. For a position with fixed bounds they are constants, which is
why substituting them costs nothing at runtime.

<Aside kind="note">
Hold onto the direction: **the real reserves are the virtual reserves shifted toward the origin**, so
the real curve is the same hyperbola moved down and left until it touches both axes at the range
endpoints. Everything that looks exotic about v3 in a chart is that one move.
</Aside>

## A position, with numbers

Take an ETH/USDC pool priced in USDC per ETH, with `P = 2025`, `Pa = 1600` and `Pb = 2500`. Those
are chosen so the roots are integers — `sqrt(P) = 45`, `sqrt(Pa) = 40`, `sqrt(Pb) = 50` — and every
step checks by eye.

**Subgoal 1 — get the token amounts for a given `L`.** The in-range amounts, derived in full in the
technical note that works out the whitepaper's terse equations, are:<Cite src="elsts-v3-liquidity-math" />

```text
x = L * ( 1/sqrt(P) - 1/sqrt(Pb) )
y = L * ( sqrt(P)   - sqrt(Pa)   )
```

Both are linear in `sqrt(P)` or its reciprocal, which is the fact the next lesson is built
on.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-math-primer-2" /> Substituting: `x = L * (1/45 - 1/50) = L/450` and
`y = L * (45 - 40) = 5L`.

**Subgoal 2 — pin `L` by choosing a deposit.** Say the LP wants the position to contain exactly
1 ETH at the current price. Then `L/450 = 1`, so `L = 450`, and `y = 5 * 450 = 2250` USDC. The LP
deposits **1 ETH and 2,250 USDC** — at 2,025 USDC per ETH, a position worth 4,275 USDC.

**Subgoal 3 — compute the reserves the position is pretending to have.**

```text
x_virtual = L / sqrt(P) = 450 / 45 = 10     ETH
y_virtual = L * sqrt(P) = 450 * 45 = 20250  USDC
check: 10 * 20250 = 202500 = L * L
```

**Subgoal 4 — confirm the offsets reconcile.**

Both match the deposit.

**Subgoal 5 — price the impersonation.** A v2 pool holding 10 ETH and 20,250 USDC offers the same
depth at 2,025 and is worth `10 * 2025 + 20250 = 40500` USDC there. The position bought that depth
for 4,275, a ratio of about **9.5x** that nobody chose as a parameter — it fell out of `Pa` and `Pb`.
Widen the range toward zero and infinity and the offsets go to zero and the ratio goes to one, which
is v2 again. That is the honest framing of capital efficiency: not free leverage, but the price of
giving up every trade outside your bounds.

The 9 ETH and 18,000 USDC in the offsets are *not in the contract*. Nobody deposited them, nobody can
withdraw them, and no accounting entry refers to them. They exist so one formula can be reused.

## Where the price walks out

Return to the opening scene: the same position after the price rises past 2,500. At `P >= Pb` the
position holds no X and its Y amount is fixed:<Cite src="elsts-v3-liquidity-math" />

```text
y = L * ( sqrt(Pb) - sqrt(Pa) ) = 450 * (50 - 40) = 4500 USDC
x = 0
```

There is the 4,500 from the first paragraph. The single ETH was sold as the price crossed from 2,025
to 2,500, for `450 * (50 - 45) = 2250` USDC on top of the 2,250 already there — an average sale price
of 2,250, neither end of the range nor the mean of the two.

Push it one step: traversed from `Pa` all the way to `Pb`, the position sells
`450 * (1/40 - 1/50) = 2.25` ETH for 4,500 USDC, an average of exactly **2,000, the geometric mean of
1,600 and 2,500**. That is general, and it falls out of the amount formulas: divide
`L * (sqrt(Pb) - sqrt(Pa))` by `L * (1/sqrt(Pa) - 1/sqrt(Pb))`, the `L` cancels, and what is left is
`sqrt(Pa) * sqrt(Pb)`.

Downward is the mirror. At `P <= Pa` the position holds 2.25 ETH and no USDC — 3,600 USDC of value at
1,600, against 3,850 for having held the original deposit. The 250 USDC gap is
[impermanent loss](/c/impermanent-loss) concentrated, and Uniswap's own v2 documentation frames it
the same way: divergence is measured against holding, and fee income is a separate term that may or
may not cover it.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-understanding-returns" />

**A position pushed out of range holds exactly one of the two tokens because its curve segment has
been fully traversed.** Not a liquidation, not a stop-loss, not a bug: the endpoint of a curve that
was deliberately given endpoints.

  belief="An out-of-range position is broken, or has been closed by someone."

  why="Every other instrument that converts fully to cash has been terminated. This one has run to the end of its own price axis, and the whitepaper is explicit that if the price reenters the range the liquidity becomes active again."

## What the contract actually stores

One correction before you carry the model forward. The pool **does not store virtual reserves**. The whitepaper says so: the contract could be thought
of as having virtual reserves describing its behaviour between two adjacent ticks as if it followed
the constant product formula, but instead of tracking them the pool tracks two different values,
liquidity `L` and the square root of the price.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" /> The live contract
bears it out — `slot0` holds a `sqrtPriceX96` and a tick, `liquidity` is a separate top-level
`uint128`, and there is no `reserve0` or `reserve1`.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-pool" /><Cite src="mixbytes-v3-ticks" />

So virtual reserves are a **derivation device**, not a state variable. You use them to get from
`x * y = k` to the position formulas, and then you put them away.

Second caveat: everything above describes *one* position. A pool has many, overlapping and disjoint,
and what it tracks is the **sum of `L` over positions currently in range**, which changes only at the
tick boundaries where positions begin and end.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-pool" /> Between two
boundaries the pool is one constant-product segment. The fourth lesson is that step up.

<Misconception
  belief="Because a position is a constant-product pool in disguise, its depth is a single number like a v2 pool's."
  reality="Depth is a function of price. A pool can be extremely deep at the current price and effectively empty two percent away, depending on where LPs placed their bounds."
  why="Aggregators publish one TVL figure per pool, a sum over positions that says nothing about their distribution — and the distribution, the liquidity histogram over ticks, is what determines execution."
  src="mixbytes-v3-ticks"
/>

One consequence to expect before you meet it: recomputing these amounts off-chain will disagree with
the contract by a few wei. The core conversion library takes an explicit `roundUp` boolean on both
`getAmount0Delta` and `getAmount1Delta` and every call site picks a
direction,<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-sqrtpricemath" /> and the periphery helpers most integrations
call are built on those.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-periphery-liquidityamounts" /> Floating point rounds to
nearest, which is a different policy. The third lesson returns to it once `L` is defined.

<Check type="predict" concept="virtual-reserves">
Two LPs open positions on the same ETH/USDC pool at 2,025 USDC per ETH. **A** picks 1,600 to 2,500
and deposits 1 ETH plus 2,250 USDC — the position worked above, `L = 450`. **B** picks 400 to 10,000
and deposits so that they also hold exactly 1 ETH now. Show the arithmetic for:

1. B's `L`, and how much USDC B deposited.
2. Which position offers more depth at 2,025, and by how much.
3. What each holds after the price falls to 1,500.

<Answer>
**1 — B's liquidity and deposit.** `sqrt(400) = 20` and `sqrt(10000) = 100`. Using
`x = L * (1/sqrt(P) - 1/sqrt(Pb))` with `sqrt(P) = 45`:

```text
1 = L * (1/45 - 1/100) = L * (100 - 45)/4500 = L * 55/4500
L = 4500/55 = 81.81...
```

Then `y = 81.81 * (45 - 20) = 2045.45` USDC. B deposits 1 ETH and about 2,045 USDC — nearly the same
money as A, for an `L` of about 82 against A's 450.

**2 — depth at the current price.** Depth *is* `L`: the response to a small trade is governed by the
active `L` and nothing else. A's 450 against B's 82 is roughly **5.5 times the depth for about the
same capital**, bought with B's extra width — and the comparison holds only while both are in range.

**3 — at 1,500 USDC per ETH.** A is *below* `Pa = 1600`, so A is fully in ETH:
`x = 450 * (1/40 - 1/50) = 2.25` ETH and no USDC. B is still *in range*, since 1,500 sits between 400
and 10,000, and with `sqrt(1500) = 38.7298` holds
`x = 81.81 * (1/38.7298 - 1/100) = 1.294` ETH and `y = 81.81 * (38.7298 - 20) = 1532` USDC.

**The point of the comparison.** A bought about 5.5 times the depth and earns fees at about 5.5 times
the rate *while the price stays inside 1,600 to 2,500* — and stops earning entirely once it does not.
B earns less per unit time and is still working. Neither is better; they are different bets on
realised volatility, and the design hands that choice to the LP rather than making it for them.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

You have the substitution the rest of the module runs on: **a v3 position is a v2 pool with two
constant offsets, chosen so the curve terminates on the axes at the range bounds.**

- The next lesson replaces `P` with an integer tick index and `sqrt(P)` with a fixed-point integer.
  The reason it stores the *square root* is already visible: both amount formulas above are linear in
  `sqrt(P)` and `1/sqrt(P)`, and in nothing else.
- The lesson after that defines `L` as `sqrt(x * y)` over the virtual reserves you just built, and
  shows why one number describes a position.
- The last lesson puts many positions on one price axis and shows the pool is these curves stitched
  together at ticks — which is where the swap loop, the tick bitmap and the fee accounting come
  from.<Cite src="rareskills-v3-concentrated-liquidity" />

To check the model against the chain first: read `slot0` and `liquidity` from any v3 pool, rebuild
the virtual reserves as `L / sqrt(P)` and `L * sqrt(P)`, and compare their product to
`L^2`.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-pool" /><Cite src="uniswap-v3-math-primer-2" /> If those agree you
are holding the model correctly, and only the integer representation stands between you and the real
formulas.
