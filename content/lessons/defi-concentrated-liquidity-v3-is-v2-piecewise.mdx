Open `UniswapV2Pair.sol` and the swap is a comparison. Reserves in, reserves out, check that the
product did not shrink, done — no loop, no branch on price.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" />

Open `UniswapV3Pool.sol` and the swap is a `while` loop that runs until the requested amount is
exhausted or a price limit is hit, and each pass through it fetches the next initialised tick,
computes a step, and possibly crosses.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-pool" />

That difference is the whole of v3, and it is smaller than it looks. **The loop body is v2.** Each
iteration is a constant-product swap at a fixed liquidity. The loop exists because the liquidity
changes between iterations, and it changes at exactly the prices where LPs chose to put their range
bounds.

This lesson makes that claim precise, then shows that the three parts of the codebase people find
hardest — the swap loop, the tick bitmap, the fee accounting — are consequences of it rather than
separate ideas.

## The claim, stated exactly

Between two adjacent initialised ticks, a v3 pool **is** a constant-product pool with a fixed `L`.
Nothing about its behaviour in that interval differs from v2 with virtual reserves `L / sqrt(P)` and
`L * sqrt(P)`.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" />

At an initialised tick, the pool applies the net liquidity of every position that begins or ends
there and continues on a new constant-product segment with a new `L`. The whitepaper is exact about
the mechanism: whenever the price crosses an initialised tick, virtual liquidity is kicked in or
out.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" />

So the pool is a **chain of v2 curves joined at ticks**, and everything else is the bookkeeping
needed to (a) know which link you are on, and (b) pay each LP only for the swaps that happened while
their bar covered the price.<Cite src="rareskills-v3-concentrated-liquidity" />

## The swap loop, read as the framing

Here is the loop's structure, stripped to the parts that carry the argument. The real thing is in
`UniswapV3Pool.swap`.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-pool" />

```text
while amount remaining is non-zero and price limit not reached:
    find the next initialised tick in the direction of travel
    target = min(that tick's price, the caller's price limit)
    do ONE constant-product swap step from the current price toward target,
        using the CURRENT liquidity
    charge the fee and add it to the global per-unit-liquidity accumulator
    if the step reached the target and the target was an initialised tick:
        cross it: liquidity += liquidityNet, flip the fee-growth-outside values
```

Read it as four claims.

**Claim 1 — the inner step is v2.** `SwapMath.computeSwapStep` takes a start price, a target price,
one liquidity value and an amount, and returns how far the price moved and what was
exchanged.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-pool" /> It has no notion of ticks, positions or ranges. It is
the constant-product arithmetic of the first lesson, expressed in the integers of the
second.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-sqrtpricemath" />

**Claim 2 — the loop only exists because `L` changes.** If `L` never changed, the first iteration
would always finish the swap. Every extra iteration corresponds to one boundary where somebody's
range began or ended.

**Claim 3 — the price limit is a first-class stopping condition, not slippage protection bolted on.**
The loop terminates on either exhausting the amount or reaching `sqrtPriceLimitX96`. A swap that
would move the price past the caller's limit stops early and returns a partial fill, which is a
different failure mode from a revert and one that routers have to handle explicitly.

**Claim 4 — the loop's cost is the number of segments, not the size of the trade.** Two trades of
wildly different notional cost about the same gas if they traverse the same number of segments, and
this follows from claims 1 and 2 rather than being an empirical observation.

### One honest complication

The loop does not step segment by segment in the tidy way the framing suggests. It calls
`nextInitializedTickWithinOneWord`, which searches only inside the current 256-bit word of the tick
bitmap.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-pool" /><Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-tickbitmap" /> If there is no
initialised tick in that word, it returns the word boundary anyway, and the loop takes an iteration
that crosses nothing.

This bounds each iteration's work to one storage word, which is what makes the search cheap — and it
means **iteration count is not the same as ticks crossed**. A swap through a long empty stretch still
pays one loop pass per 256 tick-spacings, so a trace will disagree with any model that counts only
crossings.

<Aside kind="note">
A shape worth recognising in on-chain search structures: the data structure is sized to the machine,
not to the problem. 256 ticks per word because a word is 256 bits; one search per word because a cold
`SLOAD` is the expensive operation. The leak into the gas profile is deliberate.
</Aside>

## Crossing a tick: two numbers, one of which is a trick

When the price crosses an initialised tick, exactly two kinds of state update happen.

**The liquidity delta.** Each tick tracks `liquidityNet` — the amount of liquidity added, or if
negative removed, when the tick is crossed going left to right. The whitepaper notes the value does
not need to be updated when the tick is crossed, only when a position with a bound there is
modified.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" /> Crossing right-to-left applies the negation. Because
positions ending and positions beginning at the same tick are netted in advance, a tick with a
hundred positions costs the same to cross as a tick with one — which is the whitepaper's stated
property that the gas cost of an initialised tick crossing does not depend on how many positions are
kicked in or out there.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" />

Netting creates one problem the design has to solve: a tick whose net is zero might still be
referenced by live positions, so it cannot be un-initialised. The pool therefore also keeps
`liquidityGross`, a gross tally whose only job is to answer "is this tick referenced at all", which
is what tells the contract whether to flip the bit in the tick bitmap.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" />

**The fee-growth flip.** This is the trick, and it is the most elegant thing in the contract.

### Deriving the fee accounting

The problem: a position must be paid for exactly the fees generated while the price was inside its
range, and the contract must compute that in constant time, without iterating positions, and without
touching a position's storage on every swap.

**Subgoal 1 — accumulate globally, per unit of liquidity.** The pool keeps `feeGrowthGlobal0X128` and
`feeGrowthGlobal1X128`: the total fees earned **per unit of virtual liquidity** over the contract's
whole history, as unsigned 128x128 fixed point. The whitepaper suggests thinking of them as what one
unit of unbounded liquidity deposited at initialisation would have
earned.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" /> A swap step adds `feeAmount / liquidity` to the relevant
accumulator, and does so **only when liquidity is non-zero**.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-pool" />

**Subgoal 2 — store, per tick, the fees that accrued on the far side.** Each initialised tick keeps
`feeGrowthOutside`, meaning the growth that happened on the other side of that tick from the current
price. "Other side" is relative to where the price is *now*, which is why it must be maintained on
crossings.

**Subgoal 3 — get above and below from one stored number.** Given the global `f_g`, the tick's stored
`f_o(i)`, and the current tick `i_c`:<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" />

```text
fees above tick i:   f_a(i) = f_g - f_o(i)   if i_c >= i,  else f_o(i)
fees below tick i:   f_b(i) = f_o(i)         if i_c >= i,  else f_g - f_o(i)
```

**Subgoal 4 — subtract the two ends.** The fees per unit of liquidity accrued *inside* a range with
bounds `i_l` and `i_u` is the global total minus what happened below the lower bound minus what
happened above the upper bound:<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" />

```text
f_r = f_g - f_b(i_l) - f_a(i_u)
```

**Subgoal 5 — make maintenance a single operation.** When a tick is crossed in either direction, its
stored value is replaced by `f_o := f_g - f_o`.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" /> One subtraction.
"Outside" swapped sides, so the complement is exactly the new value.

**Subgoal 6 — notice what this buys, and what it costs.** A position's earnings are
`L * (f_r_now - f_r_at_last_touch)`: computable on demand, and no swap ever writes to a position. The
cost is that `f_o` is meaningful only *relative to the current price*, and because a tick's `f_o` is
initialised lazily — by convention as if all fees to date had occurred below it — the whitepaper warns
that comparisons of `f_o` between ticks are not meaningful and not guaranteed
consistent.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" />

That warning is the practical takeaway for anyone building on this. **`feeGrowthOutside` is not a
quantity. It is one term of a difference**, and reading it in isolation — in an indexer, a dashboard,
a subgraph mapping — produces a number that looks plausible and means nothing.

## What the framing predicts

A model earns its keep by making predictions you would not otherwise make. This one makes four.

**Depth is a step function of price.** The liquidity histogram over ticks *is* the depth profile, and
a pool can be extremely deep at the current price and near-empty two per cent
away.<Cite src="mixbytes-v3-ticks" /> A quote computed from a single "pool liquidity" number errs by
an amount set by the distribution, not by your trade size alone. It follows that **two pools with the
same TVL are not substitutes** — TVL is area under the histogram, execution depends on height at the
price.

**Gas is a function of distance travelled in price space**, not notional. A large trade in a deep
pool may cross nothing; a small trade in a fragmented pool may cross many segments. Each crossing
touches tick storage the transaction has not touched before, and a first touch of a slot costs 2,100
gas against 100 for a warm one on the configuration this curriculum
measures<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /> — the shape of the cost, not the total, since the total
depends on what the transaction has already visited. See
[cold versus warm access](/c/cold-vs-warm-access).

**Liquidity can be supplied for one block.** If depth is the sum of active bars, anyone can add a
very large bar spanning one tick spacing immediately before a known large swap and remove it
immediately after, capturing most of that swap's fees with almost no price risk. Just-in-time
liquidity is not an exploit; it is what a step-function depth profile with permissionless
instantaneous mint and burn permits by construction. It is also one of the first things v4 hooks were
used to penalise.

## Where the framing stops being literally true

Two limits, both worth stating because carrying the model past them produces confident wrong answers.

**The contract does not evaluate a piecewise invariant.** You can write down a piecewise formula for
the whole curve, and it is a good picture — RareSkills works exactly that formula and then heads a
section with the warning that it is for illustration
only.<Cite src="rareskills-v3-concentrated-liquidity" /> The implementation iterates. It never has the
whole curve in hand, holds no per-segment reserves, and stores no aggregate `k`. What it stores is
one price, one active `L`, and the deltas needed to reconstruct the rest.

**The segments belong to the pool, not to a position.** Everything about a *position* — the bar, the
two offsets, the amount formulas — is per-position. The piecewise chain of curves is an aggregate
over all positions. It is easy to slide from one to the other and end up computing a swap against a
single LP's virtual reserves, which will be wrong by however much other liquidity is
active.<Cite src="elsts-v3-liquidity-math" />

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="v3-is-v2-piecewise">
A quoting service estimates output for a v3 swap like this:

```text
1. read slot0.sqrtPriceX96 and the pool's `liquidity`
2. treat the pool as constant product with virtual reserves
      x = liquidity / sqrtP        y = liquidity * sqrtP
3. apply the v2 output formula with the pool's fee tier
4. cache the result for 12 seconds
```

It is accurate to a basis point on the 0.05% USDC/USDT pool and badly wrong on a 1% pool for a
long-tail token, in a direction that always favours the quote. Explain both, and say what a correct
estimate has to do differently.

<Answer>
**Why the stable pool looks fine.** Step 2 is a valid model *while the price stays inside the current
segment*. In a tightly pegged pair the liquidity is piled into a few spacings around the peg with a
nearly flat profile, so a normal trade never leaves a region of near-constant `L`. The assumption
happens to hold; it was never tested.

**Why the long-tail pool is wrong.** Two independent failures compound.

1. *The active `liquidity` is only this segment's.* The 1% tier has tick spacing 200, about 2.02 per
   cent between initialisable ticks.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" /> A trade of any size in a
   thin pool crosses those boundaries, and beyond the first one the real `L` is different — usually
   smaller, because concentrated positions cluster near the price. Using the current `L` for the
   whole trade assumes depth continues that never existed.
2. *It always errs the same way.* Assuming liquidity persists beyond where it stops can only
   **overstate** output. The error cannot be negative, which is why "it is usually within a basis
   point" was never evidence of correctness — the residual is one-directional, exactly the signature
   of a modelling assumption rather than noise.

**The 12-second cache is a third bug.** Any mint or burn reshapes the histogram, and both are
permissionless and instantaneous. A cached quote is a claim about a past block's histogram — and in
the JIT case that histogram is *deliberately* different for one block.

**What a correct estimator does.** Reproduce the loop: walk the tick bitmap in the direction of
travel, take a constant-product step to each initialised tick, apply that tick's `liquidityNet`, and
continue until the amount is consumed.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-pool" /> That needs tick data, not
just `slot0`, which is why accurate v3 quoting requires an on-chain quoter that simulates or an
indexer that maintains the tick table. The absence of a closed form is a consequence of the design.

**The generalisable lesson.** When a model's error is small *and* always signed the same way, that is
not accuracy with noise. It is a systematic bias, and the sign usually names the assumption you made.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

The module's claim is now complete: **v3 is v2 with the liquidity switched on and off at ticks, plus
the accounting needed to charge and pay for that correctly.** Three payoffs.

**Reading the contract.** The bitmap, `liquidityNet` and `feeGrowthOutside` are each answers to one
question the piecewise design forces: where is the next boundary, what changes there, and who was in
range while it happened.

**Reasoning about execution.** Depth is a distribution, gas tracks distance, a stale quote is a stale
histogram. Those cover most of the surprises a router or market-making integration will meet.

**The next module.** Uniswap v4 keeps this maths entirely and changes the container: every pool
inside one contract, balances netted across a whole sequence of operations, and a per-pool hook
contract called at fixed lifecycle points. Segments and accumulators still apply. What becomes
negotiable is who runs code inside a swap — a security question rather than a mathematical one.
