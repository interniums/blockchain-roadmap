An aggregator returns two routes for the same trade. One goes through a pool you recognise. The other
is eleven basis points better and goes through a pool whose `PoolKey` has a hook address you have
never seen.

In v3 that choice was about liquidity and fee tier. Both pools ran the same bytecode; the only
question was which had better depth at your size. In v4 the second route means **an unaudited
contract, written by whoever created that pool, executes inside your transaction, with the ability to
change what your trade does.**

This lesson is about what that contract can do, why none of it is a bug, why simulation does not
settle the question, and what actually protects you — which turns out to be a short and rather
unglamorous list.

## What a hook can do to your swap

The `PoolManager` calls the hook before and after the core logic on that pool's operations:
initialise, add and remove liquidity, swap, donate.<Cite src="uniswap-hooks-docs" /><Cite src="uniswap-v4-hooks-concept-docs" />
That placement — inside the operation, in the same transaction, with control returned to it — is the
whole capability. Five concrete consequences.

**It can charge you an arbitrary additional amount.** A dynamic-fee pool accepts a per-swap fee
returned from `beforeSwap`, and the hook can also update the stored LP fee.<Cite src="uniswap-v4-dynamic-fees-docs" />
Trail of Bits' guidance to hook authors — bound every fee, limit how fast privileged changes can move
it, do not derive it from cheaply manipulable inputs<Cite src="zealynx-v4-security" /> — is guidance
precisely because none of that is enforced by the protocol.

**It can supply the trade itself.** With `beforeSwapReturnDelta` set, a `BeforeSwapDelta` that
consumes the user's entire specified amount leaves the `PoolManager` with nothing to swap; the hook
provides the trade instead. Trail of Bits calls this a NoOp swap and says to treat such a hook as a
custom AMM.<Cite src="zealynx-v4-security" /> The pool's concentrated-liquidity curve — the thing you
spent a whole module learning to reason about — is simply not consulted.

**It can revert.** Hook callbacks execute in the same transaction as the pool action, and the
`PoolManager` preserves atomicity by reverting the parent action when the hook
reverts.<Cite src="zealynx-v4-security" /> A hook can therefore refuse a swap, and can refuse it
selectively.

**It can condition on who you are.** `msg.sender`, `tx.origin`, the calldata, the block number and
its own storage are all readable inside the callback. The visibility that makes a hook attackable
when it forgets caller checks<Cite src="zealynx-v4-security" /> also makes it able to
discriminate.

**It can take value through paths that are not the swap.** `donate` has its own before and after
callbacks<Cite src="uniswap-v4-hooks-concept-docs" />, and the return-delta flags on
`afterAddLiquidity` and `afterRemoveLiquidity` let a hook move value on liquidity operations
too.<Cite src="uniswap-v4-core-hooks-library" />

**None of this is a vulnerability.** It is the extension point working as designed — the same
capability that lets a hook implement limit orders, a volatility-aware fee, an oracle or a
time-weighted market maker.<Cite src="uniswap-v4-hooks-concept-docs" /> There is no version of
"powerful hooks" that excludes "hostile hooks", any more than there is a version of "arbitrary
contract calls" that excludes malicious contracts.

## The trust boundary you already know

When you approve a token to a router, you trust the router's code with your balance, and everyone
learned to check what they approve and to whom. **Routing through a pool is the same transfer of
trust, applied to the execution of the trade rather than to the allowance** — arriving without an
approval prompt, without an explorer warning, and often without the user learning the hook's address
at all.

Trail of Bits' split is the useful model. The `PoolManager` enforces protocol mechanics: pool
initialisation rules, swap and liquidity maths, hook-callback sequencing, end-of-session settlement.
Hook developers validate the application-specific assumptions their hooks
add.<Cite src="zealynx-v4-security" /> Read that as a statement about *whose bugs you are exposed
to*: the protocol half has had concentrated scrutiny, and the application half is per-pool, written
by strangers.

### The two incidents, and what they have in common

Trail of Bits names Cork and Bunni as the app-level incidents that show what can go wrong, together
accounting for more than $20M in losses: Cork at roughly $12M in May 2025, Bunni at $8.4M in
September 2025.<Cite src="zealynx-v4-security" />

**Cork** was an access-control gap: data from an untrusted path reached hook logic affecting
redemptions, which combined with a pricing issue elsewhere gave the attacker a way to drain
funds.<Cite src="zealynx-v4-security" />

**Bunni** was a rounding bug in the hook's idle-balance accounting. The attacker pushed the pool's
price tick with a flash loan, then made 44 tiny withdrawals that each shrank the active balance
disproportionately to the shares burned.<Cite src="zealynx-v4-security" />

The common factor is the one that matters here: **neither stemmed from a flaw in the Uniswap v4 core
protocol or the `PoolManager`; both arose from application-specific authorisation and accounting
logic built around hooks.**<Cite src="zealynx-v4-security" /> And in Bunni's case every transaction
satisfied the manager's settlement invariant, because the defect was in the hook's internal
accounting rather than in the manager's.<Cite src="zealynx-v4-security" />

That is the previous lesson's warning arriving with a number attached. `CurrencyNotSettled` proves
conservation for the manager, not correctness for you.<Cite src="uniswap-v4-core-poolmanager-source" />

## Why simulating first is not enough

You already simulate before sending. It is the right habit and it does not resolve this.

**Subgoal 1 — list what a callback can read.** The hook runs as ordinary EVM code. It can read
`msg.sender`, `tx.origin`, `block.number`, `block.timestamp`, its own storage, and any external
contract's state.

**Subgoal 2 — list what differs between a simulation and the real send.** An `eth_call` is issued
from whatever sender the caller chooses, against a chosen block, with no other transactions ordered
around it. The real transaction has a fixed sender, lands in a later block, and sits somewhere in an
ordering it does not control.

**Subgoal 3 — conclude.** Every input in step one that differs in step two is a branch a hook can
take. A hook that behaves for `eth_call` from a zero-address sender at the current block and
misbehaves for your address two blocks later is not doing anything exotic; it is reading values the
EVM hands it.

**Subgoal 4 — take the honest position.** Simulation still catches accidental breakage, reverts and
gross mispricing, and forces a hostile author to write more code and leave more evidence. It does not
establish safety. Treat it the way you treat a passing test suite: evidence about the paths you
exercised.

## What actually protects you

The defences are ordinary. That is not a disappointment; it is the point. Nothing about hooks
requires a new class of protection, and reaching for a clever one instead of the boring one is how
people get hurt.

**A real minimum-output bound.** You know [slippage tolerance](/c/slippage-tolerance) as protection
against price movement. Here it is protection against the counterparty, and it binds *whatever* the
hook does: extra fee, custom delta, hostile pricing — all of them show up as less output, and a
minimum-output check on the final amount rejects all of them identically.

There is one subtlety that turns this defence off, and it is easy to walk into. **If your minimum is
computed as a percentage of a quote obtained by simulating against the same pool, the hook set both
numbers.** A hook that quotes generously and executes poorly defeats a relative bound completely. The
bound has to come from a source the hook does not control — an independent price, a different venue,
or your own valuation.

**A deadline.** Bounds the window in which a stale, favourably-quoted transaction can be executed
under different conditions. Cheap, and it costs nothing when unused.

**Knowing which pool you are routed through.** In v3, "which pool" meant a pair and a fee tier. In v4
it means all five `PoolKey` fields, and the fifth is the one that matters.<Cite src="uniswap-v4-core-poolkey" />
An integration that logs a pool as "USDC/WETH 0.05%" is now logging an ambiguous identifier.

**Reading the hook address before you trade.** Mask the low fourteen bits and you know exactly which
callbacks the manager will invoke.<Cite src="uniswap-v4-core-hooks-library" /> It is free, needs no
RPC call, and separates the pools that merely observe from the pools whose hook can move value —
bits 3 through 0, the return-delta flags. It does not tell you the hook is safe. It tells you how
much review the pool needs before you send size through it.

**Checking whether the hook is a proxy.** The address pins the callback set permanently; it does not
pin the code. If the address is a proxy, the upgrade admin, delay, storage layout and implementation
checks are part of the hook's security boundary, and immutable versioned deployments are
preferable.<Cite src="zealynx-v4-security" /> A hook you reviewed last month is not necessarily the
hook running today.

**Preferring the hookless pool for size.** A zero hook address means v3-style concentrated liquidity
under v4's accounting. When the price improvement on an unknown pool is small and the notional is
large, the trade is bad on expectation regardless of how the hook turns out.

## The LP side is worse, and the difference is time

Everything above is about a swapper, whose exposure is one transaction bounded by one minimum-output
check. A liquidity provider's exposure is different in kind, and this is the asymmetry to carry away.

An LP's capital sits inside the pool across blocks, and exiting is itself a hook-mediated operation.
Trail of Bits is direct about the failure mode: if reward distribution, dust cleanup or other
non-essential code reverts inside an `afterRemoveLiquidity` callback, **users cannot exit their
positions** — and required external reads can do the same, so a stale price feed, a paused lending
protocol or any reverting dependency can block a withdrawal.<Cite src="zealynx-v4-security" />

This does not require malice. A merely fragile hook — a reward token that gets paused, an oracle that
starts rejecting stale data, a decimal mismatch on a zero balance — produces the same outcome as one
designed to trap capital.<Cite src="zealynx-v4-security" /> The guidance to authors is to keep
non-essential code out of the main user flow, wrap optional external calls in try/catch, and provide
an exit-safe fallback<Cite src="zealynx-v4-security" /> — none of which an LP can impose after the
fact.

Size the review accordingly.

**Does not establish:** anything about behaviour.

**3 — Determine whether the hook is a proxy, and who can upgrade it.** If it is, everything you learn
from reading the code has a shelf life set by the upgrade admin and any timelock.<Cite src="zealynx-v4-security" />

**Does not establish:** that the current implementation is honest.

**4 — Read the hook's code, specifically `beforeSwap` and `afterSwap`.** Look for branches on

**Does not establish:** that it is the code that will run, unless check 3 came back clean.

**Does not establish:** behaviour for the next one — a hook can count.

**6 — Re-run the comparison honestly.** Eleven basis points on 4M is $4,400, against the cost of

any risk is priced. **The most common correct answer is to take the hookless pool** — and noticing
that is the real skill, because a sophisticated attack surface tempts people into sophisticated
defences when the available move is to decline.

**What the simulation bought.** It established that the pool is functional and not obviously broken
at the current block from the fork's sender. It did not establish that the hook will behave the same
way for the treasury's address, two blocks later, at full size — because a contract can read all
three of those.

## What this unlocks

The module's claim is complete. **v4 moved the trust boundary from the protocol to the pool**, and a
pool's hook address is where that boundary is written down.<Cite src="uniswap-v4-core-poolkey" />

Three things to carry:

- **The architecture and the risk are the same fact.** The singleton makes pools cheap to create;
  cheap creation plus a hook field in the key means the same pair has many pools with different code
  inside them. You cannot keep the composability and discard the trust
  question.<Cite src="uniswap-v4-poolmanager" />
- **Hook review is application security, not protocol security.** The checklists that apply are the
  ones from the security track — caller checks, allowlists, labelled balances, bounded fees, exit
  paths that cannot revert, fuzzing of nested callbacks and hostile
  tokens.<Cite src="zealynx-v4-security" /> Nothing in it is v4-specific except the vocabulary.
- **The user-side defence has not changed and does not need to.** A real minimum-output bound, a
  deadline, and knowing which pool you are routed through. The same three that protected you from
  sandwiching protect you from a hostile hook, because all of them terminate in "you received less
  than you demanded".

If you go on to write a hook rather than trade against one, the seven failure patterns Trail of Bits
enumerates are the place to start, and the shortest version of them is this: the `PoolManager`
guarantees its own mechanics, and every assumption your hook adds on top of those is yours to
enforce.<Cite src="zealynx-v4-security" />
