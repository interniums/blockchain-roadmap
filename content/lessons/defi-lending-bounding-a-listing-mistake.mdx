Two risk memos land on the same listing proposal.

The first argues the loan-to-value should be 0.75 rather than 0.80. It has a volatility study, a
drawdown analysis, and a comparison against three similar assets. It is careful work.

The second is four lines long and argues the supply cap should be 5 million tokens rather than 50
million. It contains no model at all.

If the listing turns out to be fine, the first memo was the useful one. If the listing turns out to be
built on something false — a bad oracle, an unbacked token, a governance capture, a bridge that can be
made to mint — the first memo changes the outcome by a few percent and the second changes it by an
order of magnitude. This lesson is about the parameters that keep working after the risk model has been
falsified, the design choice about who the loss lands on, and the person who now makes both decisions
on your behalf in the modern stack.

### Deriving the loss bound

**Subgoal 1 — define the loss precisely.** When a listing is wrong, the protocol's loss is not the value
of the bad collateral. It is the value of the *real* assets that left the pool against it — the debt
drawn and never repaid. The bad collateral is worth whatever it is worth; it is the WETH or the USDC on
the other side that is gone.

**Subgoal 2 — write the bound.** The most that can be borrowed against an asset is its total permitted
supply times its price times its loan-to-value:

```text
maxLoss ≲ supplyCap × price × LTV
```

subject also to the borrow cap on whichever asset is being drawn.

**Subgoal 3 — differentiate.** With a cap of 50M tokens at $1, moving LTV from 0.80 to 0.75 moves the
bound from $40M to $37.5M — a 6.25% improvement, purchased with weeks of analysis. Moving the cap from
50M to 5M moves the bound from $40M to $4M — 90%, purchased with a number.

**Subgoal 4 — state the epistemic difference, which is the actual point.** An LTV is a claim about a
distribution: *this asset's price does not usually move more than that in the time it takes to
liquidate*. Its protective effect is conditional on the claim being roughly true. A cap makes no claim
about anything. It says: whatever is true, no more than this much can leave. The cap's value is
therefore highest in exactly the scenario where every other parameter was set on a false premise — which
is the scenario that produces the losses worth talking about.

### Two details that bite

**Caps are denominated in whole tokens, not in value.**<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> A cap set to
50M when the token trades at $1 bounds $50M of supply. If the token appreciates tenfold, the same cap
now bounds $500M — the bound moved by a factor of ten with no governance action and no notification. A
cap in units is a value bound that drifts, and it drifts upward exactly for the assets that are doing
well, which is exactly when a large position is most likely to be built.

**The same encoding means opposite things in adjacent fields.** A supply cap of zero means *no cap*. An
unbacked mint cap of zero means *minting disabled*.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> Both are the zero
value of a whole-token field, thirty bits apart in one word. An engineer generalising from one to the
other writes a configuration script that removes a limit while believing it imposed one. Read the field
semantics per field; there is no convention to lean on.

## The counterexample: when a cap provably does not bound the loss

This one is worth the space because the source is a protocol documenting a limitation of its own
product, in its own words, which is the rarest and most useful kind of documentation.

Morpho's guidance for vault curators describes a donation-based inflation path against Vaults V1. The
setup: a vault has listed a market whose oracle reports a price significantly above the true market
price — specifically, where collateral tokens can be bought below `oraclePrice × LLTV`. The
attack:<Cite src="morpho-security-considerations" />

1. Acquire shares in the vault by supplying, if the supply queue is not empty.
2. Donate assets to the vault on the broken market — by supplying *on behalf of* the vault — which
   inflates the vault's share price.
3. Buy the collateral cheaply, below the oracle price, and borrow against it on the broken market to
   recoup the donation.
4. Withdraw the now-inflated vault position, draining the other depositors. Repeat in small batches.

And then the sentence that matters here: **"Supply caps do not protect against this. Caps only limit
reallocations (curator/allocator actions), not donations (direct supply on behalf of the vault). A
vault with a faulty oracle market in its withdrawal queue and a cap of zero is fully vulnerable. The
potential loss is not bounded by the supply cap."**<Cite src="morpho-security-considerations" /> The
documented bound is instead the total debt takeable using collateral acquired below
`oraclePrice × LLTV × σ`, where `σ` is the attacker's share of the vault's total supply — and if the
supply queue is open, `σ` can approach 1.<Cite src="morpho-security-considerations" />

**The general lesson is not about Morpho.** A cap bounds a quantity along the path where the cap is
checked. Here the cap is enforced on allocation and the attacker's route in is donation, so the check
is simply not on the path. Before you trust any cap, enumerate every route by which value can enter the
capped position and confirm the check sits on all of them. In practice, the routes to look for are the
ones that do not go through the protocol's own accounting: direct token transfers, supply-on-behalf,
rebasing balances, and airdropped collateral.

The documented mitigations are instructive too, because none of them is a smaller cap. Remove unused
markets from the withdrawal queue entirely — a market in the withdrawal queue with a zero cap is still
vulnerable, and only removal eliminates the risk. Monitor for oracle-to-market divergence. And build an
automated kill switch that empties the supply queue on a significant deviation, which Morpho names as
"the single most effective automated mitigation".<Cite src="morpho-security-considerations" />

## Pooled or isolated: who the loss lands on

Caps decide how big the loss can be. The market structure decides whose it is.

**In a monolithic pool**, every collateral type backs one liquidity pot per borrowable asset. A supplier
of USDC is exposed to every asset the DAO has ever listed as collateral against USDC, whether or not
they have heard of it, because the debt drawn against any of them is drawn from the same pot. Listing
risk is socialised by construction — one governance vote adds a counterparty to every existing
supplier's position.

**Aave's isolation mode** is containment inside that structure rather than a replacement for it. An
asset flagged for isolation can be used as collateral, but positions using it may borrow only assets
with the isolation-borrowing bit set, up to a total debt ceiling stored in bits 212–251 with its own
decimals. The outstanding figure lives in `ReserveData.isolationModeTotalDebt`, and
`resetIsolationModeTotalDebt` — which zeroes it — requires that asset to have a zero debt ceiling
first.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> The neighbouring **siloed borrowing** bit does the reverse
containment: it constrains what a *borrower* of a risky asset may hold alongside
it.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> Two different bits, two different blast radii, both in the same
word.

**Morpho Blue** replaces the structure. A market is an immutable five-tuple — collateral token, loan
token, oracle, interest rate model, LLTV — created permissionlessly, with the LLTV and IRM chosen from
governance-approved sets, and no parameter changeable
afterwards.<Cite src="morpho-market-docs" /><Cite src="mixbytes-morpho-blue" /> A loss in one market
falls on that market's suppliers and nobody else. Supplied collateral is not rehypothecated, which is
part of why the model can defend a higher target utilisation.<Cite src="morpho-irm-docs" />

### The cost that makes this a trade rather than an upgrade

Fragmentation. The same asset pair exists many times over with different LLTVs, different oracles and
different rate models, and each instance holds a slice of the liquidity that one pooled market would
have concentrated. Two things depend directly on that depth.

**Withdrawal availability.** From the utilisation lesson: withdrawable equals `(1 − U) · S`. Splitting
one market of size `S` into five of size `S/5` does not change aggregate availability, but it does mean
a supplier's exit depends on *their* market's utilisation rather than the aggregate — and small markets
have lumpier utilisation, because one borrower is a larger fraction of them.

**Liquidation execution.** A liquidator seizing collateral must sell it. Their willingness to act is
priced against the slippage of that sale, and the incentive available to absorb it is a decreasing
function of LLTV: `LIF = min(1.15, 1/(β·LLTV + (1 − β)))` with `β = 0.3`.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" />
Thin markets with high LLTVs are the combination where liquidation stops being profitable earliest.

So the choice is not safety against risk. It is: **losses are pooled and liquidity is deep, or losses
are contained and liquidity is scattered.** Both statements are true of both designs; you are choosing
which pair you want.

Identical loss, a fifty-fold difference in per-supplier severity, and neither number is the "safer"
one in the abstract. The monolithic version spreads a survivable haircut over people who never
consented to the listing. The isolated version concentrates a position-ending haircut on people who
did — which is the correct allocation of a consequence, and also the version that produces a total
loss for a specific set of depositors rather than a bad quarter for everyone.

Now add the curator. If a vault held 30% of that isolated market and the vault itself has $500M under
management, its depositors absorb `30% × $10M = $3M` spread across $500M — a 0.6% haircut, spread over
depositors whose exposure to that market was an allocation decision they did not make and probably
could not name. The accounting isolation held perfectly at the market boundary and then the loss was
re-pooled one layer up, across a different and larger population.

That is the precise sense in which isolated markets do not eliminate contagion. They relocate the
socialisation boundary from the protocol to the vault, and the vault is chosen by a curator.

<Aside kind="note">
Isolation also changes what a *degraded* market looks like, and Morpho documents the degraded state
precisely. When an oracle reverts — say the provider deprecates the underlying feed — the market does
not become a black hole. `supply`, `withdraw`, `repay` and `supplyCollateral` all continue to work
because they need no price. `borrow` and `liquidate` revert because they do. `withdrawCollateral`
works only if the borrower has *exactly* zero borrow shares, since it triggers a health
check.<Cite src="morpho-security-considerations" /> So lenders exit normally given liquidity, and
borrowers exit by repaying in full and then withdrawing — but one remaining borrow share is enough to
strand the collateral. That is what a contained failure looks like: a market that stops doing new
business and lets its participants out, rather than a protocol-wide pause.
</Aside>

## Curated vaults: the risk decision moved, it did not vanish

Isolated markets are primitives, not products. A supplier facing a permissionless market factory faces
hundreds of combinations of collateral, oracle, rate model and LLTV, and has to price each one. Almost
nobody will.

Curated vaults close that gap. A vault takes deposits and allocates them across markets according to a
published mandate, with per-market caps, run by a curator. The supplier's question changes shape rather
than disappearing: from *do I trust this DAO's risk framework* to *do I trust this curator's mandate,
their caps, and their incentive to chase yield with my money*. That is a genuinely different question,
and it has a different failure mode — not a bad governance vote but a discretionary allocator whose
compensation is a function of the yield they report.

### The term of the deposit that nobody reads: how losses are realised

Two vaults, same protocol, same brand, opposite behaviour on the day it matters.

**Vault V1.0** "automatically realizes the loss on all existing depositors at the moment of the bad-debt
liquidation event. The share price of the vault will instantaneously
decrease."<Cite src="morpho-bad-debt-docs" /> Everyone holding shares at that instant takes a
proportional hit. The loss is priced immediately and there is nothing to run from.

**Vault V1.1** does not. "The share price will not decrease. The last depositors will face the bad debt
unless action is taken."<Cite src="morpho-bad-debt-docs" /> The shortfall sits in an on-chain
`lostAssets` figure, and the amount lost by a given event is `lostAssets_after −
lostAssets_before`.<Cite src="morpho-bad-debt-docs" /> Read that policy as a mechanism rather than a
preference: if the share price does not fall, the early withdrawers leave at a price that no longer
reflects the vault's assets, and the residual concentrates on whoever remains. That is a first-mover
advantage encoded in the accounting — the structure the last lesson of this module analyses as a run.

**Vault V2** takes the other road and socialises automatically: each adapter reports the current value
of its investments through a `realAssets()` function, so a loss in an underlying position reduces the
reported value and is distributed proportionally across all shareholders through share price
depreciation.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" /> Morpho's own documentation now marks Vaults V1 as
legacy, and flags the composition hazard explicitly: a V2 vault allocating into a legacy V1.1 vault
through the `MorphoVaultV1Adapter` will not see those losses in its share price until the V1.1 vault
itself accounts for them.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" /> A vault that socialises losses,
holding a position in a vault that does not, is a vault that socialises losses late.

### Curation is an operational role, and operations have their own failure modes

Two documented hazards, both of which are about the *process* rather than the risk model.

**Adapter removal is timelocked, and the timelock is a window.** While a `removeAdapter` proposal is
pending, an allocator can still call `allocate` on that adapter. `removeAdapter` does not revert when
the adapter holds assets, so anything allocated during the window is silently lost on
execution.<Cite src="morpho-security-considerations" /> The documented prevention is to set both the
absolute and relative caps to zero for every id exclusive to the adapter *before* submitting the
removal.<Cite src="morpho-security-considerations" />

**Ordering matters during an incident.** In the faulty-oracle response, deposits must be paused first —
by emptying the supply queue, which stops anyone increasing their share `σ` — and no market may be
re-added to the supply queue until the affected market has been fully removed from the withdrawal
queue, because re-opening deposits first restores the attack
vector.<Cite src="morpho-security-considerations" />

Neither of these is a smart-contract vulnerability. Both are runbook steps whose omission loses
depositor money. If you are evaluating a curator, the question "what is your kill switch and who can
pull it at 3am" is a better predictor than any backtest of their allocations.

itself, isolation mode off. A council member argues that the supply cap is "growth-hostile" and should

<Answer>
**The bound as proposed.** `40M × $1.00 × 0.72 = $28.8M` of real assets drawable against this listing —
the debt that leaves the pool and does not come back if the token turns out to be worth nothing.

**The bound with the cap removed.** Unbounded. There is no other parameter in the proposal that limits
total exposure: LTV limits the ratio, not the size. "Conservative LTV" and "unbounded exposure" are
compatible statements, and the second one dominates.

**Way one the bound is exceeded: price appreciation.** The cap is denominated in whole
tokens.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> If the token trades at $4 a year from now, the same 40M cap
bounds `$160M × 0.72 = $115.2M`. Nobody voted for that increase. A cap in units silently re-prices
itself, and it re-prices upward for assets that are performing, which is when the largest positions
get built.

**Way two: a path that does not pass the cap check.** The cap constrains supply through the protocol's
own accounting. It does not constrain value arriving by other routes, and Morpho's curator guidance
documents exactly this failure for vaults — caps limit allocations, not donations, so "the potential
loss is not bounded by the supply cap".<Cite src="morpho-security-considerations" /> Before trusting
this bound, enumerate every path by which this collateral or its value can enter a position and confirm
the cap is checked on each.

**Also worth flagging: the missing borrow cap is on the wrong asset.** The exposure is not to borrowing
*this* token; it is to borrowing WETH or USDC *against* it. A borrow cap on the token constrains almost
nothing here. The constraint that matters is the supply cap plus, if isolation mode were enabled, the
debt ceiling.

**Best single change: enable isolation mode with a debt ceiling.** That bounds the total debt this
collateral can support directly — in value terms rather than in token units, which fixes the
appreciation problem — and restricts which assets can be borrowed against it, so a failure cannot reach
every reserve in the pool.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> What it gives up is real and should be
stated in the proposal: isolated collateral cannot be combined with other collateral in a position, so
the listing is much less useful to exactly the sophisticated borrowers who generate the fee revenue
that justified listing it.

**The framing to carry.** Every risk memo should contain one sentence in this form: *if every
assumption in this document is wrong, the protocol loses at most X.* If that sentence cannot be
written, the listing has no bound — and no amount of care about the LTV supplies one.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next and final lesson of this module** takes the case where the bound was not in place and the
premise was false in the most complete way available: collateral that was never backed at all, supplied
to a pool whose code behaved exactly as written. It follows the loss from the deficit accounting that
recognises it, through the run it triggers among suppliers who did nothing wrong, to the pre-committed
tranche that is burned to absorb it.

**[The break exercise for this module](/c/lend-isolated-vs-pooled)** is the direct test of the claim
above: build two isolated markets sharing one collateral token but reading different oracles, and show
an attacker profiting from the discrepancy while the accounting isolation holds perfectly. Then add a
supply cap and measure exactly how much of the loss it removed.

**[In the oracles module](/c/orac-sanity-bounds)**, the sanity bounds, rate limits and staleness checks
that would have made several of the failure modes above visible get their own treatment — including
what it costs to have a monitor that fires and no automated action attached to it.
