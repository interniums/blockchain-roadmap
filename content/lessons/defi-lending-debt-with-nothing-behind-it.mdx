In April 2026 an attacker forged a cross-chain message to a liquid restaking protocol's bridge and
released a large quantity of rsETH that nothing backed. They supplied it to Aave as collateral and
borrowed real WETH against it. Aave's contracts did not misbehave at any point: they read a price,
read a token balance, checked a health factor, and made a loan that was fully collateralised
according to every number available to them.<Cite src="coindesk-kelp-aave" />

**The figures are disputed and this module publishes none of them as fact.** Reporting on the loss to
the lending protocol spans roughly $177M to $236M, and the quantity released by the bridge is quoted at
both $292M and $293M. The spread is not sloppiness on any one outlet's part — it comes from the ETH
price mark used and from whether borrowings on a second network are counted, and the borrowings did
span two networks.<Cite src="coindesk-kelp-aave" /> When you see a single confident number for this
incident, that is the tell that someone picked one.

What is not disputed is the shape, and the shape is the lesson. Bad debt is usually taught as a
liquidation-latency problem — keepers were too slow, gas was too high, the price gapped. Here there was
no liquidation price at all. The collateral was worthless from the instant it was deposited, so there
was never a healthy region to fall out of.

## When bad debt becomes arithmetically inevitable

Before the case where no price existed, take the case where one did, because the threshold is
derivable in four lines and almost nobody can state it.

Morpho documents three regions of a position, using LTV, the liquidation LTV, and the **Liquidation
Incentive Factor** (LIF):<Cite src="morpho-bad-debt-docs" />

- `LTV < LLTV` — healthy, not liquidatable.
- `LLTV < LTV ≤ 1/LIF` — a liquidator can repay part or all of the debt and seize part of the
  collateral.
- `1/LIF < LTV` — a liquidator can seize **all** the collateral by repaying only a share of the debt.
  There is no incentive for anyone to repay the remainder. That remainder is bad debt.

### Deriving the third boundary

**Subgoal 1 — write what a liquidation does.** A liquidator repays `R` of debt and receives collateral
worth `R × LIF`. The `LIF` is the whole of their compensation; on Morpho the protocol takes no
liquidation fee.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" />

**Subgoal 2 — find the repayment that exhausts the collateral.** To seize all collateral `C`, the
liquidator needs `R × LIF = C`, so `R = C / LIF`.

**Subgoal 3 — ask when that is less than the whole debt.** Bad debt exists exactly when `C/LIF < D`.
Divide through by `C`: `1/LIF < D/C`, which is `LTV > 1/LIF`. That is the boundary, and it contains no
assumption about keeper speed, gas price or market depth. Past it, a *perfectly efficient, instantly
available, zero-cost* liquidator still leaves debt behind, because taking the last of the collateral
already over-pays them for less than the full debt.

**Subgoal 4 — put the incentive formula in.** Morpho sets
`LIF = min(M, 1/(β·LLTV + (1 − β)))` with `β = 0.3` and `M = 1.15`.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" />
Where the cap does not bind, `1/LIF` simplifies beautifully: it is just `0.3·LLTV + 0.7`. So the
boundary is a straight line in LLTV, and the window between "liquidatable" and "unrecoverable" can be
tabulated for the governance-approved LLTV set:

Read the last column as the amount of runway the liquidation system has. In a 98% market, the
collateral price has **1.4%** to fall between the moment the position becomes liquidatable and the
moment liquidating it stops covering the debt. That is not a keeper-performance problem; at 1.4% it is
barely a block. And note the direction of the coupling from the correlation lesson: the markets with
the thinnest windows are the high-LLTV correlated-basket markets — precisely the ones whose positions
all cross together.

Morpho also splits the state usefully: bad debt is **unrealized** while the liquidation has not yet
happened, and **realized** once it has and the entire collateral position has been
seized.<Cite src="morpho-bad-debt-docs" /> Which one you are looking at decides whether the loss is
still a market fact or has become an accounting one.

## The case with no boundary at all

Now return to April 2026, and notice which line of the derivation fails.

Every step above assumed `C` — the collateral's value — was a real quantity that a liquidator could
seize and sell. Unbacked collateral has a price feed reading and no realisable value. There is no LTV
at which liquidation becomes attractive, because `LIF × R` of a worthless asset is worthless at every
`R`. The position was never healthy, never liquidatable, and never recoverable; it was bad debt at the
moment of the first borrow, and the protocol's books recorded it as a well-collateralised loan.

Two observations follow, and both are uncomfortable.

**The control that failed was the collateral listing, not the contract.** A lending pool given a price
feed and a token balance cannot distinguish backed supply from unbacked supply. That is not a bug in
the pool; it is the pool's interface to the world. The question "does this collateral exist" is not one
any function in the Pool contract is asked, and the audit scope that covers `borrow`, `liquidate` and
`supply` cannot reach it.

**A price-cap adapter would not have helped, and Aave had one for this asset.** Aave's price-feed
repository holds custom adapters for cases where "plain Chainlink pricing is not correct and/or not
enough for Aave risk requirements", and its `RatioCapAdapter` family for liquid staking tokens
explicitly lists rsETH among the assets with rate-based caps.<Cite src="aave-price-cap-adapters" />
What that adapter does is cap the reported value from above, based on a maximum allowed yearly growth
rate against a snapshot ratio.<Cite src="aave-price-cap-adapters" /> It is a defence against the
*exchange rate* being inflated. The exchange rate was not inflated. The number of tokens claiming that
exchange rate was. A per-unit price control has nothing to say about a supply control, and there was no
supply control on the path.

## Recognising the loss: what deficit accounting actually does

Aave v3.3 introduced explicit on-chain deficit accounting, and reading the changelog is worth doing
because the reasoning is entirely about incentives rather than arithmetic.

**The problem it names.** Some liquidations leave an account "with zero collateral and some remaining
debt", and "such debt is unlikely to be repaid and adversely affects the protocol by continuing to
accrue interest".<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" /> That phrase is the whole hazard. Left as ordinary
debt, unrecoverable debt keeps compounding through the borrow index derived in the previous lesson, so
the protocol's books show a growing asset that will never be collected — and every supplier's balance,
which is a scaled balance times a rising index, overstates what is actually recoverable.

**The mechanism.** A verification step runs conceptually *after* the liquidation. It compares whether
the borrower's total collateral equals the collateral liquidated in base currency; if it does and the
borrower's total debt exceeds the debt repaid in base currency, the remaining variable debt tokens are
burned and the amount is accounted to the reserve as a **deficit**.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" />

**Where it is stored, and who can clear it.** The `deficit` field re-uses the deprecated
`__deprecatedStableBorrowRate` slot in `ReserveData` and is read through
`getReserveDeficit`.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" /><Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> It is reduced
through `eliminateReserveDeficit`, callable only by "a permissioned entity (the registered `Umbrella`
on the `PoolAddressesProvider`)", which burns aTokens to decrease the reserve's deficit — only up to
the currently existing deficit, and only if the caller has no open borrow
positions.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" />

**Two acknowledged limitations, stated in the changelog itself.** The feature defines bad debt as zero
collateral in base currency with non-zero debt; accounts with *any* remaining collateral are excluded,
on the reasoning that they "potentially can be overcollateralized again".<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" />
And for positions already in bad debt before the upgrade, the feature "does not offer any solution",
with the recommendation that the DAO clean them up via
`repayOnBehalf`.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" /> Recognition is forward-looking; it does not reach
backwards.

### The dust problem, and why it needed a revert

The most instructive part of the same changelog is not the accounting — it is the incentive bug the
accounting created.

The cleanup only triggers when collateral reaches exactly zero, and running it costs the liquidator
slightly more gas. So a liquidator who leaves a dust of collateral behind avoids the cost and avoids
triggering the cleanup — and the changelog says so plainly: when dust is left the cleanup "will not
clean up debt, as it only triggers when the collateral is zero", and "as the bad debt cleanup slightly
increases gas, for liquidators there is an incentive to leave
dust".<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" /> A feature designed to stop bad debt accumulating had created a
reason for the party executing it to route around it.

The fix is a revert. If either debt or collateral after a liquidation would fall below `MIN_LEFTOVER_BASE`
without being exactly zero, the transaction reverts. `MIN_LEFTOVER_BASE` is defined as
`MIN_BASE_MAX_CLOSE_FACTOR_THRESHOLD / 2`, so that a position anywhere in
`[0, MIN_BASE_MAX_CLOSE_FACTOR_THRESHOLD]` can always be closed in full through the 100% close factor,
while a 50% liquidation above that range always leaves at least half the
threshold.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" />

Two neighbouring changes share the motive. The close factor now applies to the whole position rather
than per reserve, and a 100% close factor is permitted whenever the total principal or debt on the
reserve being liquidated falls below `MIN_BASE_MAX_CLOSE_FACTOR_THRESHOLD` — both to stop small
positions being ground into unliquidatable dust by repeated 50% liquidations.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" />
The changelog's own sizing note is the clearest statement of the constraint anywhere in this material:
at a $2,000 threshold and a 1% bonus, "a liquidation cannot cost more than $20 before no longer being
liquidated by an economically reasonable liquidator".<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" />

<Aside kind="warn">
A widely repeated claim is that Aave's close factor is always 50%. The v3.3 changelog says the constant
50% was **v2**, and that v3 alternates between a default of 50% and a maximum of 100%, "currently
applied when a user health-factor deteriorates below a certain
threshold".<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" /> The changelog does not name that threshold and this lesson
therefore does not either — a specific number circulates in secondary material and could not be
confirmed against the source. If you need it, read `LiquidationLogic` in the deployed version you are
actually integrating with.
</Aside>

Recognition recovers nothing. It buys three things: the phantom interest stops compounding, the
shortfall becomes a number the protocol can act on, and the rate model finally sees it — v3.3 requires
callers to pass `reserve.deficit + reserve.unbacked` into `calculateInterestRates`, on the rationale
that both represent unbacked tokens on the
pool.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" /><Cite src="aave-v3-rate-strategy-docs" />

## The second harm: the loss and the run are different events

Here is the part that gets collapsed into the word "safe".

A shared lending pool has no pro-rata redemption. Withdrawals are first-come, first-served against the
`(1 − U) · S` of idle liquidity. So once a loss is known but not yet realised into the accounting, the
individually rational action for every supplier is to withdraw before the others — and it is rational
precisely *because* everyone else is doing it.

**Work the incidence.** A pool has `S = 100` supplied, `B = 90` borrowed, of which 10 is unrecoverable.
Total recoverable value is 90. Available liquidity is 10.

  alt="The same 10 of unrecoverable debt in a pool with 100 supplied and 90 borrowed: realised immediately, every supplier takes 10% and recovers 90c; left unrealised, the first 10 of claims exit at par and the remaining 90 of claims share 80, recovering 88.9c."

The pool's total loss is identical in both rows. What differs is who bears it, and the second row pays
a premium to speed. Now push it: if the pool had been at `U = 0.80`, twenty could have exited at par
and the remaining 80 of claims would share 70 — **87.5c**. More available liquidity makes the outcome
*worse* for the stragglers, because more of the par-value exits complete before the door shuts. That is
the opposite of the intuition, and it is why "the pool has plenty of liquidity" is not reassurance
during a solvency question.

**Then the door shuts and the price of money spikes.** The withdrawals drive `U` to 1, where the borrow
rate reaches `base + slope1 + slope2`. Neither consequence lands on anyone responsible: borrowers who
have not touched their positions pay the maximum rate, and suppliers are quoted an enormous APY on a
position they cannot exit — a number that appears on aggregator league tables and pulls fresh deposits
into a pool that is already in a run.

You have seen this structure once already, one layer up. A vault that realises bad debt into the share
price at the moment of the event removes the first-mover advantage; a vault that does not, so that "the
last depositors will face the bad debt unless action is taken", creates
one.<Cite src="morpho-bad-debt-docs" /> Same mechanism, same remedy: **realise early, and the race
disappears.**

## Backstops: naming the loss-absorbing tranche in advance

If a deficit exceeds what the protocol's own reserves absorb, the remainder lands somewhere. The only
real question is whether that somewhere was chosen before the event or discovered after it.

Aave's answer is **Umbrella**, described as a modular on-chain risk management system that "automates
bad debt coverage for Aave v3 pools" and replaces the previous Safety Module. Users stake aTokens —
aUSDC, aUSDT, aWETH — or GHO; if a deficit occurs in a given asset, the corresponding staked assets are
burned to offset it, "removing the need for governance decisions or manual
intervention".<Cite src="aave-umbrella-docs" /> The mechanics that matter:

- **Scope.** "Staking risk is isolated to the specific asset and network where the aTokens are staked.
  For example, staking aUSDC helps cover bad debt in USDC only."<Cite src="aave-umbrella-docs" /> This
  is the design's strongest property, and it is an argument about *pricing*: a tranche holder can only
  underwrite intelligently if they can name what they are underwriting.
- **Vehicle.** Each stake is an ERC-4626 `StakeToken` per asset per network, with an exchange rate that
  starts at 1:1 and "only decreases if slashing occurs".<Cite src="aave-umbrella-docs" />
- **Trigger.** `UmbrellaCore` slashes automatically "when a deficit in the corresponding Aave pool
  exceeds the configured offset", reducing total assets in the `StakeToken`; a minimum assets floor
  prevents full depletion, and slashed assets go to the Aave
  Collector.<Cite src="aave-umbrella-docs" /> On the pool side, the registered Umbrella is the
  permissioned caller of `eliminateReserveDeficit`.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" />
- **First loss.** Offsets mean the DAO absorbs an initial slice. The documentation's stated example is
  USDT staking with "a 100,000 USDT offset, meaning the Aave DAO covers the first 100,000 USDT of bad
  debt before any staker assets are affected".<Cite src="aave-umbrella-docs" /> Treat that as an
  illustration of the mechanism, not a live constant — offsets are governance parameters and must be
  read from the deployment.
- **Exit.** A 20-day cooldown and a 2-day withdrawal window, during which funds "remain
  slashable".<Cite src="aave-umbrella-docs" /> A tranche you can leave the moment losses look likely is
  not a tranche.

### The scale problem nobody states plainly

Aave's own documentation gives the calibration: during the first month of v3.3 operation, approximately
**$400** in total deficit accumulated across all pools, against nearly **$9.5 billion** in outstanding
borrows.<Cite src="aave-umbrella-docs" />

That base rate is what makes the mechanism economically viable — stakers are paid to underwrite a risk
that almost never materialises — and it is exactly why the tail case needs separate reasoning. A
tranche calibrated on four hundred dollars a month is not thereby a tranche that covers a nine-figure
event, and nothing about the design claims it is. A backstop **caps how far the loss travels**; it does
not make anyone whole.

**And there is more than one answer.** In the same period, Curve's founder proposed a market-based
recovery pool for a much smaller bad-debt position — around $700K — rather than a treasury-funded
repayment, and the contrast with a donation-funded remedy was the explicit point of the
reporting.<Cite src="coindesk-curve-bad-debt" /> Both answer the same question: *who is the
loss-absorbing tranche, and did they consent?* A protocol that has not answered it before the loss
answers it afterwards, in a forum, under time pressure, in favour of whoever is loudest.

<Check type="explain" concept="lend-bad-debt-is-a-bank-run">
A lending market discovers at 09:00 that one of its collateral assets is partially unbacked. The
protocol is over-collateralised in aggregate — recoverable value exceeds total supplier claims by a
comfortable margin once the bad position is written down. The team's incident channel proposes, in
order: (1) pause new borrows, (2) publish a transparency post explaining that the protocol remains
solvent, (3) work through the weekend on a recovery plan.

Say what will happen between 09:00 and 12:00, which of the three actions helps and which actively
hurts, and name the one action missing from the list.

<Answer>
**What happens.** The transparency post is the starting gun. Every supplier who reads it correctly
concludes that withdrawing now returns par and withdrawing later returns less, because the loss is not
yet realised into anyone's balance and the pool pays first-come-first-served against `(1 − U) · S`.
Utilisation climbs toward 1, the borrow rate runs up the steep branch, and shortly afterwards nobody
can withdraw at any price. The suppliers who moved first are whole; the ones who waited for the
recovery plan hold the entire shortfall plus an illiquid position.

**(1) Pausing new borrows — mildly helpful, and less than it looks.** It stops the hole growing. It
does nothing about the exit race, because borrowing was not the mechanism draining liquidity;
withdrawing was.

**(2) The transparency post — helps and hurts, and the hurt is the part to plan for.** Publishing is
right and there is no version of this where concealment is better. But a post that says "we are solvent"
and does not say "the loss has been realised into share prices as of block N" is telling suppliers that
a par-value exit is still available. It converts a private worry into a coordinated race. The fix is
sequencing, not silence.

**(3) Working through the weekend — irrelevant to the harm being caused right now.** A recovery plan
addresses the loss. The run is a separate harm on a separate clock, and by Monday its distribution is
already settled.

**The missing action: realise the loss immediately, before or with the announcement.** Write the
shortfall down against every supplier pro rata — the deficit-accounting move — so that withdrawing at
09:15 and withdrawing at 17:00 return the same price per unit. That removes the first-mover advantage
entirely, and with it the reason to race. It is the same design decision that separates a vault which
realises bad debt into the share price at the moment of the event from one that leaves it for "the last
depositors" to face.<Cite src="morpho-bad-debt-docs" />

**The general principle.** The loss and the run are two harms with two remedies. Recognition fixes the
*incidence* — who pays — and can be done in a transaction. Only new liquidity fixes the *exit*, and
that takes days. A protocol that has only planned for the first will spend its incident explaining the
second.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**[The liquidations module](/c/liq-bad-debt-threshold)** picks up where the `1/LIF` derivation stopped:
how the incentive is discovered rather than set, why flash-loan liquidators dropped the capital
requirement to zero without changing the failure mode, and why liquidation profitability is inversely
coupled to congestion — so the incentive is smallest exactly when it needs to be largest.

**[The oracles module](/c/orac-lp-token-and-rate-oracles)** takes up the listing control that failed
here, including the feed class that dominates 2026 incidents: rate oracles on yield-bearing wrappers,
which report an issuer's accounting rather than a market's opinion.

**The module reflection** asks you to pick a live lending market and name, before checking, the single
parameter change that would most reduce its worst-case loss — then to find what the parameter actually
is and say what the DAO gave up to keep it there. You now have the three candidates worth considering,
ordered by how much they help once everything else was wrong: the cap, the isolation ceiling, and the
oracle.
