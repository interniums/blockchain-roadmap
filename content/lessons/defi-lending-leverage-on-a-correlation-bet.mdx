That is a market where $100 of a liquid staking token supports $94.50 of borrowed
ETH.<Cite src="morpho-market-docs" /> Deposit, borrow, buy more collateral, repeat, and the loop
converges on roughly eighteen times leverage. The same protocol's approved list tops out at 98%, which
is fifty times.<Cite src="morpho-market-docs" />

Nobody thinks a liquid staking token is fifty times safer than ETH. The high number is not a statement
about the asset's volatility at all. It is a statement about a *relationship* — and the whole of this
lesson is about what that substitution buys, what it costs, and what happens on the day the
relationship stops holding.

## What efficiency mode actually configures

Aave's version of the idea is a named category, configured wholesale. `configureEModeCategory` takes an
id and a struct:<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" />

```solidity
struct EModeCategory {
    uint16  ltv;                   // custom loan-to-value for the category
    uint16  liquidationThreshold;  // custom liquidation threshold
    uint16  liquidationBonus;      // custom liquidation bonus
    uint128 collateralBitmap;      // which assets may be collateral here
    string  label;
    uint128 borrowableBitmap;      // which assets may be borrowed here
}
```

The documentation states the purpose plainly: "In eMode, the protocol allows very high borrowing power
to borrow assets of the same category", and category 0 "is reserved for volatile heterogeneous assets
and it's always disabled".<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> A user opts in with
`setUserEMode(uint8 categoryId)`, where 0 means no category.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" />

Three details in that struct are worth pausing on.

**Collateral and borrowable are separate bitmaps.** An asset can be usable as collateral inside a
category without being borrowable inside it. The older design carried a single eMode category per
asset in bits 168–175 of the reserve configuration; those bits are now marked
deprecated.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> The refinement matters because "these assets move together"
and "I am willing to be short this asset against that one" are different claims, and the two-bitmap
form lets a risk framework make the first without implying the second.

**All three risk parameters are overridden together.** LTV, liquidation threshold *and* liquidation
bonus. The bonus moving is the part people miss, and it is not incidental — a thinner buffer needs a
different incentive to clear, for reasons the last section of this lesson makes concrete.

**The category may replace the oracles.** Each category "may or may not have a custom oracle to
override the individual assets' price oracles".<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> Hold that one. It is
the hinge.

## Why a thinner buffer is defensible

Recall the result from the previous lesson: the health factor is `C · LT / D`, so liquidation is
driven entirely by the **ratio** `C/D`. Neither leg's own volatility appears anywhere in the formula.

**Subgoal 1 — write the quantity the buffer must cover.** For a normal pair, `C` and `D` are close to
independent, so the ratio inherits roughly the combined variance of both legs. For ETH collateral
against a dollar stablecoin, the ratio's distribution is essentially ETH's distribution.

**Subgoal 2 — substitute a correlated pair.** For wstETH collateral against WETH debt, the two legs are
tied by a staking exchange rate that moves slowly and monotonically — Aave's adapter family for these
assets is built around exactly that property, describing liquid staking tokens as assets "that grow in
value over time".<Cite src="aave-price-cap-adapters" /> The ratio's distribution is no longer ETH's —
it is the distribution of the *tracking error* between wstETH and ETH.

**Subgoal 3 — restate the parameter.** The buffer sized against a 10% tail in ETH becomes a buffer
sized against a 2% tail in the tracking error. Same required confidence, an order of magnitude less
buffer. That, and only that, is the argument for a 94.5% LLTV.

**Subgoal 4 — name what has been substituted.** The old buffer rested on an observed price
distribution. The new one rests on a claim about a relationship. The first can be estimated from
history and is wrong in a graded way; the second is a proposition that is either true or, on some
particular Tuesday, discretely false.

### The leverage this buys, and the amplification that comes with it

Looping — deposit, borrow, swap into more collateral, deposit again — converges to a maximum leverage
of `1 / (1 − LTV)` on the initial equity. Reading it off the approved
list:<Cite src="morpho-market-docs" />

Now do the part the leverage table hides. At leverage `L`, a divergence of `ε` in the ratio moves the
borrower's equity by approximately `L · ε`. At 18.18x, a **1%** divergence between wstETH and ETH is an
18% loss of equity. At 50x it is a 50% loss. The thin buffer and the high leverage are the same fact
seen twice, and the leverage multiplies exactly the risk the buffer stopped covering.

And liquidation arrives sooner than the headline suggests. Open at LTV 0.90 in a 94.5% LLTV market — a
deliberately conservative 10x rather than the maximum — and the ratio need only fall by
`0.90 / 0.945 = 0.9524`, a **4.76%** divergence, for the position to become seizable. Four and three
quarter percent is not a market crash. It is a Tuesday, for an asset whose redemption is queued.

### The band shrinks too, and unwinding is not the reverse of building

Two further consequences fall straight out of the previous lesson's identity, `HF = (LT/LTV) / u`.

**The band narrows in health-factor terms.** Outside a category, an illustrative asset at LTV 0.80 and
LT 0.825 gives a fully drawn borrower a health factor of `0.825/0.80 = 1.031`. Inside a category at LTV
0.93 and LT 0.95, the same borrower sits at `0.95/0.93 = 1.0215`. Both parameters rose, and the
protective gap between them fell by roughly a third measured this way. Raising LTV and liquidation
threshold "together" does not preserve the buffer; it preserves the appearance of preserving it.

**The loop does not run backwards at the same speed.** Building an 18x position is a sequence of
borrow-swap-deposit steps, each of which is optional and can be paused. Unwinding it is a sequence of
withdraw-swap-repay steps, each of which requires two things the build did not: that the pool has
undrawn liquidity to let the collateral out, and that the market will take the collateral at something
near the oracle price. Both of those are exactly what a correlation break removes. A borrower who
notices the divergence early and decides to deleverage is competing for the same exit as every
liquidator and every other looper, through a withdrawal path governed by utilisation — the subject of
the next lesson. Leverage that can only be added on the way up is not a position; it is a one-way
option written against the pool's liquidity.

## The hinge: the oracle can encode the same assumption twice

Go back to the sentence held in reserve: an e-mode category may override the individual assets' price
oracles.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" />

Consider what a sensible override looks like for wstETH against WETH. The economically correct price
of wstETH in ETH is its staking exchange rate — the amount of ETH each share redeems for. Pricing it
that way is not sloppy; it is arguably more correct than a market quote, because the redemption is
real and the market quote can be noisy. Aave's own price feed repository is built around exactly this
family: it holds "custom oracle adapters used when plain Chainlink pricing is not correct and/or not
enough for Aave risk requirements", and its `RatioCapAdapter` is described as being for liquid staking
tokens that grow in value over time, with the price "capped based on maximum allowed yearly growth
rate" using a snapshot ratio and timestamp as reference, returning the capped ratio when the current
one exceeds the maximum.<Cite src="aave-price-cap-adapters" />

Read the direction of that protection carefully. The cap bounds the reported value **from above**. It
is a defence against the exchange rate being inflated — by a bug, by a manipulated rate provider, by an
issuer's accounting. It is not, and does not claim to be, a mechanism for reporting that the secondary
market has decided the token is worth less than the rate says.

So here is the composition that deserves your attention. The category's high LTV assumes the two assets
move together. The category's oracle prices one asset *in terms of* the other by a rate. If the market
begins to disagree with that rate, the position's collateral value does not move, its health factor
does not move, and the protocol's view of its own solvency does not move. The assumption is not
double-checked by the price feed; it is re-asserted by it.

<Aside kind="warn">
This is not an argument against rate-based feeds — a market feed for a thin LST is its own hazard, and
a manipulable one. It is an argument for asking one question of every correlated-asset listing: **is
there any input to this market that can tell it the correlation has broken?** Morpho's curator
guidance reaches the same place from the other direction, warning to be "especially cautious with
NAV-based oracles updated by the token issuer" because "these can diverge from DEX prices during
exploit scenarios", and recommending automated monitoring that detects when oracle prices diverge from
market prices.<Cite src="morpho-security-considerations" /> That monitor exists precisely because the
protocol itself has no such input.
</Aside>

## What a correlation break does that an ordinary crash does not

Three effects, and they compound rather than add.

**Every position crosses at once.** In a heterogeneous market, borrowers hold different assets at
different leverage, so a price move liquidates them across a distribution. In a correlated category
they are all the same trade at similar leverage, correlated by construction — the category *is* the
correlation. A 5% divergence does not liquidate 5% of the book; it liquidates the slice of the book
above a leverage line, all in the same block.

**The liquidator's incentive can be zero or negative in real terms.** This is the arithmetic worth
carrying. A liquidator repays debt and seizes collateral at the oracle price plus a bonus. If the
oracle reports the collateral at a rate the market no longer honours, the liquidator's real margin is:

```text
real margin  ≈  liquidation bonus  −  market discount to the oracle price  −  gas  −  slippage
```

At a 5% bonus and a 5% market discount, the real margin before costs is zero. Morpho's liquidation
model makes this concrete from the other side: its liquidation incentive factor is
`LIF = min(1.15, 1 / (β·LLTV + (1 − β)))` with `β = 0.3`, which is a *decreasing* function of
LLTV.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" /> The higher the LLTV, the smaller the bonus — so the markets
with the thinnest buffers are also the markets whose liquidators have the least room to absorb a
discount. High-LLTV correlated markets are, by design, the ones where liquidation stops paying first.

**The exit liquidity is the asset that just broke.** The liquidator ends the transaction holding the
depegging collateral and has to sell it. The venue where that sale happens is the same venue whose
price move started the event, and it is being sold into by everyone else at the same moment. Forced
selling deepens the discount, which pushes more positions under, which forces more selling — the
reflexive loop that [liquidation cascades](/c/liq-cascade) formalise later in this track.

Put the three together and the failure mode is not "some borrowers lost money". It is: a wave of
simultaneous liquidations, arriving at the exact moment liquidating stops being profitable, into a
market with no depth. That is the shape of a correlated-basket insolvency, and it converts one asset's
credibility problem into a solvency event for an entire market.

<Check type="predict" concept="lend-correlation-break">
A DAO proposes a new efficiency category pairing a yield-bearing wrapped stablecoin, `yUSD`, as
collateral against a major dollar stablecoin as the borrowable asset. Proposed parameters: LTV 0.93,
liquidation threshold 0.95, liquidation bonus 2%. The category will use a custom oracle that prices
`yUSD` at its vault share price — assets under management divided by shares — rather than at a market
quote. The risk report notes that `yUSD` has never traded below $0.998.

Name the maximum leverage this permits, the divergence that liquidates a position opened at LTV 0.90,
and the two failure modes the design cannot see. Then state the single parameter change that would
most reduce the worst case, and what it gives up.

<Answer>
**Leverage.** `1 / (1 − 0.93) = 14.3x` on the initial equity.

**Divergence to liquidation from LTV 0.90.** The ratio must fall by `0.90 / 0.95 = 0.9474`, so a
**5.26%** divergence. At 14.3x leverage a mere 1% divergence has already removed roughly 14% of the
borrower's equity.

**Failure mode one: the oracle cannot report a discount.** A share price computed as assets over shares
is an accounting statement about the issuer's book, not a price. If the market decides the underlying
assets are impaired, the share price does not move until the issuer marks them down — and the issuer is
the party with the least incentive to do so quickly. "Never traded below $0.998" is a fact about a
market the protocol is not reading. This is exactly the case Morpho's curator guidance flags: NAV-based
oracles updated by the token issuer can diverge from DEX prices during exploit
scenarios.<Cite src="morpho-security-considerations" />

**Failure mode two: the incentive is smaller than the plausible discount.** A 2% liquidation bonus
means a liquidator breaks even at a 2% market discount before gas and slippage. Any impairment large
enough to matter is larger than the bonus, so at the moment liquidation is needed, no rational
liquidator calls the function. The positions do not get liquidated slowly — they do not get liquidated
at all, and the residual becomes bad debt with no liquidation price. This is the same structure as
Morpho's high-LLTV markets having the lowest LIF.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" />

**Best single change: a supply cap on `yUSD`.** Not a lower LTV. Lowering LTV from 0.93 to 0.90 changes
the loss when the premise is wrong by a few percent; it still assumes the oracle reports reality. A cap
bounds the total debt drawable against the asset regardless of whether every other parameter was set on
a false premise — the reasoning developed in full later in this module. What it gives up is real:
the market stays small, the yield opportunity is rationed, and the DAO forgoes the fee revenue on
everything above the cap. That is the trade, and it should be made explicitly rather than by leaving
the cap unset.

**The habit.** For any correlated-asset category, write down three numbers before the parameters: the
divergence that liquidates a typical position, the discount at which liquidators stop, and the input
that would tell the protocol the divergence happened. If the third does not exist, the first two are
decoration.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** turns to the other state variable that governs a lending pool — utilisation — and
shows that the number every dashboard prints as "capital efficiency" is simultaneously the measure of
whether suppliers can leave at all. The kinked rate curve that prices it turns out not to be a pricing
device.

**The last lesson of this module** returns to correlated collateral in its harshest form: an asset that
was not merely mispriced but unbacked, supplied to a pool that had a rate-based feed for exactly that
token, and borrowed against successfully because nothing in the system was designed to answer the
question "does this collateral exist".

**[The oracles module](/c/orac-lp-token-and-rate-oracles)** takes up rate oracles, exchange-rate feeds
and yield-bearing wrappers as an attack surface in their own right, which by 2026 is where the live
manipulation research points rather than at spot-price flash loans.
