The interface says *78% of borrow limit used*, with a bar that is comfortably not red. A reasonable
reader concludes there is 22% of something left.

There is not. That position is liquidated by a 24% fall in the collateral price, and the number 22
does not appear anywhere in that calculation. The gap is not a rounding artefact or a bad UI — it is
what happens when a system with two thresholds is rendered as a system with one. This lesson derives
both, derives the identity that connects them to the bar, and then does the thing almost nobody does:
converts a health factor into an actual price move, correctly, in both directions.

## Two parameters, sixteen bits each, one storage word

Start where the values live, because the layout tells you they were designed as separate knobs rather
than one knob with a safety margin bolted on. Aave v3 packs a reserve's entire risk configuration into
a single `uint256`:<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" />

Everything a risk framework decides about an asset is in that word.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> No
document, no policy PDF, no committee minutes — one word, and a governance transaction that rewrites
it. v3.3 even flipped the bitmasks so that the common operation, *reading* the configuration, needs
one fewer instruction than it used to.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" /> That is the level at which risk
policy is implemented.

The two we care about now sit adjacent:

- **Loan-to-value (LTV)**, also called the collateral factor, caps the debt a position may *open or
  increase to* as a fraction of collateral value. An asset with an LTV of zero cannot be enabled as
  collateral at all.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" />
- **Liquidation threshold (LT)** is a strictly larger ratio at which the position becomes *seizable*.
  When the health factor is below 1, a liquidator repays some `debtToCover` on the borrower's behalf
  and receives a proportional, discounted amount of the collateral plus a liquidation bonus "to cover
  market risk".<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" />

### What the band actually buys, in the right units

Take an illustrative asset with LTV 0.80 and LT 0.825. Both are governance parameters and both move;
read them from the reserve configuration rather than from a lesson.

**Subgoal 1 — open at the maximum.** With collateral value `C`, the largest debt permitted is
`D = 0.80 · C`.

**Subgoal 2 — find the collateral value at which seizure becomes possible.** That is the `C'` where
`C' · 0.825 = D`, so `C' = 0.80·C / 0.825 = 0.9697·C`.

**Subgoal 3 — convert to the units a borrower thinks in.** The position survives a **3.03%** fall in
the collateral price, not a 2.5% fall. The band is 2.5 *percentage points of ratio*, and ratio points
are not price points. A borrower who reads "2.5% buffer" off the difference between two published
numbers has mis-stated their own risk by twenty percent, in the safe-sounding direction.

**Subgoal 4 — state what the band is for.** It is the price move a borrower who opened at maximum
leverage may absorb before anything can be taken from them. Without it, a position could be opened and
liquidated in consecutive blocks on a single oracle tick, which would make maximum-LTV borrowing a
trap rather than a product.

There is a second, less obvious payoff, and it is the strongest argument for two numbers rather than
one. Because LTV governs only *new* borrowing and LT governs seizure, the two can be moved
independently. Lowering an asset's LTV — to zero, in the limit — stops it backing any further debt
without, by itself, making a single existing position liquidatable. A single-parameter design cannot
express "no new leverage against this asset, and no forced selling today". That is precisely the
action a risk committee wants available on the morning it stops believing in a listing.

### The one-parameter alternative, and what it costs

Two numbers is a choice, not a law. Morpho Blue markets carry a single **LLTV** — liquidation
loan-to-value — chosen at market creation from a governance-approved set and immutable thereafter: 0,
38.5%, 62.5%, 77%, 86%, 91.5%, 94.5%, 96.5%, 98%.<Cite src="morpho-market-docs" /> Its documentation
is blunt about what the single number means: at an LLTV of 80%, $100 of collateral supports $80 of
borrowing, and "if above like $80.0001, the position is liquidatable".<Cite src="morpho-market-docs" />

Borrow to the limit and you are one wei of price movement from seizure. There is no band, because
there is no second parameter to hold one.

Read that as a deliberate transfer rather than an oversight. In the two-parameter design the protocol
chooses the buffer for you and enforces it by refusing the last slice of borrowing power; in the
one-parameter design the borrower chooses their own buffer by simply not borrowing to the limit. What
the pool gives up is the ability to act on the two decisions separately — with one number, "stop new
leverage against this asset" and "start seizing positions" are the same governance action, which is
why in an immutable market neither is available at all. What it gains is that there is nothing to
change, so nobody has to be trusted not to change it. That trade — a risk framework you must trust
versus parameters nobody can move — is the axis the whole isolated-versus-pooled question turns on
later in this module.

## Health factor, and the identity that explains the bar

Health factor is the collateral, weighted asset by asset by that asset's liquidation threshold,
divided by total debt value:

```text
HF = Σ (collateralValue_i × liquidationThreshold_i) / totalDebtValue
```

Below 1, the position may be liquidated. Aave's `getUserAccountData` returns it directly, alongside
`totalCollateralBase`, `totalDebtBase`, `availableBorrowsBase`, the blended
`currentLiquidationThreshold` and the blended `ltv` — all in the base currency the price feed
uses.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> Morpho Blue states the same quantity with a single parameter per
market: `HEALTH_FACTOR = COLLATERAL_VALUE_IN_LOAN_TOKEN × LLTV / BORROWED_AMOUNT`, healthy above 1.0,
liquidatable below.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" />

Now connect it to the bar the interface draws. Let `u` be "fraction of borrow limit used", which is
`u = D / (C · LTV)`. Substituting into the health factor for a single-collateral position:

```text
HF = C · LT / D
   = C · LT / (u · C · LTV)
   = (LT / LTV) / u
```

Three consequences drop straight out of that identity, and each one corrects a common reading.

**At 100% of borrow limit, the health factor is not 1.** It is `LT / LTV`, which for our illustrative
asset is `0.825 / 0.80 = 1.031`. A fully drawn position is 3.1% of *health* away from seizure, which
is the 3.03% price move derived above.

**The bar and the health factor are reciprocal, not linear.** At `u = 0.78` the health factor is
`1.031 / 0.78 = 1.322`.

**Neither number is a price distance.** For that, do the last step.

### From health factor to a price move, in both directions

This is the step almost every dashboard omits, and it has two different answers depending on which leg
of the position is the volatile one.

**Subgoal 1 — identify the quantity that actually has to move.** `HF = C · LT / D`. Liquidation is
`HF = 1`. Since `LT` is constant, what must change is the *ratio* `C / D`, and it must fall by exactly
a factor of `HF`. Nothing else in the formula matters.

**Subgoal 2 — solve the volatile-collateral case.** Stable debt, so `D` is fixed and `C` must fall by
the factor `1 / HF`. The price fall that liquidates is `1 − 1/HF`. At `HF = 1.322`: `1 − 0.7564 =
24.4%`. At `HF = 1.5`: **33.3%**. At `HF = 1.05`: **4.76%**.

**Subgoal 3 — solve the volatile-debt case.** Stable collateral, so `C` is fixed and `D` must rise by
the factor `HF`. The price rise that liquidates is `HF − 1`. At `HF = 1.5`: **+50%**. At `HF = 1.05`:
**+5%**.

**Subgoal 4 — say precisely what differs, and resist the overclaim.** These two are the *same ratio
move* expressed from opposite ends: a 33.3% fall and a 50% rise both take `C/D` down by a factor of
1.5. So it is not true that one position is intrinsically closer to liquidation than the other at
equal health factor. What *is* true, and what matters:

- The two percentages differ — 33.3 against 50 — so a single sentence like "health factor 1.5 means
  you are 50% away" is right in at most one of the two cases, and the discrepancy grows with health
  factor (at `HF = 1.05` the two answers are 4.76% and 5%, nearly equal; at `HF = 3` they are 66.7%
  and 200%).
- Falls are bounded at 100% and rises are not. "The collateral halves" and "the debt asset doubles"
  are not equally probable events for the same asset, and the second is not bounded by anything.
- The distribution that governs your risk is the distribution of the **ratio**, not of either leg.
  That is the entire subject of the next lesson: if you can make the two legs move together, the
  ratio barely moves, and a much thinner buffer starts to look defensible.

### What the ratio form tells you about oracle risk

One more consequence falls out of "only the ratio moves", and it is the reason this formula is the
right place to start thinking about price feeds.

Every term in the health factor is a *value*, produced by multiplying a token amount by a price in
some common base currency — Aave's `getUserAccountData` returns collateral, debt and available borrows
"in the base currency used by the price feed".<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> Now suppose every feed
the protocol reads is wrong by the same multiplicative factor `k`. The numerator scales by `k`, the
denominator scales by `k`, and the health factor does not move at all. A uniform error in the base
currency is invisible to liquidation logic, and harmlessly so.

Suppose instead that one feed is wrong by `k` and the others are correct. Now the ratio moves by the
full `k`, and every position with that asset on one side re-prices. The health factor is not exposed
to *price* error; it is exposed to **relative** price error between the collateral leg and the debt
leg. That is why a collateral listing's oracle is not judged on absolute accuracy but on whether it
can diverge from the feed used for the asset borrowed against it — and it is why an oracle that prices
an asset by a rate rather than by a market is a different kind of object from one that prices it by
trades. Both produce a number; only one of them can report that the market disagrees with the number.
That distinction is the hinge of the next lesson, and it gets its full treatment in
[the oracles module](/c/orac-price-feed).

## Two ways a position dies without you

The health factor formula contains no user action. Both of its inputs move on their own.

**Oracle updates.** `C` and `D` are *values*, so every price update recomputes them. A position opened
at a comfortable health factor is liquidatable the instant a feed reports a number that makes it so,
with no transaction from the borrower and no warning surface that is guaranteed to reach them.

**Interest accrual.** `D` grows continuously. Morpho's interest rate model documents the borrow leg as
a per-second rate compounded continuously — `borrowAPY = e^(borrowRate × 31,536,000) − 1`, with
`secondsPerYear = 31,536,000`.<Cite src="morpho-irm-docs" /> Take a position sitting at `HF = 1.01`
with a flat oracle. The debt must grow by a factor of 1.01 to reach `HF = 1`, so the time to
liquidation is `ln(1.01) / r`:

Nothing happens in the market. Nobody trades. The position is seized on a schedule set entirely by the
borrow rate — and the borrow rate is not a constant either, which is why a position that had thirty
days of runway can have six after a liquidity event moves utilisation. That coupling is the subject of
the lesson after next.

## Two refinements that change the answer

**Blending, and why a value-neutral rebalance can hurt you.** With multiple collaterals the numerator
is a weighted sum, so the *composition* matters, not only the total. Take $10,000 of collateral
against $5,000 of debt.

Same total collateral value, same debt, and the health factor moved 11%. The blended threshold — the
`currentLiquidationThreshold` that `getUserAccountData` returns<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> —
fell from 0.825 to 0.735. A borrower who "diversified their collateral" without changing its value
moved measurably closer to liquidation, and no dollar amount on their screen changed.

**`HF` below 1 is necessary, not sufficient.** Aave's `ReserveData` carries a `liquidationGracePeriodUntil`
timestamp: until it passes, liquidations are not allowed on that reserve; set it in the past and they
are.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> A seizable position is not necessarily a seized one, and code
that assumes "health factor below 1 implies liquidation is callable now" is modelling the formula
rather than the contract. This is a small instance of a habit worth generalising: the health factor is
a *policy* expressed as arithmetic, and the arithmetic is surrounded by switches.

<Check type="predict" concept="lend-health-factor">
A position holds $50,000 of a collateral with LTV 0.75 and liquidation threshold 0.80, and has
borrowed $30,000 of a dollar stablecoin. The interface shows "80% of borrow limit used".

(a) Compute the health factor two ways — from the definition and from the `(LT/LTV)/u` identity — and
confirm they agree. (b) State the collateral price fall that makes the position liquidatable.
(c) The borrower now swaps $20,000 of the collateral into a second asset with liquidation threshold
0.55, keeping total collateral value and debt unchanged. Recompute, and say what the borrower would
have seen on a dashboard that displays only dollar values.

<Answer>
**(a) Health factor, twice.**
From the definition: `HF = (50,000 × 0.80) / 30,000 = 40,000 / 30,000 = 1.3333`.
From the identity: the borrow limit is `50,000 × 0.75 = 37,500`, and `u = 30,000 / 37,500 = 0.80`,
matching the interface. Then `HF = (LT/LTV)/u = (0.80/0.75)/0.80 = 1.0667/0.80 = 1.3333`. They agree,
as they must — the identity is the definition rearranged.

**(b) The price move.** Debt is a dollar stablecoin, so this is the volatile-collateral case and the
fall that liquidates is `1 − 1/HF = 1 − 0.75 = 25%`. Check it directly: at a 25% fall the collateral is
worth $37,500, and `37,500 × 0.80 = 30,000`, exactly the debt. Note what the dashboard's "80% used"
would have suggested — 20% of something — and that the true answer is 25%.

**(c) After the swap.** The blended numerator becomes `30,000 × 0.80 + 20,000 × 0.55 = 24,000 + 11,000
= 35,000`, so `HF = 35,000 / 30,000 = 1.1667`. The blended liquidation threshold has fallen from 0.80
to 0.70, and the liquidating price fall has tightened from 25% to `1 − 1/1.1667 = 14.3%`.

**What a dollar-only dashboard shows:** $50,000 of collateral and $30,000 of debt, before and after.
Unchanged. Every number the borrower can see is identical, and the distance to liquidation has almost
halved. The information that moved lives in bits 16–31 of two different reserve configuration
words.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" />

**The transferable habit.** When you read a lending position, never stop at collateral value and debt
value. Recover the weighted threshold, then convert the health factor into a price move on whichever
leg is the volatile one. Those two steps turn a number that feels like a score into a statement about
the market.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** takes the observation that only the *ratio* moves and turns it into a product:
efficiency mode raises both thresholds for baskets of assets a risk framework declares correlated,
because if the two legs move together the buffer only has to cover the tracking error between them.
It also examines what happens on the day that declaration turns out to be a governance opinion rather
than a market fact — when every position in the basket crosses its threshold in the same minute, and
the collateral the liquidators must sell is the asset that just broke.

**The bad-debt lesson** at the end of this module uses the same ratio arithmetic to derive the point
past which liquidation stops being profitable at all, so the residual debt has nothing behind it. That
threshold is a function of the liquidation incentive, and you now have every piece needed to derive it.

**In [the liquidations module](/c/liq-why-liquidation-exists)** the mechanics of actually seizing the
collateral get their own treatment — close factors, incentives, the keeper set, and why the incentive
is smallest exactly when it needs to be largest.
