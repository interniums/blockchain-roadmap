An analytics dashboard reports the reserve at 95% utilisation and labels the row *capital efficiency*.
A supplier with 20 units in that reserve opens the app and finds they can withdraw 5.

Both screens are reading the same number. `U = 0.95` is a statement about how productively the pool's
capital is deployed, and it is simultaneously the statement that 95% of the pool has left the building
and cannot be recalled. Utilisation is not a metric with a risk side-effect; it is one variable
wearing two jobs, and every interesting property of a lending pool comes from the fact that the same
scalar sets the price of money and the width of the exit.

## One variable, two readings

Utilisation is total borrows over total supplied:

```text
U = B / S
```

**Reading one — the price input.** In a classic pool `U` is the *sole* argument to the borrow rate.
Not volatility, not the collateral's quality, not the borrower's leverage. One scalar in, one rate out.

**Reading two — the exit gauge.** The tokens that are not borrowed are the tokens sitting in the pool,
so the amount any supplier can withdraw right now, in total, is:

```text
withdrawable = S − B = (1 − U) · S
```

At `U = 0.95` and `S = 100`, exactly 5 units exist to be withdrawn — by everybody, collectively, on a
first-come basis. At `U = 1` the figure is zero, and it is zero regardless of how over-collateralised
every borrower is. **Solvency and liquidity are different properties**, and utilisation measures the
second one while every dashboard narrates the first.

Two derivatives make the exit gauge sharper than it looks.

**Withdrawing raises utilisation, and the effect accelerates.** Take `w` units out and `U` becomes
`B / (S − w)`. Differentiating, `dU/dw = B / (S − w)²`. At `S = 100`, `B = 95`, that is `95/95² ≈ 0.0105`
per unit near the limit: each unit withdrawn adds about one full percentage point of utilisation. The
door narrows as you walk through it, and the last available unit takes `U` to exactly 1.

**Repaying and depositing are not equivalent fixes.** To bring `U` from 0.95 back to 0.90 with supply
held at 100, borrowers must repay 5 units. To do it with borrows held at 95, suppliers must deposit
`95/0.9 − 100 = 5.56` units. Slightly more work on the supply side, and — crucially — the two are done
by different people, with different motivations, reachable only through different incentives.

## The kinked curve, and what its second slope is for

Aave v3's model "is based on two slopes, one before the `OPTIMAL_USAGE_RATIO` point of usage and
another from that point to 100%".<Cite src="aave-v3-rate-strategy-docs" /> The strategy contract
exposes exactly the primitives you need: `getBaseVariableBorrowRate`, `getVariableRateSlope1` (the
variable rate slope for usage between 0 and `OPTIMAL_USAGE_RATIO`), `getVariableRateSlope2` (the slope
above it), and `getMaxVariableBorrowRate` — all in ray. `calculateInterestRates` takes the reserve
state and returns two values: the liquidity rate and the variable borrow
rate.<Cite src="aave-v3-rate-strategy-docs" />

The piecewise form those primitives assemble into is:

```text
U ≤ U_opt :   r(U) = base + slope1 · (U / U_opt)

U > U_opt :   r(U) = base + slope1 + slope2 · ((U − U_opt) / (1 − U_opt))
```

Two checks confirm you have written it correctly. At `U = U_opt` both branches give `base + slope1`, so
the function is continuous — there is a kink, not a jump. And at `U = 1` the upper branch gives
`base + slope1 + slope2`, which must be what `getMaxVariableBorrowRate` returns for that
reserve.<Cite src="aave-v3-rate-strategy-docs" /> If your reimplementation disagrees with the on-chain
getter at `U = 1`, the error is in your parameters, not in the shape.

Between `U = 0.90` and `U = 0.95` the borrow rate goes up elevenfold. That is not a curve someone fitted
to data. It is a control action.

Notice, too, how little the curve knows. Its only argument is a ratio of two aggregates. Two reserves
sitting at `U = 0.90` — one with ten thousand borrowers holding small positions against liquid
collateral, one where a single address holds ninety percent of the debt against a thinly traded token —
produce identical rates, because the model cannot see the difference. Everything about *who* the
borrowers are and *what* backs them is handled elsewhere, by the parameters of the previous two lessons
and the caps of the next but one. The rate curve is a liquidity controller, not a risk model, and
reading it as a risk signal is a category error that dashboards encourage by placing the two side by
side.

### Deriving why the second slope has to be brutal

**Subgoal 1 — establish what the pool is trying to control.** Utilisation is the state variable, and the
target region is "comfortably below 1" so that suppliers can leave. The pool needs a way to push `U`
down when it drifts up.

**Subgoal 2 — enumerate the actuators the pool actually has.** A pool cannot recall a loan; the debt is
over-collateralised and outstanding, and there is no maturity to call. It cannot queue withdrawals; a
queue is a claim on future liquidity that would itself need enforcing, would need an ordering rule that
is not front-runnable, and would convert a liquid deposit into an illiquid one — which is the thing
suppliers deposited to avoid. It cannot mint the missing tokens. What is left is that two groups of
people can restore liquidity voluntarily: borrowers by repaying, suppliers by depositing. Neither takes
instructions.

**Subgoal 3 — identify the only lever that reaches both groups.** The borrow rate. It is simultaneously
the borrower's cost of staying and — via the supply rate, which the next lesson derives — the
supplier's reward for arriving. One number, both audiences, opposite signs.

**Subgoal 4 — size the lever against the job.** Recall the arithmetic above: pulling `U` from 0.95 to
0.90 needs 5% of the pool repaid or 5.6% freshly deposited, and it needs that within hours, not
quarters. A borrower repays when the rate exceeds what the borrowed capital is earning them; a supplier
deposits when the rate exceeds their next-best yield. Moving 5% of a pool inside a day means clearing
the marginal borrower's alternative and the marginal supplier's alternative *by a wide margin*, because
neither of them is watching continuously and both face transaction costs. A gentle slope produces a
gentle response, and a gentle response is no response on the timescale that matters.

**Subgoal 5 — name the mechanism.** The kink is a rationing device implemented as a price. Where a
bank rations the last units of liquidity with a queue and a discretionary decision, a pool rations them
by making the last units unaffordable. Same function, different instrument, and the instrument was
chosen because the other one is not implementable without reintroducing a party with discretion.

<Aside kind="note">
There is an asymmetry inside this design that is rarely stated out loud. The party who pays the
rationing price is the borrower, and the borrower is frequently not the party who caused utilisation
to rise. A single large supplier withdrawing pushes `U` up, and every borrower's rate jumps — including
borrowers who have not touched their position in months. The steep slope is, in effect, a charge
levied on borrowers to fund the supplier's option to leave on demand. Whether that is the right
allocation is a design question with no obviously correct answer; what is not defensible is being
surprised by it. If you are building a product on top of a lending pool, the rate your users pay is a
function of other users' withdrawal decisions, and no amount of care about your own position changes
that.
</Aside>

## A different answer to the same problem: an adaptive curve

The static kink has a weakness that follows directly from the derivation above. `base`, `slope1`,
`slope2` and `U_opt` encode a governance guess about where the market's equilibrium rate sits. If the
guess is wrong — if the market's clearing rate at 90% utilisation is 9% and the curve says 5% — the pool
sits pinned above the kink permanently, and the "emergency" region becomes the normal operating region.

Morpho's response is to make the curve itself move. The `AdaptiveCurveIRM` — currently the only
governance-approved interest rate model in its set — "is engineered to maintain the ratio of borrowed
assets over supplied assets... close to a target of 90%", and works through two mechanisms in
combination.<Cite src="morpho-irm-docs" />

**The curve mechanism** is the familiar part: a multiplicative shape around the target. Starting from a
rate of 4% at the 90% target, utilisation moving to 100% takes the rate instantly to 16% — a factor of
four — and utilisation collapsing to 0% takes it to 1%, a factor of four the other
way.<Cite src="morpho-irm-docs" />

Note where that model *cannot* be changed from. A Morpho market's IRM address is one of the five
parameters fixed at market creation, chosen from a governance-approved set — currently a set of
one — and immutable
thereafter.<Cite src="morpho-market-docs" /><Cite src="morpho-irm-docs" /> Adaptivity is therefore the
only mechanism available to it: nobody can re-tune the curve, so the curve has to re-tune itself.

**The adaptive mechanism** moves the whole curve over time by shifting the target rate itself. When
utilisation sits above target the curve shifts upward, encouraging repayment; below target it shifts
downward, encouraging borrowing. The speed depends on the distance from target: utilisation held at 45%
halves the target rate after 10 days; held at 95% doubles it after 10 days; held at 100% doubles it
after 5 days, which is the maximum speed the model permits.<Cite src="morpho-irm-docs" />

The design rationale is worth reading as an argument about the *first* reading of utilisation rather
than the second. Morpho's documentation notes that supplied collateral is not rehypothecated, and that
removing that systemic risk "removes the liquidity constraints imposed by liquidation needs", enabling
"higher target utilisation of capital and lower penalties for illiquidity".<Cite src="morpho-irm-docs" />
In other words: how high a target utilisation you can defend is a function of what else the pool is
using its liquidity for, not a universal constant.

What the adaptive design gives up is predictability for the borrower. Under a static kink you can read
your future rate off a published curve given a guess about utilisation; under an adaptive one the curve
you are standing on is drifting under you at up to a doubling per five days. Neither is strictly
better. The static model asks governance to know the market rate; the adaptive model asks the borrower
to accept that nobody does.

## Two things utilisation is measured against, and one it cannot see

**The denominator is virtual, on purpose.** Aave does not compute the pool's liquidity from the ERC-20
balance of the aToken contract. `CalculateInterestRatesParams` carries a `usingVirtualBalance` flag and
a `virtualUnderlyingBalance` — "the virtual balance of underlying asset used for mintable
assets"<Cite src="aave-v3-rate-strategy-docs" /> — and `ReserveData` stores `virtualUnderlyingBalance`
as "the virtual balance of the underlying asset for yield calculation purposes", with bit 252 of the
reserve configuration recording whether virtual accounting is enabled at
all.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" />

The consequence is a defence you should recognise from elsewhere in your training. If utilisation were
computed from `token.balanceOf(pool)`, anyone could move the entire reserve's interest rate by sending
tokens to the contract — a [donation attack](/c/donation-attack) whose payload is the rate curve rather
than a share price. Tracking an internal balance that only protocol operations update closes that path
entirely. The general shape is one worth internalising: any protocol variable derived from a raw token
balance is a variable an attacker can write to for the cost of a transfer.

**The supply-side remedy has a cap on it.** Above the kink, the two ways out are borrowers repaying and
suppliers depositing. But depositing is bounded by the reserve's supply cap, in bits 116–151 of the
configuration, denominated in whole tokens, with zero meaning no cap.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" />
A reserve simultaneously at its supply cap and above its kink has had one of its two actuators disabled
by a risk parameter — the rate can climb as high as governance set it and the deposit response it is
trying to summon is not permitted to arrive. This is not a bug; a supply cap is there for reasons the
lesson after next develops at length. It is an interaction, and it is exactly the kind that gets found
during an incident rather than during a parameter review. When you read a reserve's configuration, read
the caps and the curve together and ask which of the two feedback paths is actually open.

## One quiet detail: recognised losses now enter the rate

`calculateInterestRates` takes a struct with an `unbacked` field — "the amount of unbacked
tokens".<Cite src="aave-v3-rate-strategy-docs" /> Aave v3.3 changed the usage assumption without
changing the interface: callers must now pass `reserve.deficit + reserve.unbacked` rather than
`reserve.unbacked` alone, on the stated rationale that "both values represent unbacked tokens on the
pool".<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" />

`deficit` is the protocol's accounted bad debt, which the last lesson of this module covers in full.
The point to take now is structural: once a loss is *recognised*, the rate model sees it, because the
quantity of phantom supply in the pool is an input to the price of money. Before that change, an
unrecoverable loss sat in the books as ordinary debt and the curve priced the reserve as if it were
whole. Which direction the computed rate moves as a result is a property of the strategy contract's
arithmetic and should be read out of `DefaultReserveInterestRateStrategy` rather than inferred from the
changelog — the changelog states the input, not the sign.

<Check type="predict" concept="lend-utilisation">
A USDC reserve has `S = 500M` supplied and `B = 480M` borrowed. Its rate model has `base = 0`,
`slope1 = 6%`, `U_opt = 0.92`, `slope2 = 75%`.

(a) Compute utilisation, the amount withdrawable, and the current borrow rate. (b) A treasury holding
40M in the reserve decides to exit. Describe exactly what happens, including the resulting borrow rate.
(c) A colleague proposes reducing `slope2` from 75% to 20% because "the current rate is punitive to
borrowers who did nothing wrong". State what that change does to the pool, and what a better-targeted
fix would be.

<Answer>
**(a) The current state.** `U = 480/500 = 0.96`. Withdrawable is `(1 − 0.96) × 500M = 20M`. The rate is
above the kink: `r = 0 + 6% + 75% × ((0.96 − 0.92)/(1 − 0.92)) = 6% + 75% × 0.5 = 43.5%`.

**(b) The 40M exit.** Only 20M exists. The treasury withdraws 20M — and that single transaction, if it
lands, takes supply to 480M against borrows of 480M, so `U = 1.00` and the rate goes to its maximum,
`6% + 75% = 81%`. The remaining 20M of the treasury's position cannot be withdrawn at any price until
either a borrower repays or a new supplier deposits. Note who is paying the 81%: every borrower in the
reserve, including those who have not transacted since before the treasury decided anything. Note also
that the treasury is now competing for the next unit of liquidity with every other supplier, on a
first-come basis, which is the structure the final lesson of this module analyses as a run.

**(c) The proposed change.** Halving-and-then-some of `slope2` removes the pool's only actuator. At
`slope2 = 20%` the rate at `U = 0.96` falls to `6% + 20% × 0.5 = 16%`, which is comfortable enough that
a leveraged borrower will happily sit there indefinitely. Utilisation then has no force pushing it
down, the pool spends its life against the ceiling, and the supplier who wants 40M back never gets it.
The proposal treats the high rate as the problem when the high rate is the *response* to the problem.

**A better-targeted fix.** The complaint — that borrowers pay for a supply-side event — is legitimate.
The parameters that address it without disabling the control loop are `U_opt` and `slope1`: raising the
kink means the steep region starts later, so ordinary operation stays in the gentle branch, while the
emergency response remains intact when it is genuinely needed. If the reserve is *chronically* above
the kink rather than occasionally, that is evidence the curve's guess about the equilibrium rate is
wrong, and the fix is to re-fit the curve — or to adopt a model that re-fits itself, as the adaptive
design above does.<Cite src="morpho-irm-docs" />

**The transferable diagnosis.** When a lending pool is behaving badly, ask first whether you are
looking at the disease or the immune response. A rate spike above the kink is the immune response, and
suppressing it does not treat anything.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** derives the number this one deliberately withheld: what a *supplier* earns. It is
not the borrow rate, nobody sets it, and it falls straight out of utilisation and one more parameter.
The same lesson takes up how a pool with a million accounts accrues interest to all of them without
iterating over any of them — which is also why a cached balance is wrong within one block.

**The module's last lesson** takes the exit-gauge reading of utilisation to its conclusion: when a loss
becomes known but is not yet realised, withdrawing first is individually rational for every supplier,
the race pins utilisation at 1, and the loss and the run turn out to be two separate harms with two
separate remedies.

**[The practice for this module](/c/lend-rate-kink)** has you build the kinked model and fuzz its
properties — monotonicity, continuity at the kink, and the relationship between the two rates — rather
than spot-checking values, which is the only way to be sure your reimplementation matches a curve you
cannot see the source of.
