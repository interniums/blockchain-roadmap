The reserve page shows two numbers, stacked, in the same typeface:

```text
Borrow APY   6.00%
Supply APY   2.55%
```

Between them, no explanation. A reader can be forgiven for concluding that the protocol takes 57.5% of
the interest, which would be an extraordinary rate of extraction and is not what is happening.

The supply rate is not a fee schedule and it is not a parameter. Nobody sets it. It is a derived
quantity, it falls out of two numbers you already have, and the derivation is four lines long. This
lesson does those four lines, then answers the question they raise — how a pool credits interest to a
million accounts without touching a million storage slots.

Both halves matter for different reasons. The first is the difference between reading a yield figure
and understanding what you are being paid for; it is also the arithmetic that makes an entire class of
comparison — this protocol pays more than that one — meaningless without two further numbers. The
second is the accounting mechanism that every integration against a lending pool eventually collides
with, usually in the form of a balance that changed while nobody was looking.

## Deriving the supply rate from conservation

**Subgoal 1 — identify the only source of yield.** A lending pool produces no revenue of its own. Every
unit of interest a supplier receives was paid by a borrower. In a unit of time, borrowers pay
`r_b · B`, where `B` is total borrows. That is the entire pot. Nothing else enters it.

**Subgoal 2 — remove the protocol's cut.** Aave's `calculateInterestRates` takes a `reserveFactor`
parameter, documented as "the reserve portion of the interest that goes to the treasury of the
market".<Cite src="aave-v3-rate-strategy-docs" /> Suppliers therefore share `(1 − RF) · r_b · B`.

**Subgoal 3 — spread it over the right denominator.** This is the step everyone skips. Suppliers are
paid *on their whole deposit*, but the pot was generated only by the borrowed fraction. Dividing the
pot by total supply `S`:

```text
r_s = (1 − RF) · r_b · B / S
    = r_b · U · (1 − RF)
```

**Subgoal 4 — check it against a second, independent statement of the same fact.** Morpho documents
`supplyAPY = borrowAPY × utilization × (1 − fee)`, where the fee is the market's governance-set
fee.<Cite src="morpho-irm-docs" /> Different protocol, different vocabulary, identical identity —
because it is not a design choice. It is what conservation of interest looks like when supply and
borrows are different quantities.

Now the opening numbers make sense. At `U = 0.50` and `RF = 0.15`:

```text
r_s = 6.00% × 0.50 × 0.85 = 2.55%
```

The protocol took 15%. Utilisation took the other 43%, and "took" is the wrong verb: half the pool was
never lent, so it earned nothing, and averaging that zero across every supplier is the whole of the
gap.

<Aside kind="note">
Notice what is missing from the derivation: a governance parameter for the supply rate. There is no
`setSupplyRate`. Aave's strategy contract returns both rates from one call — `calculateInterestRates`
"returns only two values: the liquidity rate and the variable borrow rate", both in
ray.<Cite src="aave-v3-rate-strategy-docs" /> The liquidity rate is an *output*. If you are reading a
protocol's code looking for where the supply APY is configured, you will not find it, and that absence
is the design.
</Aside>

## The supply curve is not the borrow curve shifted down

Substituting the previous lesson's kinked borrow rate into `r_s = r_b · U · (1 − RF)` gives a curve of a
different *shape*, not a parallel one. Below the kink `r_b` is linear in `U`, so `r_s` is quadratic;
above it, quadratic with a far larger coefficient. Take the same illustrative parameters — `base = 0`,
`slope1 = 5%`, `U_opt = 0.90`, `slope2 = 100%` — with a 15% reserve factor:

Three things in that table are worth more than the arithmetic that produced them.

**The supply rate collapses toward zero at low utilisation, however attractive the borrow rate looks.**
At `U = 0.10` a 0.56% borrow rate becomes 0.05% for suppliers — a factor of eleven, not a fee. Anyone
evaluating a new market on its borrow rate is reading the number that does not apply to them.

**The third column is the same variable as the first two.** The supply APY and the withdrawal
availability are both functions of `U`, moving in opposite directions by construction. There is no
region of this curve where a supplier is well paid *and* can leave.

**The bottom row is the one to sit with.** An 89% supply APY, and zero withdrawable. That is not a
generous market; it is a market whose price is screaming that it has run out of liquidity. Yield
aggregators sort by the second column.

### What the reserve factor actually costs, in the units of the other variable

The reserve factor scales the whole expression, so its effect is easy to state exactly: a reserve
factor of `RF` costs a supplier the same as utilisation being `(1 − RF)` times lower. A 15% reserve
factor at `U = 0.50` pays precisely what a zero-reserve-factor pool at `U = 0.425` pays. That
equivalence is the honest way to communicate the parameter, and it is why the two deductions in the
opening example should never be described in the same breath: one is a 15% cut of the interest, and the
other is the fact that half the pool was idle.

Differentiating the identity gives the sensitivity in one line: relative changes in `r_s` are the sum
of the relative changes in `r_b` and `U`. A ten percent relative move in utilisation matters exactly as
much as a ten percent relative move in the borrow rate — and utilisation is the one that moves without
any parameter changing.

## Why a headline supply APY is three numbers compressed into one

Once you have `r_s = r_b · U · (1 − RF)`, comparing supply APYs across protocols stops being possible
without unpacking. Three reserves, three completely different positions, one identical headline:

/>

Read them as a risk analyst rather than as a yield shopper.

**Reserve A** is a pool at half utilisation with no protocol tax. A supplier can withdraw half the pool
today. The 4% is being paid because borrowing is expensive, and the pool is liquid.

**Reserve B** is at 100% utilisation. Nobody can withdraw anything, at any price, until a borrower
repays. The 4% is the yield on a position you cannot exit — which is not the same asset as the 4% in
column A, and no interface distinguishes them.

**Reserve C** charges borrowers 20% at a quarter utilisation. Under a two-slope model, a rate that high
so far below the optimal usage ratio means a high base variable borrow rate or a steep first
slope<Cite src="aave-v3-rate-strategy-docs" /> — which is how a risk framework prices an asset it is
nervous about. The 4% is compensation for something.

Same number, three different products. Any lending analysis that compares headline supply APYs without
recovering utilisation and the reserve factor is comparing three variables through one, and the
variable it is most likely to be blind to is the one that decides whether the money comes back.

<Aside kind="note">
One more decompression before you compare: APY and APR are not the same statement. Morpho's model
documents the borrow APY as `e^(borrowRate × 31,536,000) − 1`, with `borrowRate` a per-second rate and
`secondsPerYear = 31,536,000` — continuous compounding.<Cite src="morpho-irm-docs" /> A nominal 20%
compounded that way is `e^0.20 − 1 = 22.14%`, and a nominal 60% is `e^0.60 − 1 = 82.21%`. The gap is
negligible at 2% and enormous in the steep region above the kink, which is exactly where somebody is
most likely to be quoting you a number.
</Aside>

## How the pool pays a million accounts without touching them

The supply rate applies continuously, to everyone, at once. The obvious implementation — iterate over
holders and credit each one — is not merely expensive, it is unbounded: cost grows with adoption, and
the pool would become unusable at exactly the point it succeeded.

Put a figure on "expensive". A storage write to a slot that already holds a non-zero value costs
**2,900 gas**, plus **2,100** when the slot is cold, measured on forge 1.7.1 with solc
0.8.36.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /> Crediting a million accounts is therefore at least
2.9 billion gas for a single accrual event, and accrual is not an event — it is a continuous property.
The design cannot be repaired by optimisation; it has to be avoided.

### The substitution

**Subgoal 1 — state the invariant to preserve.** Over any interval, every supplier's balance should
grow by the same multiplicative factor. That is what "everyone earns the same rate" means.

**Subgoal 2 — factor the common part out of the per-account state.** If every balance grows by the same
factor, write each balance as a constant times a shared, time-varying quantity:

```text
balance_i(t) = scaledBalance_i × I(t)
```

`scaledBalance_i` is per account and changes only when that account acts. `I(t)` is per reserve and
changes with time. All the movement has been moved into one variable.

**Subgoal 3 — define the write path.** When account `i` deposits `a` at time `t`:

```text
scaledBalance_i += a / I(t)
```

and when it withdraws, the reverse. The index is never rewritten per account; the account is
normalised into the index's units at the moment it touches the pool.

**Subgoal 4 — locate the real thing.** Aave's `ReserveData` holds `liquidityIndex` and
`variableBorrowIndex`, both `uint128` in ray, alongside `currentLiquidityRate`,
`currentVariableBorrowRate` and a `lastUpdateTimestamp` (`uint40`) that the documentation describes as
"used for yield calculation".<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> The current values are read through
`getReserveNormalizedIncome` and `getReserveNormalizedVariableDebt`.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" />
Two indices, not one, because suppliers and borrowers accrue at different rates — which is the supply
rate derivation, expressed as two separate integrals.

**Subgoal 5 — check the cost.** One index update per reserve per interaction, independent of the number
of accounts. `O(1)`, forever. This is not an optimisation of the naive design; it is the only reason a
lending pool with a large user base is possible on chain at all.

**Subgoal 6 — notice where the protocol's own cut went.** The treasury cannot be paid every second
either. `ReserveData` carries `accruedToTreasury`, and the documentation marks it
**scaled**.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> The reserve factor is implemented as one more scaled
balance riding the same index, not as a transfer. Every participant in the pool, including the DAO, is
a number multiplied by the same rising figure.

### Working the arithmetic, and watching the dust

Indices are stored in ray — 27 decimals — as are both rates that drive
them.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /><Cite src="aave-v3-rate-strategy-docs" /> Take a supply index of
`1.05e27` and a deposit of 1,000 tokens with 18 decimals.

**Mint.** `scaled = 1000e18 × 1e27 / 1.05e27 = 952.380952380952380952` tokens' worth of scaled units.
The true value is `952.380952380952380952380952...`, so the integer division has discarded a remainder.

**Read, later, at an index of `1.10e27`.**
`balance = 952.380952380952380952 × 1.10 = 1047.619047619047619047`, against an exact value of
`1000 × 1.10/1.05 = 1047.619047619047619047619...`.

The account is short by roughly two ten-thousandths of a wei-scale unit. That direction is not
incidental. Truncating on mint means the pool holds marginally more than the sum of its obligations;
truncating the other way — rounding the scaled balance *up* — would mean each deposit created a claim
slightly larger than the deposit, and the repeated-small-operations version of that is a drain rather
than a rounding note. This is the [rounding direction](/c/rounding-direction) discipline from the core
track applied to the one place in a lending pool where every single balance passes through a division.

### What this changes about integrating with a lending pool

**A balance is a view that multiplies, not a stored number.** `balanceOf` reads a scaled balance and a
current index. The result changes continuously with no transaction touching the account. Any cached
balance is stale within a block, and code that snapshots a balance, does something, and compares is
comparing against a moving reference.

**Two reads in one transaction can disagree.** The index advances when the reserve's state is updated,
so a balance read before an external call into the pool is not guaranteed to equal one read after it.
If you need a stable figure across a sequence, read the *scaled* balance, which only your own
operations change.

**Sum-of-balances does not equal total supply, exactly.** Every account's balance passed through its
own truncation. An invariant test asserting exact equality will fail on a fuzz run and the failure will
be correct. The invariant worth asserting is that the sum of balances never *exceeds* the total, which
is the property the rounding direction was chosen to guarantee — the shape of specification your
[invariant work](/c/invariant-as-spec) in the core track was preparing you to write.

**Transfers move scaled units.** Transferring "1,000 tokens" of an interest-bearing balance transfers
`1000 / I` scaled units, and reading the recipient's balance afterwards returns approximately 1,000,
dust excluded. Integrations that reconcile transfer amounts against post-transfer balance deltas will
find off-by-a-few-wei discrepancies that are correct behaviour, not a bug in either contract.

<Aside kind="warn">
The index is also where a protocol's accounting can be quietly reused. Aave v3.3 stores a reserve's
`deficit` by re-purposing the deprecated `__deprecatedStableBorrowRate` storage slot in
`ReserveData`, exposed through a new `getReserveDeficit`.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" /> A field
carrying one meaning in an older deployment carries a different meaning in a newer one, at the same
slot. If you have an indexer, a subgraph or a monitoring job that reads `ReserveData` positionally
rather than by name, an upgrade like that changes what your dashboard is showing without changing a
line of your code.
</Aside>

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="lend-index-accounting">
A vault contract deposits user funds into a lending pool and issues its own shares. Two functions
below are wrong in different ways. Name both defects, say which one loses money and which one merely
reports the wrong number, and give the corrected approach for each.

```solidity
uint256 public totalDeposited;              // "how much we put in"
mapping(address => uint256) public shares;
uint256 public totalShares;

function deposit(uint256 amount) external {
    asset.transferFrom(msg.sender, address(this), amount);
    pool.supply(address(asset), amount, address(this), 0);

    uint256 newShares = totalShares == 0
        ? amount
        : amount * totalShares / totalDeposited;
    shares[msg.sender] += newShares;
    totalShares        += newShares;
    totalDeposited     += amount;            // (1)
}

function pricePerShare() external view returns (uint256) {
    return totalDeposited * 1e18 / totalShares;   // (2)
}
```

<Answer>
**Defect one — `totalDeposited` is a running sum of principal, not a balance.** It is incremented by the
deposited amount and never by accrued interest, because interest arrives through a rising index rather
than through any transaction the vault observes. The correct value is
`aToken.balanceOf(address(this))`, which is a scaled balance multiplied by the current
index at read time.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" />

**This is the one that loses money — through the share price used at mint.** Because `totalDeposited`
undercounts the vault's real assets, `amount * totalShares / totalDeposited` issues *too many* shares
to every new depositor. Concretely: the vault holds 1,000 principal that has accrued to 1,050, with
1,000 shares outstanding. A depositor adding 1,050 should receive 1,000 shares. They receive
`1050 × 1000 / 1000 = 1,050`, and now own 51.2% of a pool to which they contributed 51.2% of the
capital but zero of the accrued interest. Every existing holder has been diluted by the interest the
vault earned. Repeat the deposit in a loop and the dilution is systematic, not incidental.

**Defect two — `pricePerShare` reports the same stale figure.** This one loses no money directly; it
misreports. But it is worse than a cosmetic bug, because it is the number every integrator, dashboard
and downstream oracle will read, and it will look plausible — flat, stable, and wrong by exactly the
accrued yield. A rate oracle built on this getter would price the vault share below its true value
permanently, which is its own category of hazard.

**The correction for both.** Delete `totalDeposited`. Read the live balance:

```solidity
function totalAssets() public view returns (uint256) {
    return aToken.balanceOf(address(this));
}
```

and compute shares and price per share from `totalAssets()`. Then check the rounding: shares issued on
deposit should round **down** and assets returned on withdrawal should round **down**, so that every
truncation leaves the residual inside the vault rather than in the caller's hands. Note that switching
to a live balance re-opens the first-depositor share-inflation question you met in the core track —
which is why a live-balance vault also wants a
[dead-shares or virtual-offset defence](/c/inflation-first-depositor-attack) rather than only the fix
above.

**The habit worth extracting.** In a system where balances accrue through an index, *any* state variable
you maintain that shadows a balance is guaranteed to diverge from it. The question to ask of a shadow
variable is never "is it updated correctly" — it is "why does it exist at all".
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** turns from how the pool accounts to how much damage a single wrong listing can do,
and derives why the crude parameters — supply caps, borrow caps, isolation-mode debt ceilings — are the
only risk levers whose effect does not depend on the risk model being right. It also examines a case,
documented by a protocol against its own product, where a cap provably does *not* bound the loss.

**The final lesson** needs everything from this one. Bad debt is debt that keeps accruing through the
borrow index against collateral that no longer exists, so the phantom asset grows on precisely the
mechanism just derived — and the first thing a protocol does about it is stop the index from touching
it.

**In production**, the index-accounting habits above are what your monitoring is built on: a balance
that must be read rather than cached, a total that must be recomputed rather than accumulated, and an
invariant that holds with an inequality rather than an equality.
