A bank underwriter deciding whether to lend you money has four instruments, and only one of them is
the collateral. They can check that you are who you say you are. They can look at what you have
repaid before. They can, if it goes wrong, sue you, garnish wages, report the default to an agency
that will price your next loan for years, and — last — seize the thing you pledged.

A lending contract has exactly one of those four, and it is the last one. Everything else in this
module is a consequence of that sentence, so it is worth being precise about why the other three are
gone rather than merely absent.

## Three instruments a contract cannot hold

**Identity is not scarce.** Address creation is free and unlimited. There is no "you" for the contract
to recognise across loans, so defaulting carries no reputational cost: the defaulter funds a fresh
address and returns as a first-time borrower. Every mechanism that depends on a borrower having
something to lose *outside the current transaction* fails at this step, and fails completely. This is
not a claim that on-chain identity is impossible — it is a claim that identity a *permissionless*
protocol can rely on requires an issuer, and an issuer is a permission.

**Recourse requires a court.** Suing presumes a legal person, a jurisdiction, and a body willing to
enforce a judgement against assets it can reach. A protocol has an address. Even where the borrower is
a known, named, solvent entity, the protocol cannot act on that fact; some human has to, off-chain,
under a contract that exists off-chain.

**Repossession only reaches what the protocol already holds.** A contract can move tokens it has
custody of. It cannot reach a car, a salary, or a token in a wallet it was never given control of. The
seizure right is not a general power over the borrower's property; it is a power over one specific
pre-positioned balance.

What survives is a claim on assets already inside the system. And a claim on assets already inside
the system is only worth something if those assets are worth more than the debt, because otherwise
the borrower's optimal move — at the moment the collateral is worth less than what they owe — is
simply to stop repaying and keep the borrowed funds. There is nothing further to lose.

That is the whole derivation. Every loan a permissionless pool makes must be backed by collateral
worth strictly more than the debt, because the pool's only enforcement instrument is the collateral,
and an instrument worth less than the obligation does not enforce anything.

<Aside kind="note">
This is a *structural* result, not an efficiency result. It does not say under-collateralised on-chain
credit is hard, or expensive, or awaiting better tooling. It says that a design offering it has
reintroduced one of the three missing instruments somewhere, and that the place it was reintroduced
is where the actual trust now lives. When you meet such a design, the interesting question is never
"how do they underwrite" — it is "who can they sue, and what stops the borrower creating a second
address".
</Aside>

## Sizing the excess: what the buffer has to cover

"Worth more than the debt" is a floor, not a parameter. The parameter is how much more, and it is
derivable from the sequence of events that has to complete successfully between the position becoming
unsafe and the debt being retired.

**Subgoal 1 — establish what the protocol is actually racing.** The protocol does not need collateral
worth more than the debt *today*. It needs collateral that will still be worth more than the debt at
the moment somebody finishes selling it. Between those two moments the price keeps moving.

**Subgoal 2 — enumerate what consumes the buffer.** Four things, in order of when they bite. The price
move between the last oracle update the protocol acted on and the price a seller actually gets. The
discount a liquidator demands for doing the work, which comes entirely out of the collateral — on
Morpho the whole liquidation incentive goes to the liquidator and the protocol takes no
fee.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" /> The gas the liquidating transaction costs, which the
liquidator prices into that discount. And the slippage of selling the seized amount into whatever venue
exists, which grows with position size and shrinks with market depth — and which is worst precisely
during the price move that triggered the liquidation.

**Subgoal 3 — notice that these are not independent.** Volatility raises the first term, congestion
raises the third, and a crash empties the fourth. They peak together. A buffer calibrated against
average conditions is calibrated against conditions that do not occur when it is needed.

**Subgoal 4 — read the parameter back off a real system.** Morpho Blue's governance-approved set of
liquidation loan-to-value values is discrete and published: 0, 38.5%, 62.5%, 77%, 86%, 91.5%, 94.5%,
96.5% and 98%.<Cite src="morpho-market-docs" /> The 98% entry says the designers believe there exists
a pair of assets for which two percent of collateral value covers all four terms above. The 38.5%
entry says there exist pairs for which sixty-one and a half percent does not obviously suffice. Same
protocol, same code, a sixteen-fold difference in the implied buffer — because the buffer is a claim
about the asset pair, not about lending.

Note also *who* makes the claim there. Market creation on Blue is permissionless, and every parameter
is selected at creation and persists in perpetuity — only the LLTV and the interest rate model must
come from governance-approved sets.<Cite src="morpho-market-docs" /> The buffer is chosen once, by
whoever deploys the market, and then nobody, governance included, can move
it.<Cite src="mixbytes-morpho-blue" />

The rest of this module is the machinery that turns that single constraint into a working system: two
thresholds instead of one, a health factor that moves without you, a price of money that doubles as an
exit gauge, and — when the buffer turns out to have been sized on a false premise — a loss that has to
land on somebody named in advance.

## The two cases that look like counterexamples

Both are real, both are in production, and both are more instructive than the rule itself.

### The flash loan: atomicity as a substitute for recourse

A flash loan is genuinely uncollateralised, and it is the only such loan on the system. Aave's `flashLoan`
takes an array of assets and amounts and an `interestRateModes` array where a mode of `0` means "do not
open any debt — the amount plus fee must be paid in this case, or the call reverts if the funds cannot
be transferred back".<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> Pass mode `2` instead and the flash loan converts
into an ordinary variable-rate borrow against the `onBehalfOf` account, which must already hold the
collateral and the borrow allowance — at which point it is not uncollateralised at all.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" />

Look at what replaced the collateral in the mode-`0` case. Not a credit assessment. Not identity. The
transaction is the unit of atomicity, so a state in which the loan is outstanding and unrepaid is a
state that the EVM will never commit. The lender has not been given a claim over the borrower's
assets; it has been given the ability to *un-happen* the loan. That is a stronger enforcement
instrument than a court, and it is available for exactly as long as one transaction, which is why the
technique is confined to operations that both start and finish inside one call frame. You already know
this machinery from [flash loans](/c/flash-loan); what is worth carrying forward is the framing:
over-collateralisation and atomicity are two answers to the same enforcement question, and a protocol
that has neither is making an unsecured loan whether it calls it that or not.

### Credit delegation: the recourse moved, it did not disappear

Aave's credit delegation lets a supplier delegate borrowing power to another address. The delegator
calls `approveDelegation` (or `delegationWithSig`) on the variable debt token for some amount, and the
delegatee then calls `borrow` on the Pool passing the delegator's address as
`onBehalfOf`.<Cite src="aave-credit-delegation-docs" /> The delegatee posts no collateral. The Aave
documentation describes this as giving delegatees "access to uncollateralized
liquidity".<Cite src="aave-credit-delegation-docs" />

Now read the sentence in the same document that says where the enforcement went: the terms of the
borrow position "are agreed upon between the supplier and borrowers, which can be either offchain via
legal agreements or onchain via smart contracts".<Cite src="aave-credit-delegation-docs" /> The
protocol's exposure did not change at all. The debt sits on the delegator's account, backed by the
delegator's collateral, and the borrow reverts outright if it would push the delegator's health factor
below the liquidation threshold.<Cite src="aave-credit-delegation-docs" /> From the pool's point of
view this is an ordinary over-collateralised loan. What is uncollateralised is the *second* loan — the
one from the delegator to the delegatee — and that one is enforced by a legal agreement between two
identified parties, in a jurisdiction, exactly as the underwriter at the top of this lesson would have
done it.

Delegation is therefore not a counterexample. It is the clearest available demonstration of the rule:
the instruments the protocol lacks were reintroduced, off-chain, by the party willing to bear them.

<Check type="explain" concept="lend-overcollateralisation">
A team proposes an under-collateralised lending market. Borrowers are whitelisted institutions that
have signed a master loan agreement with a legal entity the DAO controls; the entity indemnifies the
pool for defaults up to a stated limit. They argue this is on-chain credit, finally solved. Name what
the design has actually done, and identify the two questions a supplier must ask that would not arise
in an over-collateralised market.

<Answer>
**What it did.** It reintroduced identity (a whitelist), recourse (a master loan agreement in a named
jurisdiction) and a substitute for repossession (an indemnity from a balance sheet). That is a
complete answer to the enforcement problem. It is also, definitionally, a permissioned system: the
whitelist is the permission, and someone decides who is on it. Nothing here is illegitimate — it is
simply a different product, and the protocol's solvency now depends on off-chain facts that no chain
state reflects.

**Question one: what is the indemnity worth, and when?** In an over-collateralised market the backing
is a token balance the pool holds and can sell in the same transaction as the liquidation. Here it is
a promise, payable after a default is established, probably after a dispute, capped by a stated limit
and by the indemnitor's assets. A supplier is now holding unsecured credit exposure to that entity,
which is a different risk from the one advertised.

**Question two: does the pool's own accounting distinguish this exposure from the rest?** If the
whitelisted debt sits in the same pool as ordinary over-collateralised debt, a default socialises
across every supplier of that asset, including those who never opted into the credit product. That is
the [pooled-versus-isolated](/c/lend-isolated-vs-pooled) question this module reaches later, and it
decides whether this is a product for the suppliers who want it or a hidden liability for everyone.

**The framing that generalises.** The right question about any under-collateralised design is never
"how good is the underwriting" but "name the party who can be compelled, and name what compels them".
If the answer is off-chain, so is the security of the loan.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** splits the single ratio you have been carrying — "collateral worth more than the
debt" — into the two distinct parameters every real system uses, and shows why the gap between them is
the only thing standing between a maximum-leverage borrower and immediate seizure. It also derives the
health factor properly, including the part almost every interface gets wrong: what a given health
factor means in *price* terms depends on which leg of the position is the volatile one.

**Later in this module**, the same constraint reappears as the question of who eats the loss when the
buffer was sized against a premise that turned out to be false — which, in the largest 2026 case, was
not a mispriced asset but one that was never backed at all.

**Later in this track**, [liquidation](/c/liq-why-liquidation-exists) converts the seizure right into
cash before the buffer is exhausted, and [oracles](/c/orac-price-feed) are what tell the protocol the
buffer is shrinking. Both are implementation details of the one instrument a permissionless lender
actually has.
