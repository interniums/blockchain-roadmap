A liquidation is a forced sale into the market that just moved against the position. That sentence
contains a feedback loop, and the loop has a gain, and the gain is computable.

This lesson computes it. Then it shows the two channels by which the loop crosses a boundary it is
usually assumed to respect: from a venue you do not control into your oracle, and from one "isolated"
market into another through a denominator they happen to share. The second channel is the one that
surprises people who have already thought carefully about the first.

## The loop, and the condition under which it runs away

Set up the two directions of causation separately, then close them into a cycle.

**Subgoal 1 — how much selling a price move produces.** Let `D(p)` be the total debt in a market that
is liquidatable at collateral price `p`. A fractional fall `δ` makes `ΔD = D(p(1−δ)) − D(p)` newly
eligible. Define the **liquidation density**

```text
ρ  =  ΔD / δ          [dollars of debt per unit fractional price fall]
```

Retiring that debt at incentive factor `LIF` seizes and sells collateral worth `ΔD · LIF` — the
`b_gross` multiplier from lesson one, showing up as a market order.

**Subgoal 2 — how much price move a given amount of selling produces.** From the constant-product
relation you already have: with reserves `(x, y)` and `p = y/x`, selling `Δx` gives
`p'/p = (1 + Δx/x)^{-2}`, so the fractional price fall is about `2·Δx/x` for small trades. In value
terms, selling `S` into a venue whose collateral-side reserve is worth `R` moves price down by about
`2S/R`.

**Subgoal 3 — close the loop and read the gain.** The second-round price fall is
`δ' = 2 · ΔD · LIF / R`. The loop amplifies when `δ' > δ`:

```text
2 · ρ · LIF / R  >  1        ⟺        ρ  >  R / (2 · LIF)
```

**Subgoal 4 — put a number on it so the scale is legible.** Take $50M of exit depth and `LIF = 1.05`.
The boundary is `ρ = 50,000,000 / 2.1 ≈ $23.8M` per unit fall — which per *one percent* of price
movement is about **$238,000 of newly liquidatable debt**. If a 1% move makes more than a quarter of a
million dollars of debt eligible, the cascade sustains itself against fifty million dollars of depth.

Check the arithmetic in the other direction: $238k of debt at 1.05 sells $250k of collateral;
`2 × 250,000 / 50,000,000 = 1%`. The move reproduces itself exactly. That is what a gain of one means.

**Subgoal 5 — notice that `R` is not a constant.** This is the part that makes the loop dangerous
rather than merely reflexive. Every round consumes depth, so `R` falls, so the threshold `R/(2·LIF)`
falls, so a loop that started subcritical can become supercritical after two rounds without anything
else changing. The system does not have a fixed gain; it has a gain that rises as it runs.

**Subgoal 6 — find what stops it.** Two things. `D` is finite, so the loop exhausts the positions
eventually; or outside capital arrives and replenishes `R`. It does not stop from running out of
blocks, because nothing in the mechanism is rate-limited by anything except block time.

That last observation is the argument for a close factor that lesson two did not make. A cap on how
much one call may retire is a **rate limiter on the loop**: it forces `ΔD` to arrive in slices across
more transactions and more blocks, which buys wall-clock time for outside liquidity to show up. It
does not change the total that must eventually be sold. It changes the rate, and rate is the only
variable an outside buyer responds to. Aave's own framing of the close factor is that it limits how
much of a debt position can be repaid in a single liquidation, which is the same sentence read as a
throughput bound rather than a borrower protection.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" />

<Aside kind="note">
The `2` in `2S/R` is specific to constant product. For a concentrated-liquidity venue the local
relation is different and generally *worse* inside a narrow active range and better once the trade
walks into deeper ticks — which means the effective `R` for a cascade is not the number on a TVL
dashboard but the depth actually available within the price band the cascade traverses. Substitute
your venue's real depth profile; the structure of the condition is unchanged.
</Aside>

<Check type="predict" concept="liq-cascade">
A market has $80M of collateral-side depth on the venue liquidators exit through, a 1.08 incentive
factor, and a position book in which roughly $600,000 of debt becomes liquidatable for every 1% fall
in the collateral price. Is the loop subcritical or supercritical? Then say what happens after two
rounds if each round consumes 15% of the depth.

<Answer>
**Round one.** `ρ` per unit fall is `600,000 / 0.01 = $60M`. The boundary is
`R / (2·LIF) = 80,000,000 / 2.16 ≈ $37.0M`. Since `60M > 37.0M`, the loop is **supercritical** from the
start. Concretely: a 1% fall makes $600k eligible, which sells `600,000 × 1.08 = $648,000`, which moves
price by `2 × 648,000 / 80,000,000 = 1.62%`. The move amplified by a factor of 1.62.

**Round two.** Depth is now `80M × 0.85 = $68M`. The 1.62% fall makes `1.62 × 600,000 ≈ $972,000`
eligible, selling $1.05M, moving price by `2 × 1,050,000 / 68,000,000 ≈ 3.09%`. Amplification is now
1.91 rather than 1.62 — the gain rose because the denominator shrank.

**Round three.** Depth `≈ $57.8M`. The 3.09% fall makes about $1.85M eligible, selling $2.0M, moving
price about 6.9%. Gain 2.24.

**The shape.** Roughly geometric with a growing ratio: 1%, 1.6%, 3.1%, 6.9%. Four rounds — under a
minute of block time — take the price down more than 12%, and the arithmetic is accelerating rather
than converging. Nothing in the protocol notices, because every individual liquidation was profitable,
correctly priced and entirely within parameters.

**What would have changed it.** Getting `ρ` below $37M per unit fall, which means fewer positions
clustered near the threshold — the job of caps, of not marketing high leverage on one asset, and of
not having every borrower use the same round-numbered LTV. Or a higher `R`, which is not a parameter
anybody controls. Note that lowering `LIF` from 1.08 to 1.05 moves the boundary only from $37.0M to
$38.1M: the incentive factor is in the condition but it is not the lever, because it enters linearly
while depth enters as the whole denominator.
</Answer>
</Check>

## The reference event, and how to cite it honestly

10 October 2025 replaced Black Thursday as the canonical cascade, on scale rather than on novelty. A
market carrying very large open interest deleveraged over roughly a day with no circuit breakers, and
the widely reported total is about nineteen billion dollars
liquidated.<Cite src="coindesk-19b-liquidation" />

That number requires a caveat that is not optional. Exchange liquidation feeds are known to
under-report — many venues publish at most one liquidation per second per symbol, so simultaneous
liquidations collapse into a single reported event — and some analysts argue the true figure is
materially higher.<Cite src="coindesk-19b-liquidation" /> **The headline is a reported floor, not a
measurement.** Cite it as a lower bound or do not cite it.

<Misconception
  belief="The reported liquidation total for a cascade is a measurement of what was liquidated."
  reality="It is the sum of what venues chose to publish, under publication rules that deliberately down-sample. Treat every such headline as a floor whose distance from the true figure is unknown, and never build a comparison between two events on two such numbers."
  why="A single large number is easier to write, easier to remember and easier to cite than a bound with a stated sampling artefact attached to it."
  src="coindesk-19b-liquidation"
/>

What is safe to carry from the event is the structure. Very large leveraged open interest, no
mechanism anywhere in the stack that slows the loop, and the on-chain consequence arriving as bad debt
rather than as a failure of any contract: Curve's CRV-collateral LlamaLend market accumulated bad debt
during the crash because prices moved faster than keepers could profitably act on while gas costs
spiked.<Cite src="coindesk-curve-bad-debt" /> No contract was exploited, no parameter was misconfigured
by any conventional standard, and a hole appeared anyway.

## Channel one: the oracle carries the cascade in

Here is the fact that makes on-chain lending permanently exposed to venues it has no relationship with.

An on-chain lending market computes health from an oracle. A push oracle aggregates off-chain sources
and writes on a deviation threshold or a heartbeat, and its consumers are expected to check freshness
and handle staleness themselves.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" /> Those sources are the venues
where leveraged derivatives trade. So when perpetual-futures positions on a centralised exchange are
force-closed, the resulting price is the price your lending market reads — and note that the
aggregation is doing its job correctly. Nothing is manipulated. The feed is faithfully reporting a
market in which a lot of leverage is being unwound.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" />

Walk the consequence carefully, because the surprising part is what is *absent*:

- nothing was borrowed on chain;
- nothing was sold on chain;
- no transaction touched the lending protocol;
- and every position's health factor moved.

A lending protocol's tail risk is therefore a function of leverage sitting on venues it cannot
observe, cannot cap and cannot pause. Supply caps bound how much of an asset your market
accepts;<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> they do not bound how much of it is levered somewhere else,
and it is the somewhere-else that sets your oracle.

This also disposes of the idea that a protocol-level circuit breaker helps. A breaker can only fire on
something it observes, and by the time the cascade is observable on chain it is already in the price —
which means the breaker fires after the health factors have already moved, and the only thing left to
suspend is the liquidation that would have retired the debt.

<Misconception
  belief="On-chain lending is insulated from centralised-exchange leverage, because the two systems have separate venues and separate users."
  reality="The oracle is the transmission channel and it runs one way, into you. Off-chain leverage sets the price your health factors are computed from, so a deleveraging you cannot see produces liquidations you must handle."
  why="On-chain and off-chain are treated as separate systems because the venues are separate, and the oracle is filed as a data dependency rather than as a risk dependency."
  src="coindesk-19b-liquidation"

## Channel two: markets that share a denominator are not isolated

The second channel is subtler and better documented, and it is the reason "isolated markets" is a
weaker guarantee than it sounds. It has a clean case study.

In June 2024, Curve Lend's CRV-long market went through a liquidation cascade. The market itself was
large: nearly 200M CRV supplied, about $70M of collateral at the time, with peak debt of $55M, and CRV
fell to around $0.20 on 12 June.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" /> Lenders withdrew crvUSD and
drove borrow rates to the maximum allowable, which is what a bank run looks like in a rate
curve.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" /> That is channel-zero, an ordinary cascade in one
market — and Curve Lend markets are siloed, so on the accounting alone the damage should have stopped
there.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" />

What happened next is the interesting part.

Every other Curve Lend market prices its collateral against crvUSD, so those markets read the spike as a fall in their own collateral's price, and sUSDe borrowers were liquidated."

**Step one: liquidation demand became demand for the debt asset.** Retiring debt denominated in crvUSD
requires acquiring crvUSD. A cascade is therefore a large, sudden, one-directional bid for the
stablecoin — and crvUSD's price spiked and stayed above peg over many
blocks.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" />

**Step two: the mechanism that should have dampened it was blocked by a safety check.** Curve's
PegKeepers deposit crvUSD into their pools when the price rises, but PegKeeper V2 had added a
consistency check between the pool's spot price `get_p()` and its EMA `price_oracle()` — introduced to
prevent a spam attack in which someone sandwiches `update()` calls — and the report demonstrates that
this deviation check prevented the PegKeepers from supplying, keeping the stablecoin above
peg.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" /> The first successful `update()` came roughly 525 blocks
after the depeg began, about 45 minutes later.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" />

**Step three: the depeg propagated into unrelated markets.** All Curve Lend markets denominate their
prices against crvUSD rather than against USD, so a spike in crvUSD is read by every market as a
*reduction* in its own collateral's price.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" /> Users in the
sUSDe-long market — a market recently deployed to allow up to 35× leverage on sUSDe, advertised three
days earlier — were inadvertently liquidated, having reasonably prepared for sUSDe losing its own peg
and not for the *numeraire* gaining one.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" />

The report's own conclusion is the sentence to memorise: in certain scenarios the siloed markets in
Curve Lend, designed to mitigate risks to users, actually have generalised dependencies that may cause
knock-on effects between siloed markets.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" />

<Misconception
  belief="Isolated markets contain their risk, so a blow-up in one cannot reach another."
  reality="Isolation bounds credit exposure — lenders in one market cannot be made to absorb another market's bad debt. It does not bound the shared inputs: a common numeraire, a common oracle, a common stabilisation mechanism, a common exit venue. Those are correlations that isolation was never designed to sever."
  why="The word isolated describes the accounting boundary, and every reader hears it as describing the risk boundary."
  src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident"
/>

Generalise the shape and you have a checklist. Two markets are coupled if they share any of: the
numeraire prices are quoted in; the oracle or its underlying venues; the stablecoin liquidators must
acquire to repay; the exit venue collateral is sold into; or the backstop that funds shortfalls. A
market that is isolated in its accounting and shares four of those five is isolated in one dimension
and coupled in four. Morpho's own market documentation is explicit that a market is defined by its
own loan token, collateral token, oracle, interest model and LLTV, which is a precise statement about
what isolation covers and, by omission, what it does not.<Cite src="morpho-market-docs" />

## Channel three: a stale oracle bunches the cascade

One more transmission path, visible in the March 2020 record and worth naming because it inverts the
usual advice about lag.

When gas is high and price feeds cannot update, on-chain prices go stale. Stale prices are a temporary
*reprieve*: the Glassnode account notes that the lag gave some participants time to top up or pay off
their vaults before the feed updated.<Cite src="glassnode-makerdao-black-thursday" /> But when the
Medianizer finally updated, the reported price dropped by over 20% in one step and many vaults were
liquidated immediately and en masse.<Cite src="glassnode-makerdao-black-thursday" />

Read that as a claim about `ρ`. A smooth price path spreads `ΔD` over many small moves. A stale feed
that jumps 20% delivers the whole of `D(p(1−0.20)) − D(p)` in a single block, at a single price, into a
single instant of depth. The lag did not remove the liquidations; it queued them and released them
simultaneously, at the worst possible value of `R`.

The same record shows the second-order failure this produces. Vault-automation systems could not rescue
users, because the price went from above their configured minimums to below a 150% collateralisation
ratio instantaneously, so users with safeguards in place were liquidated
anyway.<Cite src="glassnode-makerdao-black-thursday" /> Any protective mechanism that reads the same
oracle inherits the same discontinuity. Your stop-loss and your liquidation trigger fire on the same
tick.

## What actually dampens a cascade

In order of how much they buy, and none of them is the liquidation bonus.

**Caps.** Supply caps, borrow caps and debt ceilings bound `D` directly, which is the only term in the
condition that a protocol both controls and can measure.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" />

**Position dispersion.** `ρ` is a property of how borrowers cluster, not of any parameter. A market
where everyone borrows at the maximum LTV has a spike in `ρ` at one price; a market with dispersed
utilisation has a flat `ρ`. Interfaces that default to maximum leverage manufacture the spike.

**Rate limits on retirement.** Close factors and minimum-lot rules slow `ΔD` per block. Aave's v3.3
changes cut in both directions here — position-scoped closing and a 100% close factor below a
threshold make retirement *faster*, which is right for dust and wrong for
cascades.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" /> The trade-off is real and the release notes do not pretend
otherwise.

**Partial liquidation as a liquidator choice.** Morpho lets liquidators repay any amount up to the
full debt precisely so they can trade gas cost against market impact on large
positions.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" /> A liquidator who splits a large position across
blocks is, incidentally, damping the loop — self-interest and system stability pointing the same way,
which is rare enough to notice.

**Not a circuit breaker.** Pausing liquidations during a cascade stops the selling and stops the debt
retirement, which converts a price problem into a solvency problem. There are situations where that is
the right trade; it is not a damper.

## What this unlocks

**The rest of the module** is one design that takes this lesson as its premise. If the damage comes
from a discontinuity — a step function at health factor one that converts a smooth price path into a
sudden market order — then remove the discontinuity. Curve's LLAMMA spreads the conversion across a
price range so that a 1% move produces 1%-worth of selling rather than a threshold crossing, which in
the language of this lesson is an attempt to make `ρ` finite and small everywhere instead of infinite
at one price.

**Whether it works** is the subject of lessons six through eight, and the answer is that it moves the
cost rather than removing it — with two documented failures in LLAMMA markets to prove
it.<Cite src="coindesk-curve-bad-debt" />

**The habit to keep.** Before evaluating any liquidation design, write down `ρ`, `R`, and the list of
things your "isolated" market shares with its neighbours. Those three decide whether a bad hour is an
incident or a headline, and none of them appears in a parameter table.
