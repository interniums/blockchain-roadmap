Everything so far has been an argument with a step function. Health factor crosses one and a
discontinuity fires: a large market order appears, a borrower loses a fixed percentage of equity in a
single transaction, and a keeper must be present at that exact instant or the position rots. Lesson
five showed that the discontinuity is what gives the cascade its gain — a smooth price path arriving
as a sudden block of forced selling.

Curve's LLAMMA is a different answer to the same problem, and the difference is not a parameter
change. There is no liquidation call. There is no close factor. There is no liquidation bonus. The
position's composition is a continuous function of price, maintained by ordinary arbitrage, and the
borrower's exposure to their collateral falls smoothly as the price does.

Curve's own user documentation uses the right image: fixed-price liquidation is like a step, where
crossing one price can close the loan immediately; LLAMMA is more like a ramp, exchanging collateral
in stages across a range, with friction at every step in the form of conversion
losses.<Cite src="curve-liquidation-bands" /> Hold on to the second half of that sentence. This lesson
builds the ramp; the next one prices the friction.

## The ladder

LLAMMA — Lending-Liquidating AMM Algorithm — is the contract that rebalances a loan's collateral by
creating arbitrage opportunities, offering better prices inside the AMM than external markets so that
traders do the converting.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> Each market deploys its own AMM
holding the collateral and the borrowable asset — an ETH/crvUSD market's AMM contains ETH and
crvUSD.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

Structurally it is a Uniswap-v3-style AMM using bands, and if you have
[virtual reserves](/c/virtual-reserves) and [ticks](/c/tick) you have most of it
already.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /><Cite src="uniswap-v3-whitepaper" /> A band is a price
range into which liquidity is deposited; the bands form a fixed price grid; and when a loan is
created, the collateral is distributed **equally** across the `N` bands the borrower
chose.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> `N` ranges from a minimum of 4 to a maximum of 50, and the
contract enforces it with `MIN_TICKS` and `MAX_TICKS` constants of exactly those
values.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /><Cite src="curve-crvusd-controller-docs" />

The one structural difference from a Uniswap position matters for what follows: liquidity is spread
across a range of bands rather than concentrated in one, and the collateral sitting in each band is
idle until the price enters that band's range.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

### Where the band edges are, derived

The grid is geometric, and the contract states the rule in a comment on the very function that decides
which bands your loan lands in:

```text
p_oracle_up(n)  =  base_price · ((A − 1) / A)^n
```

with `A` documented as the band width factor and the relative band size given as
`1/A`.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-amm-docs" />

**Subgoal 1 — read what the exponent means.** Each band is a constant *ratio* below the one above it,
not a constant number of dollars. Write `r = (A − 1)/A = 1 − 1/A`. Band `n`'s upper edge is
`base_price · r^n`, so its lower edge is `base_price · r^(n+1)` — a fall of `1/A` in relative terms.
For `A = 100`, each band is 1% wide.

**Subgoal 2 — get the width of the whole ladder.** `N` bands span a price ratio of `r^N`, so the total
range is

```text
range  =  1 − (1 − 1/A)^N   ≈   1 − e^{−N/A}
```

For `A = 100`: `N = 4` gives `1 − 0.99⁴ = 3.9%`; `N = 10` gives 9.6%; `N = 50` gives 39.5%. That single
line is the leverage-versus-smoothness dial in geometric form, and the next lesson turns it into the
LTV formula.

**Subgoal 3 — distinguish two price pairs that look alike.** The glossary is precise and the
distinction is load-bearing. `p_oracle_up` and `p_oracle_down` are the edges of band `n` in the steady
state where the AMM price equals the oracle price, reached when `x = 0` or `y = 0` respectively.
`p_current_up` and `p_current_down` are the value of the AMM's own price `p` at constant `p_oracle`
when `y = 0` or `x = 0` for that band.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-amm-docs" /> The first pair describes
where the ladder sits; the second describes what the AMM will quote *right now*. They coincide only at
equilibrium, and the gap between them is the entire liquidation mechanism.

## Three states a band can be in

Because the collateral is real and the bands are real, a band's contents tell you where the price is
relative to it. The documentation names exactly three cases.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

**Only the collateral token.** The band has not been soft-liquidated; the price is above its upper
edge. These are the bands above the `active_band`.

**Both tokens.** This is the band the price is currently inside, and it is by definition the
`active_band`. Continuous liquidation is happening here.

**Only the borrowable token.** The band has already been soft-liquidated; arbitrage has exchanged all
of its collateral for crvUSD. These are the bands below the `active_band`.

Two consequences fall out of this that no threshold-based design has.

**The position's composition is a deterministic function of price.** Not of when you last checked, not
of whether a keeper was awake, not of what order transactions arrived in. Where the price sits in the
ladder determines what you hold. That is what makes progressive conversion possible with no discrete
event to schedule.

**Liquidation is per band, not per user.** The documentation is explicit: liquidation happens on a
band basis, so if multiple users have liquidity in the same band being soft- or de-liquidated, it
happens for all of them together rather than for one at a
time.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> This is what makes the design scale — a single arbitrage
trade rebalances every position that shares that band, so cost per position falls as the market grows,
where a discrete design pays `G` per position per liquidation.

## How the conversion actually happens

Nothing in LLAMMA is triggered by the contract. Soft- and de-liquidation are not automatically
triggered by the smart contract; instead the AMM creates an arbitrage opportunity using two
prices.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

**`price_oracle`** is the collateral price fetched from an external oracle contract. **`get_p`** is the
AMM's own price.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> The design deliberately makes `get_p` more
sensitive than the oracle, so that any oracle movement opens a gap that pays somebody to
close.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

**When the oracle price falls, `get_p` falls faster.** The documentation's example: `get_p` is 830
while `price_oracle` is 850, so arbitrageurs buy ETH inside the LLAMMA for 830 and sell it elsewhere
for 850. That decreases the ETH in the bands, because it is being bought, and increases the crvUSD,
because it is being sold — which *is* soft liquidation.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

**When the oracle price rises, `get_p` rises faster.** `get_p` at 860 against a `price_oracle` of 850
means arbitrageurs buy ETH outside and sell it into the AMM, depositing ETH and removing
crvUSD — de-liquidation.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

**When they are equal there is no opportunity and nothing happens.**<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

The arbitrageurs are incentivised to maintain `get_p = price_oracle`, and the AMM exposes
`get_amount_for_price`, which returns how much needs to be exchanged to reach a given price plus a
boolean for whether to pump or dump the collateral.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> A helper
function, published by the protocol, for the party extracting value from the borrower — which is the
same relationship as `users_to_liquidate` in a discrete design, and the same reason it exists.

The AMM charges a static exchange fee that governance can adjust with `set_fee`, and those fees go to
the liquidity providers — who are the borrowers, since the borrowers' collateral is the
liquidity.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> So the borrower is paid a little, out of the same
arbitrage that is costing them, and the documentation frames the fee as a counter to the losses from
liquidation and to the interest rate.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

## The worked example, and the ten dollars that are missing

Curve's own numbers, because they contain the point of the next lesson hidden in a footnote.

Take a loan backed by 2 ETH at an ETH price of $1,000, with a total debt of 1,750 crvUSD, using 4
bands.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> Equal distribution puts 0.5 ETH in each band.

**Above the range.** All four bands are 100% ETH. There is nothing to liquidate, so nothing
happens.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

**Price enters the first band.** LLAMMA starts selling ETH into crvUSD. With the price roughly in the
middle of the band, about 0.25 of that band's 0.5 ETH has been
converted.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

**Price reaches the band's lower edge.** The whole 0.5 ETH of that band has been soft-liquidated —
into, in the documentation's words, "let's say, 490 crvUSD".<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

Stop there. The band's price range was around $1,000 per ETH, and 0.5 ETH sold for 490 crvUSD rather
than 500. Curve's own footnote answers the obvious question directly: the shortfall is because losses
occur during soft liquidation.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> Ten crvUSD, or 2% of the amount
converted, in one band, on one pass down.

That is the liquidation bonus. It has not been abolished; it has been atomised into a per-band, per-
crossing charge and paid to arbitrageurs instead of to a designated liquidator. Lesson seven works out
what it costs over a path rather than over a single crossing, and the answer is not obviously smaller
than a one-time penalty.

**Below the entire range.** The price can walk all the way through the ladder, at which point the
entire collateral has been converted and the loan is fully backed by
crvUSD.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> The documentation attaches a condition to this that is
easy to skim past and belongs in lesson eight: this is only possible if the health of the loan is
still above 0%.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

<Check type="predict" concept="llamma-bands">
A borrower opens an ETH/crvUSD LLAMMA loan with 10 ETH and `N = 10` bands, in a market with `A = 100`,
at an oracle price of $3,000. The price falls to the bottom edge of the fourth band and then stops.

Work out approximately where the bottom of the whole liquidation range sits, how much of the borrower's
ETH has been converted, and what happens to the remaining six bands if the price sits still for a week.

<Answer>
**Where the range ends.** Bands are `1/A = 1%` wide, so ten bands span
`1 − 0.99¹⁰ = 1 − 0.9044 = 9.6%`. If the top of the ladder is near $3,000, the bottom is around
`3,000 × 0.9044 ≈ $2,713`. The whole protection range is about $287 wide on a $3,000 asset — narrow,
because ten bands at `A = 100` is a narrow ladder.

**How much has converted.** Collateral is distributed equally, so each band holds 1 ETH. At the bottom
edge of band four, bands one through four are fully soft-liquidated and bands five through ten are
untouched. **About 4 ETH has become crvUSD; about 6 ETH remains as ETH.** The position is now roughly
40% stable and 60% volatile, and that composition is a fact about where the price is, not about who
did what.

**Sanity-check the conversion price.** Band four's edges are around
`3,000 × 0.99³ = $2,911` down to `3,000 × 0.99⁴ = $2,882`. The 4 ETH did not all sell at $2,882; each
band sold across its own range, so the blended proceeds are roughly the average of the ladder from
$3,000 down to $2,882 — minus the conversion losses on every crossing.

**What happens over a still week.** Two different things, and separating them is the point of the
question.

*Nothing more converts.* The price is not moving, so `get_p` and `price_oracle` are in equilibrium and
there is no arbitrage opportunity, so bands five through ten sit
untouched.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> Curve's user documentation says the same thing from
the borrower's side: losses in liquidation protection only occur while inside the protection
range and while conversions are happening.<Cite src="curve-liquidation-bands" />

*Health still falls.* Interest accrues per block regardless of whether the loan is being liquidated,
and the documentation lists interest as one of the two factors that decrease loan health, alongside
soft- and de-liquidation losses.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> A week of stillness inside the
range is a week of health decay with no offsetting conversion.

*And the borrower is restricted.* While in liquidation protection they cannot add collateral, remove
collateral, or borrow more; they can repay, and only a *full* repayment exits the range — repaying 99%
does not.<Cite src="curve-liquidation-bands" /> So the obvious defensive move, topping up collateral,
is unavailable precisely when it is wanted.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What the design removed, precisely

Line the two mechanisms up and the differences are all structural rather than parametric.

**No discrete event.** A discrete design has a moment: a transaction, a `LiquidationCall` log, a step
in the borrower's balance. LLAMMA has a continuous function of price and a stream of ordinary swaps.

**No close factor.** There is nothing to cap, because no single call retires a fraction of the debt.
The equivalent question — how fast can conversion happen — is answered by `A` and by how fast the
price moves, not by a parameter with branches.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" />

**No liquidation bonus.** No governance-set discount, no per-asset bitmap field, no incentive factor
formula.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" /> The compensation to whoever does the converting is the
gap between `get_p` and `price_oracle`, which is set by how sensitive the AMM is rather than by a
vote.

**No privileged caller.** Anyone arbitraging two venues participates without knowing they are
liquidating anybody.

**And it reverses.** De-liquidation is the same mechanism running the other way: if the price recovers,
the crvUSD is converted back into collateral, and this can continue until all the assets backing the
loan are the original asset again.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> A discrete liquidation is
final; a soft one is a position on a spectrum that can walk back up.

  belief="Soft liquidation means you cannot be liquidated."
  reality="It means the conversion is continuous rather than discrete. A hard liquidation path exists and is used: a loan becomes eligible for it when health falls below zero, which depends on accrued interest and accumulated conversion losses rather than on the price being inside or outside the band range."
  why="'No liquidation event' is marketed as 'no liquidation', and the band range is mistaken for the thing that determines hard liquidation when the documentation says the range explicitly does not."
  src="curve-llamma-explainer"
/>

That misconception is worth pausing on because Curve's own documentation goes out of its way to
correct it: hard liquidation is when other users repay the debt and receive the collateral, a loan is
only eligible when health is below 0%, and it is very important to understand that the liquidation
range does not reflect prices at which a loan is hard-liquidated — it depends only on the health of
the loan.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> Two independent quantities that both use the word
liquidation.

<Aside kind="warn">
Being below the entire range is not safety. Curve's user documentation spells out the case: with the
whole position converted to crvUSD you are protected from further collateral declines and no longer
taking conversion losses, but health now decreases from interest alone, and if the price stays far
below the range for a long time interest alone can eventually push health to
zero.<Cite src="curve-liquidation-bands" /> A fully de-risked position that is still a loan is still a
loan.
</Aside>

## What this design inherits

Two dependencies carry straight through from the discrete world, and neither is softened.

**It depends on arbitrageurs.** The conversion is not performed by the protocol; it is performed by
people responding to a price gap. That is a keeper assumption with a much larger population and a much
lower barrier to entry — but it is still a keeper assumption, and its quality shows up directly in the
borrower's losses. Curve names three factors that reduce those losses, and one of them is more
efficient arbitrage.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

**It depends on an oracle.** `price_oracle` is fetched from an external contract, and the whole ladder
is indexed to it.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> A discrete design has one oracle-sensitive
price per position. LLAMMA has a range. Lesson eight is about what an attacker does with that.

And one dependency is new. Because the borrower's collateral *is* the AMM's liquidity, the quality of
the conversion depends on how much liquidity is in the market — the second of Curve's three factors is
simply more liquidity.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> The June 2024 incident report makes the
same observation from the failure side, attributing inefficient liquidation of a large CRV position to
the relatively poor on-chain liquidity profile of CRV.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" /> Thin
markets are punished here exactly as they are punished in a discrete design; the punishment is just
spread out.

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson** turns `A` and `N` into the maximum-LTV formula, derives it from the geometric sum
rather than accepting it, and then prices the friction — because a mechanism that charges 2% per band
crossing charges it every time the price crosses, and prices cross back and forth.

**The mental model to carry.** A LLAMMA borrower is a concentrated-liquidity provider who did not
choose to be one. Their collateral is the inventory, the price range is their position, and the
arbitrageurs' profit is their loss. Everything you know about
[impermanent loss](/c/impermanent-loss) applies, with one asymmetry: an ordinary LP earns fees
proportional to the volume they facilitate, while this LP is holding a loan against the same
inventory.

**A question to ask about any continuous-liquidation design.** Where did the discrete cost go? It never
disappears — it becomes a rate, and a rate multiplied by an amount of time or an amount of volatility
is still a number. The next lesson computes it.
