Curve publishes one formula for the maximum a LLAMMA borrower can take out, and it is the kind of
formula a curriculum normally asks you to accept:

```text
LTV  =  100%  −  loan_discount  −  100 · N / (2 · A)
```

with the worked pair that a 9% loan discount at `A = 100` gives about 89% at `N = 4` bands and about
66% at `N = 50`.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> The same formula appears in the Controller
documentation, with the accompanying statement that the higher the number of bands, the lower the
LTV.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-controller-docs" />

Notice the word the documentation actually uses: the maximum LTV can be **approximated** using the
following function.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> That word is doing real work, and this lesson
starts by finding out what it is hiding — because the approximation is a first-order expansion, and
one of Curve's own two examples sits well outside the range where a first-order expansion is
accurate.

## Deriving the formula

The contract carries its own derivation in a comment on `calculate_debt_n1`, the function that decides
which band a new loan starts in:<Cite src="curve-crvusd-amm-docs" />

```text
x_effective    = y / N · p_oracle_up(n1) · sqrt((A−1)/A) · sum_{k=0..N−1} ( ((A−1)/A)^k )
d_y_effective  = y / N / sqrt( A / (A−1) )
p_oracle_up(n) = base_price · ((A − 1) / A)^n
```

Take it apart in four steps, and write `r = (A − 1)/A = 1 − 1/A` throughout.

**Subgoal 1 — establish what `x_effective` is measuring.** It is the collateral `y`, valued in the
borrowable asset, under the conservative assumption that every band's collateral gets converted at
that band's own price rather than at today's spot. It is "what this position is worth if the price
walks the whole ladder", which is the right basis for a loan limit and the wrong basis for a mark.

**Subgoal 2 — find where the square root comes from.** Band `n` has edges `base_price · r^n` at the top
and `base_price · r^(n+1)` at the bottom. Its collateral is not converted at either edge; it is
converted progressively across the band. The representative price for a geometric range is the
geometric mean of its edges:

```text
√( base_price·r^n  ×  base_price·r^(n+1) )  =  base_price · r^n · √r
```

There is the `sqrt((A−1)/A)`. It is not a fudge factor — it is the average conversion price within one
band, and the same quantity appears in the AMM's constructor as the immutable
`_sqrt_band_ratio`.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-amm-docs" />

**Subgoal 3 — sum over the ladder.** Each of the `N` bands holds `y/N` of collateral, and band `k`
below the top converts at `p_top · r^k · √r`. Summing a geometric series with ratio `r`:

```text
sum_{k=0..N−1} r^k  =  (1 − r^N) / (1 − r)  =  A · (1 − r^N)
```

because `1 − r = 1/A`. So the whole position's conservative value, as a fraction of `y · p_top`, is

```text
f(N, A)  =  (A / N) · √r · (1 − r^N)
```

**Subgoal 4 — expand to first order and recover the published formula.** With `ln r = −1/A − 1/(2A²) − …`:

```text
r^N        ≈ 1 − N/A − N/(2A²) + N²/(2A²)
1 − r^N    ≈ (N/A)·[ 1 + 1/(2A) − N/(2A) ]
(A/N)(1−r^N) ≈ 1 − (N−1)/(2A)
√r         ≈ 1 − 1/(2A)
f(N, A)    ≈ 1 − N/(2A)
```

Multiply by `(1 − loan_discount)` and drop the second-order cross term and you have exactly
`LTV ≈ 1 − loan_discount − N/(2A)`.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> The published formula is the
first-order expansion of the geometric expression the contract actually uses.

### Where the approximation stops being one

The expansion parameter is `N/(2A)`. Check Curve's two examples against it.

At `N = 4`, `A = 100`: `N/(2A) = 0.02`. Compute `f` exactly — `r = 0.99`, `r⁴ = 0.960596`,
`(100/4)(0.039404) = 0.985099`, times `√0.99 = 0.994987` — and you get `f = 0.98016` against the linear
`0.98`. Agreement to two decimal places, which is why the documentation's 89% figure is
sound.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

At `N = 50`, `A = 100`: `N/(2A) = 0.25`. That is not a small number, and `f` computed exactly is
`(100/50)(1 − 0.99⁵⁰) · √0.99 = 2 × 0.394994 × 0.994987 = 0.78603` against the linear `0.75`. The two
expressions have separated by three and a half points of the collateral's value.

**What to conclude, and what not to.** Conclude that `LTV = 100% − loan_discount − 100·N/(2A)` is a
*conservative* linear approximation whose error grows with `N/A`, and that Curve says so by calling it
an approximation.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> Do not conclude a competing LTV number from
this derivation: `x_effective` feeds a further computation that solves for the starting band `n1`, and
the deployed `loan_discount` is a market parameter — the Controller's getter returns
`90000000000000000`, or 9%, for the market the documentation
uses.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-controller-docs" /> The number you can act on comes from calling the
contract. The number you can *reason* with is the shape: LTV falls roughly linearly in `N` and rises
with `A`, and the linear form understates the available LTV at high band counts rather than
overstating it.

## What the dial actually trades

Two quantities move together when you turn `N`, and they move in opposite directions for the borrower.

**Range width.** From the last lesson, `N` bands span `1 − r^N`. At `A = 100`: four bands is a 3.9%
range, fifty bands is a 39.5% range. A wide range means you enter liquidation protection on a small
move and stay in it through large ones — Curve's user documentation puts it as more bands equals a
wider range.<Cite src="curve-liquidation-bands" />

**Conversion granularity.** With the same collateral spread over more bands, each band holds less, so
each crossing converts less and the ramp is gentler. Curve lists the number of bands as one of the
factors losses depend on, with more bands typically meaning fewer
losses.<Cite src="curve-liquidation-bands" />

**Maximum leverage.** Falls, per the formula above.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

So `N = 4` is a borrower saying: give me maximum leverage, and if the price comes for me convert my
position in four large steps across a narrow 4% window. `N = 50` is a borrower saying: give me two
thirds of that leverage, start converting me early, and do it in fifty small steps over a 40% window.
Neither is safe on both axes, which is why the choice sits in the loan-creation call — `create_loan`
takes `N` as an explicit argument, bounded by `MIN_TICKS` and
`MAX_TICKS`.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-controller-docs" />

Controller V3 adds a second dial: `set_extra_health` lets a user add a buffer before creating a loan,
so the position enters soft liquidation with higher health, and the documentation shows the effect
directly — a user with 1% extra health enters soft liquidation at about 4.94% health rather than about
3.94%.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-controller-docs" /> It is available in a named subset of markets rather
than everywhere.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-controller-docs" />

## The cost of the ramp

Now the friction, which is the half of this design that marketing does not carry.

**Subgoal 1 — name what the borrower has become.** Their collateral is liquidity in a concentrated
range, and arbitrageurs trade against it. That is a liquidity provider, and the loss an LP takes to
arbitrageurs when the price moves is a well-studied quantity — loss-versus-rebalancing, the gap
between the LP's position and a continuously rebalanced portfolio holding the same
exposure.<Cite src="arxiv-lvr" /> The LLAMMA borrower is an LP with one crucial difference: they did
not choose the position for fee income, and the fees flow back to them only as a partial
offset.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

**Subgoal 2 — measure one crossing.** Curve's worked example converts one band's 0.5 ETH into "let's
say, 490 crvUSD" at an ETH price around $1,000, with the footnote attributing the shortfall to losses
that occur during soft liquidation.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> Treat the figure as
illustrative rather than as a parameter — the actual gap depends on the AMM's sensitivity, the static
fee governance sets through `set_fee`, and how efficiently arbitrage
competes.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> What it illustrates is that a crossing is not free and
the cost is a fraction of the amount converted.

**Subgoal 3 — measure a round trip.** Down through a band, the collateral is sold below the fair price.
Up through the same band, it is bought back above the fair price. The borrower has bought high and sold
low, once, and paid the arbitrageur the difference twice. Curve's user documentation states this
explicitly: these losses happen in both directions, on the way down and on the way back
up.<Cite src="curve-liquidation-bands" />

**Subgoal 4 — find what the total scales with.** Not the size of any single move. A single large
move down through the whole ladder costs one pass. A price that oscillates within one band for a week
costs a pass for every oscillation. Curve lists the four drivers and the fourth is the one to
memorise: market volatility, time in range, number of bands, and **sideways volatility** — repeated
up-and-down movement inside the range causing multiple conversions and increasing
losses.<Cite src="curve-liquidation-bands" />

So the borrower's cost is proportional to **realised volatility inside the range**, integrated over
time. That is a fundamentally different quantity from a one-time penalty, and comparing them requires
knowing the price path rather than knowing the mechanism.

**Subgoal 5 — establish that it does not reverse.** This is the sentence borrowers most often get
wrong. Losses from conversions reduce total collateral value permanently: enter protection with 10 ETH,
take losses, exit later, and you have less than 10 ETH — a reduction that does not recover even if
prices fully recover.<Cite src="curve-liquidation-bands" /> De-liquidation restores the *composition*
of the position. It does not restore the value the conversions cost. The documentation's own summary
is that the direction of conversion can reverse during a recovery, but conversion losses do
not.<Cite src="curve-liquidation-bands" />

The corollary catches people: even if the price is rising, health can still fall while inside the
protection range, because conversion losses continue until you exit the range completely, and a rising
price does not guarantee improving health unless it moves above the full
range.<Cite src="curve-liquidation-bands" />

<Check type="explain" concept="llamma-soft-liquidation-loss">
Two borrowers open identical $100,000 positions on the same collateral at the same moment. One uses a
discrete-liquidation market with an 80% liquidation threshold and a 6% bonus. One uses a LLAMMA market
whose protection range begins at the same effective price.

Compare their outcomes on two price paths, then say what a borrower should actually take from the
comparison.

**Path A.** The price falls 25% over four hours, in an orderly line, and stays there.

**Path B.** The price falls 6% into the protection range, then oscillates within the range for three
weeks — up 3%, down 3%, repeatedly — before finishing exactly where it started.

<Answer>
**Path A — the discrete borrower does worse.** They cross the threshold on the way down and are
liquidated at a 6% bonus on whatever fraction the close factor permits, in one transaction, at
whatever the oracle said at that instant. The loss is immediate, large and final, and if the fall was
fast enough that liquidators could not act in the profitable band, part of it becomes bad debt that
the market absorbs rather than the borrower.

*The LLAMMA borrower* is converted progressively across the ladder as the price descends. They pay
conversion losses on one pass through each band, ending fully in the stable asset with a position
whose value fell with the collateral but which was never sold at a single bad instant. One clean pass
down is the case this design was built for, and it wins it.

**Path B — the LLAMMA borrower does worse, and the discrete borrower pays nothing.** The price never
came near the threshold, so no liquidation occurred and no bonus was paid. Meanwhile the LLAMMA
position sat inside its range for three weeks of chop, paying conversion losses on every crossing in
both directions.<Cite src="curve-liquidation-bands" /> The price finished where it started; the
collateral did not, because the losses are permanent.<Cite src="curve-liquidation-bands" /> Health
fell throughout from conversions *and* from interest, which accrues per block regardless of what
liquidation is doing.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

**What a borrower should take from this.** Three things.

*The mechanism does not decide the outcome; the path does.* LLAMMA converts a jump-risk into a
volatility-risk. If your view is that the asset makes large one-directional moves, that is a good
trade. If your view is that it chops, it is a bad one.

*Entry into the range is the decision point, not health approaching zero.* Curve's own guidance is
blunt about this: treat entry into liquidation protection as a signal to act, because health above
zero only means the position has not yet been fully liquidated while conversions keep reducing
it.<Cite src="curve-liquidation-bands" /> Remaining in the bands is described as an aggressive strategy
requiring continuous monitoring.<Cite src="curve-liquidation-bands" />

*And the defensive options narrow exactly when you need them.* Inside protection you cannot add
collateral and cannot borrow more; you can repay, and only full repayment exits — 99% does
not.<Cite src="curve-liquidation-bands" />
</Answer>
</Check>

## The three mitigations, and what they really say

Curve names three factors that reduce soft-liquidation losses: more bands, more liquidity, and more
efficient arbitrage.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

Read them as a description of what the design depends on rather than as advice.

**More bands** is the borrower's lever and costs leverage, per the formula
above.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

**More liquidity** is not the borrower's lever at all. It is a property of the market they joined, and
it is the same `R` that decided everything in the discrete half of this module. The June 2024 report
attributes inefficient liquidation of a large CRV position directly to the relatively poor on-chain
liquidity profile of CRV.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" />

**More efficient arbitrage** is the keeper assumption, restated. The population is larger and the
barrier lower than for a discrete liquidator, but the borrower's loss is set by how competitively that
population prices the gap between `get_p` and `price_oracle`. Thin arbitrage means a wider gap means a
larger loss per crossing.

Which is to say: two of the three mitigations are the same two variables — depth and keeper quality —
that governed the discrete designs. The mechanism changed. Its dependencies did not.

<Aside kind="note">
There is one genuine structural improvement, and it is worth stating precisely so it is not oversold.
In a discrete design the compensation to the keeper is a *governance-set constant* that is paid in
full whenever the threshold is crossed, regardless of how easy the trade was. In LLAMMA it is a
*market-cleared spread* that competition can compress toward the arbitrageurs' costs. That is the same
argument as the descending auction from lesson two — discovered price beats a guessed one — applied
continuously instead of per event.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" />
</Aside>

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson** is the tail this design still has. Health, defined precisely from the contract
rather than from analogy, goes below zero and a hard liquidation becomes available to anyone. Two
documented failures in LLAMMA markets end there, and one of them is an attacker choosing the moment.

**The calculation to keep.** `f(N, A) = (A/N)·√r·(1 − r^N)` and its expansion `1 − N/(2A)`. You now
know where the published formula comes from, why it is called an approximation, and in which direction
it errs. That is a better possession than the formula.

**The reframing to carry into any protocol that claims to have removed a cost.** Ask what units the
cost is now denominated in. LLAMMA converted a percentage-of-position into a rate-per-unit-of-realised-
volatility. That is a real design achievement and it is not a reduction — it is a change of variable,
and whether it is cheaper is an empirical question about the path your collateral actually takes.
