LLAMMA's hard-liquidation path is one function, and its docstring calls what it performs a "bad
liquidation".<Cite src="curve-crvusd-controller-docs" /> That is the honest name for it: the soft path
is the design, and this is what runs when the design has already failed for a particular position.

This lesson is about that tail. It has two halves. The first is arithmetic — what health actually is,
computed from the contract rather than from the fuel-gauge analogy, and which of its inputs moves in
which direction. The second is adversarial: a soft-liquidation ladder is indexed to an oracle along
its entire length, which means an attacker who can move the oracle does not merely trigger one
threshold. They choose where every position in the market sits.

## Health, from the source

Curve's user-facing documentation describes health as a fuel gauge that empties to zero, with high
health around 10 or higher, medium around 5, critical below 2, and full liquidation at
zero.<Cite src="curve-liquidation-bands" /> Useful for a UI, useless for reasoning. The contract is
precise:

```text
health = 10**18 − liquidation_discount
health = get_x_down(user) · health / debt  −  10**18
```

with the docstring stating that health is normalised to 1e18, that liquidation starts when it is below
zero, and — the clause worth stopping on — that *devaluation of collateral does not cause
liquidation*.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-controller-docs" />

**Subgoal 1 — read the formula in plain terms.** Divide through by 1e18:

```text
health  =  get_x_down · (1 − liquidation_discount) / debt  −  1
```

So health is positive exactly while

```text
get_x_down · (1 − liquidation_discount)  >  debt
```

`get_x_down` is the AMM's conservative downside valuation of the position in units of the borrowed
asset — what the position would be worth if the price walked down through the remaining bands. The
Controller's own parameter documentation confirms the role: `loan_discount` is described as the
discount of the maximum loan size compared to `get_x_down()`, and `liquidation_discount` as the same
discount "for bad liquidation purposes", used to discount the collateral value when computing health in
order to incentivise liquidators.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-controller-docs" />

**Subgoal 2 — recognise the shape.** This is the same inequality as the discrete world's bad-debt
threshold from lesson four, `C ≥ D · LIF`, with two substitutions. `C` has become `get_x_down`, a
band-derived valuation rather than a spot mark. And `LIF` has become `1/(1 − liquidation_discount)`,
which is the same quantity written the other way round. LLAMMA did not invent a new solvency condition.
It changed what goes into `C`.

**Subgoal 3 — work out what that substitution buys.** Because `get_x_down` is computed from where the
position sits in the ladder rather than from spot, a price fall *below* the range does not reduce it —
which is exactly what the docstring means by devaluation not causing
liquidation.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-controller-docs" /> Curve's user documentation says the same from
the borrower's side: with the position entirely converted, you are protected from further declines
and health only decreases from interest.<Cite src="curve-liquidation-bands" /> A fully soft-liquidated
position is genuinely insulated from further downside, and that is a real property no discrete design
has.

**Subgoal 4 — work out what it costs.** Only two things reduce health, and Curve names both: the
interest rate, which increases debt every block regardless of whether the loan is being liquidated at
all, and — more importantly, in the documentation's own ranking — the losses taken when the position
is soft- or de-liquidated.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> Both terms are monotone. Interest only
rises; conversion losses only accumulate. **Health has no path back up that does not involve the
borrower repaying.**

That is the whole tail in one sentence. The mechanism removed the discontinuity at a price; it did not
remove the monotone decay of a quantity that triggers liquidation when it reaches zero.

<Aside kind="note">
The `full` flag on `health` and `health_calculator` selects whether to take into account the price
difference above the highest user's band.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-controller-docs" /> The two answers
can differ by an order of magnitude — the documentation's own example returns about 5.03 for one and
about 0.041 for the other on the same position.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-controller-docs" /> If you are
building monitoring on top of this, know which one you are reading, because one of them includes
value that only exists while the price stays above your bands.
</Aside>

## The hard path

When health goes below zero, other users may repay the debt and receive the
collateral.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /> The documentation is emphatic about what does *not*
determine this: the liquidation range does not reflect prices at which a loan is hard-liquidated; it
depends only on health.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

The interface is a discrete liquidator's interface, and by now every piece of it should look familiar.
`liquidate(user, min_x, use_eth)` takes a minimum acceptable amount of assets to receive, documented
as protection against liquidators being sandwiched.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-controller-docs" />
`liquidate_extended` adds a `frac` — the fraction to liquidate, with 1e18 meaning 100% — plus a
`callbacker` and its arguments, so a caller can hand control to their own contract mid-execution and
work capital-free.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-controller-docs" /> `tokens_to_liquidate(user)` returns the
amount of the borrowed asset required, and `users_to_liquidate(_from, _limit)` returns a paginated
array of positions eligible for hard liquidation.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-controller-docs" />

Note the shape of the incentive: there is no separate bonus parameter on the way in. The liquidator's
profit comes from the `liquidation_discount` already embedded in the health calculation — the position
becomes liquidatable while its band-derived value still exceeds the debt by that discount, and the
gap is the payment. Same trade as Aave's bonus, expressed as a haircut in a solvency test rather than
as a markup on a seizure.

### The direction that surprises people

Everything so far suggests the danger is downward. It is not, and the counter-case is documented.

Consider a position fully soft-liquidated: entirely in the stable asset, below its range, health
decaying only from interest. Now the collateral price rises rapidly. De-liquidation converts the
stable asset back into collateral — and conversion in *that* direction costs the borrower too, because
losses happen in both directions.<Cite src="curve-liquidation-bands" /> A fast recovery re-exposes the
position to the volatile asset while charging it for the privilege.

LlamaRisk's June 2024 report states the case in exactly these terms for a large CRV position: due to
the soft-liquidation design, the worst scenario would be if the CRV price increased *rapidly*, because
the position would become fully exposed to CRV and simultaneously drop in health, potentially resulting
in a hard liquidation of the full collateral amount.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" /> In that
episode the price declined instead, soft liquidation transitioned exposure into crvUSD, and hard
liquidation followed in an orderly way with the founder noting that the soft path had bought
liquidators time to prepare funds for an OTC liquidation.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" />

Curve's own user documentation carries the general form of the warning: full liquidation can still
happen during a price recovery if health is already critically low inside the
range.<Cite src="curve-liquidation-bands" />

Two events make the point. Curve's CRV-collateral LlamaLend market accumulated bad debt during the
10 October 2025 crash because prices moved faster than keepers could profitably act on while gas costs
spiked, and the founder later proposed a market-based recovery pool rather than a treasury
bailout.<Cite src="coindesk-curve-bad-debt" /> That is lesson four's coupling, arriving in a market
that was supposed to be immune to it. The second event is the rest of this lesson.

## The ladder is an oracle-indexed object

Here is the structural point, and it inverts a common intuition.

A discrete design has one oracle-sensitive price per position: the price at which health factor
reaches one. Move the oracle to that price and the position becomes liquidatable; move it anywhere
else and nothing happens.

LLAMMA has no such price — and the naive conclusion is that removing the single trigger removes the
attack target. The opposite is true. The AMM's quoted price tracks `price_oracle`, the band mechanics
depend on arbitrageurs responding to the gap between `price_oracle` and
`get_p`, and every position's composition is a function of where the oracle says the price
is.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" /><Cite src="curve-crvusd-amm-docs" /> There is no single
sensitive price because **the whole range is sensitive**. An attacker who controls the oracle controls
where in the ladder every position sits, and can therefore choose to place all of them in soft
liquidation at once.

## 2 March 2026: what a controllable rate does to a ladder

The demonstration is a single transaction, and its trace has been published in detail by an
independent analysis. Handle the numbers carefully — the governance post-mortem thread itself returns
HTTP 403 to automated retrieval, so what follows is what the transaction-level analysis reports, with
the disagreements named rather than resolved.<Cite src="gov-curve-sdola-postmortem" />

**The defect.** The sDOLA/crvUSD LlamaLend market's price oracle, a `CryptoFromPoolVaultWAgg` contract,
computed its price by calling `VAULT.convertToAssets(10**18)` on the sDOLA vault — a spot read of an
ERC-4626-style exchange rate.<Cite src="darknavy-sdola-llamalend" /> That rate is
`shares × totalAssets() / totalSupply`, and `totalAssets()` derives from `DolaSavings.balanceOf(sDOLA)`,
which changes immediately when DOLA is staked or unstaked.<Cite src="darknavy-sdola-llamalend" /> The
oracle contract's own source describes it as suitable only for vaults that cannot be affected by a
donation attack, and the sDOLA contract itself carries a comment warning that any protocol in which
sudden, large and atomic increases in the value of an asset may be a security risk should not integrate
the vault.<Cite src="darknavy-sdola-llamalend" />

**The execution**, at block 24566937, 03:00:11 UTC. Two flash loans from Morpho Blue — 10,000,000 USDC
and 15,986.108 WETH.<Cite src="darknavy-sdola-llamalend" /> The attacker opened a WETH-collateral
crvUSD loan to borrow 25M crvUSD, routed value into the sDOLA/crvUSD LLAMMA through the AMM's own
`exchange`, and then moved the rate: `sDOLA.redeem` of about 10,607,802 sDOLA, which unstaked about
12,613,130 DOLA from `DolaSavings` and crashed `convertToAssets()`, followed by a re-stake of
190,777.47 DOLA to calibrate how depressed the rate stayed.<Cite src="darknavy-sdola-llamalend" /> Then
one call to `users_to_liquidate()` and 27 calls to `liquidate(user, 0)` from a helper contract deployed
in the same transaction.<Cite src="darknavy-sdola-llamalend" /> The market's `n_loans` went from 30 at
the pre-state to 4 at the post-state.<Cite src="darknavy-sdola-llamalend" /> The oracle's
`convertToAssets` selector was called 78 times inside the one
transaction.<Cite src="darknavy-sdola-llamalend" /> Attacker net token change: +6.737 WETH and
+227,325.57 DOLA, about $240,567.<Cite src="darknavy-sdola-llamalend" />

**Where sources disagree, and why it matters.**

*The direction of the oracle move.* The transaction-level analysis is explicit that the attacker moved
the rate **down**, by mass-redeeming sDOLA to crash `convertToAssets()`, and notes that this is the
opposite of the direction the sDOLA source comment warns
about.<Cite src="darknavy-sdola-llamalend" /> Summaries of the governance thread instead describe the
`DolaSavings.stake()` call as *inflating* the oracle.<Cite src="gov-curve-sdola-postmortem" /> Since
sDOLA was the collateral, only a downward move makes positions liquidatable, so the trace's account is
the mechanically coherent one — but the thread cannot be fetched to check, and you should treat the
direction as reported-with-a-disagreement rather than settled.

*The size of the borrower loss.* The figure most often repeated is 27 borrowers and roughly $10.9M of
debt. In the published trace, a `create_loan` of approximately 8,286,547 sDOLA collateral for
approximately 10,904,020 crvUSD appears as the **attacker's own follow-on loan** in the same market,
after the liquidation phase.<Cite src="darknavy-sdola-llamalend" /> Whether the circulating figure is a
coincidence or a misreading of that line cannot be established from a thread that returns 403. The 27
liquidation calls are confirmed by selector count; the debt figure attached to them is not.

**The generalisable defect.** You met it in the security track: a yield-bearing wrapper's exchange
rate is a function of mutable state, so it is an oracle, and an oracle any caller can move in one
transaction is an oracle an attacker can move.<Cite src="darknavy-sdola-llamalend" /> What LLAMMA adds
is amplification. In a discrete market, moving a rate downward makes liquidatable those positions that
happened to sit near the threshold. Here, one move relocates every position in the market along the
ladder at once — which is why one transaction closed 26 of 30 loans.

You are reviewing a proposed LlamaLend-style market with a liquid-staking token as collateral. The
oracle multiplies a professional USD feed for the underlying by the LST contract's own `exchangeRate()`
view. The team argues that because soft liquidation is continuous, there is no threshold to attack and
the design is therefore more oracle-resilient than a discrete market would be.

Say whether the argument holds, and give the review findings you would write.

<Answer>
**The argument is exactly backwards, and the reason is structural rather than a matter of degree.**

Continuous liquidation does not remove the oracle-sensitive point; it smears it across the whole band
range. In a discrete market, an attacker who can move the oracle by `x%` can liquidate the positions
whose thresholds lie within `x%` of spot. In a band market, the same move relocates *every* position
in the market along the ladder, converting collateral for all of them and moving all of their health
in the same direction at once. The 27-liquidation transaction is what that looks like when someone
does it.<Cite src="darknavy-sdola-llamalend" />

**Findings I would write.**

*Finding one — the rate leg is an unbounded oracle.* Identify precisely what `exchangeRate()` is a
function of and who can change those inputs in one transaction. If it reads a balance that a
permissionless stake, unstake, deposit or reward distribution modifies, it is atomically movable and
the professional feed on the other leg does not help, because the two legs multiply and the composite
inherits the weaker one.<Cite src="darknavy-sdola-llamalend" />

*Finding two — the oracle must not be able to jump.* This is the remediation the incident points at,
and it is a property of the feed rather than of the collateral: a lending oracle should have no path to
an instantaneous price change, so smoothing is required regardless of how the collateral is
technically designed. In practice that means an EMA or time-weighted read of the rate, plus a bound on
per-update movement — the same construction as Aave's price cap adapters, which impose a maximum
allowed growth rate on a yield-bearing asset's price so a rate that moves faster than yield can
possibly accrue is refused.<Cite src="aave-price-cap-adapters" />

*Finding three — the oracle must be replaceable.* An immutable oracle address on a live market is a
permanent liability, because the fix for finding two is a new oracle. Ask, before deployment, what the
upgrade path is; if the answer is "there is none", the design decision has already been made and it
cannot be revisited after an incident.

*Finding four — consumption checks are still required.* Freshness, decimals, the behaviour of any
fallback path.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" /> The professional leg being professional says
nothing about how it is read.

*Finding five — bound the blast radius.* Supply caps limit how much of the collateral the market
accepts, which is the only control that works when the other four fail. Note the asymmetry the
incident illustrates: because band liquidation operates per band rather than per user, one
manipulation reaches every borrower who shares the affected range, so the exposure to bound is the
market, not the largest position.<Cite src="curve-llamma-explainer" />

**What I would not write.** A finding recommending a switch to discrete liquidation. That trades this
failure mode for the ones in lessons four and five, and the choice between them is about which price
paths the collateral actually takes, not about which mechanism is safer in the
abstract.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" />
</Answer>
</Check>

## What the module adds up to

Every design in these eight lessons pays a stranger to retire debt, and every one fails when the
stranger's arithmetic goes negative or the number it is computed from is wrong.

**Fixed discount** fails silently: the function exists, calling it is a loss, nobody calls it, and the
shortfall becomes a reserve deficit or a vault share price write-down.

**Descending auction** fails loudly: it clears fairly at whatever the one reachable bidder offered.

**Continuous conversion** fails slowly and then suddenly: monotone health decay ending in the same
permissionless hard liquidation the design was built to avoid — or immediately, if somebody owns the
oracle the ladder is indexed to.

What they share is that solvency is outsourced to an incentive and to a price, and neither is under
the protocol's control at the moment it matters.

## What this unlocks

**The reflection for this module** asks you to pick a design and write the conditions under which it
produces bad debt, then sort those conditions into what a protocol can control, what it can only
price, and what it can only observe afterwards. You now have the material for all three columns: caps
and close factors in the first, discounts and thresholds in the second, and depth, keeper liveness and
oracle integrity in the third.

**The practice worth doing first** is the rate-oracle attack in miniature: a mock ERC-4626 vault whose
`convertToAssets` is consumed as a lending oracle, plus a permissionless call that moves it inside one
transaction. Make a healthy position hard-liquidatable in a single block using only unpermissioned
calls, then add a smoothed or rate-limited read and watch the same test fail. Twenty minutes of work
reproduces the shape of an eight-figure market's worst day.

**Where this connects onward.** The oracle module treats manipulation cost as a quantity you compute
rather than a property you assert, and the MEV module takes up the ordering market that captures the
surplus in every fixed-discount design. Both are the other side of this module's central claim: a
liquidation system assumes a keeper shows up and the oracle is fresh, and it is precisely when it
matters that both assumptions fail together.
