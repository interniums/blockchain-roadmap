Every analysis in the first three lessons treated the keeper set as a constant. A profitable call
exists, therefore someone makes it. The parameter tables, the incentive formulas, the auction curves
all sit on that one sentence, and the sentence is false in a specific and predictable way.

The keeper set is a population of bots, and every bot is a stack: an RPC provider, a funded hot wallet,
a price read, a route to an exit venue, a builder relationship, and an arithmetic check that must come
out positive at the prevailing gas price. Each of those degrades under load. All of them are under
load at the same time, because the thing that puts them under load is the thing that produces
liquidations.

**The liveness assumption is negatively correlated with the event it exists to survive.** That is the
single most important structural fact about liquidation reliability, and everything in this lesson is
a consequence of it.

## Six dependencies, one correlated failure

Take the stack apart and ask what a violent hour does to each layer.

**Price observation.** A push oracle updates on one of two triggers — a deviation threshold or a
heartbeat — so between updates the on-chain price is stale by construction, and consumers are told to
check freshness and handle the stale case themselves.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" /> During a
fast move the deviation trigger fires more often, which is more transactions competing for the same
congested blocks. March 2020 is the canonical demonstration: with gas prices high and the queue long,
price feeds could not update quickly enough, Chainlink's ETH feed stalled for hours, and Maker's
Medianizer reported $166 while the real price was around
$130.<Cite src="glassnode-makerdao-black-thursday" />

**Node freshness.** A bot decides from a state it read. If its provider is a block behind, every
health factor it computes is a block stale — and one block of staleness during a 43%-in-a-day move is
a materially different number.<Cite src="glassnode-makerdao-black-thursday" />

**RPC capacity.** Providers rate-limit per key. The moment your bot most wants to poll every position
in the market is the moment every other consumer of that provider also wants to, and the throttle is
a shared resource. Morpho's description of its own keeper population names the dependency: performing
a liquidation requires access to an RPC node and a wallet with sufficient assets, and competitive
liquidators run bots that continuously monitor loan positions and oracle
prices.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" /> Continuous monitoring is a continuous request rate.

**Gas balance.** A hot wallet holding 2 ETH is comfortable at 5 gwei and thin at 400 gwei. The March
2020 record shows mean hourly gas reaching roughly 200 gwei on the day and over 400 gwei the day
after.<Cite src="glassnode-makerdao-black-thursday" /> Wallets that ran dry did not fail because of a
bug.

**Exit depth.** The `V²/R` term from lesson one has `R` in the denominator, and `R` is what everyone
else is also consuming. Morpho names the same dependency per asset: for a collateral like cbBTC or
stETH the question is whether that market's specific incentive factor outweighs the costs of
liquidating *that* collateral type.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" />

**Inclusion.** Even a correct, funded, profitable transaction has to land. This is the layer that
produced the March 2020 zero-DAI auctions: bids submitted at ordinary gas prices were not processed
fast enough, so a bot willing to pay for priority won auctions against no competition at all with bids
of zero, taking more than $8M of ETH essentially for free and leaving at least $4.5M of DAI
unbacked.<Cite src="glassnode-makerdao-black-thursday" />

None of these is a keeper being careless. Every one of them is a dependency behaving exactly as
designed, under conditions the design was not tuned for.

## The gas coupling, worked

The band from lesson one is `V = (R/2)(b ± √(b² − 4G/R))`. Two of its three inputs are exogenous.

**Subgoal 1 — establish which term moves and in which direction.** `b` is a storage constant and does
not move. `G` rises with the base fee and with priority competition. `R` falls as depth is consumed.
Both movements shrink the discriminant `b² − 4G/R`.

**Subgoal 2 — put realistic multipliers on the movement.** Take a twentyfold gas increase and a halving
of depth, which is conservative against the March 2020 record of gas rising by an order of magnitude
and staying there for a day.<Cite src="glassnode-makerdao-black-thursday" />

**Subgoal 3 — read both roots, not just the lower one.** Starting from `b = 0.05`, `G = $20`,
`R = $10M`, the calm band runs from about $400 to about $499,600. At `G = $400` and `R = $5M` it runs
from about $8,275 to about $241,725. The floor rises twentyfold; the ceiling halves.

**Subgoal 4 — name what that does to the *set* of positions.** A market's positions are not uniformly
distributed in size. The floor moving from $400 to $8,275 does not strand a few positions; it strands
the entire retail tail, which in most markets is a large fraction of the position count and a small
fraction of the value. The ceiling falling from $500k to $242k does the opposite: it strands a small
number of positions carrying a large fraction of the value, which must now be liquidated in slices,
each slice paying `G` again.

**Subgoal 5 — find the point where the band vanishes.** The discriminant is zero when `G = b²R/4`. At
`b = 0.05` and `R = $5M` that is `G = $3,125`. Not a plausible gas cost — which is the useful result.
For a liquid market the band does not close from gas; it closes from `R`. Set `G = $400` and solve for
`R`: the band vanishes when `R = 4G/b² = $640,000`. **Depth, not gas, is what actually extinguishes
the incentive** — and depth is the input nobody puts in a risk parameter table.

Aave's release notes concede the same conclusion in prose: liquidations are highly influenced by gas
prices, liquidation bonus and secondary market liquidity, and the dust threshold must be chosen
best-effort for average network conditions.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" />

<Aside kind="warn">
`G` is not only the gas of your own logic. The liquidation call path reads configuration bitmaps,
reserve data, user positions and oracle answers, and each unfamiliar account or slot is charged at the
cold rate — 2,600 for a cold account access, 2,100 for a cold storage read on the measured
configuration, against 100 warm.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /> A liquidator's transaction is
storage-read-heavy before it does anything interesting, which is why the fixed term dominates for
small positions and why the floor is where it is.
</Aside>

## Where liquidation stops being possible

Now the second derivation, and it is the one that defines a lending market's actual solvency boundary.

The naive version of this is optimistic and worth stating so you can see where it breaks. Morpho's own
FAQ says that if the collateral price continues to fall after a position becomes liquidatable, the
position becomes *increasingly* attractive for liquidators because they can seize a larger percentage
of the collateral relative to its real market value, and that there is no price floor — the position
remains liquidatable until someone repays the debt.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" /> Both
sentences are true. Neither says the trade stays *profitable*, and that is the gap this section
closes.

**Subgoal 1 — write the condition for a full close.** A liquidator repaying the whole debt `D` receives
`D · LIF` in collateral, capped by the collateral that exists. Full closure is possible only while

```text
C  ≥  D · LIF        ⟺        D/C  ≤  1/LIF
```

So the LTV at which a single-shot full liquidation stops working is `1/LIF`. For a Morpho market with
`LLTV = 0.86`, the formula gives `LIF ≈ 1.044`, so that ceiling is about
95.8%.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" />

**Subgoal 2 — convert that into a price budget, which is the number you can actually act on.** At the
moment the position becomes liquidatable, `D/C = LLTV`. Debt is fixed in the short run; `C` falls with
price. The ratio `C/C_trigger` at which full closure becomes impossible is

```text
C/C_trigger  =  LLTV · LIF  =  0.86 × 1.044  ≈  0.898
```

**A further 10.2% fall in the collateral price consumes the entire distance between "liquidatable" and
"cannot be fully closed".** That is the whole cushion. Ten percent, in an asset that was volatile
enough to need an 86% LLTV in the first place. Run the same arithmetic for a more conservative Aave
market with a 0.80 liquidation threshold and a 5% bonus and you get `1 − 0.80 × 1.05 = 16%` — better,
and still a single bad hour.

**Subgoal 3 — refine it, because partial liquidation changes the endpoint.** Below `1/LIF` a full close
is impossible, but a *partial* one is not. Repaying `v` requires only `v · LIF` of collateral, so
liquidators grind the position down while

```text
v · (LIF − 1)  >  G      and      C_remaining  ≥  v · LIF
```

Combining, they keep going while `C_remaining ≥ LIF · G / (LIF − 1)`. At `LIF = 1.05` and `G = $20`
that is $420. The process therefore does not halt at a clean LTV; it grinds until the *remaining
collateral* is worth roughly a few hundred dollars, and everything not covered is bad debt of size
`D − C/LIF`.

This is why Aave defines the condition the way it does. Its v3.3 bad-debt feature checks, after a
liquidation, whether the account has zero collateral in base currency but retains some level of debt;
if so, the remaining variable debt tokens are burned and the amount is recorded as a `deficit` on the
reserve.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" /> The definition is not arbitrary — it is the endpoint of the
grind above, and the `MIN_LEFTOVER_BASE` rule exists to force liquidations to actually reach it rather
than stopping one dust-sized step short.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" />

<Check type="explain" concept="liq-bad-debt-threshold">
A market lists a volatile asset at an 80% liquidation threshold with a 6% net liquidation bonus.
Overnight the price gaps 22% below the level at which positions became liquidatable, with no
intervening blocks in which the position could have been closed at a better price. A $1,000,000
position was at exactly the threshold before the gap.

Work out what the liquidators do and what the protocol is left holding. Then say which parameter, if
any, would have prevented it.

<Answer>
**Set up the numbers.** At the trigger, `D/C = 0.80`. Take `D = $800,000` and `C = $1,000,000`. After a
22% fall, `C = $780,000`. Debt is unchanged at $800,000.

**Is a full close possible?** It needs `C ≥ D · 1.06 = $848,000`. We have $780,000. **No.** The
position is already past the point where anyone can close it in one call at a profit.

**What actually happens.** Partial liquidation still works. Liquidators repay `v` and take `v · 1.06`,
grinding the collateral down until what remains is too small to bother with — roughly
`1.06 × G / 0.06`, which at a $50 all-in cost is about $880. So essentially all $780,000 of collateral
leaves, retiring `780,000 / 1.06 ≈ $735,850` of debt.

**What the protocol is left holding.** `800,000 − 735,850 ≈ $64,150` of debt against approximately zero
collateral. That is bad debt, and it is now the lenders' loss: on Aave it would be burned and recorded
as a reserve `deficit`;<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" /> in a Morpho vault it would be realised through
share price, with every shareholder absorbing it proportionally as `totalAssets`
falls.<Cite src="morpho-bad-debt-docs" /> Note the shape of the loss: the *bonus itself* accounts for
$44,150 of it. Six percent of $735,850 went to liquidators out of collateral that was needed to cover
the debt.

**Which parameter would have prevented it — and this is the point.**

*Not the bonus.* Raising it to 10% makes the full-close ceiling stricter, not looser: `1 − 0.80 × 1.10
= 12%` of price budget instead of 15.2%. A larger bonus means less price movement can be absorbed
before full closure fails, and a larger share of the collateral leaves as incentive. It is the wrong
direction.

*Not the close factor.* Nothing here was limited by how much one call could repay.

*The liquidation threshold, yes — at a price.* Dropping it from 0.80 to 0.70 gives a price budget of
`1 − 0.70 × 1.06 = 25.8%`, which absorbs the 22% gap with room. It also means every borrower in the
market can borrow less against the same collateral, forever, to buy protection against an event that
may not recur. That is the real trade, and it is a business decision rather than a risk-model output.

*The supply cap, most of all.* A $1M position in an asset whose price can gap 22% is a statement about
how much of that asset the market agreed to accept. Caps are the parameter that bounds the loss when
the other parameters fail, and they are the one that costs the protocol revenue rather than costing
borrowers leverage — which is exactly why they are the hardest to get raised in a governance forum and
the easiest to raise too far.

**The honest summary.** The gap moved faster than the parameter set could absorb, and no keeper
behaviour was available that would have changed the outcome. This is what "structural" means.
</Answer>
</Check>

## The 2025 and 2026 evidence

Two on-chain events are worth carrying, and both need careful handling because the numbers attached to
them are less solid than the mechanisms.

**10 October 2025.** A market carrying very large open interest deleveraged over roughly a day with no
circuit breakers anywhere in the stack. The widely reported total is about nineteen billion dollars
liquidated.<Cite src="coindesk-19b-liquidation" /> Treat that figure as a **reported floor rather than
a measurement**: exchange liquidation feeds are known to under-report, with many venues publishing at
most one liquidation per second per symbol, and some analysts argue the true figure is materially
higher.<Cite src="coindesk-19b-liquidation" /> What is safe to carry is the shape, not the scalar.

**The on-chain consequence.** Curve's CRV-collateral LlamaLend market accumulated bad debt during that
crash, and the mechanism reported is exactly this lesson: prices moved faster than keepers could
profitably act on while gas costs spiked.<Cite src="coindesk-curve-bad-debt" /> The figure attached to
it in coverage is roughly $700,000, and Curve's founder later proposed a market-based recovery — a
dedicated pool — rather than a treasury bailout.<Cite src="coindesk-curve-bad-debt" /> Two caveats
belong with that. The number comes from secondary coverage of a governance proposal rather than from
the proposal itself, and the Curve governance forum returns HTTP 403 to automated retrieval, so it has
not been confirmed against the source. And the market in question uses a *soft* liquidation design,
which is the second half of this module — so this is evidence that continuous liquidation does not
exempt a market from the coupling either.

**The prior case.** In June 2024 a liquidation cascade in the same CRV-long Curve Lend market left it
with a substantial amount of bad debt, described as a temporary condition because the debt could be
cleared once CRV prices recovered sufficiently, and attributed to the relatively poor on-chain
liquidity profile of CRV precluding efficient liquidation.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" />
That is the `R` term in the discriminant, named in a post-mortem two years before the bigger event.

## What a protocol can do about it

Four responses, in descending order of how much they actually buy.

**Cap the exposure.** Supply caps, borrow caps and debt ceilings bound the size of the loss that the
liquidation mechanism can fail to prevent. Aave carries all three in the same configuration bitmap as
the bonus.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> This is the only lever that works when the incentive fails,
because it is the only one that does not depend on anybody showing up.

**Fund the shortfall explicitly.** Aave v3.3 introduced `deficit` on the reserve — reusing the
deprecated stable borrow rate storage slot — readable through `getReserveDeficit`, and an
`eliminateReserveDeficit` function callable by a permissioned entity, the registered Umbrella on the
PoolAddressesProvider, which burns aTokens to reduce it.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" /> Umbrella is
Aave's own name for that backstop layer and has its own documentation; treat its activation timeline
and per-asset coverage as things to read from governance records rather than to recall, because the
published summaries of both disagree.<Cite src="aave-umbrella-docs" /> The design goal is that bad debt
stops being an invisible accounting hole and becomes a named liability with a funding path; the notes
also state plainly that for positions already in bad debt the upgrade offers no solution and
recommends cleaning them up via `repayOnBehalf`.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" />

**Socialise it immediately rather than eventually.** Morpho Vault V2 realises losses through share
price: each adapter reports current value via `realAssets()`, the vault writes `totalAssets` down when
that falls, and `convertToAssets` therefore returns less for every share without burning
any.<Cite src="morpho-bad-debt-docs" /> The trade-off is honest accounting against a share price that
can fall, versus a hole that sits in the market indefinitely until someone
intervenes.<Cite src="morpho-bad-debt-docs" />

**Reduce `G`, not `b`.** Everything the protocol does to make the liquidation call cheaper widens the
band at the bottom without increasing the collateral dumped per dollar retired. Aave's v3.3 bitmask
flip and dedicated getters — reducing gas by up to about 16k in some paths — are exactly this kind of
work, and it is unglamorous for the same reason it is effective.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" />

<Check type="recall" concept="liq-gas-price-coupling">
State, in one sentence each, why raising the liquidation bonus is a poor response to congestion-driven
liquidation failure, and what the better response is.

<Answer>
**Why it fails.** A higher bonus raises the profit per liquidation and simultaneously raises the
collateral dumped per dollar of debt retired, so it buys reliability at the price of a steeper cascade
— and it cannot make an incentive uncorrelated with the conditions that suppress it, because the terms
it competes against, gas and depth, are exogenous and move together.

**What works instead.** Bound the exposure with caps so the failure is small, and reduce `G` so the
band's floor stays low — both act on the inequality without acting on the price, which is the only
place a protocol has real leverage.

**Worth adding.** A higher bonus also *tightens* the full-close ceiling: the price budget between
liquidatable and unclosable is `1 − LT · LIF`, which shrinks as `LIF` grows. The parameter you reach
for to make liquidations more certain makes bad debt arrive sooner.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson** is what happens when the forced selling this lesson describes is itself the thing
moving the price — the reflexive loop, its amplification condition, and the two ways it crosses from
one venue into another.

**A number to compute for any market you touch.** The price budget `1 − LT · LIF`. It is one
multiplication, it tells you how much room exists between "eligible" and "unrecoverable", and almost
nobody publishes it because it is not a parameter — it is a consequence of two parameters that are
usually set by different people at different times.

**A habit for reading incident reports.** When a post-mortem says liquidations failed, ask which of the
six dependencies broke and whether the profitable trade existed at all. Those are different failures
with different fixes, and the reflex to blame the keepers hides both.
