The last lesson ended with a menu and no argument for any item on it. Three protocols, three answers
to the same question — what discount does the liquidator get. One decides it in a governance forum
months in advance. One lets a clock decide it in the next four minutes. One, which the second half of
this module is about, refuses to have a discrete discount at all.

This lesson works the first two, plus the parameter that sits across both and gets quoted wrong more
often than any other number in DeFi.

## Design one: fix the discount, race for it

Aave and Morpho do the same thing, and the Compound-derived designs that preceded them share the
shape. A parameter says how much collateral a liquidator receives per unit of debt repaid. Anyone may
call. The instant the position becomes eligible, the call succeeds.

In Aave the parameter lives in the reserve configuration bitmap at bits 32–47, immediately after LTV
at 0–15 and the liquidation threshold at 16–31 — three per-asset numbers packed in one
slot.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> E-mode categories carry their own `liquidationBonus` alongside
their own `ltv` and `liquidationThreshold`, so the same collateral has a different bonus depending on
which category the borrower is in.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" />

Morpho removes the governance step entirely by making the incentive a function of the market's
liquidation LTV, capped at 1.15 with `β = 0.3`:<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" />

```text
LIF = min( 1.15 ,  1 / ( 0.3·LLTV + 0.7 ) )
```

Read the curve rather than the constants. It is decreasing in `LLTV`, and it has to be: at `LLTV = 1`
the denominator is 1 and the incentive is zero, because a market that only liquidates when debt equals
collateral has nothing left to pay a bonus out of. The cap binds where `1/(0.3L + 0.7) = 1.15`, which
is `L ≈ 0.565` — so every Morpho market with a liquidation LTV below about 56.5% pays the same maximum
15%.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" /> Aave reaches a similar place by per-asset governance; the
difference is who is accountable for the number.

### Where the surplus actually goes

This is the part the parameter's name hides.

Consider a position where the liquidator's gross take is $4,300 — Morpho's own worked example: an
$86.01k debt liquidated at 1.05 seizes $90.31k of collateral.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" />
Suppose your all-in cost is $200. Your economic profit is $4,100.

Now put four other bots on the same opportunity. None of you can bid a *smaller discount* — the
discount is not a variable, it is a constant in storage. The only dimension left is inclusion:
priority fee, private orderflow, a bundle with a builder. Competition therefore pushes what you will
pay for ordering toward $4,100, and the marginal winner captures approximately nothing.

So the borrower loses exactly $4,300, set by governance or by the formula; the liquidator keeps their
costs plus whatever competition has not yet eroded; and the ordering market absorbs the rest. The
protocol gets its debt retired and captures none of it. Morpho states the mechanism plainly:
first-come-first-served, no auction or bidding mechanism, and in competitive environments this leads
to MEV opportunities and potential gas price wars, with advanced liquidators using Flashbots or other
private transaction methods.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" /> This is the direct wire between
lending design and [searcher](/c/mev-searcher) economics, and it is why
[liquidation flow](/c/mev-liquidation) is among the most contested orderflow on chain.

<Aside kind="note">
The corollary is that liquidation MEV is not a leak to be plugged. It is the payment mechanism. The
same competition that extracts the bonus is what produces sub-block liquidation latency in normal
conditions. Suppress the competition and you have not saved the borrower a cent — the discount is
still fixed at 5% — you have only removed the reason anyone hurries.
</Aside>

## The parameter across both designs: the close factor

A close factor caps how much of a position one liquidation call may retire. It is the single most
misquoted number in this subject, and it repays five minutes of arithmetic.

Aave's Pool documentation states it: liquidators can only close a certain amount of collateral defined
by a close factor, currently 0.5, meaning a maximum of 50% of the amount pending to be repaid in a
position, with the liquidation discount applying to that amount.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> That
sentence is where "Aave's close factor is 50%" comes from, and it is not the whole picture.

### What a 50% cap costs, derived

Take a position with debt `D` in a market with net bonus `b`, where a liquidation costs `G` all-in. From
the last lesson, a call is worth making while the repaid amount exceeds roughly `G/b`.

Now liquidate repeatedly under a 50% cap. Round 1 repays `D/2`. Round 2 repays `D/4`. Round `k` repays
`D/2^k`. Each round is worth making while

```text
b · D / 2^k  >  G      ⟺      2^k  <  b·D / G
```

so the number of profitable rounds is about `log₂(b·D/G)`, and the process stops with residual debt of
roughly `G/b` — the minimum profitable size, exactly. The close factor does not *risk* leaving dust; it
**guarantees** leftover debt on the order of the profitable floor, on every position it touches, by
construction.

Two further costs compound it. The cap was applied per reserve rather than per position, so a borrower
with $1.5k each of GHO, USDC and DAI debt could have only $1.5k liquidated per call rather than the
full $3k.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" /> And the bad-debt cleanup introduced in the same release only
triggers when collateral reaches zero, so leaving dust *prevents* cleanup — while the cleanup itself
costs slightly more gas, giving liquidators a positive incentive to leave
dust.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" />

### What v3.3 did about it

Three changes, all verifiable in the release notes rather than in tutorials.

**The cap became position-scoped.** In v3.3 the close factor applies to the whole position, so the
$3k example above is liquidatable in one call.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" />

**A size-dependent 100% close factor.** Liquidations up to a 100% close factor are allowed whenever
the total principal or the total debt of the user on the reserve being liquidated is below
`MIN_BASE_MAX_CLOSE_FACTOR_THRESHOLD`.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" /> This is the derivation above,
implemented: below the threshold, halving is what creates the dust, so stop halving.

**Forced cleanup.** No debt or collateral below `MIN_LEFTOVER_BASE` may remain after a liquidation; if
either would be left below it and neither is exactly zero, the transaction reverts.
`MIN_LEFTOVER_BASE` is defined as `MIN_BASE_MAX_CLOSE_FACTOR_THRESHOLD / 2`, which guarantees full
liquidation is available across the whole range from zero to the threshold and that a 50% liquidation
above the threshold always leaves at least half of it.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" />

The v3.3 notes also carry the acknowledged limitation, and it is the honest sentence in the whole
document: liquidations are still highly influenced by gas prices, liquidation bonus and secondary
market liquidity, so `MIN_BASE_MAX_CLOSE_FACTOR_THRESHOLD` has to be chosen in a best-effort way to
allow liquidations under *average* network conditions.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" /> Their own
worked figure: a threshold of $2,000 means that at a 1% bonus, a liquidation cannot cost more than $20
before an economically reasonable liquidator stops.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" />

Read that as a confession about the tail. The threshold is calibrated for average conditions, and the
event it must survive is by definition not average.

Morpho takes the opposite position and it is worth seeing the contrast: liquidators can repay up to
100% of the borrower's debt in a single transaction, and can also choose any smaller amount, which
lets them optimise gas cost against profit on large positions where a full liquidation would cause
significant market impact.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" /> No cap, caller's choice. The dust
problem moves from the protocol's parameter to the liquidator's judgement, and the residual bad debt,
when it happens, is socialised through vault share price rather than sitting in a reserve
deficit.<Cite src="morpho-bad-debt-docs" />

<Check type="predict" concept="liq-close-factor">
A market has a 5% net bonus and liquidations cost $30 all-in. A borrower has $12,000 of debt and the
market enforces a 50% close factor per call. How many liquidation calls are profitable, roughly what
debt is left when they stop, and what happens to that residue?

<Answer>
**Rounds.** Repaid amount at round `k` is `12,000 / 2^k`, profitable while `0.05 × 12,000/2^k > 30`,
i.e. while `2^k < 0.05 × 12,000/30 = 20`. So `k` up to 4 (`2⁴ = 16 < 20`), and round 5 fails
(`2⁵ = 32`). **Four profitable calls.**

**Residue.** After four calls the debt is `12,000/16 = $750`. Check it against the floor: `G/b =
30/0.05 = $600`. The residue sits just above the floor — a fifth call would repay $375 for a $18.75
bonus against $30 of cost, so nobody makes it.

**What happens to it.** It sits. It accrues interest, which makes the position *less* healthy without
making it more liquidatable, because the bonus scales with the shrinking debt while gas does not. It
is the exact shape Aave describes as dust debt: no incentive to liquidate, and an ever-increasing
liability to the protocol.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" /> And because the collateral is not zero, the
v3.3 bad-debt burn does not fire either — that path triggers only when an account ends with zero
collateral and non-zero debt.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" />

**What fixes it.** Not a bigger bonus — that shifts the floor to $375 and buys one more round.
Allowing a 100% close factor below a threshold removes the halving that manufactured the residue in
the first place, which is precisely the v3.3 change.<Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" />
</Answer>
</Check>

## Design two: let a clock discover the discount

Maker's answer is not to know the right discount. Its Liquidations 2.0 Clipper offers the collateral
above the market and lets the price fall until someone takes it.

The mechanics are legible in the source. An auction is started by `kick`, which sets the opening price
from the feed, marked up by a configured multiplier: the comment states it as
`top = val · buf / par`, where `val` is the collateral's unitary value in USD, `buf` is a
multiplicative factor to increase the starting price, and `par` is the reference per
DAI.<Cite src="dss-clip-source" /> From there the price is a pure function of elapsed time, delegated
to a pluggable calculator: `price = calc.price(top, now − tic)`.<Cite src="dss-clip-source" />

A taker calls `take(id, amt, max, who, data)`. `amt` caps how much collateral to buy and `max` is the
maximum acceptable price, enforced by `require(max >= price)` — the taker's own slippage
guard.<Cite src="dss-clip-source" /> The auction ends for one of three reasons: the collateral runs
out, the debt is fully covered, or it needs a reset.

The reset condition is the interesting one:

```text
done = (now − tic > tail)  ||  (price / top < cusp)
```

`tail` is the time elapsed before auction reset and `cusp` is the percentage drop before auction
reset.<Cite src="dss-clip-source" /> Past either bound the auction is stale — the starting price it
was struck from no longer describes the market — and `redo` restarts it from a fresh feed
price.<Cite src="dss-clip-source" />

### What this buys, derived

Take a taker whose all-in cost of executing and exiting is a fraction `c` of the notional. They act as
soon as the auction price falls to `p_market · (1 − c)`. With several takers of differing efficiency,
the auction clears at the *lowest* `c` present — the discount is set by the best-equipped participant's
costs rather than by a governance estimate of the worst-equipped participant's costs.

That is the whole argument for the design. In calm conditions `c` is small, the clock barely has to
run, and the borrower keeps almost all of their equity. A fixed 5% bonus pays 5% whether the market is
calm or violent; a descending auction pays what the moment actually requires.

The cost is three things. **Latency**: retiring the debt takes as long as the price needs to fall, and
the collateral keeps moving meanwhile. **Complexity**: a stateful auction with a pluggable price
curve, reset logic and a dust rule is a far larger surface than one function. **A separate trigger
incentive**: nobody is paid by the discount for *starting* the auction, so `kick` pays its caller out
of protocol surplus — a flat `tip` plus a proportional `chip` of the debt — and `redo` does the same
for whoever resets a stale one.<Cite src="dss-clip-source" /> You have not removed the keeper
assumption; you have created a second one and funded it from a different pocket.

### Why the design exists at all

Maker did not arrive at a descending auction from theory. It arrived from 12 March 2020.

The predecessor was an ascending English auction: keepers bid against each other for fixed lots of
ETH. On the day, ETH fell 43% from $194 to $111, Ethereum's mean hourly gas price reached roughly
200 gwei and over 400 gwei the day after, and bids submitted at ordinary gas prices were not being
included.<Cite src="glassnode-makerdao-black-thursday" /> With competition unable to reach the
contract, one bot — later joined by copycats — won auctions with bids of zero DAI, taking more than
$8M of ETH essentially for free and leaving at least $4.5M of DAI
unbacked.<Cite src="glassnode-makerdao-black-thursday" /> Borrowers whose vaults were sold for zero
received nothing; the owner of one vault who should have received about 74 ETH received
zero.<Cite src="glassnode-makerdao-black-thursday" /> The immediate governance response raised the
maximum lot size from 50 to 500 ETH and lengthened auctions.<Cite src="glassnode-makerdao-black-thursday" />

The auction was not exploited. It cleared correctly. With one bidder able to participate, the clearing
price of a competitive auction is that bidder's bid, and the bid was zero.

  belief="An auction guarantees fair price discovery, so an auction-based liquidation is safer than a fixed discount."
  reality="An auction discovers the best price among participants who can reach it. Its guarantees are conditional on inclusion, and inclusion is exactly what fails during the events that produce mass liquidations. In March 2020 the auctions cleared fairly at nearly nothing."
  why="Auction theory is taught with a costless, always-available bidding channel. On a congested chain the channel is neither."
  src="glassnode-makerdao-black-thursday"
/>

The Clipper's design answers that specific failure. A descending price means a taker never needs to
outbid anyone — they need only to arrive before someone else does, and arriving late costs them the
better price rather than the whole opportunity. `take` is atomic and instant rather than a
multi-block bidding process. And `cusp` bounds how far the price may fall before the auction must be
re-struck from a fresh feed, which caps how deep a single stale auction can go.<Cite src="dss-clip-source" />

What it does not fix: if *nobody* can land a transaction, a descending auction falls to `cusp`, resets,
falls again, and resets again. Faster than an English auction, cheaper for whoever eventually arrives,
and still dependent on somebody arriving.

<Check type="explain" concept="liq-dutch-auction">
Your team runs a lending market with a fixed 8% bonus and is considering a switch to a descending
auction starting 10% above the oracle price and decaying to a 25% discount over 20 minutes. A risk
reviewer objects: "we would be replacing a known 8% cost to borrowers with an unknown cost that could
reach 25%." Answer the objection, then name what the switch actually costs you.

<Answer>
**The objection is measuring the wrong end of the distribution.** Under the fixed design borrowers pay
8% on *every* liquidation, including the ones that happen on a quiet Tuesday when a searcher would
have taken the trade for 40 basis points. Under the auction they pay 40 basis points on the quiet
Tuesday and up to 25% in the one hour a year when nothing else clears. Compare the expected cost, not
the worst case, and then compare the worst cases as worst cases: 25% versus 100%, because a fixed 8%
bonus that no longer clears produces bad debt and a borrower who loses everything.

**What the switch actually costs you, in order of how much it will hurt.**

*A second keeper dependency.* Someone must call `kick` to start each auction and `redo` to reset stale
ones, and neither is paid by the discount. Maker funds both from protocol surplus with a flat fee plus
a proportional cut of the debt being auctioned.<Cite src="dss-clip-source" /> That is a new budget
line and a new liveness assumption.

*Non-atomicity for the borrower's position.* Under a fixed discount the debt is retired in one
transaction. Under an auction there is a window in which the collateral is committed and the debt is
not yet repaid, and the price keeps moving during it. Your reset parameters — how long before a reset,
how far a fall before a reset — are what bound that exposure.<Cite src="dss-clip-source" />

*A dust rule you now have to design.* Partial takes leave partial auctions. Maker handles this with a
minimum-lot constraint: below it, buyers must take the entire remaining lot or nothing, and purchase
amounts are minimally reduced to respect the limit.<Cite src="dss-clip-source" /> Without something
equivalent you get the close-factor dust problem back, in a new place.

*Integration surface.* A fixed discount is one call that composes trivially with a flash loan. An
auction is a stateful object that liquidators must monitor. Maker mitigated this by building the
callback into the taker path — `take` sends the collateral and then calls `clipperCall` on the
receiver before collecting payment.<Cite src="dss-clip-source" /> That is the subject of the next
lesson, and it is not optional: an auction nobody has integrated with is an auction with one bidder.
</Answer>
</Check>

## The comparison worth carrying

Strip both designs to what actually differs.

**Who sets the discount.** Fixed: governance, or a formula over `LLTV`.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" />
Auction: the clock, bounded by `buf` at the top and `cusp` at the bottom.<Cite src="dss-clip-source" />

**Who captures the surplus above the marginal liquidator's cost.** Fixed: the ordering
market.<Cite src="morpho-liquidation-docs" /> Auction: the borrower, because a discount not reached is
a discount not paid.

**What the design assumes.** Fixed: that a profitable call is made promptly. Auction: that a profitable
take is made before reset, *and* that somebody starts and restarts the auction.

**How each fails.** Fixed: silently, by nobody calling — the function still exists and calling it is a
loss. Auction: loudly, by clearing at whatever the one reachable bidder offered.

Both failure modes have the same root and it is not the discount mechanism. It is that the keeper set
is treated as a constant in the analysis and is a variable in reality — the subject of lesson four,
after the next lesson establishes what a modern keeper actually is. The 10 October 2025 cascade is
where both designs were tested simultaneously at scale.<Cite src="coindesk-19b-liquidation" />

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson** is the liquidator itself: a contract that holds no inventory, commits no capital,
and reverts if the arithmetic does not work. Once you have built one, the phrase "the incentive must
exceed the liquidator's cost" stops being abstract, because you will have written the line that
computes it and the line that reverts when it is negative.

**A habit for reading any lending protocol.** Find three things before forming an opinion: where the
discount comes from, what caps a single call, and who pays whoever triggers the mechanism. In Aave:
a bitmap field, a close factor with branches, nobody.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /><Cite src="aave-v3-origin-33" />
In Maker: a decaying curve, a minimum-lot rule, `tip` plus `chip`.<Cite src="dss-clip-source" /> In
Curve's Controller: a `liquidation_discount` folded into the health calculation and a caller-chosen
`frac` on `liquidate_extended`.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-controller-docs" /> Those three answers predict
most of how a protocol behaves under stress.
