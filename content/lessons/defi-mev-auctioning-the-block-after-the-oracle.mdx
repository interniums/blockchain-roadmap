Lesson four ended on a line that should have been uncomfortable. A lending protocol sets a liquidation
bonus to buy speed, its own oracle's write is what creates the opportunity, and the money ends up with
a builder the protocol has no relationship with.

Every other mechanism in this module has had to fight over *who gets to be somewhere in an ordering*.
This one is different in a way that makes it unusually tractable: **the profitable position is known in
advance, and it is always the same position.** Immediately after the oracle update. Nowhere else.

A thing that is known in advance can be sold instead of raced for.

## Naming the quantity precisely

Chainlink's own documentation gives the definition and the boundary. Oracle-extractable value is a
subset of MEV created during the transmission of oracle reports on chain and their subsequent
consumption by on-chain applications: when a price report reveals that a collateralised position is
eligible for liquidation, searchers compete to place a liquidation transaction immediately after the
oracle update transaction — **backrunning**.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> Because oracles supply
the updates, the resulting liquidation MEV is inherently oracle-related.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" />

Two properties follow that separate OEV from everything earlier in this module.

**There is no user to front-run.** The value is not created by a trader's pending transaction; it is
created by the oracle's write changing state. That is why filing OEV under front-running is a category
error — the party losing value is the protocol, not a trader.

**The winning position is a constant.** For a sandwich, the attacker must solve for where to sit and
how large to go. Here the answer is "next", every time, for every liquidatable position the update
unlocks. Uniform, predictable prizes are exactly what auctions are for.

## How the auction is actually wired

The deployed mechanism is worth reading in mechanical detail, because it answers the obvious objection
before you can raise it: *what happens if the auction fails and the price does not get published?*

Chainlink's Smart Value Recapture extends standard price feeds with an optional **private transmission
flow**, using a dual-aggregator architecture to enable an auction for the opportunity to backrun
liquidations.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> The steps:

**Dual transmission.** The data network, triggered by [heartbeat or deviation
threshold](/c/orac-deviation-and-heartbeat), sends a price report to the **standard aggregator** via
the public mempool — the traditional flow.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /><Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" />
*Simultaneously*, it sends the same report to the **SVR aggregator** through a private channel, such
as an orderflow auction.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" />

**The auction.** In the private channel, searchers see the incoming oracle report and bid to backrun
the price update with a liquidation transaction; builders select the highest bid and bundle that
liquidation into the same block.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> If no bid is placed, the price
update can still be published on chain without any bundled liquidation.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" />

**Settlement.** The winning bundle is published, the SVR feed updates with the new price, and in the
same block the backrunning transaction uses the freshly updated price to liquidate the position — so
the oracle-related MEV is partly recaptured by the DeFi protocol and the oracle
network.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" />

**The fail-safe.** If the private route fails or times out, the SVR feed automatically reverts to the
standard feed price after a **configurable delay**, which can be set to any number of
seconds.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> This is what keeps the feed from stalling when the private
channel is unavailable.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" />

Integration is deliberately trivial: SVR feeds keep the same aggregator interface and data structure
as standard price feeds, so most protocols point at a different address and change
nothing else.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> Every SVR proxy is complemented by a "Standard" proxy,
so a protocol that prefers not to use it can opt out by reading from
that.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" />

<Aside kind="note">
Note which market it plugs into. The private channel is an orderflow auction of the kind lesson six
derived — reveal controlled information, sell the position immediately after, return proceeds to a
nominated party.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-share-repo" /><Cite src="flashbots-mev-share-announcement" />
The difference is only in who the nominated party is. There, the user who created the opportunity;
here, the protocol whose parameter created it. The auction machinery is the same machinery.
</Aside>

## Worked derivation: what the protocol is trading

The mechanism is not free, and the cost is paid in a currency lesson four already gave you a formula
for.

**Subgoal 1 — establish that the total does not change.** Under a race, `n` searchers compete for a
prize worth `V` and bid up to `V - g`; the surplus lands with whoever sells the position. Under an
auction, `n` searchers bid for the same prize with the same costs and the same competitive dynamic. The
clearing bid is the same. **What changes is the recipient of the bid, not its size.**
Chainlink's risk section states the same thing without algebra: MEV does not disappear, SVR recaptures
only the oracle-related portion, and protocol builders should remain MEV-aware
regardless.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" />

**Subgoal 2 — find what recapture depends on.** Since the bid tracks the competitive floor, the amount
recovered depends on how contested the auction is. The documentation says exactly this: actual results
depend on market conditions, competition among searchers, and factors such as protocol
design.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> The mechanism is also agnostic to which liquidator wins,
maximising for the highest payment and therefore for the highest recapture
rate.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> That is the correct objective for the protocol and worth
noticing: it is *not* optimising for the fastest liquidation, only for the largest payment among
liquidations that clear.

**Subgoal 3 — price the delay.** The auction requires a window. Chainlink names it as the first entry
under risk: SVR introduces a small, configurable delay to allow for the auction, and in the event of a
private-route failure the dual aggregator reverts to the standard price after a
delay.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" />

Now apply lesson four's solvency inequality. The buffer between breach and bad debt is:

```text
d <= 1 - LT * (1 + b)
```

where `d` is the adverse collateral move tolerated between breach and execution. A recapture delay
directly consumes `d`. So:

```text
recapture revenue   is bought with   a slice of the solvency buffer
```

**Subgoal 4 — read the parameter you actually control.** The delay is configurable in
seconds,<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> and `LT` and `b` are governance
parameters.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> So the choice is a three-way one, and it is quantitative
rather than philosophical: a longer window makes the auction more contested and the recapture larger,
and spends more of the same budget that protects against a cascade. Chainlink's guidance points the
same way — protocols should parameterise for the delay
risk.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" />

**Subgoal 5 — note where the trade is worst.** Both terms move against you at once during volatility.
That is when `d` is consumed fastest *and* when the recapture is largest, so the temptation to widen
the window is strongest exactly when the buffer is thinnest. A delay parameter chosen on a calm day is
a parameter chosen under the wrong conditions.

## What it does not do

**It does not reduce the borrower's cost.** The bonus is unchanged; only its destination moves. Whether
any of the recaptured value reaches the liquidated borrower is a distribution decision made by whoever
receives it — recaptured revenue is split between the integrating protocol and the oracle network at a
rate the documentation says may be subject to change.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> This module
publishes no percentage for that split, precisely because the source says it is subject to revision:
treat any specific figure as something to read off the current documentation on the day you need it.

**It does not cover other MEV.** SVR is purpose-built for recapturing non-toxic liquidation-related
OEV via backrunning and **cannot be used for harmful forms** such as front-running or sandwich
attacks.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> That constraint is a feature — the mechanism sells only the
position after the update — and it also bounds what integrating buys you.

**It does not change your other oracle obligations.** The feed is read through the same interface,
which means every consumption rule you already have still applies: [staleness
checks](/c/orac-staleness-check), decimals, the fallback path, and the developer responsibilities the
data-feeds documentation sets out.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" /><Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" />

<Check type="explain" concept="mev-oev">
A lending protocol's risk committee is asked to approve an OEV integration. The proposal says:
"Recaptured revenue funds the safety module at no cost to users, since liquidations still happen in
the same block."

Name the cost the proposal has omitted, and say what you would require before approving.

<Answer>
**The omitted cost is the delay, and it is denominated in the same budget that protects against bad
debt.**

The auction needs a window, and that window is configurable in
seconds.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> "Liquidations still happen in the same block" describes the
happy path, in which a bid arrives promptly. The relevant question is the unhappy path: what happens
when no bid arrives, or the private route times out, and the feed falls back to the standard price
after the configured delay.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> During that interval the protocol is
operating on a stale price by design.

By the inequality from lesson four, `d <= 1 - LT*(1+b)`, every second of delay is spent from the
buffer between a health-factor breach and bad debt. So the proposal is not "revenue at no cost". It is
"revenue purchased with solvency margin", which may still be a good trade — but it has to be sized.

**What to require before approving**, in order:

*The delay parameter, and the worst-case behaviour at that value.* Not the expected latency — the
configured fallback interval, and what the position's health looks like after an adverse move of the
size your collateral can make in that interval.

*The recomputed buffer.* Take your current `LT` and `b`, compute the tolerated move, and subtract what
the delay can consume during a volatile period. If the remaining margin is thinner than the committee
would have approved as a parameter change on its own, the integration is a parameter change and should
be reviewed as one.

*The fallback path, tested.* The fail-safe is that the SVR feed reverts to the standard price if the
private route fails.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> Verify it in a fork test, and confirm your
staleness handling behaves correctly on the SVR proxy — a different address with the same interface is
exactly the shape of integration where a configuration error is
invisible.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /><Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" />

*The revenue split, read from the current documentation on the day of the vote*, since it is stated as
subject to change.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" />

*The distribution decision, made explicitly.* Recapture nominates a recipient; it does not decide what
that recipient does. If the committee intends borrowers to benefit, that is a separate proposal, and
"at no cost to users" should not be used until it exists.

*The opt-out, documented.* Every SVR proxy is complemented by a standard
proxy,<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> so reversal is a configuration change. Write down who can make
it and how quickly.

**What a good approval looks like.** "We accept a delay of X seconds, which reduces our tolerated
adverse move from A% to B%; we have modelled that against our collateral's realised volatility; the
recaptured revenue goes to the safety module; and reverting to the standard proxy takes one governance
action we have rehearsed."
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The last lesson** takes every mechanism in this module to a chain where one party holds the entire
ordering right. The OEV idea survives that transition better than most — an operator who controls
ordering can sell the position after an oracle update without needing a builder market at all — which
makes it a useful bridge.

**In protocol design**, the general move is worth abstracting away from oracles entirely. Wherever your
protocol *creates* an ordering-dependent opportunity by its own action — a rebase, a rate update, a
parameter change, an epoch rollover — the position after that action is an asset. You can let it be
raced for, or you can sell it and nominate the recipient. The mechanism exists and the integration
surface is small.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" />

**In review**, add one question to the oracle section of your checklist: *what becomes possible in the
transaction immediately after our feed updates, and who currently gets it?* Most protocols have never
computed the answer, and it is usually larger than the fee revenue they are optimising.
