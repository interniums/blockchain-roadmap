Every response in the last two lessons leaves one thing standing. A protected endpoint hands the
transaction to a builder in the clear. An orderflow auction publishes hints on purpose. An intent
removes the executable path but the solver's settlement transaction is still assembled, still seen,
and still ordered by somebody.

**At some point before ordering is fixed, a party reads your transaction.** Everything so far has
argued about *which* party. This lesson is about the two live attempts to make that sentence false —
one by encrypting the transaction, one by putting the reader inside hardware nobody can look into —
and about what each one asks you to trust instead.

Both are unshipped. That is not a reason to skip them: they are being specified in detail, the
specifications name their own weaknesses precisely, and reading those weakness sections is the fastest
way to understand what encryption actually costs.

## The idea, and the two specifications that exist

Encrypt pending transactions so their contents are unreadable until after ordering has been committed.
The attacker who needs to see a transaction in order to place trades around it now sees ciphertext,
and by the time the plaintext exists the position has already been assigned.

This has moved from essays to draft specifications. Two are on the Standards Track as of August 2026,
and they take noticeably different routes.

**EIP-8105, Universal Enshrined Encrypted Mempool.** It proposes to enshrine an encrypted mempool into
the protocol, letting users encrypt transactions until they have been included in a block — protecting
them from front-running and sandwiching and increasing censorship-resistance
guarantees.<Cite src="eip-8105" /> Its defining choice is to be **encryption-technology agnostic**: the
design supports arbitrary decryption key providers, which can be based on threshold encryption, MPC
committees, TEEs, delay encryption, or FHE schemes.<Cite src="eip-8105" /> Traditional plaintext
transactions remain supported, and progression of the chain is guaranteed even if decryption key
providers fail.<Cite src="eip-8105" /> Status: **Draft**.<Cite src="eip-8105" />

**EIP-8184, LUCID.** It specifies a mechanism for carrying encrypted transactions through Ethereum's
public inclusion pipeline while keeping contents hidden until after the scheduling decision is
fixed.<Cite src="eip-8184" /> By requiring **commitment before reveal** and delaying decryption until
that point, it aims to protect MEV-sensitive orderflow, limit probabilistic front-running by
minimising pre-execution information leakage, and broaden the practical censorship resistance of
inclusion lists.<Cite src="eip-8184" /> It too stays open to different off-protocol decryption schemes
— including trustless self-decryption — rather than coupling Ethereum to a single cryptographic
construction.<Cite src="eip-8184" /> Status: **Draft**.<Cite src="eip-8184" />

Notice what the two agree on, since agreement across independent proposals is the closest thing to a
settled result here. Neither enshrines a cryptographic scheme. Both treat the decryption mechanism as
pluggable, and both are explicit that the protocol change is about the *pipeline* — commitment,
scheduling, reveal — rather than about the cryptography.<Cite src="eip-8105" /><Cite src="eip-8184" />

### What it looks like from the execution layer

EIP-8105 is worth reading concretely, because it lands in machinery you already know.

It adds **two new EIP-2718 transaction types**. Type `0x05` carries an encrypted payload alongside a
key-provider identifier and a key identifier; type `0x06` is the decrypted form the block eventually
executes.<Cite src="eip-8105" /> A system contract called the **key provider registry** is deployed in
the execution layer, allowing any account to register a key provider and receive a unique id;
registration specifies a contract with a decryption function and a key-validation
function.<Cite src="eip-8105" /> The beacon chain replicates that registry in its state, analogously
to the mechanism that handles deposits.<Cite src="eip-8105" /> And the Payload Timeliness Committee —
the body EIP-7732 introduces for attesting to payload reveal<Cite src="eip-7732" /> — gains additional
responsibilities.<Cite src="eip-8105" />

That last dependency is the practical headline. **This is not a change you ship on its own.** It sits
downstream of enshrined proposer-builder separation, which was itself at Review status and unshipped
as of August 2026.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

## What it costs, in the specification's own words

The most useful part of EIP-8105 is not the mechanism. It is the security-considerations section,
because it enumerates the trust the design cannot remove — and it is more specific than any critique
written from outside.

**Users must trust their key provider in two directions at once.** Not to release decryption keys
early, which would allow front-running and sandwiching attacks; and not to release them late, which
would prevent execution of the transaction *while the envelope fee still has to be
paid*.<Cite src="eip-8105" /> The second failure is the one people do not anticipate: a key provider
that goes quiet does not merely delay you, it charges you for the privilege.

**Withholding is not a bug in the design; it is permitted by it.** The protocol explicitly allows
decryption key providers to withhold keys under conditions of their choosing, so that they can
implement rules about who may use which keys — based on prior payment, for instance, or to prevent key
identifier front-running.<Cite src="eip-8105" /> Keys withheld unjustifiably may be used in custom
slashing mechanisms and reliability metrics, since the protocol records which keys were present and
which were not.<Cite src="eip-8105" /> Read carefully: the accountability is *available to be built*,
not built in.

**The trust extends beyond your own provider.** A user sending an encrypted transaction must trust not
only their own key provider but any key provider used for **earlier transactions in the same
block**.<Cite src="eip-8105" /> The reason is subtle and worth carrying: a key provider can choose to
publish or withhold *after* observing the decryption keys for following transactions, which gives them
one bit of influence over the pre-state of later transactions — and a maliciously chosen "decryption"
scheme could be far stronger, allowing crafted keys to modify or set parts of the decryption result
outright, which effectively enables front-running.<Cite src="eip-8105" />

**There are no in-protocol incentives.** The proposal enshrines neither a fee mechanism for key
providers nor punishments for misbehaviour, leaving incentive models to be implemented off-chain —
agreements with builders, per-transaction payment by users, or operation as a public
good.<Cite src="eip-8105" /> That is a deliberate design choice with a familiar shape: the same shape
as the relay, which is also an essential component with no protocol-level obligations.

**And concentration reappears.** If every user trusted only their own provider, builders could include
transactions from a single provider per block, which the EIP notes would make it difficult for
providers with small market share to compete and risks creating a **key provider
monopoly**.<Cite src="eip-8105" /> The design's compromise is to have providers declare which other
providers they trust, forming a directed trust graph that users implicitly accept by using the
provider's keys.<Cite src="eip-8105" />

### What encryption does not remove

Run the two conditions from lesson one. Encryption attacks condition two's *inputs* — the information
needed to target a specific transaction — and leaves condition one untouched. Somebody still chooses
the ordering; they now choose it blind.

Three categories survive.

**Everything computable from post-execution state.** Once the block executes, the pool is dislocated
and the backrun is there for whoever is next. Encryption delays the information; it does not prevent
the opportunity from existing afterwards, and DEX arbitrage — the largest and most competitive
category — never needed to see anybody's pending transaction in the first
place.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

**Statistical targeting.** LUCID's own framing names the residual as *probabilistic* front-running,
which it aims to limit by minimising pre-execution information
leakage.<Cite src="eip-8184" /> Limit, not eliminate. Size, timing, gas limit, sender and fee fields
leak structure even when the payload does not.

**Censorship, unless something else addresses it.** A builder cannot censor what it cannot read, which
is a genuine improvement — but a builder can still exclude by sender, and inclusion guarantees are a
separate mechanism. FOCIL, the fork-choice-enforced inclusion-list proposal, motivates itself on
exactly this: auctioning the right to build blocks to specialised entities led to a few sophisticated
builders dominating block production and a deterioration of the network's censorship-resistance
properties.<Cite src="eip-7805" /> It was at **Draft** status as of August 2026 and
[is not live](/c/inclusion-lists-are-not-live).<Cite src="eip-7805" />

## The other route: put the builder inside the box

The second attempt does not encrypt the transaction. It encrypts the *builder*.

BuilderNet's design is a multi-operator system where many parties operate the same block builder. Each
operator runs an instance of an open-source builder inside a **Trusted Execution Environment**, which
orderflow providers — apps, wallets, users, searchers — can verify and send encrypted orderflow
to.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" /> Each instance **shares the orderflow it receives with other
instances in the network**, and submits blocks to MEV-Boost relays as
usual.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" /> After an instance wins a block, refunds are calculated and
distributed back to orderflow providers based on the value they added to the
block.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" /> The first release was jointly operated by Flashbots, Beaverbuild
and Nethermind, using an open-source refund rule.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" /><Cite src="bytenoob-buildernet" />

The enclave is not there to protect users from builders. It is there to make **sharing between
competitors possible at all**: the network runs in TEEs to prevent operators from leaking data or
tampering with their code, which is what lets a searcher's bundle cross between instances without any
operator reading it.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" />

### Worked derivation: why sharing is the whole design

This is where lesson five's algebra pays off, and the result is sharper than the marketing.

**Subgoal 1 — recall the two facts that produced concentration.** Block value is superadditive in
orderflow, so `V(A ∪ B) >= V(A) + V(B)`. And in a first-price auction the winner bids toward the
runner-up's value and keeps `V₁ - V₂`.

**Subgoal 2 — apply sharing.** If every instance receives the union of all orderflow sent to any
instance,<Cite src="buildernet-intro" /> then every instance has the same input set. Since they run
bit-identical software,<Cite src="buildernet-intro" /> they compute the same block value:
`V₁ = V₂ = ... = V_n`.

**Subgoal 3 — read what happens to the gap.** `V₁ - V₂ → 0`. The term that was the builder's profit,
and that was funding payments for exclusive flow, collapses. There is no durable advantage to
accumulate, because the input that generated the advantage is no longer exclusive to anyone.

**Subgoal 4 — read what happens to the proposer.** The winning bid, which tracked `V₂`, now tracks
`V₁`. The proposer receives close to the block's full value rather than the runner-up's. BuilderNet's
own analysis is consistent with this and honest about the timing: because builders already withhold
value from validators today, the launch was not expected to change validator economics significantly
at first, with the shift arriving as orderflow providers come to retain a greater share of the MEV
they produce.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" />

**Subgoal 5 — name the target precisely.** The design attacks **exclusive-orderflow lock-in**, not MEV.
The extraction still happens; what changes is that no single operator owns the position from which to
do it, and the value is refunded toward the parties who generated the flow rather than retained as
builder margin.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" />

**Subgoal 6 — find the substitution.** The trust did not disappear. It moved from *"this operator will
not read my orderflow or misuse it"* to *"this hardware enclave is sound and its attestation chain is
honest"*. That is a different assumption, arguably a better one — it is falsifiable by a hardware
vulnerability rather than by an operator's private decision — but it is an assumption, and it has a
vendor.

### The censorship property, stated exactly

One detail of BuilderNet is easy to misread in both directions. Each operator chooses their own
compliance policy: they share all orderflow equally with other operators, but may exclude transactions
from certain addresses **in their own blocks**.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" />

So the guarantee is not "no censorship". It is that one operator can include a transaction another
operator excludes, which makes the network's overall censorship resistance greater than that of any
individual centralised builder.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" /> That is a real improvement stated at
exactly its true strength — and it is a good model for how to write this kind of claim.

<Answer>
**Level one — the schedule claim is not supported.**

Two encrypted-mempool EIPs existed at **Draft** status in August 2026.<Cite src="eip-8105" /><Cite src="eip-8184" />
Draft is the start of the Standards Track, not the end. The more developed of the two also depends on
the Payload Timeliness Committee introduced by enshrined PBS,<Cite src="eip-8105" /><Cite src="eip-7732" />
and that proposal was itself at Review and unshipped.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> Planning a year of
engineering around a Draft whose prerequisite is also unshipped is planning around two independent
schedule risks. The nearby cautionary case is FOCIL: widely discussed, genuinely important, and still
at Draft.<Cite src="eip-7805" />

**Level two — the effect claim is overstated even if it ships.**

Encryption removes pre-execution information about *contents*. It does not remove ordering discretion,
and the specifications say so: LUCID's stated goal is to **limit** probabilistic front-running by
minimising pre-execution leakage.<Cite src="eip-8184" /> Post-execution opportunities are untouched —
the dislocation your swap creates is still there for the next transaction, which is the identity from
lesson seven. And the advocacy for encryption explicitly preserves arbitrage, liquidations and
backrunning as desirable.<Cite src="encrypted-mempool-org" />

More concretely for this team: your slippage bound is what stops a bad fill, and bad fills arrive from
ordinary volatility and thin depth as well as from attackers. Removing one source of adverse execution
does not make an unbounded `amountOutMin` safe.

**Level three — the trust claim is missing.**

The argument treats "encrypted" as strictly better with no cost column. The specification's own
security considerations disagree: users must trust key providers not to release keys early *and* not
to release them late — late release preventing execution while the envelope fee is still
owed<Cite src="eip-8105" /> — must additionally trust providers used by earlier transactions in the
same block,<Cite src="eip-8105" /> and get no in-protocol incentives or punishments to rely
on.<Cite src="eip-8105" /> A team that would have to explain a stuck-and-charged transaction to its
users should read that list before treating the change as pure upside.

**What to recommend instead**, in priority order:

*Ship the bounded things now.* Computed slippage bounds, real deadlines, per-size routing policy.
These reduce loss today, are unaffected by any fork, and are the ones that would still matter after
encryption ships.

*Choose routing as a policy, not a default.* The comparison from lessons six and seven — who sees it,
which positions are for sale, what comes back — resolved differently by trade size. That is
implementable this quarter.

*Track the specifications rather than the commentary.* Status fields on the EIPs, and the
prerequisite's status, are two facts that answer the schedule question in a minute and do not go
stale.<Cite src="eip-8105" /><Cite src="eip-8184" /><Cite src="eip-7732" />

*Treat shared-orderflow building as the nearer-term structural change*, because it is deployed rather
than proposed, and because it changes the market your transactions already travel
through.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" />

**The general habit.** "It will be fixed at the protocol layer" is a claim with a status field
attached. Look it up before it costs you a roadmap.

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** is the smallest and most concrete mechanism in the module, and the only one where
the value is created by a protocol rather than by a user — which means, unusually, that the recipient
can be chosen in advance instead of fought over.

**Lesson ten** moves the whole question to a chain with one sequencer, where the encryption debate has
a very different shape: there is no builder market to decentralise, and the ordering policy is
whatever one operator decides it is.

**When you next assess a protocol-layer promise**, the routine from this lesson generalises. Find the
specification. Read its security-considerations section before its abstract. Check the status field,
then check the status field of anything it depends on. Specifications that name their own weaknesses
precisely — as both of these do — are the most useful documents in the space, and almost nobody reads
that far.<Cite src="eip-8105" />
