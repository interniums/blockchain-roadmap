The advice is one line long and it is everywhere: change your wallet's RPC endpoint, and you are
protected from MEV.

Half of that is true, and it is the useful half. The other half is a claim about a threat model that
nobody stated, and this lesson is about what actually changed. The short version: **the extraction did
not move away from your transaction. The extractor moved.**

## What a protected endpoint does

Ethereum's documentation describes the original mechanism precisely: Flashbots extends execution
clients with a service that allows searchers to submit MEV transactions to validators **without
revealing them to the public mempool**, which prevents those transactions from being front-run by
generalised frontrunners.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

A protected user-facing endpoint applies the same idea to ordinary users. Your transaction goes to one
or more builders directly instead of being gossiped, so it never enters
[the public broadcast](/c/mempool-is-a-public-broadcast) that every sandwich bot
watches.<Cite src="flashbots-protect-docs" />

Three consequences follow immediately, and the third is usually left out.

**Public-mempool sandwiching stops.** Not "is discouraged" — the attack requires seeing the
transaction before it executes,<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> and the channel it was seen through no
longer carries it. This is a genuine and complete defeat of one specific attacker.

**Failed transactions stop costing you.** A private endpoint can decline to include a transaction that
would revert, so you are not charged for a failure. MEV Blocker exposes this as a distinct endpoint —
`/noreverts` — separate from its default.<Cite src="mev-blocker-site" />

**The receiving builder sees everything.** Your transaction is not encrypted, not blinded, and not
split. It is handed, in the clear, to the party that this module has just spent a lesson establishing
as the one with actual ordering discretion.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

### What the evidence actually supports

Sandwich extraction on Ethereum did fall substantially over 2024 and 2025, and the decline is
attributed to adoption of protected endpoints and intent-based routing rather than to any protocol
change.<Cite src="shutter-mev-2026" /> Take the direction seriously. Do not take the magnitude on
faith: the figures in circulation come from a small number of parties who sell mitigations, and no
neutral measurement of the same quantity over the same window was available when this module was
written.<Cite src="shutter-mev-2026" />

The same discipline applies, harder, to the question everyone asks next: *what share of Ethereum
transactions is now private?* Published answers to that question range enormously, and the reason is
that they are not answers to the same question. One source measures **share of transactions**, another
**share of gas** in top builders' blocks, another **share of value**. Those three quantities move
differently and can be individually correct while jointly contradictory.

So this module publishes no private-orderflow percentage. What it publishes is the structural claim,
which is stable: a large and growing fraction of economically significant flow reaches builders
privately, and the fraction is high enough that private orderflow is a determinant of builder bidding
rather than a rounding error.<Cite src="arxiv-private-orderflow" /><Cite src="buildernet-intro" /> If
you need a number, take it from a live source and record which of the three things you
measured.<Cite src="relayscan-io" />

## The cost, in the currency of the last lesson

Return to the feedback loop. A builder holding orderflow nobody else has can construct a more valuable
block, therefore bid higher, therefore win more slots, therefore attract more exclusive flow.

Every private route is an input to that loop. BuilderNet's launch analysis names the mechanism
directly: concentration accelerated as major orderflow providers made **exclusive orderflow deals**
with block builders to internalise their MEV — opaque off-chain agreements that entrenched a duopoly
and created an unequal playing field for apps and users without access to the same preferential
treatment.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" /> Independent analysis of builder bidding reaches the same
structural conclusion about private flow's role in the market.<Cite src="arxiv-private-orderflow" />

Ethereum's own documentation anticipated the endpoint of this trend, and its language is stronger than
most commentary. In response to sandwiching and front-running, traders may conduct off-chain deals
with validators for transaction privacy; "dark pools" are the larger version, functioning as
permissioned, access-only mempools open to users willing to pay certain
fees.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> The documentation's assessment: this trend would diminish
Ethereum's permissionlessness and trustlessness, potentially transforming the chain into a
**pay-to-play mechanism that favours the highest bidder**, and would accelerate the centralisation
risks described elsewhere on the page.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

That is the honest framing of the trade. An individual user who routes privately is strictly better
off against the attacker they were worried about. The aggregate of all such users is a market
structure in which the ordering right is held by fewer parties, none of whom the protocol
knows.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" /> Both statements are true, they are not in tension, and a defence
of private routing that mentions only the first is incomplete rather than wrong.

<Aside kind="warn">
Two operational costs that do not appear in the pitch. **Liveness**: a private endpoint may simply not
include your transaction, and you will not see it pending anywhere, because it is not anywhere.
**Cancellation**: [replacing a transaction](/c/tx-cancel-is-a-replacement) relies on competing for the
same nonce in a pool, and there is no pool. Providers offer their own cancellation
interfaces,<Cite src="flashbots-protect-docs" /> which means the escape hatch is now also a
service you depend on rather than a property of the network.
</Aside>

## Orderflow auctions: selling the backrun instead of suppressing it

Private routing is a suppression mechanism. The second response in this lesson is a redistribution
one, and it starts from a question the first never asks: **your transaction creates value — who should
get it?**

The design is MEV-Share and its relatives. Take a user's transaction, reveal a controlled amount of
information about it to searchers, auction the right to be placed **immediately after** it, and return
part of the winning bid to the user.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-share-repo" /><Cite src="flashbots-mev-share-announcement" />

The word *after* is doing all the work. Only backrunning is sold. A searcher who buys the position
cannot be placed in front of the user, so the sandwich is impossible by construction rather than by
policy — there is no front leg available at any price.

### Worked derivation: what the rebate actually is

**Subgoal 1 — establish that a swap creates a backrun opportunity.** A swap moves the pool along its
curve,<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> so immediately afterwards the pool disagrees with the wider
market by exactly the amount the swap moved it. That disagreement is an arbitrage, and lesson two gave
its exact value: `P*x + y - 2*sqrt(k*P)`, evaluated at the post-swap reserves and the unchanged
external price `P`.

**Subgoal 2 — put a number on it, using the swap from lesson three.** The pool held 1,000 ETH and
3,000,000 stable; the user spent 100,000 stable and received 32.2581 ETH. Post-swap reserves are
967.7419 and 3,100,000, so the pool now quotes `3,100,000 / 967.7419 = 3,203.3` against a market still
at 3,000. Applying the formula with `P = 3,000` and `k = 3e9`:

```text
backrun value = 3000 * 967.7419 + 3,100,000 - 2*sqrt(3e9 * 3000)
              = 2,903,225.8 + 3,100,000 - 6,000,000
              = 3,225.8 stable
```

**Subgoal 3 — notice whose money that is.** The user paid that dislocation. It is their own price
impact, sitting in the pool, waiting for the first person who arrives. In the public-mempool world it
goes to whoever wins the race; under a sandwich it is taken *and* the front leg is taken as well.

**Subgoal 4 — see what the auction changes.** Selling the backrun position converts a race into a bid.
By the common-value argument from lesson one, the bid climbs toward the full 3,225.8 less execution
cost — and then a share `ρ` of that bid is returned to the
user.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-share-announcement" /> The user's expected recovery is `ρ` times a
quantity they generated themselves.

**Subgoal 5 — find what `ρ` and the revealed value depend on.** Searchers cannot bid for what they
cannot evaluate. MEV-Share's answer is **programmable privacy**: the user chooses how much information
is shared, in units the documentation calls **hints**, and states the trade-off flatly — sharing more
hints can increase refunds, while sharing fewer hints can increase
privacy.<Cite src="flashbots-protect-mev-share-docs" /><Cite src="flashbots-mev-share-repo" />

So the user's problem is a maximisation, not a switch:

```text
maximise   ρ * V(H)  -  risk(H)
```

where `H` is the hint set, `V(H)` the value searchers can compute from it, and `risk(H)` the residual
exposure from having revealed it. Both terms increase in `H`. A full reveal is the public mempool with
a rebate; a null reveal is full privacy with no rebate.<Cite src="flashbots-protect-mev-share-docs" />

Read the two `no rebate` rows and the derivation is confirmed by the product: **maximum privacy and a
rebate are mutually exclusive**, because the rebate is paid out of value that only exists once
somebody can see enough to bid on it.<Cite src="mev-blocker-site" /> That is not a limitation of a
particular implementation. It is subgoal 5.

## Where each response actually sits

Put the three routing options side by side on the two questions from the previous lesson.

**Public mempool.** Seen by: everyone. Reordered by: the winning builder, with every searcher able to
bid for a position relative to you.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> You receive nothing, and
generalised frontrunners may copy your transaction outright if it is profitable to
copy.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

**Private endpoint.** Seen by: the endpoint operator and the builders it forwards
to.<Cite src="flashbots-protect-docs" /> Reordered by: those builders. You receive nothing, unless the
endpoint also runs an auction. Public-mempool attackers are removed
entirely.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

**Orderflow auction.** Seen by: the operator, the builders, and any searcher subscribed to whatever
hints you chose to publish.<Cite src="flashbots-protect-mev-share-docs" /> Reordered by: those
builders, but with front-running structurally excluded.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-share-repo" /> You
receive a share of the backrun bid.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-share-announcement" />

The pattern across the three rows is the point of the lesson. Moving right does not reduce the amount
of value the transaction generates — that quantity is a property of the trade, computed in subgoal 2.
What changes is **who is allowed to see it, which positions relative to you are for sale, and whether
any of the proceeds return to you.**

<Check type="explain" concept="mev-private-orderflow">
A wallet team is choosing a default route for user swaps. Their proposal: send everything through a
single protected endpoint with maximum privacy settings, because "any information leak is a risk, and
rebates are small enough to ignore".

Evaluate the reasoning. Say what they have optimised for, what they have given up, and what you would
measure before agreeing.

<Answer>
**They have optimised for a threat they can name against costs they have not measured.**

**What maximum privacy buys.** Complete removal of public-mempool observers, and — on the endpoints
that offer it — no charge for reverting transactions.<Cite src="mev-blocker-site" /> Both are real. For
a wallet whose users mostly make small swaps that were never worth sandwiching, this may be close to
the whole of the available benefit.

**What it gives up, in order of size.**

*The rebate, by construction rather than by accident.* The endpoint tables make it explicit: the
maximum-privacy settings return **no rebate**, because there is nothing for a searcher to bid
on.<Cite src="mev-blocker-site" /> By subgoal 2 the forgone quantity is the backrun value the user's
own price impact created — 3,225.8 stable on the worked example, roughly 3.2% of a 100,000 trade. "Small enough to ignore" is a claim about a number, and it is a number the team can compute
from their own median trade size and the depth they route against. If their users trade small against
deep pools it is genuinely negligible; if they trade large against thin pools it dwarfs the fee the
wallet charges.

*Optionality per transaction.* A single global default applies the same trade-off to a 50-dollar swap
and a 500,000-dollar one, and the correct answer differs. The `H` in subgoal 5 should be a function of
the trade, not a constant.

*Fallback.* One endpoint is one point of failure for inclusion, and there is no pending pool to
inspect when it silently drops something.<Cite src="flashbots-protect-docs" /> Cancellation becomes an
operator-provided interface rather than a network property.<Cite src="flashbots-protect-docs" />

**What they have not accounted for at all.** Routing all of a wallet's flow to one destination is an
exclusive orderflow arrangement, whatever it is called internally, and exclusive orderflow is the
input to the concentration loop.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" /> A wallet with meaningful volume is not
a neutral participant in that market; it is a supplier to it. That belongs in the decision even if it
does not change it.

**What to measure before agreeing**, in order of how quickly it settles the argument:

*Distribution of realised price impact across their actual swaps.* This gives the backrun value being
forgone, per trade, from the formula in subgoal 1 — not an estimate, an evaluation.

*Observed sandwich rate on comparable public-mempool flow of the same sizes.* This gives the benefit
side. If it is near zero for their size distribution, the maximum-privacy default is protecting
against something that was not happening.

*Inclusion latency and drop rate through the candidate endpoint versus the public path.* Liveness is
the cost users will actually complain about.

*Whether a per-trade policy is feasible in their stack.* If it is, the global default is a placeholder
rather than a decision.

**The recommendation this analysis usually produces** is not one endpoint but a policy: privacy-maximal
for small transactions where the rebate is genuinely negligible and the revert protection is the real
benefit, hint-sharing for large ones where the forgone rebate is material, and more than one
destination so that inclusion does not depend on a single operator. Which is more work than changing
an RPC URL, and is the actual answer.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** is the structural version of everything here. Private routing hides an executable
transaction from some observers; intents do not publish an executable transaction at all, so there is
no path to be placed around and the derivation in lesson three has nothing to optimise
against.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-share-repo" /> The cost moves to a different place entirely — the
competitiveness of whoever constructs the transaction on your behalf.

**Lesson eight** attacks the same information problem cryptographically rather than contractually, and
inherits the question this lesson raises: when a trusted recipient cannot be removed, what can it be
replaced with, and what does the replacement cost?

**Lesson nine** applies the auction idea from the second half of this lesson to a case where the value
is created by a protocol rather than by a user — and where, unusually, the recipient can be nominated
in advance.

**In your product**, the artefact is the three-row comparison. For your default route, write down who
sees the transaction, which positions relative to it are purchasable, and what comes back. If any of
the three cells is "we do not know", that is the finding, and it is more common than not.
