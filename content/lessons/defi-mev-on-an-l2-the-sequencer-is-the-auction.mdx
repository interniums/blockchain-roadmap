Take the six-hop pipeline from lesson five — wallet, mempool, searcher, builder, relay, proposer — and
run a transaction through a rollup instead.

No public mempool to broadcast into. No builder market bidding for the slot. No relay holding a body,
no proposer signing a header sight unseen. Almost every row collapses into one: **the sequencer
receives the transaction, chooses where it goes, and publishes the
result.**<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" />

From a distance that looks like the problem being solved, and early-2026 measurement supports the
headline. This lesson is about why the correct reading is close to the opposite.

## What the measurement actually found

The relevant study is *How to Serve Your Sandwich? MEV Attacks in Private L2 Mempools*, by Gogol,
Schneider, Gorzny and Tessone, submitted January 2026.<Cite src="arxiv-sandwiching-l1-vs-rollups" />
Read its three contributions separately: two are more interesting than the conclusion everyone quotes.

**First, it extends a formal model of optimal front- and back-run sizing**, relating attack
profitability to victim trade volume, liquidity depth, and slippage
bounds.<Cite src="arxiv-sandwiching-l1-vs-rollups" /> Those are precisely the three inputs the
derivation in lesson three produced — the victim's size, the pool's depth, and `amountOutMin`. The
model is the same object, generalised.

**Second, it adds an execution-feasibility model** quantifying **co-inclusion constraints** under
private mempools.<Cite src="arxiv-sandwiching-l1-vs-rollups" /> This is the part that explains the
result. Without guaranteed atomic inclusion, an attacker must rely on sequencer ordering, redundant
submissions and priority-fee placement — which renders sandwiching **probabilistic rather than
deterministic**.<Cite src="arxiv-sandwiching-l1-vs-rollups" />

Recall from lesson three that the sandwich is not atomic: if the front leg lands and the victim's does
not, the attacker holds inventory bought at a price they moved.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> On
layer one the bundle removes that exposure. Without a bundle mechanism it returns, and it multiplies
across attempts.

**Third, it measures.** From transaction-level data across major rollups it finds that **naive
heuristics overstate sandwich activity**: most flagged patterns are false positives and the median net
return is negative.<Cite src="arxiv-sandwiching-l1-vs-rollups" /> The conclusion: sandwiching, while
endemic and profitable on layer one, is rare, unprofitable and largely absent on rollups with private
mempools.<Cite src="arxiv-sandwiching-l1-vs-rollups" />

<Aside kind="note">
The false-positive finding deserves attention if you ever measure this yourself. The obvious heuristic
— a buy and a sell of the same asset from one address bracketing a third party's trade — flags
patterns that are not attacks, at a rate that dominates the rollup
sample.<Cite src="arxiv-sandwiching-l1-vs-rollups" /> Any MEV number produced by pattern matching
without a profitability check is measuring the pattern, not the attack.
</Aside>

## Where the ordering right went

On layer one the right to order is contested every slot and the contest has a price a proposer
collects.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> Builder concentration is the module's headline complaint,
and it is a complaint about a market with few participants.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" />

On a rollup with one sequencer the right belongs to one party, permanently, without a market. Whatever
value an ordering can yield accrues to the operator by default, and whether any of it is auctioned,
rebated or kept is a policy the operator chooses and can change. Arbitrum's documentation describes
the sequencer as responsible for ordering and processing transactions, and frames decentralised
sequencing — a committee determining ordering by supermajority — as a *future* direction rather than
the current state.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" />

So the honest comparison is not "less MEV" versus "more MEV" but: on layer one, discretion held by a
small oligopoly, contested each slot, priced on public dashboards;<Cite src="buildernet-intro" /> on a
single-sequencer rollup, discretion held by one party, uncontested, under a policy that need not be
published and can change without a fork.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" /> Both are concentrations. The
second is stricter, and it is the one with no auction to observe.

## When the policy is published: an ordering auction with parameters

Some rollups have written the policy down, which turns an argument into a specification you can read.

Arbitrum's Timeboost is the clearest example. Control of an **express lane** in each round is sold by a
**sealed-bid, second-price auction**, with a default round duration of 60
seconds.<Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost-docs" /> Bidding closes 15 seconds before the round begins, so
by default participants have 45 seconds to bid; an autonomous auctioneer then verifies the bids,
determines the winner and resolves the auction on chain.<Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost-docs" /> A
minimum reserve price applies — 0.001 WETH by default, set by the chain owner, who must fix it at
least 30 seconds before the round so bidders know it in
advance.<Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost-docs" />

And the mechanism that makes the lane worth anything: transactions **not** in the express lane are
subject to a default **200-millisecond artificial delay** in their arrival timestamp before being
sequenced, which means some may be pushed to the next block — against a default Arbitrum block time of
250 milliseconds, adjustable to 100.<Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost-docs" />

The documentation is also explicit about what the lane is worth: the sum of the MEV the express-lane
controller predicts they can extract during the upcoming round, plus the MEV they extract while in
control that they did not predict.<Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost-docs" />

### Worked derivation: reading the parameters

**Subgoal 1 — convert the delay into a position.** A 200 ms penalty against a 250 ms block is 80% of a
block time. Two transactions arriving at the same instant, one in the lane and one not, are stamped
200 ms apart — so the express-lane transaction is sequenced first within the block, and if the
non-express one crosses the block boundary it lands a whole block later. At the 100 ms block-time
setting the delay exceeds two block times, so the effect is stronger, not
weaker.<Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost-docs" /> **The lane does not buy a small advantage; it buys
first placement, every time, for a minute.**

**Subgoal 2 — price it.** The lane's value is the MEV extractable in the round, by the documentation's
own definition.<Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost-docs" /> That is the same quantity every earlier lesson
has been about, now sold explicitly, with a receipt.

**Subgoal 3 — apply the auction format.** Sealed-bid second-price makes truthful bidding dominant, and
the winner pays the **second-highest** bid. So the chain captures `V₂` while the winner keeps
`V₁ - V₂` — the identical shape to lesson five's first-price result, reached by a different route.

**Subgoal 4 — read the consequence.** Concentration among searchers cuts the chain's revenue, for the
same reason concentration among builders cuts the proposer's: a single dominant bidder pays the
reserve price and keeps everything above it. Which is why the reserve price is a chain-owner parameter
updatable until 30 seconds before a round<Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost-docs" /> — it is the floor
under `V₂` when `V₂` is thin.

**Subgoal 5 — say what changed for a user.** Their transaction can now be deterministically preceded
by somebody who paid for the right. The mechanism is disclosed, the proceeds go to the chain rather
than an external builder, and the advantage is bounded and published. None of that removes the
extraction; it makes it a documented product with a price.

## What to actually check about a rollup

Five questions, and none of them is "does it have MEV".

**What is the ordering policy, in writing?** Arrival-timestamp order, discretionary ordering in a
private mempool, an explicit auction, encrypted ordering. If first-party documentation does not say,
that is the answer.

**Who can change it, and how fast?** Operator configuration can change between one block and the next.
The party that can alter contracts and the party that orders transactions are distinct roles that fail
differently — the same separation the rollup-stages framework already makes you ask
about.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />

**Where do the proceeds go?** To the chain's treasury, to a rebate for users, to the operator's
balance sheet. Timeboost publishes this structure; many chains do not.<Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost-docs" />

**What is the escape path, and how slow is it?** [Forced inclusion](/c/forced-inclusion) via layer one
is the guarantee that the sequencer cannot exclude you permanently. It is a censorship remedy on the
order of hours, not an ordering remedy on the order of
milliseconds.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" /><Cite src="l2beat-stages" />

**What breaks if the sequencer stops?** A liveness question, separate from the ordering question — but
the same party holds both.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" />

<Check type="explain" concept="mev-l2-sequencer">
A team is moving their exchange from layer one to a rollup and writes in the migration doc: "Sandwich
risk drops to near zero — recent academic measurement confirms sandwiching is rare and unprofitable on
rollups with private mempools. We can therefore relax our default slippage tolerance from 0.5% to 3%
to reduce failed swaps."

The first sentence is accurate. Explain why the second does not follow.

<Answer>
**The citation is used correctly and then applied to the wrong quantity.**

**What the study supports.** Sandwiching is rare, unprofitable and largely absent on rollups with
private mempools, because without guaranteed atomic inclusion attackers depend on sequencer ordering
and redundant submissions, making the attack probabilistic rather than
deterministic.<Cite src="arxiv-sandwiching-l1-vs-rollups" /> A well-supported claim about one attack
in one environment.

**Why the conclusion does not follow — three independent reasons.**

*Slippage tolerance does not only bound sandwiches.* It bounds every source of adverse execution:
price impact from your own size, genuine movement between quote and execution, and any other
transaction landing in between. Widening it to 3% authorises a worse fill from all of those,
regardless of who is attacking. Their failed swaps are telling them about depth, not adversaries.

*The adversary was not removed, only the anonymous one.* The sequencer sees everything and chooses
every ordering.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" /> Where an express lane exists, somebody may have
bought deterministic first placement for the round — 200 ms against a 250 ms block is first placement,
not an edge.<Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost-docs" /> The attack's difficulty on a rollup comes from the
attacker's inability to guarantee co-inclusion;<Cite src="arxiv-sandwiching-l1-vs-rollups" /> a party
that *can* guarantee it does not face that constraint.

*The finding is a measurement, not a property.* It describes rollups with private mempools in one
window.<Cite src="arxiv-sandwiching-l1-vs-rollups" /> Ordering policy is operator configuration on
most chains and can change without a fork, so a design that depends on the current policy has taken on
a dependency it cannot monitor.

**What the team should do instead.** Fix the actual complaint — failed swaps are a depth-and-sizing
problem, and the bound is computable per trade from reserves they already read to quote. Write the
chain's ordering policy into the migration doc alongside who can change it and where the proceeds go;
that is the section the doc is missing. And keep the tolerance tight: on a chain with sub-second
blocks and cheap gas a revert costs far less than on layer one, which makes a tight bound cheaper to
hold, not more expensive.

**The general error worth naming.** "This attack is rare here" was converted into "our bound against
this class of loss can be relaxed". Bounds should be derived from the loss they cap, not from the
current prevalence of one way of causing it.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The module closes here**, and the through-line is one sentence: ordering is a discretionary resource,
so it has a price, and every design in these ten lessons is an argument about **who holds the
discretion and who receives the price**.

Line up the answers. A builder oligopoly holds it on layer one and sells it to
proposers.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" /> A private endpoint hands it to one builder. An orderflow
auction sells one slice and refunds part to the user. A batch auction makes intra-batch position worth
exactly zero.<Cite src="cow-protocol-batch-auction-docs" /> An OEV auction nominates the recipient in
advance. A sequencer holds all of it and may sell an express lane by the
minute.<Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost-docs" /> Encrypted mempools try to remove the information the
targeted forms need, at the cost of a new trusted component.

**The reflection at the end of this module** asks you to draw the path your last on-chain swap took and
mark, at each hop, who could see it and who could reorder it. Do it with a real transaction. Most
engineers cannot fill in the first row — which RPC, which routing policy, which builders — and that
gap is the most actionable finding the module produces.

**The practice exercises** make two derivations executable: the sandwich optimum, where the profit
curve collapses as the tolerance tightens, and the zero-minimum-output case, where the formula
predicts the attacker takes the entire input and you get to confirm it.
