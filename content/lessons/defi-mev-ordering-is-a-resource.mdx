Two pools hold the same pair. One quotes 3,000 units of the stable per unit of the volatile asset;
the other, because somebody just sold into it, quotes 2,985. Nobody has been attacked. No contract is
broken. Both pools are doing what [the constant-product invariant](/c/constant-product) says they
should — reporting the ratio of their own reserves.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" />

The gap is worth money to whoever executes two swaps in a fixed order: buy cheap, sell dear. And the
party who decides that order is not the person who spotted the gap. It is whoever assembles the block.

That sentence is the whole module. This lesson makes it precise enough to argue with.

## The definition, and the three clauses that carry it

Ethereum's own documentation states it plainly: maximal extractable value is the maximum value that
can be extracted from block production **in excess of the standard block reward and gas fees**, by
including, excluding, and changing the order of transactions in a
block.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

**"In excess of the standard block reward and gas fees."**<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> MEV is
defined as a residual — what remains after the protocol's own payment schedule has been honoured. This
is why "MEV is just a fee" and "MEV is theft" are both category errors: the quantity is defined as the
part the fee schedule does not price.

**"Including, excluding, and changing the order."**<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> Three distinct
powers that fail differently. Exclusion is censorship. Inclusion at a chosen position is the sandwich
and the backrun. Reordering pending transactions is the classical front-run. A design that removes one
leaves the other two standing, which is why every "we fixed MEV" claim deserves the follow-up question
*which of the three*.

**"From block production."**<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> Not from a bug. The term was originally
*miner* extractable value, because under proof of work miners held inclusion, exclusion and ordering;
after the Merge validators hold those roles and mining left the protocol, so the word became
*maximal*.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> The extraction methods survived the change of consensus
mechanism intact — the strongest available evidence that they are not artefacts of a particular one.

### The general statement

Strip Ethereum out and the condition is short. You have MEV wherever both hold:

1. Some party has **discretion** over the sequence of state transitions.
2. The **outcome depends on the sequence** — A then B does not give the same state as B then A.

Neither is exotic. Condition two holds for every AMM, lending market, auction and
first-come-first-served mint. Condition one holds for every system with a block producer, a sequencer,
a matching engine or a queue manager — a centralised exchange has the analogue, and so does any
database with one writer and clients who care who commits first.

So **removing MEV means removing one of the two conditions, not fixing a defect**. Remove discretion
and you get committed ordering rules, encrypted mempools, first-come-first-served policies. Remove
ordering-dependence and you get batch auctions with a single clearing price. Every response in this
module is one of those two moves, or an argument about who receives the residual.

## Where the measurement came from

*Flash Boys 2.0* documented and quantified the deployment of arbitrage bots on decentralised
exchanges, showing those bots paying high transaction fees and optimising network latency to
anticipate and exploit ordinary users' trades.<Cite src="arxiv-flash-boys-2" /> It formalised the
mechanism they competed through — **priority gas auctions**, in which bots competitively bid up
transaction fees to obtain early block position — as a continuous-time, partial-information
game.<Cite src="arxiv-flash-boys-2" /> It coined *miner extractable value* for the general
phenomenon.<Cite src="arxiv-flash-boys-2" />

The part usually dropped from summaries is the conclusion: high fees paid for priority ordering pose a
systemic risk to **consensus-layer** security, not merely to users.<Cite src="arxiv-flash-boys-2" />

That is the point worth carrying. If the value inside a block substantially exceeds the reward for
producing it, a proposer has an incentive to produce that block *again* — to [reorg](/c/reorg) rather
than build forward. Ethereum's documentation names the same concern: where MEV in a block
significantly exceeds the standard block reward, validators may be incentivised to reorg blocks and
capture it, causing re-organisation and consensus instability.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

<Aside kind="note">
Notice what kind of argument that is. Not "extraction is unfair to users" but "extraction above a
threshold changes the fork-choice incentives of the parties whose honest behaviour the chain's safety
rests on".<Cite src="arxiv-flash-boys-2" /> Different claims, different remedies — and the second is
why MEV became a protocol-research topic rather than an application-layer annoyance. Keep them
separate when you argue about a design.
</Aside>

## Searchers: what they do, and what they actually earn

Ethereum's documentation describes the role directly. In theory MEV accrues entirely to validators,
because they are the only party who can guarantee execution of a profitable opportunity; in practice a
large portion is found by independent participants called **searchers**, who run algorithms over chain
data to detect opportunities and submit transactions automatically.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

The loop is short. Observe state — pending transactions, pool reserves, oracle updates, liquidatable
positions. Simulate a candidate **bundle**: an ordered list of transactions executed together or not
at all.<Cite src="flashbots-auction-docs" /> If it shows profit, submit it with a bid for a specific
position.<Cite src="flashbots-auction-docs" /> If it does not land, lose the simulation cost and
nothing else.

Atomicity is what makes the economics unusual. Under the bundle model a searcher submits to a builder
rather than broadcasting, and the bundle executes in the given order or not at
all.<Cite src="flashbots-auction-docs" /> That converts an on-chain gas war into an off-chain sealed
bid — which is why ethereum.org describes the Flashbots auction as having taken gas-price auctions
off-chain and lowered gas prices for ordinary users.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

### Worked derivation: where the money ends up

The claim to establish sounds wrong the first time: **the searcher who finds the opportunity is
usually not the party who keeps the value.** Work it as an auction, because that is what it is.

**Subgoal 1 — write down what is being sold.** Not the opportunity: the *position*, meaning the right
to have this bundle execute at this index in this block. The seller is whoever controls ordering,
today a builder.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> The good is rivalrous — exactly one bundle occupies
the slot after a given transaction — and non-storable, because the opportunity dies when the block is
sealed.

**Subgoal 2 — establish each bidder's valuation.** Call the gross profit from executing the bundle
*V*. For a public, mechanical opportunity such as the two-pool gap above, *V* is identical for
everyone who can read the chain, because every bidder simulates against the same state. This is a
**common-value** auction, not a private-value one, and that distinction drives the whole result.

**Subgoal 3 — write down the payoff.** Bidding *b* and winning nets `V - b - g`, where *g* is
execution cost: gas plus infrastructure. Losing nets zero. So a bidder is willing to bid anything up
to `V - g`.

**Subgoal 4 — find the equilibrium.** With `n` bidders who all know *V* and all cap at `V - g`, any
bid strictly below `V - g` is beaten by a bid a hair above it, and the loser gets zero. Bidding climbs
until it stops just short of the point where winning is worse than losing. As `n` grows, the winning
bid tends to `V - g` and the winner's surplus tends to zero. In the limit, **the entire gross
opportunity transfers to the seller of the position, less the real cost of executing it.**

**Subgoal 5 — check against a source instead of trusting the algebra.** Ethereum's documentation
reaches the same place from measurement: assuming searchers are economically rational, the fee a
searcher is willing to pay is an amount up to 100% of their MEV, and for highly competitive
opportunities such as DEX arbitrage searchers **may have to pay 90% or even more** of total MEV
revenue in fees.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> Ninety-plus percent is what "surplus tends to zero"
looks like when it is observed rather than derived.

**Subgoal 6 — read off the consequences.** Two matter for everything that follows.

*Execution cost became a competitive weapon.* Because the bid is capped at `V - g`, a searcher who
spends less gas bids more at the same total outlay. Ethereum's documentation calls this **gas
golfing** and names the techniques: addresses with long runs of leading zero bytes, which are cheaper
to include, and dust ERC-20 balances left in contracts so a storage slot is updated rather than
initialised.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> You know from gas metering why the second works; here it
is a bidding advantage rather than a micro-optimisation.

*The persistent surplus does not sit with the searcher.*<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> It sits with
whoever sells the position. That is the sentence determining who this module is actually about, and
why the next four lessons study extraction while the five after them study the supply chain.

### Where the derivation stops applying

The common-value assumption in subgoal 2 is exactly where searcher profit lives. A searcher is a
price-taker on blockspace and a price-maker only on opportunities nobody else has found. Ethereum's
documentation makes the point from the other end: DEX arbitrage, liquidations and sandwich trading are
the well-known opportunities and are unlikely to be profitable for new searchers, so returns sit in
the **long tail**.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

There is also a shortcut that removes the need to find anything. **Generalised frontrunners** watch
the mempool for profitable transactions, copy the payload, substitute their own address, simulate
locally, and if it still profits, submit the modified version with a higher fee — taking the original
searcher's find without understanding it.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> That matters beyond taxonomy:
broadcasting into [the public mempool](/c/mempool-is-a-public-broadcast) is structurally unusable for
anyone whose transaction is profitable to copy, which is the original reason private submission
channels exist.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

## The pipeline you already know, seen from the value side

You met [the four roles](/c/searcher-builder-relay-proposer) in the consensus track, and you know that
roughly nine in ten Ethereum blocks reach the chain through
[MEV-Boost](/c/mev-boost).<Cite src="mev-boost-docs" /> This module re-walks that pipeline with a
different question at each hop: not *what does this component do* but **who can see the transaction
here, and who can reorder it here**.

Two facts from it are needed now; both get a lesson later.

**The payment is usually not a priority fee.** When a bundle is worth something to a builder, the
value typically moves as a direct transfer to the block's fee recipient inside the
bundle,<Cite src="flashbots-auction-docs" /> not as
[a priority fee](/c/priority-fee-formula). So a transaction's own fee fields understate what was paid
for its position, sometimes by orders of magnitude.

**The proposer sells the block sight unseen.** Under the deployed design the proposer signs a header
and a bid without seeing the contents.<Cite src="mev-boost-docs" /> The consensus specification knows
nothing about any of it, and EIP-7732 — the attempt to bring proposer-builder separation in-protocol —
sat at Review status and had not shipped as of August 2026.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

<Check type="explain" concept="mev-definition">
A team ships a protocol with a one-off reward: the first address to call `claim()` after a specific
on-chain event receives a fixed bonus. There is no auction, no price, no oracle, no AMM. In review,
somebody says "there is no MEV here, because there is no trading".

Say whether that is right, and if not, name precisely what an attacker is paid for and what the team's
options actually are.

<Answer>
**Wrong, and the reason is that neither of the two conditions mentions trading.**

**Condition two holds.** The outcome depends on sequence: whoever executes first takes the bonus and
everyone after gets nothing. That is ordering-dependence in its purest form — purer than a swap, since
the payoff is a step function rather than a curve.

**Condition one holds.** A builder chooses which of the competing `claim()` calls goes first. Nothing
in the contract changes that.

**What the attacker is paid for.** Not for finding anything — the event is public and the bonus is
fixed. They are paid for winning a position. By the derivation above this is a common-value auction
with a known prize, so the bid climbs toward the bonus less execution cost and the bonus ends up with
the builder rather than with any claimant.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> The protocol pays out in
full and buys nothing, because the "reward for being first" was competed away into a payment to a
party the protocol was not trying to reward.

**The team's options**, in the taxonomy above:

*Remove the ordering-dependence.* Make the payoff independent of position — accumulate callers in a
window and split. This eliminates the category rather than reallocating it, and it is the same move
batch auctions make.

*Remove the discretion.* Commit the winner before ordering is chosen. Harder than it sounds, because
the builder chooses which reveals land.

*Decide who should get it.* If the race is the point — as it is for liquidations, later in this module
— accept that the prize will be competed away and ask whether it should be competed away *to the
protocol*. That is an auction the protocol runs rather than one it loses.

**The wrong option** is `require(msg.sender == tx.origin)`. It touches neither condition, and after
EIP-7702 the "is this an EOA" heuristics do not do what they claim.

**What to say in review**: "there is no trading here" is not a defence. Ordering-dependence is the
trigger, not asset exchange. Ask of any function whether two parties would ever want to call it in a
particular order relative to each other, or to something else on chain.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** takes the two-pool gap this one opened on and asks what the taxonomy demands:
since arbitrage is the thing keeping venue prices consistent, and since nobody's swap got worse when
it happened, *who paid for it?* The answer is a specific, measurable transfer with a name, and it is
not the person swapping.

**The sandwich and the liquidation**, lessons three and four, are the cases that break any single moral
framing. One needs a named victim to be worse off and cannot exist without one; the other is a fee a
protocol deliberately pays for its own solvency. Both are MEV under the same
definition.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> That is the point.

**The supply-chain half of the module** — builders, relays, private orderflow, orderflow auctions,
intents, encrypted mempools — is a sequence of answers to *who should hold the ordering right and who
should receive its value*. Each is subgoal 4 re-derived under a different auction design, which is why
it is worth carrying the derivation rather than the conclusion.

**In your own protocol**, keep one design-review question: *which of my functions have payoffs that
depend on their position relative to other transactions, and who chooses that position?* Write the
answer next to your [trust-assumption inventory](/c/trust-assumption-inventory). Most protocols have
never been asked.
