Every mitigation so far has worked on the same object: a transaction that names a router, a path, a
pool and a minimum output, and that somebody can therefore optimise around. Lesson three derived that
optimisation exactly. Lesson six hid the object from some observers and handed it, in the clear, to
others.

This lesson removes the object.

CoW Protocol's documentation states the distinction in two sentences worth memorising: **an intent is
not an executable user transaction. It is a signed expression of trading
constraints.**<Cite src="cow-protocol-intents-docs" /> Rather than signing a raw transaction that
executes directly on chain, users sign an "intent to trade" message specifying parameters such as the
assets and amounts they would like to trade.<Cite src="cow-protocol-intents-docs" /> Somebody else
constructs the transaction, and the settlement contract verifies the user's signature and the
settlement conditions before execution.<Cite src="cow-protocol-intents-docs" />

Run lesson three's derivation against that and it has nothing to work on. There is no `amountOutMin`
sitting in a mempool waiting to be pushed to, because there is no transaction. There is no path to
insert trades along, because no venue has been named.

That is a genuine structural change, and it comes with a genuine structural cost. Both are below.

## The part that is not the mechanism

The first thing to get right is what intents do *not* rely on, because "private" is the usual and
wrong explanation.

UniswapX signed orders are **broadcast publicly**. The documentation says so directly: swappers sign a
message using Permit2 allowing token transfer as long as the sent and received amounts match the decay
curve, and these signed order messages are broadcast publicly and can be filled by anyone acting as a
filler.<Cite src="uniswapx-overview-docs" /> Anybody can watch them. Anybody can read the amounts.

And yet the sandwich derivation still does not apply, for a reason that has nothing to do with
secrecy: **the order specifies outputs, not a route.** There is no pool for an attacker to move ahead
of you in, because you did not commit to a pool. If a filler moves a pool's price, they still owe you
the amount you signed for — and if they cannot deliver it, they simply do not fill, at no cost to
you.<Cite src="uniswapx-overview-docs" />

  reality="Some do not publish, and some publish every order in full. The protection comes from the order not naming an executable path, so there is no position to buy relative to it. Secrecy is an implementation choice on top."
  why="Intents arrived in the same conversation as protected RPC endpoints and get filed under the same heading, and 'MEV-protected' is used for both."

## Two auction shapes, and what each one guarantees

Intent systems differ in one respect that determines everything: **how the filler is chosen.**

### Shape one: the decaying auction

UniswapX is permissionless, open source and auction-based, supporting competitive pricing through
auction competition across liquidity sources, gas-free swapping, protection against MEV, and no cost
for failed transactions.<Cite src="uniswapx-overview-docs" /> Swappers create orders defining their
auction parameters and price tolerance; in a Dutch auction the order starts at a maximum price and
decays down to a minimum over time.<Cite src="uniswapx-overview-docs" /> Fillers are specialised
participants who monitor signed orders and compete to settle them, providing their own liquidity or
routing through external sources.<Cite src="uniswapx-overview-docs" /> The realised price depends on
**when the first successful filler settles within the auction
timeline**.<Cite src="uniswapx-overview-docs" />

**Worked derivation: what the decay actually clears at.**

*Subgoal 1 — model the fillers.* Filler `i` can source the tokens at cost `c_i`. Order them so
`c_1 < c_2 < ... < c_n`: filler 1 is the most efficient.

*Subgoal 2 — model the clock.* The price the user receives starts favourable and worsens with time. At
each instant there is a current price `p(t)`; filler `i` profits by acting once `p(t)` has decayed past
`c_i`.

*Subgoal 3 — find the equilibrium.* Waiting is free but risky: if filler `i` waits past the moment
filler `j` finds the order profitable, `j` takes it and `i` gets nothing. So each filler acts at the
first moment it is profitable *for them*, and the order clears at approximately `c_1` — the cost of
the **most efficient** filler.

*Subgoal 4 — read the comparison.* This is strictly better for the user than the sealed-bid structure
from lesson five, where the winner shades their bid toward the runner-up's value and keeps the
difference. A descending clock clears at the winner's own reservation, so the user captures the
surplus down to the efficiency frontier rather than down to second place.

*Subgoal 5 — find the failure mode, which is the whole point.* With `n = 1`, there is no risk of being
beaten. The single filler waits for the clock to reach the **minimum** the user signed, and takes the
entire decay range. The user's realised price equals their own worst acceptable price, and nothing
observable has gone wrong: the order filled, within its constraints, exactly as specified.

**The user's protection is `n`.** Not cryptography, not the settlement contract, not the protocol. The
number of independent parties who could have beaten the winner to it.

### Shape two: the batch auction

CoW Protocol groups an intent alongside other intents in a batch auction; once the batch is closed for
orders, the protocol runs a competition in which solvers submit
solutions.<Cite src="cow-protocol-solvers-docs" /> The solver whose solution **generates the greatest
surplus for the batch** is selected to submit the winning
settlement.<Cite src="cow-protocol-solvers-docs" /> Solvers are independent, bonded participants, and
entry is open — the documentation states that anyone with some DeFi knowledge and the ability to code
an optimisation algorithm can create a solver.<Cite src="cow-protocol-solvers-docs" />

The auction is **combinatorial**: each solver may submit multiple bids, either on individual orders or
on groups of orders, and the protocol filters out "unfair" batched bids — those delivering less to an
order than some available non-batched bid — before selecting the combination that maximises total
surplus.<Cite src="cow-protocol-batch-auction-docs" />

Two properties are worth extracting.

**Uniform clearing prices.** All orders in a batch settle at a uniform clearing price per token pair.
The documentation draws the conclusion itself: solvers submit all orders in a batch at a uniform
clearing price, **meaning there is no advantage to re-ordering trades**, which it identifies as the
basis of all MEV exploitation.<Cite src="cow-protocol-intents-docs" /> The combinatorial formulation
states the same property as Uniform Directed Clearing Prices — a directed asset pair appearing
multiple times across orders in the same auction settles at a consistent price, which makes
transaction order irrelevant within the block.<Cite src="cow-protocol-batch-auction-docs" />

**A floor, and a definition of fairness.** Settlement is guaranteed at EBBO — Ethereum best bid and
offer — so the baseline price for a trade is what on-chain AMMs
offer;<Cite src="cow-protocol-intents-docs" /> and the combinatorial auction's fairness criterion is
that each order receives as much as it would have received had that order been auctioned off
alone.<Cite src="cow-protocol-batch-auction-docs" />

That second criterion deserves a moment, because it closes a hole the batching creates. Bundling
orders together produces gas savings and matching opportunities, and it also gives a solver the option
to subsidise one order at another's expense while still winning on total surplus. Filtering out bids
that deliver less to an order than an available non-batched bid removes exactly that
manoeuvre.<Cite src="cow-protocol-batch-auction-docs" /> The rule is a reminder that "maximise total
surplus" is not by itself a user protection — somebody has to be excluded from paying for it.

One structural detail matters for how you reason about custody here. Solvers do not receive token
approvals from users: transfers happen through the protocol's own contracts using approvals the user
granted to a vault relayer, and the settlement contract verifies each signed intent and enforces the
applicable limit price, quantity and settlement constraints.<Cite src="cow-protocol-solvers-docs" /> So
a malicious solver is a pricing risk, not a custody risk — which is a narrower blast radius than
"somebody else builds my transaction" first suggests.

### Worked derivation: why a uniform price is a structural removal

This is the one design in the module that eliminates a category rather than moving it, and the
argument is two lines.

*Subgoal 1 — state what a position is worth.* The value of being at index `i` rather than index `j` in
an execution is `output(i) - output(j)`. Every mechanism in this module has been a fight over that
difference.

*Subgoal 2 — impose the uniform price.* If every order on a directed pair in the batch settles at the
same price `p`,<Cite src="cow-protocol-batch-auction-docs" /> then each order's output is
`input × p` regardless of its index. So `output(i) = output(j)` for all `i, j`, and the value of a
position is **exactly zero** — not small, not competed away, zero.

*Subgoal 3 — say precisely what this does and does not cover.* Intra-batch ordering value is gone by
construction. The batch as a whole still lands in a block, still sits somewhere in an ordering chosen
by a builder, and can still be preceded or followed by other transactions. Uniform clearing removes
ordering value *within* the batch; it does not remove the block.

The delay is what makes batching possible, not what provides the protection."

## Coincidence of wants, priced

Batching enables something the uniform price alone does not: two intents on opposite sides of the same
pair matched against each other, touching no pool at all.

A coincidence of wants is where two parties each hold the asset the other needs and exchange
directly.<Cite src="cow-protocol-cow-docs" /> The documentation's own example: one trader selling
2,000 DAI for 0.5 ETH and another selling 0.5 ETH for 2,000 DAI in the same batch are matched together
so they swap without tapping on-chain liquidity, saving them from incurring liquidity-provider
fees.<Cite src="cow-protocol-cow-docs" /> CoWs allow users to bypass LP fees and reduce gas, since
orders interact only with the protocol's own contracts.<Cite src="cow-protocol-cow-docs" /> They can be
full or partial: in a partial CoW only part of a trade is filled with opposing liquidity and the solver
sources the rest.<Cite src="cow-protocol-cow-docs" />

**Put the module's running numbers on it**, because the size of this effect is routinely understated.

Take the swap from lesson three: 100,000 units of a stablecoin into a pool holding 1,000 ETH and
3,000,000 stable, with the market at 3,000 per ETH.

Now compare that 3,225.81 with the backrun value computed in the previous lesson for the very same
swap: **3,225.81**. They are the same number to every digit, and not by coincidence — in the zero-fee
model, the arbitrageur who restores the pool to the market price captures exactly what the trader
overpaid relative to it.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" />

So the identity to carry out of this module is:

```text
your price impact  =  somebody's backrun opportunity
```

The three responses in these two lessons are three different things to do with that quantity. A
private endpoint lets it go to the builder. An orderflow auction sells it and refunds you a
share.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-share-repo" /> A matched CoW **never creates it**, because the pool was
never touched.<Cite src="cow-protocol-cow-docs" />

<Aside kind="note">
The matched case is the best outcome available and it is also the rarest — it needs a counterparty on
the opposite side of the same pair in the same batch. Most batches settle partly through CoWs and
mostly through on-chain liquidity,<Cite src="cow-protocol-cow-docs" /> so treat the number above as
the ceiling of the effect rather than the expectation. The documentation lists the shapes it can take:
simple, batching, intermediate and ring CoWs, the last three of which recover value in cases where no
direct opposite exists.<Cite src="cow-protocol-cow-docs" />
</Aside>

## Where the MEV went

It did not evaporate. Trace it.

**Under an AMM route**, the user pays price impact, the pool holds a dislocation, and a searcher
captures it — bidding almost all of it to a builder.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

**Under an intent route**, a solver constructs the settlement. Whatever ordering value exists in that
settlement is available to the solver, and the auction is what forces them to hand it to the user
rather than keep it: the winner is whoever generates the greatest surplus for the
batch,<Cite src="cow-protocol-solvers-docs" /> or, in the decaying-auction shape, whoever can fill
soonest at the user's terms.<Cite src="uniswapx-overview-docs" />

So the extraction has been converted into **quote competition**. And quote competition has a property
that every other mechanism in this module lacks: **its failures are silent.**

A sandwich leaves three transactions in a block that anyone can find. A concentrated solver set leaves
a filled order at a slightly worse price, with no event, no anomaly, and no counterfactual to compare
against. The user sees "filled" either way.

  why="'MEV-protected' is the marketing phrase, and the protection against the visible attack is genuine — which makes the invisible substitute easy to miss."

## Evaluating an intent system, concretely

Since the protection is auction competitiveness, the evaluation is about the auction. Six questions,
in the order that eliminates candidates fastest.

**How many parties actually bid, not how many are eligible?** Eligibility is cheap to advertise.
Participation is the variable in subgoal 5, and it is the one that sets your price.

**How does entry work?** Open entry with a bond is a different market from an approved list. CoW's
documentation states open entry explicitly and describes solvers as
bonded;<Cite src="cow-protocol-solvers-docs" /> UniswapX describes filling as open to
anyone.<Cite src="uniswapx-overview-docs" /> Check whether the claim matches the observed set.

**Are auction outcomes publicly verifiable?** You need to be able to reconstruct, after the fact,
which solutions were offered and why the winner won. Without that, "the auction was competitive" is an
assertion.

**What is the floor, and how is it computed?** EBBO benchmarks against what on-chain AMMs
offer,<Cite src="cow-protocol-intents-docs" /> which is a meaningful floor precisely because it is
externally checkable. A floor defined by the system's own quote is not a floor.

**Who bears failure?** UniswapX charges nothing for failed transactions and lets fees be paid in the
sell token;<Cite src="uniswapx-overview-docs" /> CoW eliminates fees for failed
transactions.<Cite src="cow-protocol-intents-docs" /> This is a real and underrated benefit: it
removes the [estimation-and-revert](/c/estimategas-failure-modes) failure mode from the user's
experience entirely.

**Where does it lose?** Every mechanism has a regime where it is the wrong choice. Intent routes carry
a solver's margin and a settlement overhead, so on small trades against deep liquidity — where price
impact is negligible and there is nothing to recover — a direct route can win. The honest test is a
comparison at several sizes, not a single quote.

<Check type="explain" concept="mev-solver-competition">
A team is integrating an intent-based route as the default for their app. The vendor's material says:
"users are never exposed to MEV bots — solvers bear the execution-path and MEV risk, and all orders in
a batch settle at a uniform clearing price, so there is no advantage to re-ordering trades."

Every clause of that is accurate. Say what it nonetheless fails to establish, and design the check the
team should run before switching the default.

<Answer>
**The claims are true and they cover a different question than the one the team is asking.**

*What the quoted material establishes.* Two things, both real. Uniform clearing prices make position
within the batch worth exactly zero, by the argument in subgoal 2 above — that is structural, not
probabilistic.<Cite src="cow-protocol-batch-auction-docs" /> And the solver bears the execution-path
risk, so the user is not exposed to a failed or degraded route.<Cite src="cow-protocol-intents-docs" />
Neither claim is marketing.

*What it does not establish.* Both statements are about **what happens after a solution is chosen**.
Neither says anything about **how good the chosen solution is**. The winner is the solver generating
the greatest surplus among those who bid,<Cite src="cow-protocol-solvers-docs" /> and "greatest among
those who bid" is bounded by who bid. If three solvers bid and they are all routing through the same
liquidity, the batch clears at a uniform price that is uniformly mediocre — and every sentence in the
vendor's paragraph remains true.

*The gap in one line.* MEV was converted from an attack into a **price**, and the vendor's guarantees
are about the absence of the attack rather than about the level of the price.

**The check to run, in four parts.**

*Part 1 — a live quote ladder.* For your three or four most common trades, at three sizes each — small,
median, and the largest you actually see — record simultaneously: the intent route's quote, a direct
AMM route's expected output net of price impact, and an aggregator's quote. Simultaneity matters more
than sample size; a quote taken thirty seconds later is a different market. Run it repeatedly across
different hours, because solver participation is not constant.

*Part 2 — the regime boundary.* Plot realised output against trade size. The intent route should win
by more as size grows, because the recovered quantity is price impact, which grows superlinearly with
size.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> If it does not — if the advantage is flat or inverts — the
surplus is being kept somewhere between you and the settlement, and that is the finding.

*Part 3 — auction participation, from the auction's own record.* Count distinct winning solvers over a
window, and their concentration. One solver winning most batches is not proof of collusion and it is
proof that subgoal 5 applies: the discipline that produces your price is thinner than the eligibility
claim suggests.<Cite src="cow-protocol-solvers-docs" />

*Part 4 — the floor, tested rather than assumed.* Verify a sample of fills against the external
benchmark the system claims — for an EBBO-style guarantee, against what on-chain AMMs actually offered
at that block.<Cite src="cow-protocol-intents-docs" /> A floor you have never checked is a floor you
are trusting.

**What the result usually is.** A size threshold above which the intent route wins clearly, below
which the direct route wins on cost, and a middle band where it depends on the pair. The right default
is therefore a policy keyed on size and pair, not a single integration — and the same conclusion the
routing lesson reached, for the same underlying reason.

**What would change the answer.** More solvers, or fewer. That is the single variable, and it is worth
monitoring on a schedule rather than checking once at integration time, because unlike a contract
parameter it can change without any announcement.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** takes the one thing intents do not remove — that a builder still sees and orders
the settlement transaction — and attacks it at the information layer, with encryption rather than with
market design. It is the same trade-off in a different currency: instead of trusting a solver set to
be competitive, you trust a decryption committee to be live and honest.

**Lesson nine** applies the auction idea to value created by a protocol's own oracle write, where the
recipient can be nominated in advance rather than competed for.

**Lesson ten** asks what happens to all of this on a chain where a single sequencer decides ordering,
and finds that the batch-auction argument survives while most of the rest does not.

**In your own app**, the artefact is the identity in the middle of this lesson: *your price impact is
somebody's backrun opportunity.* Compute it for your median trade. That single number tells you how
much is on the table, which is the only way to judge whether any of these mechanisms is worth the
integration — and whether the quote you are being shown is competitive or merely acceptable.
