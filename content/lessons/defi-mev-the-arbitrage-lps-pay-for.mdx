Ethereum's documentation gives a worked example of the most common MEV category and calls it what it
is: a searcher turned 1,000 ETH into 1,045 ETH by taking advantage of different pricing of the ETH/DAI
pair on two exchanges, in a single atomic transaction — "true, riskless
arbitrage".<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

Forty-five ETH. Nobody's swap executed worse than quoted. No contract was exploited. Both pools
enforced [their invariant](/c/constant-product) exactly.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> The
transaction would have reverted if the profit had not been there, so the searcher risked only gas.

So the question the taxonomy forces is not whether this should happen. It is: **whose forty-five ETH
was that?** This lesson answers it with arithmetic rather than sentiment, and the answer has a name.

## Why the atomic version is a different animal

Atomic arbitrage is the case where both legs live on chain and execute inside one transaction: buy on
the cheap venue, sell on the expensive one, and if the closing balance check fails, revert.

Three properties follow, and each one matters later.

**No inventory risk.** Between the two legs, no time passes and no other transaction executes. The
searcher never holds a position exposed to price. Ethereum's documentation makes exactly this
distinction when it contrasts arbitrage with sandwiching: sandwiching is riskier because it is *not*
atomic, unlike DEX arbitrage.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

**No capital requirement in principle.** The [flash loan](/c/flash-loan) makes the trade
self-financing; atomicity is the collateral. So the barrier to entry is not balance sheet, and the
bundle either executes in the submitted order or not at all.<Cite src="flashbots-auction-docs" />

**Therefore the competition is entirely over inclusion.** With risk removed and capital removed, the
only remaining variables are being able to construct the bundle and being willing to bid. That is
subgoal 4 from the previous lesson with the private-value term set to zero: a common-value auction
where the surplus goes to whoever sells the position. Ethereum's own documentation puts the observed
number on it — for highly competitive opportunities such as DEX arbitrage, searchers may pay 90% or
more of total MEV revenue in fees.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> The competitive dynamic was first
formalised as the priority gas auction.<Cite src="arxiv-flash-boys-2" />

<Aside kind="warn">
The riskless framing is specific to the on-chain-to-on-chain case. Arbitrage between a pool and a
centralised venue has a leg that does not settle atomically, so it does carry inventory risk, and for
most large pairs the reference price that the pool is being arbitraged *toward* is set on those
venues. Everything below about who pays applies to both; only the "riskless" adjective is restricted
to the atomic case.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />
</Aside>

## Worked derivation: the exact size of the transfer

Take one pool and one external price and compute the arbitrageur's profit in closed form. Everything
here is derivable from the invariant you already have — no result is being asserted.

**Subgoal 1 — state the pool and the target.** Reserves `(x, y)` with `x * y = k`, so the pool's
marginal price is `p = y / x`.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> The external market price is `P`,
and `P > p`: the pool is selling token X too cheaply. Ignore fees for now — they are charged on the
input before the invariant check,<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> so they enter below as a
threshold, not as a change of direction.

**Subgoal 2 — find where the arbitrageur stops.** They buy X from the pool until buying one more unit
stops being profitable, which is exactly when the pool's marginal price reaches `P`. The pool accepts
any trade whose post-trade reserves still satisfy the invariant,<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" />
so the post-trade reserves `(x', y')` satisfy two equations at once:

```text
x' * y' = k          (the invariant still holds)
y' / x' = P          (the pool now agrees with the market)
```

Substituting the second into the first gives `x' * (P * x') = k`, so:

```text
x' = sqrt(k / P)
y' = sqrt(k * P)
```

**Subgoal 3 — price the round trip.** The arbitrageur receives `Δx = x - x'` units of X from the pool
and pays `Δy = y' - y` units of Y into it. They sell the X externally at `P`. Profit, denominated in
Y:

```text
profit = P * Δx - Δy
       = P * (x - sqrt(k/P)) - (sqrt(k*P) - y)
       = P*x - sqrt(k*P) - sqrt(k*P) + y
       = P*x + y - 2*sqrt(k*P)
```

using `P * sqrt(k/P) = sqrt(k*P)`.

**Subgoal 4 — sanity-check the formula before trusting it.** If the pool already agrees with the
market, `y = P*x` and `k = P*x²`, so `sqrt(k*P) = P*x` and the expression collapses to
`P*x + P*x - 2*P*x = 0`. No gap, no profit. The formula behaves.

**Subgoal 5 — read what the two terms actually are.** This is the step that turns algebra into a
claim. `P*x + y` is the mark-to-market value of the pool's reserves *before* the trade, valued at the
new external price. `2*sqrt(k*P)` is the value of the reserves *after* it, at the same price — because
`P * x' + y' = P*sqrt(k/P) + sqrt(k*P) = 2*sqrt(k*P)`.

So the identity is:

```text
arbitrageur's profit  =  (pool value before)  -  (pool value after),  both marked at P
```

**The arbitrageur's gain is not merely correlated with the liquidity providers' loss. It is
numerically identical to it, trade by trade.** No modelling assumption, no volatility parameter, no
appeal to a paper: it falls out of the invariant plus the stopping condition.

### The same derivation with numbers

**Subgoal 6 — instantiate.** A pool holds 1,000 ETH and 3,000,000 units of a dollar stablecoin, so
`k = 3e9` and `p = 3,000`. The market price moves 1%, to `P = 3,030`.

```text
x' = sqrt(3e9 / 3030)   = 995.0372 ETH
y' = sqrt(3e9 * 3030)   = 3,014,962.69 stable
Δx = 1000 - 995.0372    = 4.9628 ETH taken out of the pool
Δy = 3,014,962.69 - 3e6 = 14,962.69 stable paid in
sold externally: 4.9628 * 3030 = 15,037.32
profit = 15,037.32 - 14,962.69 = 74.63
```

Cross-check with the closed form: `3030*1000 + 3,000,000 - 2*3,014,962.69 = 74.63`. Same number.

**Subgoal 7 — see what the pool actually did wrong.** The pool sold 4.9628 ETH for 14,962.69, an
average of 3,014.96 each, while the market was on its way to 3,030. The shortfall is
`4.9628 * (3030 - 3014.96) = 74.63`. The pool did not get robbed; **it rebalanced at its own curve
price instead of at the market price**, and the gap is the whole of the loss.

**Subgoal 8 — get the general scaling.** Write `P = p*(1+δ)`. Since `y = p*x` and `k = p*x²`, we have
`sqrt(k*P) = p*x*sqrt(1+δ)`, so:

```text
profit = p*x*[ (1+δ) + 1 - 2*sqrt(1+δ) ]
```

Expanding `sqrt(1+δ) ≈ 1 + δ/2 - δ²/8` and cancelling the terms in `δ` leaves `p*x*δ²/4`. The pool's
total value is `V = p*x + y = 2*p*x`, so:

```text
profit ≈ V * δ² / 8
```

Check it: `6,000,000 * 0.0001 / 8 = 75`, against the exact 74.63. The next term accounts for the
difference.

**That quadratic is the headline.** Arbitrage cost scales with the *square* of the price move, which
means it accumulates with realised variance rather than with net displacement — and a pool cannot
outrun it by hoping the price comes back. It is the discrete counterpart of the continuous-time result
the loss-versus-rebalancing paper formalises.<Cite src="arxiv-lvr" />

## Which is exactly why "impermanent loss" hides it

You know [impermanent loss](/c/impermanent-loss) as the comparison against simply holding the two
tokens.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-understanding-returns" /> Run the round trip and watch that baseline fail.

Let the price go 3,000 → 3,030 → 3,000. The second leg is the mirror of the first: the arbitrageur now
sells ETH into the pool, and the same formula with `P = 3,000` and the post-first-trade reserves gives
a profit of 74.30. Two legs, 148.93 total.

Now look at the pool. After the second arbitrage its reserves are `sqrt(k/3000) = 1000` ETH and
`sqrt(k*3000) = 3,000,000` stable — **exactly where it started**. The price returned, so impermanent
loss is zero. By the HODL baseline, nothing happened.

Compare against the other baseline. A portfolio that holds the pool's own composition at every instant
but rebalances at *market* prices sells 4.9628 ETH at 3,030 (receiving 15,037.32, not 14,962.69), then
buys them back at 3,000 (paying 14,888.40). It ends holding 1,000 ETH and 3,000,148.93 stable —
**148.93 ahead of the pool, which is the arbitrageurs' total to the cent.**

- **Impermanent loss is a function of where the price ended.** A round trip nets to

- **LVR is a function of the path.** Every leg is paid for, and nothing ever refunds

The formal treatment is the paper the term comes from. Its result is a decomposition of liquidity-
provision returns in continuous time into a **beta-like component** reflecting market-risk exposure and
an **alpha-like component** reflecting microstructural forces — accrued fees minus losses to
arbitrageurs.<Cite src="arxiv-lvr" /> Applied to the Uniswap v2 ETH-USDC pool, the same work finds that
over **99.991% of LP return variance is driven by beta exposure to market risk**.<Cite src="arxiv-lvr" />

Sit with that second number, because it is the practically useful one and it is not what most LP
dashboards imply. Almost everything an LP's value does day to day is the two assets moving. The part
that is *about being an LP* — fees earned, minus the transfer derived above — is a thin alpha term
riding on top of a much larger beta term.<Cite src="arxiv-lvr" /> A dashboard reporting fee APR in
isolation is showing one half of that thin term and omitting the other.

## Where fees enter, and what they do not do

Set the fee to `f`. The arbitrageur must now clear it before trading, so the pool price can sit
anywhere inside a **no-arbitrage band** of roughly `±f` around the market price without anyone acting.
Three consequences, in order of how often they are misstated.

**The band suppresses small moves entirely.** A price wobble smaller than the fee is never arbitraged,
so those `δ²` terms are simply not paid.<Cite src="arxiv-lvr" /> This is a real reduction, not an
accounting one.

**Above the band, the fee is a partial rebate on the transfer.** The arbitrageur still moves the pool
to the edge of the band rather than to the market price, and the fee they pay lands with the LPs,
because charging the input before the invariant check is precisely how LPs are
paid.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> The transfer shrinks; it does not reverse.

**The fee tier is therefore a two-sided choice, not a revenue dial.** A higher fee widens the band and
retains more per arbitrage, and it also loses the ordinary flow that would have paid it. Whether an LP
position is profitable is the sign of `fees earned - LVR`, not the sign of either
alone — which is the same shape as the fees-versus-divergence comparison you already
have,<Cite src="uniswap-v2-understanding-returns" /> with the second term measured against the correct
baseline.

<Aside kind="note">
This is where concentrated liquidity gets uncomfortable. Narrowing a range raises `L` over that
interval, which raises fee income per unit of capital — and by the same factor raises the size of the
rebalance the pool performs per unit of price move, and therefore LVR. Concentration is leverage on
both terms simultaneously. Anyone quoting the fee multiple without the other one is quoting half a
position.<Cite src="arxiv-lvr" />
</Aside>

## What the system buys with the money

The honest closing of the argument is that this transfer purchases something real.

Ethereum's documentation states the positive case directly: many DeFi projects rely on economically
rational actors, DEX arbitrage ensures users get the best and most correct prices for their tokens,
and without rational searchers finding and fixing economic inefficiencies, protocols would not be as
robust as they are.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> Price consistency across venues is not maintained
by any coordinating party. It is maintained by this.

So arbitrage sits on the far side of the axis the module's fourth lesson formalises. It requires no
identified user to receive a worse execution; it captures a discrepancy that existed independently of
anyone's transaction.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> Ethereum's documentation files it as the simplest
and most well-known MEV opportunity, and therefore the most competitive.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> The design question it raises is therefore not
*how do we stop it* but *who should receive it*, since somebody will. Every answer later in this module
— orderflow auctions, fee-collecting hooks, batch clearing — is a different nomination.

<Check type="explain" concept="mev-arbitrage-is-lvr">
An LP reports a year in a volatile pair: fee income 12% of position value, and a final token price
almost exactly where it started. They conclude the position returned about 12%, since "impermanent
loss went to zero when the price came back".

Say what is wrong, what number is missing, and what you would need to measure to produce it.

<Answer>
**The conclusion uses the wrong baseline, and the missing term is the one that is guaranteed not to
have come back.**

**Why the price returning does not help.** Impermanent loss is a function of the ending price ratio
only, so it is genuinely near zero. That is a true statement about a comparison nobody was actually
running. Over the year the pool rebalanced continuously, each rebalance executed at the curve price
rather than the market price, and each shortfall was paid to an arbitrageur and never returned. By the
derivation above, that is `V*δ²/8` per leg, and legs do not cancel — the quantity is quadratic, so it
is non-negative in both directions.<Cite src="arxiv-lvr" />

**The missing number is LVR**, and the right comparison is `fees - LVR`, not `fees - IL`. The correct
statement of the year is "12% in fees, minus whatever was paid to arbitrageurs, plus whatever the two
tokens did" — and the paper's decomposition names those three pieces exactly: an alpha term of accrued
fees minus losses to arbitrageurs, riding on a beta term of market exposure.<Cite src="arxiv-lvr" />

**How to measure it, without a model.** You do not need a volatility estimate; the transfer is
observable. For every swap against the pool, take the reserves immediately before and immediately
after, mark both at a reference price at that block, and take the difference. That is exactly the
`(value before) - (value after)` identity from subgoal 5. Summing it over the year gives realised LVR
in the pool's own units, and it requires only a price series and the pool's swap history.

**Two checks that keep the number honest.** First, mark against a venue the pool is actually arbitraged
against, not against the pool's own price — using the pool's price makes the difference vanish by
construction. Second, attribute per swap rather than per day; daily marks miss round trips inside the
day, which is precisely the term impermanent loss was already missing.

**The interpretation to hand back.** 12% fee income is not a return. It is one side of a subtraction,
and the other side scales with realised variance. That is the reason a high-fee position on a
high-volatility pair can still underperform holding — the same variance that generates the fees also
generates the bill.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** takes the other side of the axis. The sandwich cannot exist without a specific
person receiving less than they otherwise would, and its size is set by a number that person chose in
a settings panel. Where this lesson's transfer is invisible and continuous, that one is discrete,
attributable, and bounded by a value you can compute.

**Liquidation MEV**, two lessons on, is the case where the payment is deliberate — and lending
protocols relying on speedy liquidations is the other half of the positive case Ethereum's
documentation makes for searchers.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> Holding all three together — a
transfer nobody notices, an attack with a named victim, and a fee a protocol pays on purpose — is what
makes the toxic-versus-benign axis usable rather than moralising.

**On the design side**, the identity from subgoal 5 is the whole argument for every mechanism that
tries to keep this value inside the pool: dynamic fees that widen the band when volatility rises,
auctions for the right to be the pool's first trade in a block, oracle-following curves. Each is an
attempt to move the `(value before) - (value after)` term from the arbitrageur to the LP, and each can
be evaluated by asking how much of the alpha-side term it actually captures.<Cite src="arxiv-lvr" />

**When you next read an LP dashboard**, ask which baseline the loss column uses. If it says
"impermanent loss", it is reporting a path-independent comparison against holding, and the
path-dependent term is missing.<Cite src="arxiv-lvr" />
