An oracle update lands. Somewhere in the same block, a position that was healthy one transaction ago
is now eligible for liquidation, and within microseconds — before the block is even sealed — a bot has
repaid part of its debt and taken collateral at a discount.

The borrower lost money. A bot made money. The ordering decided who. By every criterion in this module
so far, that is MEV.

It is also the reason the lending market did not accumulate bad debt, and the reason the depositors on
the other side of that loan can withdraw. Ethereum's own documentation puts the two halves in the same
paragraph: lending protocols rely on speedy liquidations when borrowers fall below collateralisation
ratios to ensure lenders get paid back, and searchers compete to parse chain data as fast as possible
to determine which borrowers can be liquidated and to be first to submit the liquidation transaction
and collect the fee.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

This lesson works out what that fee actually buys, and then uses the result to build the only
taxonomy of MEV that survives contact with it.

## What the protocol is paying for

You know the mechanism from the liquidations module. Aave- and Compound-style liquidation sets a
discount as a governance parameter and lets anyone call the liquidation entry point the instant a
position becomes eligible.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> Ethereum's documentation describes the
borrower's side in the same terms: if liquidated, the borrower pays a liquidation fee, some of which
goes to the liquidator.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> The
[fixed-discount design](/c/liq-fixed-discount) is atomic, composable and trivially integrable, which
is why it dominates.

Read it as a purchase order rather than as a payout. The protocol is buying **liveness**: a guarantee
that somebody with capital will act, unprompted, within a very short window, at three in the morning,
during a network congestion event, on a position nobody at the protocol has ever looked at. There is
no employee for that job and no service-level agreement to sign. The discount is the offer, and the
race is the delivery mechanism.

Derive what the offer has to be worth.

### Worked derivation: the buffer, and what eats it

**Subgoal 1 — write down the state at the moment of breach.** A position has collateral of value `C`
and debt of value `D`, and the [liquidation threshold](/c/lend-ltv-vs-liquidation-threshold) is `LT` —
the protocol-determined ratio at which the position becomes
seizable.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /><Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> The health factor is
`C*LT/D`, and the position becomes liquidatable when it reaches 1, which happens at:

```text
C = D / LT
```

With `LT = 0.85`, the collateral is worth `1/0.85 = 1.176` times the debt at the instant the position
becomes seizable. **That 17.6% is the entire safety buffer**, and everything that follows is an
argument about how it gets spent.

**Subgoal 2 — charge the buffer for the bonus.** A liquidator repaying debt receives collateral worth
the repaid amount times `(1 + b)`, where `b` is the bonus.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> For the
position to close without leaving the protocol short, the collateral must cover the debt plus the
bonus:

```text
C >= D * (1 + b)
```

Substituting the breach condition `C = D/LT` and cancelling `D`:

```text
1/LT >= 1 + b        so        b <= (1 - LT) / LT
```

At `LT = 0.85` that ceiling is `0.15/0.85 = 17.6%`. A bonus above it is not merely generous — it is
arithmetically unpayable at the moment of breach, and every liquidation at the boundary would create
bad debt by construction.

**Subgoal 3 — charge the buffer for delay.** Liquidation is not instantaneous, and the collateral
keeps moving while it is pending. Let the collateral fall a further fraction `d` between breach and
execution. Solvency now requires:

```text
C * (1 - d) >= D * (1 + b)
(1 - d) / LT >= 1 + b
d <= 1 - LT * (1 + b)
```

With `LT = 0.85` and `b = 0.05`:

```text
d <= 1 - 0.85 * 1.05 = 1 - 0.8925 = 0.1075
```

**Subgoal 4 — read the units.** That 10.75% is not a percentage of anything the protocol controls. It
is a **budget denominated in adverse price movement**, and the only variable that converts it into
time is how fast the market moves. In a quiet hour it is many minutes. In the middle of a cascade it
can be a single block.

**Subgoal 5 — see what the competition actually delivers.** A liquidation race with many participants
compresses the delay to essentially zero: searchers compete to parse chain data as fast as possible
and to be first to submit,<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> so the transaction lands in the same block
as the oracle update that created the opportunity.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> With `d ≈ 0` the
delay term stops consuming buffer, and the whole 10.75% sits unused as protection against the *next*
move rather than being spent waiting.

**Subgoal 6 — invert it, which is the point.** Suppose the competition disappears — one bored keeper,
no bidding, liquidations landing minutes late. Now `d` is materially non-zero, and the inequality in
subgoal 3 must be satisfied by moving something else. The protocol's options are to cut `b`, which
makes the keeper even slower, or to cut `LT`, which reduces how much *every borrower on the platform*
may borrow against the same collateral.

**So the bonus is not a leak that better design recovers. It is the price of the `LT` everyone is
enjoying.** Take away the race and the buffer has to be paid for in reduced borrowing capacity across
the whole book. Nobody gets the bonus back; borrowers get less leverage instead.

## Who actually receives the bonus

Here is where the first three lessons pay off, and where the standard story is wrong in an
interesting way.

The liquidator does not keep the bonus. Being first to a liquidatable position is a public, mechanical
opportunity with a value everyone computes identically from the same on-chain
state<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> — a textbook common-value auction, exactly the setup from lesson
one. So the bid climbs toward the full bonus less execution cost, and the surplus lands with whoever
sells the block position.<Cite src="arxiv-flash-boys-2" />

That gives the sequence in full:

- The **borrower** pays the bonus. Their loss is fixed by governance and disclosed in
  advance.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" />
- The **liquidator** captures it for a moment and bids nearly all of it
  away.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />
- The **builder** — the seller of the ordering right — keeps the
  residual.<Cite src="flashbots-auction-docs" />
- The **protocol**, whose parameter created the whole prize, receives
  nothing.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" />

Say that last line out loud, because it is the entire premise of lesson nine. The protocol pays for
liveness it needs, at a price it sets, and the money ends up with a party that has no relationship
with it at all. Chainlink's own framing of the same observation is that the value leaks entirely to
third parties, and that an auction can route part of it back
instead.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /><Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" />

### Where the derivation stops being reassuring

Three places, and they are the ones worth carrying into a risk review.

**When the unwind is the constraint.** The bonus is paid in collateral, and a liquidator holding
collateral has to sell it. That sale is a trade against a curve, so it has deterministic price
impact,<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> and for a large enough position the impact exceeds the
bonus. The liquidation is then *unprofitable to perform* even though the protocol desperately wants it
performed. This is the case where a race with many participants delivers nothing, because zero times
many is still zero.

**When everybody is liquidating at once.** The cascade case couples the terms in subgoal 3 that the
derivation treated as independent: liquidations sell collateral, selling moves the price along the
curve,<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> and the price move breaches more
positions.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> The `d` term and the unwind impact stop
being small perturbations. The reported scale of the October 2025 event is the standing reminder — the
headline figure of roughly nineteen billion dollars in liquidations is best read as a **reported
floor** rather than a measurement, since exchange liquidation feeds are known to under-report and
analysts differ on the true total.<Cite src="coindesk-19b-liquidation" />

**When gas prices are set by the same event.** The liquidation race bids up the cost of blockspace at
exactly the moment the protocol needs transactions to land, and that cost comes out of the same bonus.
The original priority-gas-auction analysis is where this dynamic was first
formalised,<Cite src="arxiv-flash-boys-2" /> and it is the reason the same paper argued the fee
dynamics were a consensus-layer concern rather than only a user-experience
one.<Cite src="arxiv-flash-boys-2" /> Submitting the liquidation as a bundle rather than as a public
transaction moves the auction off chain but does not reduce what it
costs.<Cite src="flashbots-auction-docs" />

## The axis that actually separates the cases

Now the taxonomy. "Good MEV" and "bad MEV" cannot classify the liquidation, because it is
simultaneously a loss to a named individual and a service the system requires. Ethereum's own
documentation is careful to describe both effects and does not attempt a verdict: MEV is not all bad,
and there are both positive and negative consequences.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

Three questions do the work instead. Ask them in order.

**Question 1 — does the extraction require a specific identified party's execution to be degraded?**

Delete the user's transaction and see whether the opportunity survives. Arbitrage survives: the price
gap between two venues exists whether or not anyone swapped.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> The
sandwich does not survive: without the victim's transaction there is nothing to place trades around,
and by the previous lesson the profit is a function of that transaction's own bound. The liquidation
survives the deletion of any *user transaction* — the oracle's write is what created it.

**Question 2 — who chooses the magnitude?**

This is the question that resolves the liquidation, and it is the one most taxonomies omit. In a
sandwich, the attacker chooses how much to take, up to the victim's slippage bound; the victim's loss
is whatever the attacker's optimiser decides. In a liquidation, the loss is `b`, a governance
parameter published in advance and identical for everybody.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" /> The
borrower agreed to it when they opened the position. **A loss set by the counterparty is a different
object from a loss set by the contract**, even when the number is the same.

**Question 3 — what happens to the system if you remove it?**

Remove sandwiching and users get better execution; nothing else changes. Remove arbitrage and venue
prices diverge, which breaks the price-consistency service every downstream protocol
assumes.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> Remove liquidation racing and lending markets accumulate bad
debt or cut leverage, per subgoal 6.

### Reading the answers

The three questions produce three distinct categories rather than a spectrum.

**Extraction that requires a victim.** Sandwiching, and any generalised front-run that copies a
pending transaction's payload, substitutes its own address and resubmits at a higher
fee.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> Attacker-chosen magnitude, no systemic function. The remedy is to
remove the information or the ordering discretion that makes targeting possible — lessons six, seven
and eight.

**Capture of a public inconsistency.** Atomic arbitrage, backrunning.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />
No identified victim, magnitude set by the state of the world, and a real service delivered. This will
be captured by *somebody* regardless, so the design question is who should receive it: the LP, the
protocol, the user whose transaction revealed it.

**A fee the protocol deliberately offers.** Liquidation bonuses.<Cite src="aave-v3-pool-docs" />
Magnitude set by governance, function essential, and the only open question is whether the protocol
must let the payment leak to whoever wins the ordering fight — which is a mechanism-design question
with a live answer.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" />

<Check type="explain" concept="mev-toxic-vs-benign">
A perpetuals venue proposes to eliminate liquidation MEV by making liquidations permissioned: a single
whitelisted keeper, paid a flat retainer, is the only address allowed to call the liquidation
function. No bonus, no race, no bots. The pitch says this returns the bonus to borrowers.

Run the three questions on it, and say what the venue has actually done.

<Answer>
**Start with the arithmetic, because the pitch has an accounting error.**

The bonus was not being taken from borrowers by the bots; it was being *paid* by the protocol out of
the borrower's collateral to buy speed, and it landed with whoever sold block position. Removing the
bonus does not hand it back — it stops purchasing the thing the bonus was buying. From subgoal 3, the
solvency condition is `d <= 1 - LT*(1+b)`. Setting `b = 0` relaxes the ceiling on `d`, which sounds
good, right up until you notice what happened to `d` itself.

**Question 1 — does it require a degraded party?** No, and it did not before either. So nothing in
this proposal addresses the category that actually has victims. If the venue also has a public order
book or an AMM, sandwiching is untouched.

**Question 2 — who chooses the magnitude?** Previously governance, through `b`. Now the venue,
through the retainer — except the retainer is paid by the venue rather than out of the position, so
the borrower's disclosed cost genuinely does fall. That part of the pitch is true, and it is the only
part.

**Question 3 — what breaks if you remove it?** This is where it comes apart. The race was not a
side-effect of the bonus; it *was* the delivery mechanism for low `d`. One keeper on a retainer has no
marginal incentive to be fast, has a single point of failure, and — critically — has no reason to act
at all when the liquidation is unprofitable to unwind. The venue has replaced a competitive market
that self-selects for whoever can execute cheapest with a
[keeper liveness assumption](/c/liq-keeper-liveness-assumption) held by one party.

**What the venue has actually done:** converted a *market* risk into a *counterparty* risk, and moved
it off the borrower's balance sheet onto the protocol's. That may be the right trade — it is exactly
the trade a centralised venue makes — but it must be argued as such, with the new assumption written
down.

**The four questions to put back to them.**

*What is `d` under the new design, in the worst hour rather than the average one?* If it is minutes
instead of milliseconds, `LT` must come down, and the leverage every user gets falls. That is the
borrower's real bill for the removed bonus.

*What happens when the keeper is down, rate-limited, or its key is compromised?* There is no second
bidder. Under the old design, keeper failure was invisible because someone else took the position.

*Who liquidates the position nobody wants?* A retainer does not scale with the difficulty of the
unwind. The permissionless race at least paid more, in effect, for positions with more collateral at
stake, because the bonus was proportional.

*Is the keeper related to the venue?* If so, the venue is now the counterparty to its own users'
liquidations, and every liquidation is a transaction between related parties at a price the venue
sets. That is a governance and disclosure problem, not a technical one.

**The general lesson.** "We removed the MEV" is not a design claim until you say which of the three
categories was removed and what the system was buying with it. Here, category three was removed and
its function was silently reassigned to a single trusted party.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** turns the module around. Four lessons on what the extraction *is* have all ended
at the same sentence — the value lands with whoever controls ordering — so the second half asks who
that is, in detail: builders who accept sealed bids from searchers and optimise the
ordering,<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> relays that hold the block body, and a proposer who signs
sight unseen.<Cite src="flashbots-auction-docs" />

**Lesson nine** is this lesson's unfinished business. If the protocol sets the bonus, creates the
opportunity with its own oracle write, and receives none of the proceeds, the position after that
write is an asset it could be selling instead of losing. That is a mechanism, it is deployed, and its
limits are worth knowing.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" />

**In risk work**, the reusable artefact is subgoal 3. `d <= 1 - LT*(1+b)` is a one-line sanity check
on any lending parameter proposal: it tells you how much adverse movement your buffer tolerates
between breach and execution, and it makes explicit that raising `LT` for competitiveness is spending
the same budget that keeps you solvent during a cascade.

**When you next hear "we eliminated MEV"**, run the three questions. Most such claims turn out to have
eliminated a category the system was using, or to have moved a category rather than removed it — and
the two are distinguishable in about ninety seconds.
