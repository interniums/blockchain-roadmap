Every lesson so far has ended on the same sentence: the value lands with whoever controls the
ordering. Four different mechanisms — arbitrage, sandwiching, liquidation racing, the generalised
front-run — and one destination.

So who is that, exactly? Not the validator whose name appears on the block. Under the deployed design
the proposer signs a header and a bid without ever seeing the block's
contents,<Cite src="mev-boost-docs" /> which means the party who decided what your transaction sits
next to is somebody the protocol has no concept of.

This lesson is the pipeline, walked hop by hop, with two questions at each: **who can see the
transaction here, and who can reorder it here.**

## What proposer-builder separation is actually separating

Ethereum's documentation states the diagnosis before the remedy: the combination of block producer and
block proposer roles is what introduces most of the MEV-related
problems.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> One party both chose the ordering and had a consensus vote,
so incentives that should have been about consensus became incentives about
ordering.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

Under proposer-builder separation, validators remain responsible for proposing and voting on blocks,
while a new class of specialised entities — **block builders** — are tasked with ordering transactions
and building blocks.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> A builder creates a transaction bundle and places
a bid for its inclusion as the execution payload; the validator selected to propose checks the bids
and chooses the one with the highest fee.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> The documentation's own
summary of what this creates is the key phrase: **an auction market, where builders negotiate with
validators selling blockspace.**<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

Read that as an admission rather than a description. Blockspace was always being sold; PBS made the
sale explicit and gave it a clearing mechanism.

### The commit-reveal, and the exposure it creates

The mechanical core is a commitment scheme. Builders publish only a cryptographic commitment to a
block's contents — the block header — along with their bids.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> After
accepting the winning bid, the proposer creates a signed block proposal that includes that header. The
builder is then expected to publish the full block body after seeing the signed
proposal.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

Every trust question in this lesson lives in the word *expected*.

Two exposures exist and they point in opposite directions:

**The builder's exposure.** If the body were revealed before the proposer committed, the proposer
could take the ordering and build it themselves. The commitment prevents that, and ethereum.org names
it directly: commit-reveal removes the need for builders to trust validators not to steal the MEV
opportunity or expose it to other builders.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

**The proposer's exposure.** Having signed a header, the proposer is committed. If no body arrives,
they cannot sign a different block for that slot — that would be signing two blocks in the same slot,
which is [equivocation and slashable](/c/double-proposal-slashing).<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> The
protocol's answer is that **payment is unconditional**: the validator's fee still processes even if the
proposed block is unavailable or declared invalid by other validators, in which case the block is
discarded and the builder loses all transaction fees and MEV
revenue.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /><Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

Notice the shape of that solution. The proposer is not protected from *missing the slot*; they are
protected from *not being paid*. Those are different losses and only one of them was addressed.

<Aside kind="note">
The proposer keeps a local fallback: a validator using the Builder API is still expected to build a
block locally in case the builder fails to respond promptly, so they do not miss the proposal
reward.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> That fallback is the reason a builder outage degrades revenue
rather than halting the chain — and it is why "MEV-Boost is load-bearing" is a statement about
economics rather than about liveness.
</Aside>

## The relay, and why it is the part with no protocol behind it

Between builders and proposers sits a relay, and it exists for a reason that is easy to state and
awkward to remove.

Relayers validate transaction bundles before they reach the proposer, to protect block proposers from
spam and invalid transactions.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> MEV-Boost additionally introduces
escrows responsible for data availability, storing block bodies sent by builders and block headers
sent by validators.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> A validator connected to a relay asks for available
execution payloads and selects the payload header with the highest bid.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

So the relay holds the body, checks it, and releases it on the proposer's signature. Which means:

- The **proposer trusts the relay** to hold a valid body and to release it in time.
- The **builder trusts the relay** not to leak the contents to a competitor before the block lands.

**Neither assumption is enforced by consensus.** The API through which all of it happens is an
out-of-protocol specification maintained separately from the consensus
specs,<Cite src="builder-specs" /> and MEV-Boost itself is middleware run alongside a validator's own
clients rather than a part of either.<Cite src="mev-boost-docs" /><Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-docs" />

Ethereum's own documentation is unusually blunt about this: the Builder API is described as a
**temporary solution** aimed at providing a working implementation of proposer-builder separation,
albeit **with higher trust assumptions**, because implementing PBS properly requires changes to the
consensus protocol — specifically to the fork-choice rule.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

## Worked derivation: why building concentrates

The uncomfortable result of PBS is that it decentralised one role by concentrating another. That is
not an accident of who got there first; it follows from two properties of the good being sold.

**Subgoal 1 — establish that block value is superadditive in orderflow.** Let `V(S)` be the value of
the best block a builder can construct from a set `S` of orderflow. Claim: for disjoint sets,
`V(A ∪ B) >= V(A) + V(B)`, and often strictly.

The proof is a construction. Any block buildable from `A` alone and any block buildable from `B` alone
can be concatenated, giving `>=`. Strictness comes from combinations that neither set contains on its
own: a backrun in `B` that is only profitable after a swap in `A`; two opposing orders that can be
matched directly instead of both paying an AMM; a liquidation in `B` unlocked by an oracle update
routed in `A`. **Orderflow is complementary**, and complementarity is the technical name for returns
to scale.

**Subgoal 2 — write down the bid.** In a first-price auction against `n` competing builders, a builder
does not bid their own value. They bid enough to beat the best competitor, and keep the difference. So
for the leading builder with value `V₁` and a runner-up with value `V₂`:

```text
winning bid  ≈  V₂ + ε
builder keeps ≈  V₁ - V₂
```

**Subgoal 3 — notice what that does to the proposer.** The proposer's revenue is the *bid*, so it
tracks `V₂` — the second-best builder's value — not `V₁`. As the gap between first and second widens,
the proposer receives a smaller share of what the block was worth. BuilderNet's launch analysis
observes exactly this behaviour in the deployed market: builders do not pay the full value of their
blocks to validators, minimising their bids to capture a profit and to pay for access to exclusive
orderflow.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" />

**This inverts the usual story.** Concentration is normally described as bad for censorship
resistance, which it is. It is *also* bad for validator income, and by a mechanism that is invisible
from the validator's side, because a validator only ever observes the winning bid and never `V₁`.

**Subgoal 4 — close the loop.** A builder who wins more often is a better destination for orderflow,
because a searcher or wallet wants the highest probability of inclusion. More flow raises `V₁` by
subgoal 1. Higher `V₁` widens the gap in subgoal 2, letting the builder win more while paying less.
More wins attract more flow.

That is a positive feedback loop with no equilibrating force in it, and the empirical claim matches:
BuilderNet's own framing is that concentration in the block-building market tripled after 2022, and
that the acceleration came from major orderflow providers making **exclusive orderflow deals** with
builders to internalise their MEV — opaque off-chain agreements that entrenched a duopoly and created
an unequal playing field for apps and users without access to the same
treatment.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" /> Independent measurement of builder bidding reaches the same
structural conclusion about the role of private flow.<Cite src="arxiv-private-orderflow" />

### The number this lesson refuses to print

You will want a percentage here, and every article about builder concentration supplies one. This
module deliberately does not, and the reason is worth more than the figure.

Published 2026 measurements put two builders somewhere between roughly three quarters and four fifths
of all Ethereum blocks — but they **name different pairs of builders**, and they cannot both be
describing the same market at the same time. Different measurement windows, different denominators
(blocks won versus gas built versus value delivered), and structural changes such as one builder being
absorbed into a shared network all produce different answers to what sounds like one question.

So the honest teaching claim is qualitative and stable: **the top two dominate, the top four are close
to everything, and exclusive orderflow rather than technical advantage is the
driver.**<Cite src="buildernet-intro" /> Anything more precise is a measurement, and measurements
have dates.

If you need a current figure — for a report, a risk memo, a governance post — take it yourself from a
live dashboard and state the window and the denominator alongside
it.<Cite src="relayscan-io" /><Cite src="mevboost-pics" /> Relay-level statistics are recomputed
continuously from relay data,<Cite src="relayscan-io" /> which is the only kind of number that stays
true long enough to be worth quoting.

<Aside kind="warn">
This is a habit rather than a caveat. Every "N% of blocks" claim in this space is a
three-part statement — a percentage, a window, and a denominator — and material that carries only the
first part is unfalsifiable and usually stale. When you read one, ask which of blocks, gas or value it
counts, and over how many days. Most sources cannot answer.
</Aside>

## Enshrining it: what EIP-7732 changes and what it does not

The stated purpose of in-protocol PBS is to remove the intermediary. Ethereum's documentation frames
in-protocol PBS as reducing MEV's effect on consensus by removing MEV extraction from the purview of
validators altogether, with builders running specialised hardware capturing the opportunities
instead.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

EIP-7732 is the concrete proposal. It decouples execution validation from consensus validation both
logically and temporally, introduces a new **in-protocol entity** called builders, and adds a duty to
validators: submitting payload timeliness attestations.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> The
`ExecutionPayload` field is removed from the beacon block body and replaced by a signed commitment —
a `SignedExecutionPayloadBid` — from a builder to reveal the corresponding payload
later.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> That commitment specifies the execution block's hash and a value to be
paid to the proposer; when the beacon block is processed, the committed value is **deducted from the
builder's beacon-chain balance** and later withdrawn to an address of the proposer's
choosing.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

A subset of the beacon committee is assigned to the **Payload Timeliness Committee**, whose members
attest to whether the builder revealed the committed payload with the right block hash in a timely
fashion and whether the corresponding blob data was available in their view.<Cite src="eip-7732" />
PTC members are explicitly **not required to validate the execution payload** — execution validation
is deferred until the next beacon block.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

Read the design against the two trust assumptions from earlier and the improvement is precise:

- The relay's role as escrow disappears, because the commitment and the payment are in the consensus
  state rather than in an intermediary's database.<Cite src="eip-7732" />
- The proposer's payment no longer depends on anyone's good behaviour, because it is deducted from a
  staked balance the protocol controls.<Cite src="eip-7732" />
- The builder's protection against theft becomes a protocol rule rather than a relay
  policy.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

Three things it does **not** do, and each is worth saying out loud.

**It does not decentralise building.** Nothing in the design touches subgoal 1. Complementary
orderflow still favours whoever aggregates the most of it. The design changes who the builder trusts,
not how many builders there are.

**It introduces new failure surface.** The EIP's own security considerations enumerate the free-option
problem, builder safety and a malicious PTC.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> A valid beacon block can now exist
with no payload attached, which is a genuinely new state for every consumer of beacon blocks to
handle.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

**It has not shipped.** As of August 2026 EIP-7732 stood at **Review** status on the Standards Track,
not Final.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> Treat any specific fork or date attached to it as a claim to verify
against the current specification status rather than as a schedule.

**Wallet to RPC.** Your provider sees the full transaction. Whether it then broadcasts or routes
privately is a policy you rarely chose explicitly. Reordering power: none, but *routing* power, which
is the next lesson's subject.

**Public mempool, if it goes there.** Everyone sees it. Anyone may construct transactions around it,
and generalised frontrunners will copy it outright if it is profitable to copy.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />
Reordering power: nobody directly, but every searcher can bid for a position relative to it.

**Searcher.** Sees whatever the mempool or the orderflow provider shows. Constructs a bundle that
executes in the submitted order or not at all,<Cite src="flashbots-auction-docs" /> and submits it
with a sealed bid.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> Reordering power: proposes an ordering, cannot
impose one.

**Builder.** Sees everything routed to it, public and private. Accepts sealed-price bids from
searchers and runs optimisations to find the most profitable
ordering.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> **This is where ordering discretion actually sits.**

**Relay.** Sees the full block body, validates it, and holds it until the proposer
signs.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> Reordering power: none by design, and exclusion power in
practice, since it chooses which builders it serves.

**Proposer.** Sees a header and a number.<Cite src="mev-boost-docs" /> Reordering power: none.
Exclusion power: only by declining the whole block and building locally.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

The single most useful thing to take from that list is where the wide part is. The party with the most
information and the most discretion is the builder — a role with no protocol definition, no
registration, and until EIP-7732 ships, no consensus-enforced obligations at
all.<Cite src="eip-7732" /><Cite src="builder-specs" />

<Check type="explain" concept="mev-builder">
Your team is writing a risk memo for a treasury that holds a large position and executes on-chain. A
draft paragraph reads: "Block building is 80% controlled by two entities, so any transaction we send
has an 80% chance of being handled by a party that could extract from it. We therefore recommend
routing exclusively through a private endpoint."

Both sentences have problems. Identify them, and say what you would write instead.

<Answer>
**Sentence one has a number problem and a logic problem.**

*The number.* Published 2026 figures for the top two builders' share disagree materially — they land
in a wide band and name different pairs, so they cannot all describe the same market at the same
time.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" /> A memo that states one of them as fact inherits an unstated
window and an unstated denominator. Worse, the three plausible denominators measure different things:
share of *blocks won* speaks to inclusion probability, share of *gas built* speaks to how much
execution flows through them, and share of *value delivered* speaks to bidding power. Your treasury's
question is the first; most published figures are one of the other two.

*The logic.* Even granting the number, "handled by" does not imply "extracted from". A builder holding
your transaction can extract only if the transaction contains extractable value — the bounded quantity
you can compute from your own slippage tolerance. A transfer with no ordering-dependent payoff is
worth nothing to reorder, at any concentration level. The risk is a property of the transaction, not
of the market structure.

**Sentence two states a conclusion the analysis does not reach.**

Private routing removes public-mempool observers. It does not remove the recipient, and the recipient
is precisely the concentrated party the first sentence was worried about. Recommending exclusive
private routing *because* building is concentrated has the direction backwards: exclusive orderflow is
the input to the feedback loop that produced the concentration.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" /> The
memo would be recommending that the treasury contribute to the thing it identifies as the risk.

**What to write instead**, in four parts.

*State the exposure from your own side.* "Our largest routine transaction is a swap of size X against
depth Y, giving deterministic price impact of Z%. At our current tolerance setting of T%, the maximum
value extractable around it is E" — computed from the identity in the slippage lesson. That is a
number you own and can re-derive whenever your size changes.

*State the market structure qualitatively and date any figure.* "Block building is highly
concentrated: the top two dominate and the top four are close to everything, driven by exclusive
orderflow rather than technical advantage.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" /> Current shares should be
taken from a live relay dashboard with the window and denominator
recorded.<Cite src="relayscan-io" /><Cite src="mevboost-pics" />"

*State the trust assumptions of each routing option side by side*, rather than picking one. Public
mempool: visible to everyone, no single trusted party. Private endpoint: invisible to the public,
fully visible to the receiving builder, and contributes to concentration. Intent-based routing:
different again, and the subject of lesson seven. The memo's job is the comparison.

*Name what would change the recommendation.* Enshrined PBS removes the relay from the trust set but
not the concentration.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> Shared-orderflow designs attack the concentration
directly. Both are worth monitoring, and neither had shipped as protocol changes when this was
written.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

**The reusable discipline.** A risk memo that quotes a market-share percentage without a date is not
making a measurable claim. One that derives its own exposure from its own parameters is, and it stays
true when the market moves.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** takes the obvious response to everything above — stop broadcasting, route
privately — and works out what it actually trades. The protection against public-mempool attacks is
real and measurable. The cost is paid in exactly the currency subgoal 4 runs on.

**Lesson eight** attacks the same problem from the information layer instead of the routing layer, and
runs into the question this lesson raised about the relay: what do you do when the party you must
trust cannot be removed, only replaced?

**Lesson ten** transplants the whole pipeline onto a rollup and finds most of it missing. There is no
builder market when one sequencer holds every role at once, which turns out to be a much sharper
concentration than anything described here.

**Operationally**, the artefact from this lesson is the six-row table above. Take one transaction your
product sends and fill it in for your actual stack — your RPC provider, your routing policy, the
builders your provider forwards to. Most teams discover they cannot complete row one, which is itself
the finding.
