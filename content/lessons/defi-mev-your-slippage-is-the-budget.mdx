A swap has failed twice. The wallet says "price impact too high — try increasing slippage tolerance",
so the user moves the dial from 0.5% to 3% and the third attempt lands. The trade executed. The
interface shows a green tick.

What the user actually did was raise a spending limit. Ethereum's documentation describes the
mechanism they raised it for: a searcher watches the mempool for large DEX trades, calculates the
approximate price effect of the trade on the pair, executes an optimal buy order immediately before it
— buying cheaply — and then executes a sell order immediately after, selling at the higher price the
victim's own trade caused.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

This lesson derives the exact relationship between the dial and the attacker's takings. The
relationship is tighter than "wider is worse". It is an identity.

## The mechanics, stated so the bound is visible

Three transactions in one block, in this order.

1. **The frontrun.** The attacker buys the same token the victim is about to buy, moving the pool
   price against them.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />
2. **The victim's swap.** It executes at the degraded price, and pushes the price further in the same
   direction.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" />
3. **The backrun.** The attacker sells what they bought, into a pool the victim has just pushed
   higher.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

The attacker's position is closed inside the same block, so this is not a directional bet. But note
what it is *not*: it is not atomic. Ethereum's documentation flags exactly that — sandwiching is
riskier than DEX arbitrage because it is not atomic, and is prone to a salmonella
attack.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> If the frontrun lands and the victim's transaction does not,
the attacker is left holding inventory they bought at a price they moved.

That non-atomicity is the whole reason bundles exist. Submitting the three transactions as an ordered
bundle that executes together or not at all removes the exposure,<Cite src="flashbots-auction-docs" />
which converts the attack from a risky trade into a mechanical one. Hold onto that: it is the hinge
for lessons six and ten, where the ability to guarantee co-inclusion turns out to be the thing that
actually determines whether sandwiching is viable on a given chain.<Cite src="arxiv-sandwiching-l1-vs-rollups" />

## Worked derivation: the attacker's profit is a function of one number

The claim is that the attacker's take is determined entirely by how far below the honest output the
victim's minimum-output bound sits. Prove it by conservation rather than by optimising over the
frontrun size — the algebra is short and the result is exact.

**Subgoal 1 — set up the pool and the actors.** Reserves `(x, y)` with `x * y = k`, where X is the
token being bought and Y the token being paid; the pool sets no price, it only checks the
invariant.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> The victim swaps
`v` units of Y for X. The attacker frontruns with some amount `a` of Y, receives `A` units of X, and
returns all `A` units in the backrun. Take the fee to be zero for now; it enters at the end as a
threshold.

**Subgoal 2 — write conservation for each token across all three trades.** Token X: the attacker takes
`A` out and puts `A` back, so their net effect is zero. Only the victim removes X, taking `V_out`.

```text
x_final = x - V_out
```

Token Y: the attacker pays `a` in and takes `b` out; the victim pays `v` in.

```text
y_final = y + a + v - b
```

**Subgoal 3 — impose the invariant.** With no fee, `x_final * y_final = k = x*y` — the invariant is a
constraint checked across every operation.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> So:

```text
(x - V_out) * (y + a + v - b) = x*y
y + a + v - b = x*y / (x - V_out)
```

**Subgoal 4 — solve for what the attacker keeps.** Their profit is `b - a`. Rearranging the line
above:

```text
b - a = y + v - x*y / (x - V_out)
```

**Look at what vanished.** The frontrun size `a` is gone. The attacker's profit does not depend on how
much they bought, only on **how little the victim ended up with**. Everything about attack sizing is
instrumental: `a` is chosen purely as the lever that drives `V_out` down.

**Subgoal 5 — check the formula at the boundary.** With no attacker, the victim's output is
`V0 = x*v/(y+v)`, so `x - V0 = x*y/(y+v)` and the profit expression becomes
`y + v - (y+v) = 0`.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> No degradation, no profit. Correct.

**Subgoal 6 — establish the direction.** As `V_out` falls, `x - V_out` rises, so `x*y/(x - V_out)`
falls, so profit rises. Profit is **strictly decreasing in the victim's output**. The attacker
therefore pushes `V_out` down as far as it will go — and the only thing that stops them is the
victim's own check.

**Subgoal 7 — name what stops them.** Uniswap's router takes an `amountOutMin` bound and reverts if
execution falls below it, along with a deadline.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-router02-source" /> Below that
bound the whole bundle is worthless, because the victim's transaction reverts and the attacker is left
having moved the price for nothing. So the optimum is:

```text
V_out = amountOutMin,  exactly
```

and the maximum profit available is:

```text
max profit = y + v - x*y / (x - amountOutMin)
```

**The slippage tolerance is not a safety margin. It is the argument to the attacker's profit
function.**<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

### The same thing with numbers

Pool: 1,000 ETH and 3,000,000 units of a dollar stablecoin, `k = 3e9`. The victim spends 100,000 of
the stable to buy ETH.

```text
honest output  V0 = 1000 * 100000 / 3,100,000 = 32.2581 ETH
```

At a **1% tolerance**, `amountOutMin = 0.99 * 32.2581 = 31.9355`:

```text
max profit = 3,000,000 + 100,000 - 3e9 / (1000 - 31.9355)
           = 3,100,000 - 3,098,967.01
           = 1,032.99 stable
```

Cross-check by a different route: the victim gave up `32.2581 - 31.9355 = 0.3226` ETH, and the pool
ends at `3,098,967.01 / 968.0645 = 3,201.0` per ETH. `0.3226 * 3,201.0 = 1,032.9`. The two agree to
rounding, which is what you want before trusting a derivation.

At a **3% tolerance** — the dial the user moved — `amountOutMin = 31.290`:

```text
max profit = 3,100,000 - 3e9 / 968.7097 = 3,100,000 - 3,096,903.10 = 3,096.90 stable
```

Tripling the tolerance tripled the budget almost exactly. On a 100,000 trade that is a 3.1% haircut,
authorised by the user, on a screen that called it a reliability setting.

**Now set the tolerance to zero output.** `amountOutMin = 0` gives:

```text
max profit = y + v - x*y/x = y + v - y = v
```

The attacker's profit equals **the victim's entire input**. Not a large fraction of it — all of it.
That is the exact reason `amountOutMin = 0` is not a lax parameter but a signed authorisation to
transfer everything, and it is why a router that permits it is shipping an unbounded loss.

## What a revert was actually telling you

Take the failure that started the lesson seriously, because it contained real information.

A swap that reverts on a tight tolerance has told you a true fact: **at current depth, your size cannot
be filled at anything close to the quoted price.** Price impact is a deterministic property of the
curve and the current reserves,<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> so widening the tolerance changes
nothing about the depth. It changes only whether the contract will accept the bad fill that the depth
was always going to produce — and, by subgoal 7, how much of that gap an attacker may take on top.

The four responses that address the actual constraint:

**Reduce the size.** Price impact grows superlinearly with size relative to
reserves,<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> so halving the trade cuts the impact by more than half.
Two trades in different blocks are worse for gas and better for execution.

**Change the route.** Deeper venue, different fee tier, split across pools. The quoted number that
matters is depth, not price.

**Change the mechanism.** If you are repeatedly hitting the bound, the AMM is the wrong venue for your
size, and lesson seven is about what replaces it.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

**Compute the bound instead of choosing it.** A tolerance should be derived from expected price impact
plus an allowance for genuine price movement between quote and execution; the router will enforce
whatever number you hand it and nothing more.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-router02-source" /> A fixed 3%
applied to every trade is a constant where a function belongs.

<Aside kind="warn">
Two failure modes that look like slippage and are not. A **fee-on-transfer token** delivers less than
the pool sends, so the router's own accounting fails regardless of tolerance. And a token with
transfer hooks can revert conditionally — which is the mechanism behind the salmonella attack
ethereum.org names: a token engineered so that the sandwich's own legs behave differently from an
ordinary trade, punishing the attacker rather than the user.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> Raising
the tolerance addresses neither, and in the second case is exactly the wrong reflex.
</Aside>

## Where the fee and the gas re-enter

The zero-fee derivation gives the clean bound. Two real costs cut into it, and knowing which direction
each one pushes is what makes the model usable.

**The attacker pays the pool fee twice.** The frontrun and the backrun are both ordinary swaps, so both
are charged on input before the invariant check.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-whitepaper" /> On a round trip
that is roughly twice the fee rate applied to the attacker's own trade size — and the attacker's trade
is typically larger than the victim's, because moving the price all the way to the victim's bound
takes size. The extraction has to clear that before anything reaches the attacker.

**The attacker pays for the position.** By the previous lessons, whatever survives is bid into the
payment for ordering.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /><Cite src="arxiv-flash-boys-2" /> So the searcher
running the sandwich keeps very little of it; the value lands with whoever sold the block
position.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> The three transactions travel as one bundle so that the
ordering is guaranteed rather than hoped for.<Cite src="flashbots-auction-docs" />

Both together produce the practically important consequence: **there is a tolerance below which no
sandwich is worth constructing**, because the recoverable gap is smaller than the round-trip fee plus
gas. That threshold is not a fixed percentage — it depends on your size relative to pool depth and on
the fee tier — but it is computable from the formula above, and it is the honest reason to set the
tolerance tight rather than an appeal to caution.

## The axis this case anchors

Set the sandwich beside the previous lesson's arbitrage and the difference is structural, not moral.

Arbitrage captures a discrepancy that existed independently of any user's transaction; delete the
victim and the opportunity remains.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> The sandwich has no existence
without a specific identified transaction to be placed around, and its size is a function of that
transaction's own parameters.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-router02-source" /> It is the clearest member of
the category that **requires** a named party to be worse off.

Ethereum's own documentation splits them the same way in its effects section: sandwich trading results
in an unequivocally worse experience for users, who face increased slippage and worse
execution,<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> while DEX arbitrage ensures users get the most correct
prices for their tokens.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> Same definition, opposite verdicts, and the
remedies differ accordingly: remove the information or the ordering discretion for the first, decide
the recipient for the second.

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="mev-slippage-is-the-attack-surface">
A team ships a router helper for their app. It quotes the swap off-chain, then submits with:

```solidity
uint256 minOut = quotedOut * 995 / 1000;   // 0.5% tolerance
if (minOut == 0) minOut = 1;               // "avoid the zero-minOut footgun"
router.swapExactTokensForTokens(amountIn, minOut, path, msg.sender, block.timestamp);
```

Three separate problems. Name them and say what each one costs.

<Answer>
**Problem one: `minOut = 1` is `minOut = 0` with extra steps.**

The guard was written as if zero were a magic value. It is not — it is the endpoint of a continuum.
Substituting `amountOutMin = 1` into the derivation gives a maximum profit of `y + v - x*y/(x-1)`,
which for any realistic reserve size is indistinguishable from `v`, the victim's entire input. The
clamp converts an unbounded authorisation into an unbounded authorisation that passes a lint rule. The
correct behaviour when the computed bound rounds to zero is to **revert**: a trade whose acceptable
output is one wei is not a trade.

**Problem two: `block.timestamp` as the deadline is no deadline.**

The router's deadline modifier compares against the timestamp of the block the transaction actually
executes in,<Cite src="uniswap-v2-router02-source" /> so passing `block.timestamp` evaluates in that
same block and always passes. The transaction becomes a standing order that can sit pending and be
executed at any later moment — an option granted free to whoever holds it. The check that was supposed
to bound *staleness* has been supplied with a value that makes it vacuous, which is worse than
omitting it, because a reviewer scanning for a deadline argument sees one.

The cost is not slippage but optionality: the longer it sits, the more likely the market has moved to
where executing it is profitable for someone else and bad for the user. A deadline must come from the
caller's clock, not the chain's.

**Problem three: 0.5% is a constant where a function belongs.**

The bound is being derived from the *quote*, which is the right idea, but the tolerance itself is
fixed. Two failures follow in opposite directions. On a small trade in a deep pool, 0.5% is far wider
than the real price impact, so the helper is authorising extraction that the trade never needed — by
the formula, proportionally more than a tighter bound would. On a large trade in a thin pool, 0.5% is
tighter than the deterministic price impact of the trade itself, so it reverts every time and someone
will "fix" it by widening the constant globally, taking the first failure with them.

The tolerance should be `expected price impact for this size against this depth` plus a small
allowance for genuine movement between quote and execution — computed per trade, from reserves the
helper is already reading to produce the quote.

**What to change, in order of loss prevented.** Revert instead of clamping. Take the deadline as a
parameter. Derive the tolerance from depth. Then, separately, decide how this transaction reaches a
block — because everything above bounds the damage rather than preventing the attack, and lessons six
and seven are about the second half of that.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** turns the axis this one anchored into a working taxonomy, using the case that
refuses to fit either bucket: liquidation MEV, where the extraction is the mechanism that keeps
lending markets solvent, and removing the competition would remove the guarantee rather than return
the money to anyone.

**Lesson six** takes the misconception above head on and asks what a protected RPC actually changes:
which attacker it removes, which one it introduces, and what the aggregate effect on builder
concentration is.<Cite src="shutter-mev-2026" />

**Lesson seven** is the structural answer rather than the mitigation. If there is no executable path
published, there is nothing to place transactions around — and the derivation above stops applying
because there is no `amountOutMin` sitting in a mempool for anyone to optimise
against.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-router02-source" />

**In your own product**, the reusable move is to stop treating the tolerance as a user setting and
start treating it as a computed bound with a justification — and to run the formula above on your own
default. If your app ships a 3% default on a pair with thin depth, you can now say exactly what that
default is worth to somebody else.
