Every hardened price getter written since about 2021 has the same first eight lines and the same
missing twelfth. The eight lines are the checks: sign, timestamp, age, decimals. The missing line is
the one that says what happens when the second source disagrees with the first.

It is missing because it is the only part that requires a decision rather than a rule. The checks
have right answers. The disagreement policy has four plausible answers, three of which create a new
attack, and the fourth of which creates a liveness dependency somebody has to be willing to own.

This lesson builds the whole stack in the order the layers actually protect you, and spends most of
its length on the two places where a choice is unavoidable: what to do on divergence, and how fast
you will let a price move.

## Layer one: reject the impossible

You have [the staleness check](/c/orac-staleness-check) from the push-feeds lesson, so it gets one
paragraph. A non-positive answer must never
reach a division or a valuation, because the signed return type makes it representable and the cast
to unsigned makes it catastrophic. A zero timestamp means there is no round. An `updatedAt` older
than a maximum you chose per-feed — heartbeat plus a small buffer, from *that* feed's listing on
*that* chain — means the answer is not current enough to act on, and the documented response is to
pause operation or switch to an alternate operation mode.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" /> On
a rollup, add the sequencer-uptime condition and its grace period, because a fresh timestamp after an
outage does not mean users could reach the chain.<Cite src="chainlink-l2-sequencer-feeds" />

These are necessary and they are not interesting. Nothing in this layer bounds *how wrong* a
well-formed, recent answer may be.

## Layer two: bound the answer

The bound you were probably taught to use is no longer there. Aggregators carry `minAnswer` and
`maxAnswer`, and on most data feeds these are no longer used: they do not stop your application from
reading the most recent answer, and the documentation instructs you to evaluate whether your use case
requires a custom circuit breaker and to implement it
yourself.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-api-reference" /> For monitoring purposes, **you** must
decide what limits are acceptable.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" />

Read the history in that. Feed-level floors and ceilings once existed and once clamped — and a clamp
holding a collapsing asset at an artificial floor is itself catastrophic for anyone lending against
it. The bound did not move because bounds are wrong; it moved because a bound belongs where the risk
tolerance is, which is in the consuming protocol.

So build one. What a bound can honestly be:

**An absolute plausibility window.** Not a prediction of the price — a statement about what values are
impossible for this asset in this market. A fiat-pegged stablecoin is not worth $4. A liquid-staking
token is not worth less than a fraction of the asset it wraps. These are wide, they almost never
bind, and when they bind something is genuinely wrong.

**A movement bound relative to the last accepted value.** Tighter, more useful, and the subject of
layer four.

**A cross-check against something independent.** The subject of layer three, and the one with the
decision in it.

Chainlink's own risk-mitigation list names the operational layers people skip: circuit breakers that
pause operations during an extreme price event, contract update delays so contracts do not act until
a fresh input has arrived, a manual kill switch to sever the connection if a bug is found upstream,
monitoring alerts on deviations, and a soak period before securing real
value.<Cite src="chainlink-selecting-data-feeds" /> Only the first is a smart-contract control. The
rest are an on-call rotation, and a protocol whose oracle defence is entirely in Solidity has built
the half that cannot respond.

## Layer three: two sources, and the sentence nobody writes

Adding a second source is the standard recommendation and it is incomplete in a way that matters,
because "use two sources" does not specify a function. Enumerate the candidates, and price each one
by asking the only question that discriminates them: *what must an attacker do?*

**Take the maximum.** An attacker must move **one** source, upward. Every source you add makes
collateral over-valuation strictly easier, because they have more targets and need only the cheapest.
The worst of the four, and it appears in real code, usually chosen to be "generous to users".

**Take the minimum.** An attacker must move **one** source, downward. Every source you add makes mass
liquidation strictly easier. Symmetrically bad, and worse in reputation terms: the victims are your
own users rather than your treasury.

**Take the mean.** An attacker must move one source by roughly `N` times the distortion they want, for
`N` sources. Genuinely better than max or min — the attack is not free — but it is still a
single-source break, and the required over-shoot may be cheap on a thin venue. Note also that the
2026 analysis's result is about *weighted* means and is qualified: liquidity weights are optimal only
locally, as the distortion tends to zero, and for larger distortions weighted means become more
fragile, with optimal weights depending on the target distortion and no single choice uniformly
optimal.<Cite src="arxiv-manipulation-cost-2026" />

**Take a median of three or more.** An attacker must move a majority. Structurally the strongest of
the aggregating rules, and the one the 2026 analysis has the strongest result for: liquidity weights
maximise the minimum cost of manipulation within the class of weighted medians, at **any** distortion
level.<Cite src="arxiv-manipulation-cost-2026" /> The corollary is the trap — an *equal-weight*
median across venues of different depth is not that construction, and hands an attacker the two
thinnest venues as the cheapest path.

**Revert or pause when they diverge beyond a bound.** An attacker has no value to aim at. There is no
number they can produce that the protocol will act on. This is the only rule that is safe without
further analysis, and it is not free: it converts a manipulation risk into a **liveness** risk, and
the liveness risk is itself attackable. Moving the cheapest source enough to trip the divergence
bound halts the protocol, which is a griefing attack whose cost is the cheap source's manipulation
cost and whose payoff is whatever your downtime is worth to someone.

Two further constraints on the choice, both easy to miss.

**Independence is a claim about the underlying, not the interface.** Two feeds that both derive from
the same thin venue are one feed with two addresses. The 2026 aggregation results are derived for
independent constant-product pools; correlated sources are outside the
model.<Cite src="arxiv-manipulation-cost-2026" />

**A cross-check does not have to be symmetric.** The most common useful shape in 2026 is not two
equal sources but a fast primary and a slow bound: a professional feed as the value, and a
time-weighted on-chain price as a sanity check that must agree within a wide band. The TWAP's lag,
which disqualified it as a primary, is harmless in a bound that is only there to catch wild values.
That asymmetry — one source for accuracy, one for plausibility — sidesteps most of the max/min/mean
problem, because the second source is never used as a value.

## Layer four: capping how fast the price may move

A rate limiter bounds the change your protocol will accept per update or per unit time. It is the
most powerful control on this list and the one most often misunderstood, so take the general case
first and then the special case where it is unambiguously correct.

**The general two-sided case converts one failure into another.** Against manipulation it is real: an
attacker who wants a large distortion must now hold it across multiple accepted updates, which
multiplies their cost by the number of updates and — more valuably — creates a window in which humans
or governance can respond. Against the market it is a liability: in a genuine gap move your price is
capped below reality, positions that should be liquidated are not, and bad debt accrues from the
opposite direction. That is the same coupling as [TWAP lag](/c/orac-twap-lag), arriving through a
different mechanism. **A rate limiter buys response time. It does not buy correctness.**

**The one-sided case on a monotone quantity has no such trade-off, and this is the important
insight.** Some prices have a direction that is legitimate and a direction that is not.

A liquid-staking token's exchange rate against its underlying only rises, slowly, as staking rewards
accrue. A sudden rise is therefore either an attack or a bug; a fall is a real event you must react
to immediately. Cap the *upside* and you have removed the attack while leaving your downside
responsiveness completely intact. There is no failure mode being traded away, because the capped
direction contains no legitimate fast movement.

Aave's price cap adapters implement exactly this.<Cite src="aave-price-cap-adapters" /> The
construction is worth deriving because every parameter is forced by something.

**Subgoal 1 — anchor the cap to a point in the past, not to the previous value.** The adapter stores a
**snapshot ratio** and a **snapshot timestamp** of the exchange rate.<Cite src="aave-price-cap-adapters" />
Anchoring to the previous value would let an attacker walk the cap upward one accepted update at a
time; anchoring to a fixed past point means the ceiling advances only with wall-clock time.

**Subgoal 2 — express the permitted growth as a rate, and convert it once.** A parameter
`maxYearlyRatioGrowthPercent` is configured in basis points — 500 for 5% — and converted internally
to a per-second growth rate for gas efficiency, with the yearly form kept as the external interface
for consistency with the rest of the risk system.<Cite src="aave-price-cap-adapters" />

**Subgoal 3 — compute the ceiling from elapsed time.**<Cite src="aave-price-cap-adapters" />

```text
maxRatio = snapshotRatio + (maxGrowthPerSecond × timeSinceSnapshot)
return currentRatio > maxRatio ? maxRatio : currentRatio
```

**Subgoal 4 — stop the snapshot itself from being a lie.** Two guards, each closing a specific hole.
A `minimumSnapshotDelay`, typically 7 to 14 days, exists because LST ratios update in discrete steps:
a snapshot taken immediately before a ratio update would imply an artificially high growth rate and
lift the ceiling for everyone.<Cite src="aave-price-cap-adapters" /> And a `MAXIMUM_SNAPSHOT_TERM` of
180 days ensures snapshots cannot go stale enough for the parameters to stop reflecting
reality.<Cite src="aave-price-cap-adapters" />

**Subgoal 5 — recognise the design posture, and copy it.** The repository is explicit that maximum
precision is not the objective: the cap carries sufficient margin, and the protocol's risk parameters
provide additional protection.<Cite src="aave-price-cap-adapters" /> A cap is not trying to be the
price. It is trying to be an upper bound that no honest process ever reaches, and calibrating it
tightly is how you convert a safety mechanism into an outage.

The stablecoin variant is simpler and shows the same logic without the growth term: a single
configured maximum — 1.04, say, for a 4% cap — returned instead of the oracle price whenever the
oracle exceeds it, with a hard implementation limit of 200%.<Cite src="aave-price-cap-adapters" />
Chainlink's own guidance points at this construction by name, recommending that protocols assess
upside risk and evaluate an explicit valuation ceiling for each stablecoin, and citing the
correlated-assets price oracle as an example that **adds upside protection while preserving full
downside responsiveness**.<Cite src="chainlink-selecting-data-feeds" />

That final clause is the whole design pattern in six words. Find the direction where fast movement is
never legitimate. Cap that one only.

<Aside kind="note">
The equivalent move on a time-weighted oracle is truncation: capping how many ticks an update may
move relative to the previous one, so that a large distortion requires many
updates.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" /> The trade is stated plainly in the analysis — truncated
oracles update more slowly than untruncated ones — and the parameter is chosen by asking how many
blocks of manipulation you want to force.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" /> Median and winsorised
variants attack the same problem by changing the statistic rather than clamping the
update.<Cite src="winsorized-uniswap-oracle" />
</Aside>

## Assembling it

The layers compose in one direction, and the order matters because each one assumes the previous
passed.

1. **Reject the malformed.** Sign, round existence, age, sequencer uptime.
2. **Reject the impossible.** Absolute plausibility window for this asset.
3. **Cap the direction that has no legitimate speed.** Growth cap, stable ceiling, or truncation.
4. **Cross-check, with a written policy.** Ideally a fast primary bounded by a slow independent
   source, with divergence beyond a threshold causing a revert or a pause rather than a value.
5. **Decide what "pause" means, before you need it.** Which operations stop and which continue.
   Liquidations usually must continue, because a protocol that stops liquidating during a
   dislocation is choosing bad debt over an unpopular seizure. New borrows usually must stop.
6. **Put the rest outside the contract.** Monitoring on deviations, an alert when any bound comes
   close to binding rather than when it binds, a kill switch with a named owner, and a soak
   period.<Cite src="chainlink-selecting-data-feeds" />

<Check type="explain" concept="orac-sanity-bounds">
A team hardens a price getter for a lending market. Their design: read a professional feed and a
30-minute TWAP of a deep pool; if the two are within 2% of each other, return the feed's answer; if
they diverge by more than 2%, return the **lower** of the two, "because under-valuing collateral is
the conservative direction".

Evaluate the divergence rule, and say what you would ship instead.

<Answer>
**The rule is a minimum rule with extra steps, and it inherits the minimum rule's attack.** In the
regime that matters — the one where the sources disagree — the protocol returns whichever source is
lower. An attacker who can push either source down by more than 2% now controls the collateral
valuation of every position in the market, and needs to move only the cheaper of the two.

**Which of the two is cheaper is computable, and it is probably the TWAP.** The professional feed's
answer is produced by an off-chain network aggregating many venues; the TWAP's answer is produced by
one pool, and the previous lessons give you its price directly: distortion required, `q` from the
window, `Δx` from live reserves, cost per block, blocks needed.

**The consequence is mass liquidation, not theft.** Every position within 2% of its threshold becomes
seizable simultaneously, at a discount, to whoever ran the manipulation. That is a worse outcome for
the protocol's users than the over-valuation this rule was designed to prevent, and it is
self-inflicted by the choice of combining function.

**"Conservative" is directionless without naming the victim.** Under-valuing collateral is
conservative *for the protocol's solvency* and aggressive *against its borrowers*. A single rule
cannot be conservative for both, which is precisely why divergence needs a policy rather than a
preference.

**What to ship instead.**

*Use the TWAP as a bound, not as a value.* Return the feed's answer when the two agree within the
band. When they do not, do not return a number at all: revert.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" />
That removes the target entirely — there is no distorted price an attacker can cause the protocol to
act on.

*Widen the band, and justify the width.* 2% trips during ordinary volatility, because the TWAP lags
every genuine move by construction — that lag is not a disagreement, it is the window doing its job.
Derive the width from the largest lag the window produces during a move you consider normal, plus the
feed's deviation threshold, plus a margin. A band that trips weekly gets widened during an incident,
under pressure, by someone who does not know why it was set.

*Decide what the revert stops.* Halt new borrows and collateral withdrawals. Keep liquidations
running against a conservative price, or accept that you are choosing bad debt. Write this down as
two lists of function names.

*Accept the griefing risk explicitly.* Whoever can move the cheap source by more than the band can
halt new borrowing at a known cost. Compute that cost, decide whether the downtime is worth more to an
attacker than that, and monitor for it — because the same alert that catches a griefing attempt
catches a genuine dislocation.

*Add the growth cap where the asset allows it.* If the collateral is a yield-bearing wrapper, cap the
upside against a stored snapshot and leave the downside untouched — a defence with no trade-off,
which none of the above is.<Cite src="aave-price-cap-adapters" /><Cite src="chainlink-selecting-data-feeds" />
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**The final lesson** takes the one consequence of a price update that none of this defends against,
because it is not an attack: the block position immediately after the update is worth money, and your
protocol is currently giving it away.

**The liquidations module** is where the pause policy above gets its real content — which operations
must keep running while the oracle is untrusted, and what it costs when they do not.

**The MEV module** picks up the divergence-griefing thread: an attacker who can halt your protocol at
a known price is running an economic attack, and the tools for reasoning about it are the same ones
used for ordering value.

**Writing the missing line, tomorrow.** Open the price getter you are responsible for and find the
divergence branch. If it takes a max, a min or a mean, you now know precisely which attack you have
bought and roughly what it costs. If there is no second source, that is a simpler finding. If there
is no branch because there is no policy, write the policy first — the code is the easy part.
