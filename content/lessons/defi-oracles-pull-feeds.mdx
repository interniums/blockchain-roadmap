Two products, same asset, same afternoon. A lending market liquidates a position using a price whose
timestamp is 41 minutes old, and that is fine, because its liquidation threshold has a buffer sized
for exactly that. A perpetual futures venue two blocks later fills an order at a price whose
timestamp is 400 milliseconds old, and if it had used the lending market's number it would have paid
out on a move that already happened.

The second one cannot be built on [the push model](/c/orac-push-model) from the last lesson. Not because that model is worse,
but because its cost structure forbids it: an operator paying gas to write a price on a schedule
cannot write every feed, on every chain, several times a second. Someone has to pay for each write,
and nobody is willing to pay for the ones nobody reads.

Pull oracles are the design that falls out of taking that constraint seriously.

## The inversion

In a pull design the oracle network publishes signed price updates continuously off chain and does
not write them anywhere on chain. When an application needs a price, it attaches the latest signed
update to its own transaction, the contract verifies it, and the price is then read. Pyth states the
shape plainly: applications need to update the on-chain price before reading
it.<Cite src="pyth-pull-updates-docs" /> The flow is a single transaction that first updates and then
performs the application's own logic.<Cite src="pyth-pull-updates-docs" />

The frequency difference is the reason the design exists. In a push oracle every feed updates at a
fixed frequency the operator chooses, typically ranging from every 10 minutes to 1
hour.<Cite src="pyth-pull-updates-docs" /> Pull oracles update far more often — every Pyth price feed
updates every 400 milliseconds.<Cite src="pyth-pull-updates-docs" />

The documentation is explicit about why the gap is so large, and the reason is economic rather than
technical: **push oracles incur gas costs for price updates, and those gas costs limit their
scalability** — across update frequency, across the number of supported chains, and across the number
of feeds that can be offered at all.<Cite src="pyth-pull-updates-docs" /> A push feed for an
illiquid pair on a quiet chain costs the operator money every heartbeat whether or not a single
contract reads it. A pull feed for the same pair costs nothing until somebody wants it.

Pyth's own architecture makes the split visible: publishers submit prices to an oracle program
running on Pythnet, that program aggregates them into a single price and confidence interval, and the
result is transferred cross-chain to reach consumers.<Cite src="pyth-how-pyth-works" /> Aggregation
happens off your chain, at the network's cadence, regardless of whether anyone is watching.

Chainlink's own pull product, Data Streams, describes the same inversion in its own vocabulary: a DON
that operates like the one behind Data Feeds, except that it **signs and delivers reports to an
aggregation network rather than delivering answers on chain directly**, which is what lets it deliver
reports more frequently for time-sensitive applications.<Cite src="chainlink-data-streams-docs" />
Clients subscribe over WebSocket for sub-second data latency and verify on chain when they need
to.<Cite src="chainlink-data-streams-docs" />

Note what this means for the mental model you built last lesson. "Push versus pull" is not a
competition between two vendors. Both major networks ship both, because they solve different
problems: cheap, always-available reads for protocols that can tolerate a documented staleness
window, and caller-supplied freshness for protocols that cannot.

## Who pays, and who is liable

Two things move to the consumer at once, and teams reliably notice the first and not the second.

**The gas.** The update is part of your transaction, so you pay for its calldata and its
verification. For a perp fill that is a rounding error against the value at stake. For a read that
happens on every interaction in a high-frequency contract it is a design constraint.

**The liveness.** In a push design, the feed being current is the operator's job. In a pull design,
the on-chain price being current is *your* job, because it is your transaction that carries the
update. If the keeper that was supposed to attach a fresh update fails, or the off-chain service is
unreachable, or your bot's private key rotated, then the price in the contract is exactly as stale as
any push feed — and unlike a push feed, nobody else is going to fix it.

## Why the update is trustworthy

This is the part that changes the security question rather than merely the plumbing.

In the push model, authority comes from *write access*: a permissioned set of operators is authorised
to update the contract, and everyone else is not.<Cite src="pyth-pull-updates-docs" /> The question a
reviewer asks is "who may call the setter".

In the pull model, submission is permissionless. Pyth says so directly: anyone can permissionlessly
update the on-chain price, and anyone can submit a price update to the on-chain contract, **which
verifies its authenticity** and stores it for later use.<Cite src="pyth-pull-updates-docs" />
Authority has moved from an address to a signature. Chainlink's Data Streams draws the same line
around a named component: a Verifier Contract checks the signature from the DON to cryptographically
guarantee that the report has not been altered from the time the DON reached consensus to the point
where the data is used.<Cite src="chainlink-data-streams-docs" />

You already know the primitive. You have recovered signers with [`ecrecover`](/c/ecrecover), and you know exactly
what a recovered address proves and what it does not: it proves that whoever holds a private key
signed *these bytes*, and nothing at all about whether those bytes are true, timely, or intended for
you. Everything that makes a pull update safe is built out of that gap.

The bytes being signed are therefore the whole design. A report commits to the feed identifier, the
value, the dispersion measure, and the publish timestamp — because each of those is something a
relayer would otherwise be free to choose. Bind the feed id and a relayer cannot deliver a cheap
asset's report where an expensive one was expected. Bind the timestamp and a relayer cannot resurrect
last week's price. Bind the value and the relayer is reduced to a courier.

So the reviewer's question changes shape entirely:

- **What is the signing set?** Not one key — a network. How many signers, what threshold, whose keys.
- **How is that set rotated?** A rotation mechanism is an upgrade path, and an upgrade path is an
  authority. Who can trigger it, and what stops an attacker who captures it from rewriting the
  signing set to one they control?
- **What publish times does the verifier accept?** This is where the interesting bugs live, and the
  next section is entirely about it.

## Timing power is a real power

Do not read that misconception as reassurance. Timing power is worth money, and the documentation
treats it as an attack vector rather than a curiosity.

Pyth's best-practices page names it **adversarial selection**: pull updates give users some ability to
select which price to use in a transaction. That ability is circumscribed — on-chain prices must move
forward in time and cannot be from too far in the past — but within those constraints a user can
still choose *any* update that satisfies them.<Cite src="pyth-best-practices" /> The documentation's
own characterisation is the one to memorise: this ability is **functionally equivalent to latency**,
because it lets a user see the price in the future before using a price from the
past.<Cite src="pyth-best-practices" />

The defence named is a staleness check, and this time you set the threshold aggressively rather than
generously. The SDK exposes `getPriceNoOlderThan()`, which returns the most recent update not older
than a specified threshold, and latency-sensitive protocols are advised to reduce that threshold to a
few seconds.<Cite src="pyth-best-practices" /> Note the inversion against the push lesson: there, your
maximum age was dictated by the feed's heartbeat and could not be tightened. Here it is genuinely
your dial, and the cost of tightening it is transaction failures when updates are not fresh enough,
not a wrong price.

Two further points from the same page are worth carrying because they are about protocol shape rather
than about oracle configuration.

**No on-chain oracle can match an off-chain source's latency**, because of the overhead added by
consensus and security — so the threat model should assume adversaries see price changes a short time
before the protocol does.<Cite src="pyth-best-practices" /> The design instruction that follows is
blunt: avoid situations where a price update must race against an adversary's transaction, because
adversaries are highly likely to win those races.<Cite src="pyth-best-practices" /> They have a head
start, they can optimise network latency, and they can pay for priority
blockspace.<Cite src="pyth-best-practices" />

**Same-block round trips are the sharpest version of this.** If a protocol allows a position to be
opened and closed within the same block, a stale or manipulated price can be exploited with flash
loans and no inventory risk: open at a favourable price, close in the same transaction, keep the
discrepancy.<Cite src="pyth-best-practices" /> The remedies named are structural — separate
commitment from execution, use delayed settlement or commit-reveal so a position cannot be opened and
closed in one block, or enforce a minimum holding period or block count between entry and
exit.<Cite src="pyth-best-practices" />

Delayed settlement deserves one more sentence because it inverts the usual instinct. Rather than
executing a trade submitted at time `t` using whatever price is current when it lands, the protocol
executes it *using the price at time `t`*, which is available only after a short
delay.<Cite src="pyth-best-practices" /> The user cannot pick their price because their price was
fixed before they knew it. Freshness is not always the objective; *unpredictability at submission
time* often is.

## The band the oracle publishes about itself

A pull network aggregating many independent publishers knows something a single number cannot carry:
how much its publishers disagree. Pyth reports it, and ignoring it throws away the most useful signal
in the feed.

The aggregation algorithm is worth knowing precisely, because it explains what the band actually
measures.<Cite src="pyth-price-aggregation" /> Each publisher submits a price and a confidence
interval reflecting its own accuracy. The algorithm then gives each publisher **three votes** — one at
their price, one at their price minus their confidence, one at their price plus it — and takes the
**median of all the votes** as the aggregate price.<Cite src="pyth-price-aggregation" /> It then
computes the distance from that aggregate to the 25th and 75th percentiles of the votes and takes the
**larger of the two** as the aggregate confidence interval.<Cite src="pyth-price-aggregation" />

Three properties fall out of that construction, and each one matters to a consumer.

**It is robust to a single outlier.** If most publishers report near $100 and one reports $80, the
aggregate stays near $100, because a median cannot be dragged by one voter.<Cite src="pyth-price-aggregation" />

**Confident publishers get more influence, but bounded influence.** A publisher reporting $101 ± 1
should count for more than one reporting $110 ± 10, and it does, because its three votes cluster
tightly.<Cite src="pyth-price-aggregation" /> The documentation describes the result as a hybrid
between a mean and a median: more weight to confident publishers, while still capping any single
publisher's maximum influence.<Cite src="pyth-price-aggregation" /> A provable consequence is that
the aggregate price always lies between the 25th and 75th percentiles of the publishers'
prices.<Cite src="pyth-price-aggregation" />

**The band widens exactly when venues genuinely disagree.** Under normal conditions an asset trades
at a similar price across exchanges and the aggregate confidence reflects the individual publishers'
confidences. When publishers report distinct prices with non-overlapping intervals, the dispersion
creates a large gap between the aggregate and the quartiles, and the band widens to
match.<Cite src="pyth-price-aggregation" />

That last property is the whole point. The band is not noise in the data. It is the oracle telling
you that the market has stopped agreeing with itself — during dislocations, thin liquidity and
crashes, which is precisely when a protocol most needs to act conservatively.

### The representation, worked

Both the price and the confidence share a fixed-point exponent, and the integer values are converted
by multiplying by 10 raised to that exponent.<Cite src="pyth-best-practices" /> The documentation's
own example: `exponent = -5`, `conf = 1500`, `price = 12276250` gives a confidence interval of
`1500 × 10^-5 = $0.015` and a price of `12276250 × 10^-5 = $122.7625`.<Cite src="pyth-best-practices" />

That is a band of roughly 1.2 basis points — publishers agreeing closely. Watch the same feed during
a venue outage and the ratio is what changes, not the price.

### Worked example: using the band instead of the midpoint

A lending market values collateral and computes a borrow limit. Work the defensive construction in
the order that makes each choice forced rather than stylistic.

**Subgoal 1 — decide which direction hurts you.** For collateral, an overstatement is the dangerous
error: too-high collateral permits a borrow that should not exist. For debt, an understatement is the
dangerous error. Those are opposite ends of the same band, so a single "use the conservative value"
rule is not enough; you need the rule per leg.

**Subgoal 2 — take the pessimistic end of the band for each leg.** Value collateral at
`price − conf`, value debt at `price + conf`. Pyth's own guidance for derivative protocols is exactly
this: use the lower bound to protect against manipulation that drives the price down, and the upper
bound to protect against manipulation that drives it up.<Cite src="pyth-best-practices" /> The
protocol is now conservative in both directions simultaneously, and automatically becomes *more*
conservative as the market becomes less certain — with no governance action and no monitoring alert.

**Subgoal 3 — decide what a wide band means, in advance.** Widening is graceful up to a point, and
past that point it means something has broken in the market rather than in the feed. Define a
threshold as a ratio, `conf / price`, not as an absolute — an absolute band that is unremarkable for
an equity is enormous for a stablecoin. Past the threshold, stop opening new risk: refuse new
borrows, refuse to increase leverage. Liquidations are a separate decision and usually should
continue, because a protocol that stops liquidating during a dislocation is choosing bad debt.

**Subgoal 4 — check that the band's cost is bounded.** Widening the effective haircut by `conf/price`
is a real cost to users, borne continuously. Compute it at a typical band and at a stressed one. If
the typical cost is material, the answer is a tighter feed or a different asset, not a decision to
ignore the band.

**Subgoal 5 — write down the failure mode you have chosen.** A protocol using the pessimistic bound
is systematically slightly stricter than the market. Users will notice, support will hear about it,
and someone will eventually propose "just use the midpoint" as a growth initiative. The reason it is
there needs to be written down at the point where the decision is reversible.

<Check type="explain" concept="orac-pull-model">
A perps venue migrates from a push feed to a pull feed to reduce latency. The new design: the trader's
transaction carries a signed update, the contract verifies it, then fills the order at the resulting
price. The team's summary is that the price is now sub-second fresh instead of minutes old, so the
oracle risk has gone down.

Say what has actually improved, what has become worse, and what has stayed the same.

<Answer>
**Improved: the maximum age of the price the contract can use.** The trader supplies the update, so
the fill no longer waits for a deviation or heartbeat trigger. For a venue whose whole product is
short-horizon exposure, this is the difference between a viable design and an unviable one.

**Worse: the trader now chooses the price, within limits.** This is adversarial selection. On-chain
prices must move forward in time and cannot be too old, but within those constraints the submitter
picks any valid update — an ability the documentation calls functionally equivalent to latency,
because it lets them see the future price before using a past
one.<Cite src="pyth-best-practices" /> The push feed had no such affordance: whatever was on chain
was on chain for everyone.

**Worse: the contract now races the adversary.** The design instruction is to avoid situations where
a price update must race against an adversary's transaction, because adversaries win those races —
head start, optimised latency, and willingness to pay for priority
blockspace.<Cite src="pyth-best-practices" /> A fill priced by whichever update lands first is that
race.

**Worse, and the sharpest: same-block round trips are now cheap.** If a position can be opened and
closed in one block, a favourable update plus a flash loan is a risk-free extraction with no
inventory exposure.<Cite src="pyth-best-practices" /> The push design's staleness partly obscured
this; the pull design does not.

**Worse: liveness moved onto the venue.** No update attached means no price. That is now an
operational dependency with a pager attached to it.

**Unchanged: who decides the number.** The signing network is the same class of trust boundary it was
before. Permissionless submission does not mean permissionless
pricing;<Cite src="pyth-pull-updates-docs" /> the value is still whatever the publishers agreed,
aggregated by the network's own algorithm.<Cite src="pyth-price-aggregation" />

**Unchanged: the need for bounds.** Nothing about a signature makes a value sane. The same
non-positive-answer, plausibility and divergence checks apply.

**What the team should ship alongside the migration.** A tight `maxAge` rather than a generous
one;<Cite src="pyth-best-practices" /> a structural separation of commitment from execution — delayed
settlement, commit-reveal, or a minimum holding period — so the same-block round trip is
impossible;<Cite src="pyth-best-practices" /> and use of the confidence band rather than the midpoint,
so the venue tightens automatically when publishers disagree.<Cite src="pyth-price-aggregation" />
Latency went down. Oracle risk changed shape, and one of its new shapes is worse than the one it
replaced.
</Answer>

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson** goes back to the cheapest possible price source — reading a pool directly — and
shows why it is not an oracle at all, in a way that makes the previous two lessons' careful
machinery look like the bargain it is.

**The manipulation-cost lesson** applies to both delivery models. Whether a signed report or a
written round carries the number, somebody upstream watched a market, and that market has a price to
move.

**The hardening lesson** takes the checks from both lessons and composes them, including the question
this one deliberately left open: what your contract does when the primary and the fallback disagree.

**Choosing between the two models, tomorrow.** Ask one question first: can this product tolerate
being wrong for the feed's published window? If yes, push, and spend the saved complexity elsewhere.
If no, pull — and budget for the keeper, the staleness threshold, and the structural change that
stops a trader opening and closing inside one block.
