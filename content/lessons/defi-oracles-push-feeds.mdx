A support ticket, roughly once a quarter, at every protocol that consumes a professional feed. A user
writes in: *your app says my collateral is worth $2,940 but the exchange says $2,760, and it has been
wrong for forty minutes.* Somebody checks the aggregator, finds the last round was written 41 minutes
ago at $2,940, and escalates it as an oracle outage.

It is not an outage. It is the feed doing exactly what its configuration says it will do, and the
forty minutes are a published number that somebody on the integrating team was supposed to read
before shipping. This lesson is about that number: where it comes from, how to compute how wrong you
have agreed to be, and the one check that separates a correct consumer from the most common
integration bug in DeFi.

## The push model, stated precisely

In a push design an off-chain network of node operators collects prices from data providers, reaches
consensus on a single value, and submits it on chain — on its own schedule, paying its own
gas.<Cite src="pyth-pull-updates-docs" /> The contract stores the current price. A permissioned set of
operators is authorised to update it.<Cite src="pyth-pull-updates-docs" /> Consumers do not
participate: they call a `view` function and read what is there.

Three consequences follow immediately, and they are the whole trade.

**Reads are cheap and synchronous.** No coordination, no keeper, no extra transaction. A consumer
contract does a `STATICCALL` into a proxy and gets a tuple back. In gas terms that is a cold account
access — 2,600 on this machine's configuration — plus the aggregator's own cold storage reads at
2,100 each.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /> Cheap enough that a lending market can price every
asset in a position on every interaction without thinking about it.

**Reads are available to everyone, including people you have never heard of.** The feed is a public
good with no allowlist. This is a feature, and it is also why "who consumes this feed" is not a
question the feed operator can answer.

**The value's age is entirely outside your control.** Between updates the contract holds a number
that is, by design, no longer the market price. Not stale in the sense of broken — stale in the sense
of *finished being current*, on a schedule that was published in advance and that your contract has
no ability to influence.

<Aside kind="note">
Chainlink's Data Feeds are the canonical push implementation and the one this lesson works
against, because the mechanics are documented in enough detail to be taught precisely. The proxy
pattern matters for a practical reason: you point your contract at a proxy, the proxy points at an
aggregator, and the aggregator can be upgraded underneath you without your address
changing.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" /> Reading the aggregator directly works and is
explicitly not the recommended integration.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" />
</Aside>

## What the interface actually returns

`latestRoundData()` returns five values, and the number of integration bugs caused by ignoring four of
them is difficult to overstate.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-api-reference" />

```solidity
function latestRoundData() external view
    returns (
        uint80 roundId,
        int256 answer,
        uint256 startedAt,
        uint256 updatedAt,
        uint80 answeredInRound
    );
```

- **`roundId`** — the round identifier. Rounds are sequential; a specific historical round can be
  fetched with `getRoundData(roundId)`.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-api-reference" />
- **`answer`** — the data the feed provides. Depending on the feed this is an asset price, a reserve
  value, or another data type entirely.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-api-reference" /> It is
  `int256`, signed, which is a hint that non-positive values are representable and therefore must be
  handled.
- **`startedAt`** — when the round started.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-api-reference" />
- **`updatedAt`** — when the round was updated. **This is the field the whole lesson turns
  on.**<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-api-reference" />
- **`answeredInRound`** — deprecated. It was used when answers could take multiple rounds to
  compute.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-api-reference" /> Integration guides written before the
  deprecation compare it against `roundId`; that comparison is now
  vestigial.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-api-reference" />

Notice what is *not* returned: any statement about whether the answer is fresh enough for you. The
struct hands you a timestamp precisely because the decision is yours. Returning a value is not an
assertion of currency.

## Two triggers, and only two

A push feed does not stream. It writes a new round when one of exactly two conditions fires, and it
is worth being precise about both because most incident post-mortems in this area are really
post-mortems about a misread parameter.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" />

**Deviation threshold.** Node operators monitor the real-world value off chain. When it deviates from
the last on-chain answer by more than a configured interval, all the nodes update.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" />

**Heartbeat.** If the value stays inside the deviation band, an update fires anyway once the
configured idle interval has elapsed.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" />

Whichever comes first. The two values are per-feed and per-chain, and they are published — on the
feed's own address listing and on the data explorer.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" /> They are
not folklore; they are a configuration you are expected to look up before choosing the feed.

Two documented details that catch teams out. First, when a node detects the heartbeat has been
reached it *initiates* the round; depending on congestion and network conditions there can be a
further delay before that round lands on chain.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" /> The heartbeat
is therefore a lower bound on how current the answer is guaranteed to be, not an exact one. Second,
feeds for wrapped or liquid-staking assets can carry different heartbeat and deviation thresholds
than the underlying asset, and the same asset can be configured differently on different
chains.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" /> Combining several feeds — even ones sharing a
denominator — compounds their margins of error, and the documentation says so
directly.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" />

## Worked example: how wrong have you agreed to be?

The question a designer must answer is not "is this feed good" but "what is the largest error this
feed can present to my contract without anything malfunctioning". That has a derivation. Work it in
the order that produces a bound.

**Subgoal 1 — establish the error the deviation threshold permits at any instant.** Call the
threshold `d`. Between updates the on-chain answer may differ from the off-chain aggregate by up to
`d` without triggering anything. So at an arbitrary moment, with no market stress and nothing wrong,
your contract's price can be off by `d` relative to what the operators themselves observe. For a feed
configured at 0.5%, a $3,000 asset can legitimately be reported anywhere in a $15 band. That is the
*floor* of your error, present always.

**Subgoal 2 — establish the error the heartbeat permits.** Now suppose the market moves. The moment
the aggregate crosses `d`, an update is triggered — so under normal operation the deviation trigger
bounds the error and the heartbeat never fires. The heartbeat only becomes your binding constraint
when the deviation trigger *cannot* fire: when nodes are unable to transmit, when the chain is
congested, when the feed is degraded. In that case your error grows with the market's actual move
until the heartbeat forces a write, and that can be several hours on feeds configured for consumers
who do not need freshness.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" />

**Subgoal 3 — convert both into your own units.** Neither number means anything until it is expressed
as protocol damage. Take the gap between your
[loan-to-value cap and your liquidation threshold](/c/lend-ltv-vs-liquidation-threshold) — the
buffer you deliberately built. Ask what fraction of it a `d`-sized error consumes. If your buffer is
5 percentage points and your feed's deviation threshold is 2%, you have already spent 40% of the
buffer on the oracle before the market has done anything.

**Subgoal 4 — check the tail, not the mean.** The relevant scenario is not a typical hour. It is the
hour in which the asset gaps 30% and the chain is congested and gas is high — the same hour in which
your liquidators are also struggling. Those correlate, and the correlation is the point: your feed is
most likely to be at its maximum permitted error exactly when your protocol is least able to absorb
it.

**Subgoal 5 — state the decision as an inequality, and accept it explicitly.** Your protocol's
tolerance for being wrong must be at least as wide as the window the feed publishes. If it is not,
the answer is not "add monitoring" — the answer is that this feed is the wrong feed for this
position, and something with a tighter configuration, a different delivery model, or a smaller
exposure cap is required.

The output of this exercise is a written sentence: *this market can be wrong by up to X% for up to Y
minutes without anything being broken, and we have sized the buffer accordingly.* If a team cannot
produce that sentence, they have not chosen a feed; they have adopted one.

### The shape this produces

It helps to hold the picture. Draw the off-chain aggregate as a smooth line. Draw the on-chain answer
as a step function that only changes when a trigger fires. During a quiet stretch the step sits flat
while the smooth line wanders inside the deviation band; the gap between them is small and
oscillating, and the heartbeat eventually forces a step that barely moves anything. During a genuine
move the smooth line runs away and the steps chase it, each one triggered by the deviation crossing,
each landing a block or two after the crossing that caused it.

The area between the two lines is the region in which your protocol is wrong. It is smallest exactly
when it does not matter, and largest exactly when it does — during fast moves, and during whatever
conditions delay transmission. That asymmetry is not a defect of the design. It is the design: a push
feed spends gas in proportion to how much the price has moved, which is the only way a public good
priced in blockspace can work at all.

### Two details about rounds worth knowing before you build on them

Historical data is available: `getRoundData(roundId)` returns a past round, which is how a protocol
reconstructs what it believed at some earlier moment.<Cite src="chainlink-historical-data" /> Two
properties make naive use of it wrong.

First, `roundId` means different things at the proxy and at the aggregator, because the proxy
abstracts an aggregator that can be replaced — which also means historical data can be spread across
different aggregators.<Cite src="chainlink-historical-data" /> Code that walks round identifiers
backwards across an aggregator upgrade is not reading the history it thinks it is.

Second, the identifier increases with each new round, but **the increase may not be
monotonic**.<Cite src="chainlink-historical-data" /> Any loop that assumes `roundId - 1` exists, or
that decrements until it finds a round old enough, is built on an assumption the documentation
declines to make.

## The staleness check

Everything above collapses into a handful of lines that a correct consumer executes on every read.
Chainlink's own guidance is unambiguous: track `updatedAt`, decide whether it is recent enough, and if
it is not, **pause operation or switch to an alternate mode** while you identify the
cause.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" />

```solidity
function _price(AggregatorV3Interface feed, uint256 maxAge)
    internal view returns (uint256)
{
    (, int256 answer, , uint256 updatedAt, ) = feed.latestRoundData();
    if (answer <= 0) revert BadAnswer();
    if (updatedAt == 0) revert NoRound();
    if (block.timestamp - updatedAt > maxAge) revert StalePrice();
    return uint256(answer);
}
```

Four things are happening, and each corresponds to documented behaviour rather than to defensive
paranoia:

**Non-positive answers.** The return type is signed. A zero or negative answer must not flow into a
division or a collateral valuation.

**A round that does not exist.** An `updatedAt` of zero means there is no round to read.

**Age against a maximum you chose.** Not a maximum the feed chose — it does not have one for you. The
value should align with the feed's documented heartbeat interval, usually the heartbeat plus a small
buffer, and explicitly not an arbitrary constant.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" />

**Reverting rather than continuing.** A revert is a decision. Its consequence is a liveness
dependency: when the feed is stale, your protocol stops. That is a real cost — borrowers cannot
repay, liquidators cannot liquidate — and it must be chosen deliberately rather than inherited from a
snippet. The alternative Chainlink names is switching to an alternate operation
mode.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" />

### Three more failure modes the check does not cover

**The feed can be deprecated.** Routine maintenance on feeds, including decommissioning, happens on
an ad-hoc basis, and users may have to take action to maintain continuity — with notifications going
only to operators who registered contact details.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" /> A protocol
whose oracle address is immutable and whose team has not registered is one decommissioning away from
a permanent outage.

**Bounds are your job now.** Aggregators carry `minAnswer` and `maxAnswer` values, and the current
documentation states plainly that on most data feeds **these are no longer used and do not stop your
application from reading the most recent answer** — you must decide what limits are acceptable and
implement your own circuit breaker.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-api-reference" /><Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" />
Guidance that tells you to compare the answer against the aggregator's own floor and ceiling is
describing a mechanism that is no longer there. The next lessons build the replacement.

**On an L2, "the price is stale" and "the chain is broken" are different problems.** If a rollup's
sequencer is unavailable, users lose the standard read/write path while the L1 contracts remain
reachable by anyone technical enough — which is exactly the asymmetry that distorts liquidations. The
documented pattern is a separate uptime feed whose `answer` is 0 for up and 1 for down, plus a grace
period measured from `startedAt` after it comes back, before you resume trusting the price
feed.<Cite src="chainlink-l2-sequencer-feeds" /> Note also that these feeds are no longer being
expanded to additional networks, so their availability is a per-chain question rather than a
given.<Cite src="chainlink-l2-sequencer-feeds" />

<Aside kind="warn">
Feeds for assets with real-world market hours do not have heartbeats while their market is closed:
the contract stays callable and returns the last value published before the close, with a timestamp
that may predate the close by minutes or hours.<Cite src="chainlink-selecting-data-feeds" /> A
staleness check tuned to a crypto feed's heartbeat will either reject every weekend or accept
arbitrarily old closes, depending on which way it was written. Neither is what anyone intended.
</Aside>

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="orac-staleness-check">
A team ships this, having read that the feed's heartbeat is 3,600 seconds. The market is a lending
market on a major L2, collateral is a liquid-staking token, and `MAX_AGE` is a constant.

```solidity
uint256 constant MAX_AGE = 3600;

function collateralUsd(uint256 amount) public view returns (uint256) {
    (uint80 roundId, int256 answer, , uint256 updatedAt, uint80 answeredInRound)
        = lstUsdFeed.latestRoundData();
    require(answeredInRound >= roundId, "stale round");
    require(block.timestamp - updatedAt <= MAX_AGE, "stale price");
    return amount * uint256(answer) / 1e8;
}
```

Name every defect, and rank them by what each one costs.

<Answer>
**Defect one, and the expensive one: `answer` is not checked for sign, and the cast hides it.**
`uint256(answer)` on a negative `int256` produces an enormous positive number. Collateral is then
valued at roughly 10^77 dollars, every borrow is permitted, and the protocol is drained in one
transaction. The `require` above it does not help. This is the single highest-severity line in the
snippet.

**Defect two: the heartbeat belongs to the wrong feed.** `MAX_AGE` was taken from a general figure,
but this is an LST feed, and feeds for wrapped or liquid-staking assets can carry different heartbeat
and deviation thresholds than the underlying — and can differ again by
chain.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" /> The value must be read from *this* feed's listing on
*this* network, and set to the heartbeat plus a small buffer rather than the heartbeat
exactly.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" /> Exactly equal will reject legitimate rounds that were
initiated on time and landed a few seconds late under congestion.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" />

**Defect three: `answeredInRound >= roundId` is vestigial.** The field is deprecated; it existed for
a mechanism where answers could span rounds.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-api-reference" /> The
check is not harmful, but it is doing no work, and its presence makes a reviewer believe staleness
has been handled more thoroughly than it has.

**Defect four: no sequencer-uptime condition, on an L2.** After a sequencer outage the feed's
`updatedAt` can be fresh while users have been unable to transact, and the correct behaviour is to
refuse to price until a grace period measured from the uptime feed's `startedAt` has
elapsed.<Cite src="chainlink-l2-sequencer-feeds" /> Without it, the first block after recovery
liquidates everyone who could not reach the chain.

**Defect five: `1e8` is hard-coded.** The scale is a property of the feed, exposed through
`decimals()`, not a constant of nature.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-api-reference" /> If this
address is ever repointed at a feed with a different scale, or the code is copied to a chain where it
differs, every valuation is wrong by orders of magnitude and nothing reverts.

**Defect six, structural rather than local: there is no bound on `answer`.** The aggregator's own
`minAnswer`/`maxAnswer` are no longer used on most feeds and will not stop a wild
value.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-api-reference" /> A protocol that wants a circuit breaker has to
implement it.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" />

**Ranking.** One is a total loss in a single transaction. Four is a mass-liquidation event with real
user harm. Five is a total loss but only on a migration. Two and six are the difference between
losing a bounded amount and an unbounded amount during a genuine dislocation. Three costs nothing but
misleads the next reviewer.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where the division of labour actually sits

It is tempting to read all of the above as a list of chores the feed operator has offloaded. The
operator's own documentation is more precise than that, and the distinction it draws is worth
adopting as a review framework.

It splits the risk in two.<Cite src="chainlink-developer-responsibilities" /> **Market integrity
risks** are external market conditions affecting price behaviour and data quality in unanticipated
ways — spoofing, ramping, wash trading, cross-market manipulation, and the general vulnerability of
thinly traded assets. The operator states that developers are solely responsible for monitoring and
mitigating these.<Cite src="chainlink-developer-responsibilities" /> **Application code risks** are
the quality, reliability and dependencies of the consuming code, and developers are named as solely
responsible for instituting the risk-mitigation processes — data quality checks, circuit breakers and
contingency logic for their use case.<Cite src="chainlink-developer-responsibilities" />

Read that as an architecture statement rather than as a disclaimer. A feed operator can tell you what
its nodes observed and how they agreed; it cannot tell you whether the market they observed was real,
and it cannot know what your protocol does with the answer. Those are the two halves that stay with
you, and every control in the rest of this module lands in one of them.

The practical shape of that division for a lending market: the feed is responsible for *reporting the
market*, you are responsible for *deciding whether the market is worth believing* and for *bounding
what happens if it is not*. A team that outsources the first and assumes it has outsourced the other
two has an oracle strategy consisting entirely of a contract address.

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson** takes the other delivery model, where the freshness problem is solved by moving
the update into your own transaction — and where the liability moves with it.

**The manipulation-cost lessons** will let you price the residual risk this lesson leaves open. A
deviation threshold bounds how wrong the feed is relative to the market; it says nothing about what
it costs to move the market the feed is watching.<Cite src="arxiv-manipulation-cost-2026" />

**The hardening lesson** builds the circuit breaker that `minAnswer`/`maxAnswer` no longer
provide,<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-api-reference" /> and settles the question of what to do when
your primary and secondary sources disagree.

**Reviewing an integration tomorrow.** Find every `latestRoundData` call in the codebase and check
four things: sign, age against a per-feed constant, scale read rather than assumed, and — on an L2 —
sequencer uptime. That grep, run honestly, finds real findings in most codebases that have one.
