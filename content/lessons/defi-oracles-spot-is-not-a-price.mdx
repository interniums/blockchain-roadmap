Three lines, no dependencies, no keeper, no subscription, no trust in anybody's node operators. It
reads live on-chain state and it is the cheapest price source in existence:

```solidity
function getPrice() public view returns (uint256) {
    (uint112 r0, uint112 r1, ) = pair.getReserves();
    return uint256(r1) * 1e18 / uint256(r0);
}
```

Every argument for it is true. It is fully on-chain. It is permissionless. It has no operator, no
heartbeat, no deprecation risk, no address that can be repointed. It costs a couple of thousand gas.
And a protocol that values collateral with it can be emptied by a caller who chooses what the
function returns.

This lesson is about why that is not a bug in the AMM, why depth does not fix it, and how to compute
the actual number — because the number is small, and the smallness is the lesson.

## What the reserve ratio actually is

You know the [constant-product curve](/c/constant-product) and you know
[price impact](/c/price-impact). Put them together and the statement is precise: the reserve ratio is
the price of an *infinitesimal* trade. It is the marginal price — the slope of the curve at the
current point — and it is the one price at which no meaningful quantity can actually be transacted.

The AMM promises exactly this and advertises no more. Its whole design is that trading moves the
price, because that is the mechanism by which it never runs out of inventory. A reserve ratio is a
report of where the last trade left the curve. Reading it and calling it "the price of the asset" is
a category error the pool never invited: it is a description of the pool's internal state, not a
measurement of the world.

So the defect is not the AMM's. It belongs entirely to the consumer, for treating a state variable
that any caller may write as though it were an observation that some party had committed to.

This applies to every live-pool read, not to `getReserves` specifically. A concentrated-liquidity
pool's stored square-root price is the same object with more precision and worse depth
characteristics; a stable-swap pool's spot quote is the same object on a flatter curve. Anything that
reports the current marginal price of a venue you can trade against is the caller's choice.

## Worked example: what it costs to choose the answer

Here is the derivation, in the order that produces a number you can defend. The subgoals name what
each step is *for*, because that is what makes the method transferable to a pool you have not seen.

**Subgoal 1 — express the target as a price, not as a vibe.** The attacker does not want to
"manipulate the oracle". They want the function above to return some specific `q`, chosen because
`q` is the value at which the consuming protocol does something profitable — permits a borrow, values
a mint, skips a liquidation. Derive `q` from the consumer's own parameters, not from imagination.

**Subgoal 2 — find the swap that produces `q`.** Let the pool hold `x` of the quote asset and `y` of
the base asset, with `x · y = k` and current price `p = x / y`. Swapping `Δx` in and receiving `Δy`
out preserves the product, so the amount out is

```text
Δy = y · Δx / (x + Δx)
```

and the price after the swap is `(x + Δx) / (y − Δy)`. Substituting and simplifying — the algebra is
worked line by line in Euler's cost-of-attack note — collapses to `q = (x + Δx)² / k`, which
rearranges to<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" />

```text
Δx = √(k · q) − x
```

That is the entire capital requirement for an upward manipulation. Downward is the mirror image:
`Δy = √(k / q) − y`.<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" />

**Subgoal 3 — put real numbers in it.** Take a pool holding 50,000 USDC and 50,000 DAI, so `k` is
2.5 × 10⁹ and `p` is 1. Suppose the attacker wants `q = 4` — a quadrupling, far more than any
collateral factor requires:

```text
Δx = √(2.5e9 × 4) − 50,000
   = √(1e10) − 50,000
   = 100,000 − 50,000
   = 50,000 USDC
```

Fifty thousand dollars quadruples the reported price. Check it: after the swap the pool holds 100,000
USDC and `50,000 − (50,000 × 50,000 / 100,000)` = 25,000 DAI, and `100,000 / 25,000` is 4.

**Subgoal 4 — separate the capital from the cost.** Fifty thousand dollars is what must be *supplied*,
not what is *lost*. This is the distinction the whole module turns on, and it is where most
discussion of oracle security goes wrong. The capital comes back. Swap the 25,000 DAI back into the
pool: at zero fee you receive `100,000 × 25,000 / 50,000` = 50,000 USDC — exactly what you put in.
The round trip is free, because the curve is symmetric and you traded against yourself.

**Subgoal 5 — add the only thing that does not come back.** Fees. Redo both legs charging a 0.30%
fee on each input. Leg one: 50,000 in, 49,850 effective, out is `50,000 × 49,850 / 99,850` =
24,962.44 DAI. Leg two: 24,962.44 in, 24,887.55 effective, out is
`100,000 × 24,887.55 / 49,925.11` = 49,849.78 USDC.

The attacker put in 50,000 and got back 49,849.78. **The cost of quadrupling this pool's reported
price and putting it back was $150.22** — about one fee tier applied once to the notional, because
the fee retained on the return leg is denominated in the asset the attacker acquired cheaply. Repeat
the arithmetic at a 0.05% tier and the cost is $25.01; at 1%, $502.50.

**Subgoal 6 — remove the capital requirement entirely.** The $50,000 is needed only for the duration
of a transaction, and there is a primitive that supplies arbitrary capital for the duration of a
transaction. A [flash loan](/c/flash-loan) reduces the capital constraint to a fee — a few basis
points at most — leaving the $150 of swap fees, the flash fee, and gas.

**Subgoal 7 — compare against the prize.** The attacker borrows against collateral valued at 4× on a
protocol that read `getPrice()`. Whatever that market's borrow cap is, they take it. Against a cost
of roughly two hundred dollars.

That is the whole attack, and it has no clever step in it.

## Why "use a deep pool" is not the fix

The reflex response is that the example pool is small. Make it a real one — tens of millions of
dollars — and the numbers change. They do change, and the direction is worth stating precisely
because it is the direction that matters.

`Δx = √(k · q) − x` scales with pool size: double every reserve and `Δx` doubles. Since the cost is
approximately the fee tier times `Δx`, **cost scales linearly with depth**. Uniswap's own
documentation makes the same scaling claim for the time-weighted construction: the cost of
manipulation increases approximately linearly with liquidity.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-oracles-docs" />

So depth raises the price. It does not change the shape of the transaction. The attacker still moves
the price, still acts on it, still restores it, still does the whole thing inside one call frame, and
still faces no risk at all — because if any step fails, the transaction reverts and nothing
happened. Depth converts a $200 attack into a $200,000 attack. If the prize is $40M, you have made
the attacker's margin slightly worse.

The 2026 formalisation of AMM-based oracles makes the same point from the other end. It defines
manipulation cost as the minimal mark-to-market *loss* the attacker must incur to move the reported
price by a given factor.<Cite src="arxiv-manipulation-cost-2026" /> For an atomic round trip against
a single pool that loss is fees, and fees alone. The framework's more interesting results are about
what happens when arbitrageurs get a chance to trade against the distortion — and inside one
transaction, they never do.

## The property that is actually missing

Name it precisely, because the naming is what generalises to sources that do not look like pools at
all.

A spot read has no **elapsed time** in it. It reports a state that one transaction produced and that
the next transaction can undo. Nothing about it requires the attacker to expose their position to
anyone else, and therefore nothing about it lets anyone else charge them for it.

Uniswap's v2 documentation constructs the fix directly from this observation. Every pair measures the
market price at the beginning of each block, *before any trades take place*, which makes that price
expensive to manipulate because it was set by the last transaction in a previous
block.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-oracles-docs" /> To set that measured price out of line with the global
market, an attacker has to make a bad trade at the end of a previous block, typically with no
guarantee that they can arbitrage it back — so attackers lose money to arbitrageurs unless they can
"selfishly" mine two blocks in a row.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-oracles-docs" />

That parenthetical about two blocks in a row is not a footnote. It is the load-bearing assumption of
the entire construction. The security of a time-weighted price comes from arbitrageurs rebalancing
the pool, so anything that lets an attacker hide the distortion from them removes the
defence<Cite src="chainsecurity-multiblock-mev" /> — and proof of stake is exactly such a thing. Two
lessons from now that becomes the subject.

The mechanism itself is visible in the pair contract. `_update` runs on every trade, and when
`timeElapsed` since the last update is non-zero it adds the price, weighted by the elapsed seconds,
into two cumulative variables.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-core-pair" /> The `timeElapsed > 0` guard is what
makes it a once-per-block accumulation rather than a once-per-trade one: within a block, the second
trade contributes nothing.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-core-pair" />

<Aside kind="note">
There is a legitimate use for a live pool read, and it is worth separating from the illegitimate one.
Reading a pool to decide *how much you can trade* — sizing a swap, computing an expected output,
checking that a route exists — is reading the pool for what it is. Reading a pool to decide *what
something is worth* is the error. The tell is whether the number ends up on the left-hand side of a
solvency comparison.
</Aside>

<Check type="predict" concept="orac-spot-price-is-not-an-oracle">
A team accepts that `getReserves()` is manipulable and proposes a hardened version. Their pricing
function reads reserves from **three separate pools** for the same pair on three different DEXes,
sorts the three implied prices, and returns the median. Their argument: an attacker must now move two
pools, not one, and a median cannot be dragged by a single outlier.

Predict what an attacker does, and say what — if anything — the change bought.

<Answer>
**The attacker moves the two cheapest pools, in the same transaction, with the same flash loan.**
Nothing about the median forces the two manipulations to be separated in time, so the atomicity that
made the single-pool attack risk-free is entirely intact. The transaction becomes: borrow, swap pool
A, swap pool B, call the victim, swap back B, swap back A, repay.

**The cost is the sum of the two cheapest pools' costs, not the deepest one's.** Compute it: for each
pool, `Δx = √(kᵢ · q) − xᵢ`, and the cost is roughly its fee tier times that
`Δx`.<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" /> Sort by that number, take the two smallest, add them.
The median construction hands the attacker the *thin* venues as the cheapest path, and the deep venue
is never touched.

**So what did it buy?** A constant factor, and the factor is set by the thinnest venues rather than
the deepest. That is the precise reason the 2026 result on aggregation matters: liquidity weights
maximise the minimum cost of manipulation for weighted medians at any distortion level, while
equal weighting — which is what an unweighted median of three quotes is — leaves the cheapest path
open.<Cite src="arxiv-manipulation-cost-2026" /> An equal-weight median across venues of very
different depth is a demonstrably weaker design than it looks.

**It may also have bought a new problem.** Three DEXes means three integrations, three sets of pool
addresses that can be repointed by governance, three liquidity profiles that migrate without warning,
and three contracts whose read paths may be observable mid-update. More sources is not more
independence; it is more surface, unless the sources are genuinely uncorrelated *and* weighted by
something that reflects the cost of attacking each.

**What would have actually helped.** Removing atomicity, which is what a time-weighted construction
does — it forces the attacker to hold the distortion across at least one block boundary and expose it
to arbitrageurs.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-oracles-docs" /> That converts a fee-only cost into a
mark-to-market loss, and the size of that loss is the next two lessons.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson** builds the time-weighted construction properly: what the accumulator stores, how
you difference it, and why every second of window length is a second of staleness you have also
bought.

**The lesson after that** prices the attack on that construction — with the same
`Δx = √(k · q) − x` you just derived, applied per block — and then shows what proof of stake did to
the arbitrageur term that made the whole argument work.

**The 2026 surface lesson** applies today's reasoning to sources that do not look like pools: vault
conversion rates, staking exchange rates, LP virtual prices. Same question, harder to notice: can a
caller move this inside one transaction?

**Reviewing code tomorrow.** Grep for `getReserves`, `slot0`, `get_dy`, `balanceOf` on a pool, and
`totalSupply` on anything. For each hit, ask whether the value ends up in a solvency comparison. If it
does, and the state it reads can be written by the caller in the same transaction, you have found the
finding — and unlike most findings, this one has a dollar figure attached that you can now
compute.
