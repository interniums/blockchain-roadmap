Watch a block during a sharp move. Transaction index 3 is an oracle transmission: a new round, a
lower answer. Transaction index 4 is a liquidation bundle that seizes eleven positions across two
markets and collects the discount on each.

Nothing was front-run. No user was sandwiched. No borrower did anything at index 4 — they had been
doing nothing for hours. The entire value at index 4 was created by index 3, which is to say by the
oracle's own write, and it left the protocol permanently.

This lesson is about that adjacency: why the slot immediately after a price update is worth money,
why the money is the protocol's rather than a trader's, and what it means to treat the write as an
auctionable right.

## What creates the value

The mechanism has three steps and no cleverness.

**Step one: nothing is liquidatable until the price says so.** Between oracle updates a position's
on-chain health does not change from price movement — only from interest accrual and user actions.
The market can fall 15% and the contract's view of the borrower is unchanged until a round lands.

**Step two: the update changes many positions at once.** A single write reprices every position
denominated in that asset, so a deviation trigger after a fast move takes the liquidatable set from
empty to large in one transaction.

**Step three: the first transaction after the update collects.** Lending protocols pay a
[fixed discount](/c/liq-fixed-discount) to whoever performs the liquidation — a hefty liquidation
fee, some of which goes to the liquidator, which is where the MEV opportunity
comes in.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> Whoever lands immediately after the update takes it.

Chainlink's documentation names this class precisely. **Oracle Extractable Value** is a subset of MEV
created during the transmission of oracle reports on chain and their consumption by on-chain
applications: when a report reveals that a collateralised position is eligible for liquidation,
searchers compete to place a liquidation transaction immediately after the oracle update — known as
**backrunning** — and because oracles supply those updates, the resulting liquidation MEV is
inherently oracle-related.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" />

## Why this is not front-running

The instinct is to file this under front-running, and the filing is wrong in a way that changes what
you do about it. Front-running requires a victim transaction to run in front of; sandwiching requires
a swap to place orders around. Both extract value from a *user's* pending action, and both are
mitigated by hiding that action — private orderflow, encrypted mempools, intent-based settlement.

**Here there is no user transaction.** The borrower is not transacting. The value comes into existence
when the oracle writes, and it is a transfer the protocol's own rules authorise: a discount it chose,
paid to whoever shows up. Hiding user orderflow does nothing, because none is involved.

The documentation makes the same distinction from the other direction, calling liquidation
backrunning **non-toxic** MEV and contrasting it with harmful forms such as front-running and
sandwich attacks.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> "Non-toxic" here means the extraction does not
degrade anyone's execution — the liquidation is a service the protocol wants performed. The problem
is not that it happens. The problem is where the surplus goes.

## The arithmetic of the surplus

Make it concrete, because the number is what motivates the redesign.

**Subgoal 1 — size the prize.** An update makes debt `D` seizable at a discount `b`. The gross prize
is `b · D`. For a fixed discount this is known exactly, in advance, by anyone who can read the
protocol's state and the incoming report.

**Subgoal 2 — establish the competitive equilibrium.** [Searchers](/c/mev-searcher) compete for the
position directly after the update. Standard MEV structure: specialised searchers identify opportunities such as
liquidation or arbitrage and bid for optimal inclusion in the block via a competitive
auction.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> A rational searcher bids up to `b · D` minus gas, minus
whatever they charge for execution risk. Competition drives the bid toward that ceiling.

**Subgoal 3 — follow where the bid goes.** The value is captured by the participants in the
block-building process — searchers, builders, and validators.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> The
winning searcher keeps a thin margin; most of `b · D` flows through the builder to the proposer.

**Subgoal 4 — account for the two parties who are not in that list.** The **protocol** defined the
discount, created the opportunity by consuming the update, and receives nothing. The **borrower** paid
`b · D` and received nothing for it. Between them they funded the entire auction and neither was a
bidder.

**Subgoal 5 — notice that the discount was never competed down.** A fixed discount is not a
market-clearing price for the liquidation service; it is a constant chosen high enough that
liquidators show up on the protocol's worst expected day. On an ordinary day the service is worth far
less, and the difference is surplus the ordering auction hands to whoever wins the block position.

That is the reframe. **The oracle's write is an auctionable right**, and until someone auctions it,
the auction happens anyway, in the block-building market, with the proceeds going to parties who
contributed nothing but sequencing.

## Recapture, and what it costs

Chainlink Smart Value Recapture is the productised answer, worth studying as a design because the
shape generalises. It extends standard price feeds with an **optional private transmission flow**: by
sending updates through a **dual aggregator** architecture it enables an auction for the opportunity
to backrun liquidations, so the protocol and the oracle network share the payment a searcher offers
for that right instead of letting it leak entirely to third parties.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" />

The mechanics, as documented:<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" />

**Dual transmission.** The oracle network, triggered by heartbeat or deviation as normal, sends the
price report to a **standard aggregator** through the public mempool — the traditional flow — and
simultaneously sends **the same report** to an **SVR aggregator** through a private channel.

**Auction.** In the private channel, searchers see the incoming report and bid to backrun the update
with a liquidation transaction. Builders select the highest bid and bundle the liquidation into the
same block. If no bid is placed, the update can still be published without a bundled liquidation.

**Execution.** The winning bundle is published, the SVR feed is updated, and in the same block the
backrunning transaction uses the freshly updated price to liquidate. If the private route fails or
times out, the feed automatically reverts to the standard price after a **configurable delay**, so it
does not stall.

**Integration.** SVR feeds keep the same aggregator interface and data structure as standard feeds,
so a protocol reads them with ordinary code at a different address — and can opt out at any time by
reading the "Standard" proxy that complements every SVR proxy.

Two constraints are worth stating because they bound what this can be used for. The mechanism is
purpose-built for liquidation-related OEV via backrunning and **cannot** be used for harmful forms of
MEV such as front-running or sandwich attacks.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> And recaptured
revenue is split between integrating protocols and the oracle network **at a standard rate** — the
documentation states the existence of the split without publishing the
percentages.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> Any specific figure you encounter for that split is
secondary reporting; do not put it in a model without a first-party source.

<Aside kind="warn">
Recapture is not free of trust, and the honest reading is that it adds a component rather than
removing one. A protocol reading an SVR feed depends on a private transmission route, an auction
running correctly, and a fallback delay whose duration is a configuration
parameter.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> During that delay the SVR feed's answer can trail the
standard feed's, which means the staleness discipline from earlier in this module applies with a new
term in it: your `maxAge` must accommodate the fallback delay, or your protocol will revert on
exactly the path the fallback exists to protect. Read the delay as configured for your feed, and
treat "which proxy are we pointed at" as a reviewable fact rather than an address detail.
</Aside>

Deployment status changes faster than any curriculum can track. As documented at the time of writing,
SVR runs multiple orderflow auctions in parallel on Ethereum mainnet, is available on Base, Arbitrum
and BNB Chain, and may be activated by default for a set of major feeds on Base — with an opt-out
available.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> Check the feed pages rather than trusting this paragraph.

## Where the recaptured value should go

Recapture creates a distribution question that did not previously exist, and it is a protocol-design
question rather than an oracle one.

**To the treasury.** Simplest; offsets bad debt. Note that the protocol now earns revenue that scales
with liquidations, which is an incentive worth writing down.

**To the liquidated borrower.** Most defensible — they paid the discount — and most consequential,
because a rebate lowers the *effective* penalty, and the penalty is also the deterrent that keeps
borrowers away from their thresholds.

**To suppliers.** Sends the surplus to the parties bearing the credit risk, at the cost of a yield
that is harder to reason about.

No answer is correct independently of the protocol's other parameters, which is the point: recapture
converts a leak into a policy lever, and a lever has to be set.

<Check type="explain" concept="orac-oev">
A lending protocol's team observes that during volatile days a single searcher captures most of the
liquidation bonuses, and proposes reducing the liquidation bonus from 8% to 3% — reasoning that
searchers are clearly earning far more than the service is worth, so the protocol should stop
overpaying.

Say what this proposal gets right and what it gets wrong, and what the OEV framing suggests instead.

<Answer>
**What it gets right: in ordinary conditions the discount massively over-prices the service.** One
searcher taking most of it is evidence that the position after the update clears at well below the
bonus — exactly the surplus this lesson is about.

**What it gets wrong: the bonus is not sized for ordinary conditions.** It is sized for the worst
day — expensive gas, collateral falling while the liquidator holds it, real inventory risk. Cutting
it optimises for the days when liquidation was going to happen anyway, at the cost of the day when it
must. The failure mode is not "we save money"; it is bad debt that dwarfs every bonus saved.

**What it also gets wrong: the surplus is not going to the liquidator.** Most of the bid flows
through the builder to the proposer, because searchers compete it
away.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" /> Cutting the bonus reduces what the *ordering market* extracts
and shrinks the buffer that guarantees the service under stress. Wrong recipient.

**What the OEV framing suggests instead: keep the bonus and auction the position.** The bonus stays
sized for the bad day; the surplus on ordinary days is recaptured rather than leaked, because the
right to backrun the update is sold instead of given away. The dual-aggregator construction does this
without changing the consuming interface, since SVR feeds keep the same aggregator interface and are
read with the same code at a different address.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" />

**What the team must additionally decide.** Where recaptured value goes, whether a borrower rebate
changes behaviour near the threshold, and what the fallback delay does to their staleness check —
because a feed that falls back to the standard price after a configurable delay has a documented path
on which the answer is older.<Cite src="chainlink-svr-docs" />

**The general lesson to carry.** When a parameter appears to be overpaying, check whether the
overpayment is being competed away by someone who is not providing the service. Adjusting the
parameter attacks the symptom; auctioning the right attacks the cause.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**The MEV module** is the direct continuation. Searchers, builders, private channels, bundles,
orderflow auctions — that is the vocabulary of block-ordering markets, and OEV is one application of
it, with an unusually easy-to-point-at origin.

**The liquidations module** supplies the other half: why a fixed discount is chosen over an auction,
what a Dutch auction changes, and how keeper competition behaves when gas spikes at the same moment
as the price move.

**Reading your own protocol's blocks.** Take a volatile day, find the oracle transmissions for your
feed, and read the transaction immediately after each one. Add up what left. That is what your oracle
design donates to the ordering market — measurable today, without changing
anything.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-api-reference" />

**The module's closing question.** Every value your protocol treats as a price has an author, an age,
a cost to falsify — and now a fourth property: a moment at which it changes, which is worth money to
whoever is standing next to it. Write that column in too.<Cite src="chainlink-data-feeds-docs" />
