Same pool, same flash loan, same $150 of fees. This time the consuming protocol does not
[read reserves](/c/orac-spot-price-is-not-an-oracle); it reads a thirty-minute time-weighted average. The attacker sends the identical
transaction. The pool's reserves go to 100,000 and 25,000, the marginal price reads 4, and the
protocol's price moves by roughly seven tenths of one percent.

Nothing was blocked. No check fired. The attack simply did not accomplish anything, and the reason is
arithmetic rather than defence: the consumer is reading an integral, and the attacker contributed one
block's worth of area to a window that is a hundred and fifty blocks wide.

That is the whole idea, and this lesson is about both halves of it — what the integral buys, and what
it costs, because they are the same parameter.

## The accumulator, as built

The construction is simpler than its reputation. A pair maintains a running sum of price multiplied
by time, and any observer can recover an average over any interval by differencing the sum at two
points.

In `UniswapV2Pair._update`, which runs on every state-changing interaction, the contract computes the
seconds since the last update and — only when that is non-zero — adds the pre-trade reserve ratio,
scaled by the elapsed seconds, into two cumulative variables, one per
direction.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-core-pair" />

```solidity
uint32 timeElapsed = blockTimestamp - blockTimestampLast;
if (timeElapsed > 0 && _reserve0 != 0 && _reserve1 != 0) {
    price0CumulativeLast += uint(UQ112x112.encode(_reserve1).uqdiv(_reserve0)) * timeElapsed;
    price1CumulativeLast += uint(UQ112x112.encode(_reserve0).uqdiv(_reserve1)) * timeElapsed;
}
```

Three details in those four lines carry the entire security argument.

**The reserves used are `_reserve0` and `_reserve1` — the values from *before* this transaction's
swap.** The price being accumulated is the one the previous block left behind.

**The `timeElapsed > 0` guard makes it once per block, not once per trade.** The first interaction in
a block writes the accumulator; every later interaction in the same block contributes
nothing.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-core-pair" /> The documentation states the design intent directly:
every pair measures — but does not store — the market price at the beginning of each block, before
any trades take place.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-oracles-docs" />

**Nothing is stored per interval.** The pair keeps one running sum, described as a sum of the price
for every second in the contract's entire history.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-oracles-docs" /> A consumer
reads the cumulative value at the start and end of the interval it cares about and divides the
difference by the interval's length.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-oracles-docs" /> The consumer chooses the
window; the pool has no opinion about it.

The corollary the documentation spells out is operational and often missed: for a ten-minute average
you must sample once every ten minutes, and for a one-week average once every
week.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-oracles-docs" /> The pool stores no history for you. Somebody's contract
has to have written down the earlier cumulative value.

### What v3 changed

Uniswap v3 keeps an array of observations inside the pool, which removes the need for a consumer to
checkpoint anything — but introduces a parameter that quietly decides what window you actually get.

Each `Observation` holds a block timestamp, a **tick accumulator** (tick multiplied by time elapsed
since the pool was initialised), a seconds-per-liquidity accumulator, and an initialised
flag.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-oracle" /> Writes happen at most once per block: if an observation
already exists for this block timestamp, `write` returns
early.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-oracle" /> Because the accumulated quantity is a tick rather than a
price, and ticks are logarithmic, the resulting average is a **geometric** mean of prices rather than
an arithmetic one.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" />

The parameter is **cardinality**: the number of populated elements in the observation array, tracked
alongside `cardinalityNext`, the array's length independent of population, which can be grown by
anyone.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-oracle" /> The array is a fixed 65,535
slots.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-oracle" />

<Aside kind="warn">
This is the failure that produces silent short windows. Ask a pool for a one-hour average and you get
one only if the observation array still holds an entry from at least an hour ago; if the oldest
retained observation is more recent, **you get something shorter and nothing tells you
so**.<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" /> The number of initialised observations decides a pool's
TWAP behaviour as much as, or more than, the window you request — so the actionable step is to
monitor cardinality as an input to oracle quality, not to assume the requested window is the
delivered one.<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" /> A pool whose cardinality was sized for calm
conditions can be pushed to overwrite its own history precisely when interactions spike, which is
during volatility.<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" />
</Aside>

## Worked example: what the window demands of an attacker

Now derive the number the last lesson could not produce: how far the *spot* price must move to shift
the *average* by a useful amount. Work it on the geometric construction, because that is what a v3
pool reports.

**Subgoal 1 — write the average as a product.** Over `n` blocks with per-block prices `pᵢ`, the
geometric mean is the `n`th root of their product.<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" />

**Subgoal 2 — split the window into the blocks the attacker controls and the ones they do not.**
Suppose the spot price is manipulated to `q` for `m` blocks and sits at the market price `p` for the
remaining `n − m`. Then<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" />

```text
TWAP = ( p^(n−m) · q^m )^(1/n)
```

**Subgoal 3 — invert it, because the attacker knows the target and needs the input.** Rearranging for
`q` gives the spot price the attacker must achieve on each manipulated
block:<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" />

```text
q = ( TWAP^n / p^(n−m) )^(1/m)
```

**Subgoal 4 — pick a target derived from the victim's parameters, not from a round number.** Euler's
worked case: a lending market with a 0.75 loan-to-value cap. To borrow even slightly more than
permitted, the price must move by at least 35%, so the attacker wants a manipulated TWAP of
1.35.<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" /> Take `n = 144` blocks — roughly a thirty-minute window —
and an initial price of 1.<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" />

**Subgoal 5 — evaluate at one block.** With `m = 1` the formula is just `1.35^144`:

```text
q = 1.35^144 ≈ 5.86 × 10^18
```

Not a typo, and not a special case. To shift a 144-block geometric average by 35% using a single
block, that block's price must be raised by eighteen orders of
magnitude.<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" /> There is not enough of most assets in existence to
do it, and a flash loan does not help, because the attack necessarily spans more than one
transaction.<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" />

**Subgoal 6 — evaluate at ten blocks, and watch the curve collapse.** With `m = 10`, the exponent
becomes `144/10`:

```text
q = 1.35^14.4 ≈ 75.3
```

A seventy-five-fold manipulation — still absurd, but eighteen orders of magnitude
closer.<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" /> At `m = 20` the same formula gives `1.35^7.2 ≈ 8.7`.
At `m = 72` — half the window, about fifteen minutes — it gives `1.35² ≈ 1.82`, under a
doubling.<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" />

**Subgoal 7 — read the shape, not the individual numbers.** The requirement falls off exponentially
in the number of blocks controlled. This is the single most important property of the construction
and it cuts both ways: a TWAP is astronomically expensive against an attacker who can hold the
distortion for one block, and merely expensive against one who can hold it for a quarter of the
window. **What a TWAP prices is duration**, and every additional block the attacker can hold is worth
far more to them than additional capital.

### Arithmetic versus geometric is not a detail

The v2 accumulator sums prices; the v3 accumulator sums ticks, which are logarithms of prices. That
difference decides how much damage a single outlying block can do, and it is worth carrying because
it explains why the same nominal window behaves differently on the two versions.

An arithmetic mean is dominated by its largest term. ChainSecurity's illustration makes it concrete:
with a true price of 1 and a 100-block arithmetic average, an attacker who wants the average to read
2 does not need to hold a price of 2 for all hundred blocks — they can set the price to 101 for a
single block and leave it at 1 for the other ninety-nine, because `(101 + 99) / 100 = 2`.<Cite src="chainsecurity-multiblock-mev" />
One block of an extreme value buys the whole distortion.

A geometric mean cannot be dragged that way, which is why v3 oracles are described as much more
resilient to single-block manipulation than v2 ones.<Cite src="chainsecurity-multiblock-mev" /> The
`1.35^144` figure above is exactly this resilience expressed as a number. But resilience is not
immunity, and the same analysis names the escape: the attack can be repeated to create multiple
outliers, as many times as the attacker controls blocks within one TWAP period, and the capital
requirement shrinks dramatically with repetition because each individual manipulation can be
smaller.<Cite src="chainsecurity-multiblock-mev" />

Which returns you to the same variable. Not capital, not depth — **how many blocks in the window the
attacker gets to choose the contents of**. Everything in the next lesson is about that number.

## The other readout on the same dial

Everything above argues for a longer window. Here is the bill.

The reported price is an average over the past `n` blocks, which means that after any genuine move
the reported price is a blend of the new world and the old one, and stays a blend for a full window.
Work a crash on the arithmetic construction, which is easier to compute by hand and directionally
identical to the geometric one.

An asset falls from 100 to 60 — a 40% move — linearly over ten minutes, then stabilises. The consumer
reads a thirty-minute average. Sampling once per minute:

| Time | Spot | 30-minute average |
|---|---|---|
| End of the crash | 60 | **93.4** |
| 10 minutes later | 60 | **80.1** |
| 20 minutes later | 60 | **66.7** |
| 30 minutes later | 60 | **60.0** |

Trace the first row. The window still contains twenty minutes at 100 and ten minutes averaging 80,
giving `(20 × 100 + 10 × 80) / 30 = 93.4`. The market has repriced the asset by 40% and the protocol
believes it fell by 6.6%.

Now put a borrower in it. Suppose their position is liquidatable at 80. On spot they crossed the
threshold during the crash. On the thirty-minute average they are not liquidatable at the end of the
crash, not liquidatable ten minutes later — at 80.1 they are exactly at the boundary, ten minutes
after they should have been seized — and comfortably liquidatable only once most of a window has
passed. Liquidators cannot act during the window, because the protocol will reject the call. By the
time the price catches up, the collateral is worth 60 against a debt sized for 80, and the difference
is [bad debt](/c/liq-bad-debt-threshold) the protocol absorbs.

This is not a hypothetical failure mode invented to balance the argument. The Uniswap Labs study
names it as the concrete downside of long windows: using a TWAP with a long window makes prices
smooth but lagging, and the lag is problematic if spot prices naturally jump enough to mint bad debt
to the protocol.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" /> Their mitigation is a scoping decision rather
than a technical one — restrict which assets may be used as collateral so that such jumps are not
realistic.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" />

### Stating the trade honestly

Window length is one dial with two coupled readouts. Lengthening it raises the number of blocks an
attacker must control, and raises how far the reported price trails a real move. Shortening it does
the reverse. There is no setting that is both hard to manipulate and accurate under stress.

What a designer actually chooses is therefore not "the safe window" but **which failure they prefer**,
and the choice is worth making explicitly because it will otherwise be made by whoever copied a
constant from a tutorial. Two honest framings:

- *We would rather take bad debt occasionally than be manipulated once.* Long window, tight
  collateral scope, and a written expectation that gap moves will cost money.
- *We would rather be manipulable at a computable price than insolvent during a crash.* Short window,
  and the manipulation cost had better exceed the borrow cap — which is next lesson's arithmetic.

The design that fails is the third one, where nobody chose, the window came from an example
repository, and the collateral list grew afterwards.

<Check type="explain" concept="orac-twap-lag">
A perpetual futures venue prices its mark against a 30-minute TWAP of a deep on-chain pool, and its
funding rate against the same number. A risk reviewer says the TWAP is a good choice because it makes
the mark expensive to manipulate. A trader says the venue is systematically exploitable and has been
paying for it since launch.

Explain how both can be right, and say what specifically is being extracted.

<Answer>
**Both are right because they are talking about different sides of the same lag.** The reviewer is
correct that shifting a 30-minute geometric average by a useful amount requires holding a distorted
spot price for a large fraction of the window, and the requirement grows explosively as the number of
controlled blocks falls.<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" /> Nobody is manipulating this mark
cheaply.

**The trader is describing something that requires no manipulation at all.** During any genuine move
the mark trails the market — smooth but lagging.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" /> A trader who
watches the real market can open a position at a mark that is already known to be wrong, in the
direction it is known to be wrong, and wait for the average to catch up. That is not an exploit in
any technical sense: no contract misbehaves, no input is falsified. It is the venue selling
yesterday's price to someone holding today's.

**What is extracted, precisely.** The difference between the lagging mark and the true price,
captured by whoever notices the move first, paid for by the venue's counterparties or its insurance
fund. Because the lag is largest during fast moves, the extraction concentrates exactly when the
venue is least able to absorb it.

**The funding rate makes it worse, not better.** Funding computed from a lagging mark points in the
wrong direction for the length of the lag, so the mechanism that is supposed to pull the mark toward
the index is instead paying traders to sit on the correct side of a known error.

**Why "make the window longer" is the wrong instinct.** It raises manipulation cost and raises the
extractable lag by the same parameter. The reviewer's control and the trader's opportunity are one
dial.

**What actually fits this product.** Latency-sensitive marks are the case pull feeds exist for: a
signed update attached to the transaction, with a tight maximum age, plus a structural rule that a
position cannot be opened and closed within one block. A time-weighted on-chain price belongs in this
design as a *sanity cross-check* on the fast source — a bound that catches a wild value — rather than
as the source of truth. That is the pattern the rest of the module builds.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson** prices the attack this one only described. It combines the `Δx = √(k · q) − x`
from the spot lesson with the `q` formula derived here, produces a dollar figure per manipulated
block, and then examines the assumption the whole construction rests on: that somebody gets to trade
against the attacker in between.<Cite src="chainsecurity-multiblock-mev" />

**The aggregation question** returns there too — whether combining several pools helps, and why the
weights matter more than the count.<Cite src="arxiv-manipulation-cost-2026" />

**The hardening lesson** uses a TWAP the way most 2026 protocols now do: not as the primary source,
but as the bound that a fast feed must agree with.

**Reviewing a TWAP integration tomorrow.** Three questions. What window is actually delivered, given
the pool's observation cardinality?<Cite src="uniswap-v3-core-oracle" /> What does the reported price
say during a 40% gap, computed rather than guessed? And which of the two failures did the team choose
— because if they cannot tell you, they did not choose.
