Take a lending market that priced its collateral from a thirty-minute Uniswap TWAP, deployed in early
2022, never upgraded, never touched. Its bytecode in September 2026 is byte-for-byte what it was.
Every invariant it satisfied then, it satisfies now.

Its security is different, and the change happened at the consensus layer of a chain that has no idea
the contract exists.

That is the subject here: manipulation cost is not a property of the price source. It is a property
of the price source *plus the market structure around it*, and one of those two things changed under
every TWAP oracle on Ethereum when the network moved to proof of stake, without a single line of
application code moving. Getting this right requires first getting the definition of "cost" right,
because the number everyone quotes is the wrong one.

## Cost is loss, not capital

The headline after an oracle exploit names the flash loan, because the loan is the largest number in
the transaction. It is also the number that comes back.

The 2026 formalisation of AMM-based oracles states the correct quantity precisely: the **cost of
manipulation** is the minimal mark-to-market loss an attacker must incur to move the oracle by a
given multiplicative factor.<Cite src="arxiv-manipulation-cost-2026" /> An attacker trades against
constant-product markets to distort an on-chain oracle, arbitrageurs restore cross-pool and
cross-venue consistency, and an oracle designer chooses how to aggregate pool
quotes.<Cite src="arxiv-manipulation-cost-2026" /> Loss, not outlay. What is destroyed, not what is
borrowed.

The distinction is not pedantry; it decides which defences work. [Flash loans](/c/flash-loan) drive the *capital*
requirement to approximately zero while leaving the *loss* untouched, which is exactly why they break
spot oracles — where the loss is also near zero — and do nothing against a deep time-weighted one,
where the loss is real and must be paid across blocks.

## Worked example: a number, end to end

Chain the two derivations you already have — `q` from the window, `Δx` from the depth — and add the
step that turns them into a loss. The subgoals name each step's purpose so the pipeline transfers to
a pool you have not seen.

**Subgoal 1 — derive the distortion from the victim's parameters ([attacker economics](/c/attacker-economics), stated as an inequality).** A lending market with a 0.75
loan-to-value cap requires roughly a 35% price move before an attacker can borrow more than they
should, so the target is a manipulated TWAP of 1.35 against an unmanipulated price of
1.<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" />

**Subgoal 2 — derive the per-block spot price from the window and the blocks controlled.** With
`n = 144` blocks and `m = 10` manipulated blocks, `q = (1.35^144 / 1^134)^(1/10) = 1.35^14.4 ≈
75.30`.<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" />

**Subgoal 3 — derive the swap that reaches that spot price.** For a pool holding 50,000 of each asset,
`k = 2.5 × 10⁹`, and

```text
Δx_in = √(k · q) − x = √(2.5e9 × 75.30) − 50,000 ≈ 383,884
```

so 383,884 units must be pushed in on **each** of the ten manipulated
blocks.<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" />

**Subgoal 4 — compute what comes back out, so the difference can be valued.** The constant-product
relation gives the amount received:

```text
Δy_out = y · Δx_in / (x + Δx_in) = 50,000 × 383,884 / 433,884 ≈ 44,238
```

**Subgoal 5 — value the round trip at the *true* price, not the pool's.** This is the step that
defines the cost. The attacker handed the pool 383,884 units of one asset and received 44,238 units
of the other. At the unmanipulated price of 1, those 44,238 units are worth 44,238 — so the position
is under water by the difference:<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" />

```text
c = Δx_in − Δy_out · p = 383,884 − 44,238 ≈ 339,645 per manipulated block
```

Why value at `p` rather than at the manipulated price? Because the assumption embedded in the model
is that **arbitrageurs will profit from the attacker by returning the manipulated price back to
normal**.<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" /> The attacker cannot realise their holdings at the
price they created; somebody else takes that spread. The 339,645 is what they hand to whoever does.

**Subgoal 6 — multiply by the blocks, because the cost recurs.** The distortion must be re-established
on every manipulated block, because arbitrage undoes it in between, so the upper limit on the total
is `m · c`:<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" />

```text
C = 10 × 339,645 ≈ 3,396,454
```

**Subgoal 7 — read the bounds of the model before quoting the number.** This figure is an *upper*
limit, because a sophisticated attacker may recoup some of the slippage by arbitraging their own
transaction.<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" /> It also assumes an unrealistic pool with no
other liquidity, and the analysis notes that in reality there is often a great deal of liquidity in
real pools, both concentrated and distributed, even for stablecoin
pairs.<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" /> Distributed liquidity in the tail is what makes the
real version infeasible: reaching a target price far from the current one means buying through every
tick on the way, and even small amounts of liquidity spread across the whole range raise the cost
dramatically.<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" />

**Subgoal 8 — compare against the prize, and against the practical obstacles.** Three-and-a-third
million against whatever the market's borrow cap is. And three obstacles the arithmetic does not
capture: the attacker has no way of ensuring they are the beneficiary, because a manipulated average
emerging over half an hour alerts every opportunist watching; the funds must be acquired and cashed
out anonymously; and the whole thing is exposed for the duration rather than atomic, so it is unlike
the risk-free single-transaction attacks that dominate DeFi
exploits.<Cite src="euler-twap-cost-of-attack" />

That last point is the one that matters most for design. Uniswap's own documentation compresses the
whole model into a sentence worth memorising: moving the price 5% on a one-hour TWAP costs
approximately what is lost to arbitrage and fees for moving the price 5% *every block* for one
hour.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-oracles-docs" />

**The dominant term is the arbitrageurs.** Hold that thought for exactly one section.

## What proof of stake did to the bill

Under proof of work, the next block's producer was unknown until the block was accepted. A
manipulator could not guarantee they would be able to back-run their own transaction cheaply; the
most likely case was competing in an open auction for the right to do so, at market
price.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" /> Proof of work therefore *ensured* that manipulators lost
value to a back-run, and a protocol consuming a TWAP was safe as long as the value lost to
back-running outweighed the value gained.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" />

Under proof of stake the proposer for each slot is chosen deterministically and in advance. The
mechanism is worth naming rather than gesturing at. `get_beacon_proposer_index` derives a per-slot
seed from the epoch's RANDAO-derived seed and the slot number, then calls `compute_proposer_index`,
which samples the active validator set with an acceptance probability proportional to effective
balance.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" /> The epoch seed itself is fixed
`MIN_SEED_LOOKAHEAD` epochs in advance, which is what makes proposer and committee roles knowable
ahead of the slots they apply to.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" />

The consequence for oracles, stated by both analyses of the period: a validator controlling two
consecutive blocks can move a pool's price greatly in the first and move it back in the next
**without risk of being arbitraged against**, so the oracle records a manipulated data point and the
only cost is the pool fee, paid twice.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" /> ChainSecurity's version
is mechanically explicit: manipulate in the first block without sending the transaction to the public
mempool; bots see the arbitrage opportunity once the block is published and submit transactions to
seize it; but the attacker also controls the second block, so they place their own rebalancing
transaction at the top of it and lose nothing.<Cite src="chainsecurity-multiblock-mev" />

Now recall which term dominated. **The cost of TWAP manipulation comes almost entirely from losses to
arbitrageurs**, so if the manipulated price can be hidden between two blocks and the market
rebalanced in the second, it becomes essentially free.<Cite src="chainsecurity-multiblock-mev" />
The 339,645 per block in the worked example was the arbitrageurs' profit. Remove them and what
remains is fees.

### How far ahead, exactly — and what 2026 changed

The 2022 analyses put the lookahead at one epoch. The block producers of all 64 blocks in epochs `N`
and `N+1` are known at the time the last block of epoch `N−1` is produced, giving up to 12.8
minutes.<Cite src="chainsecurity-multiblock-mev" /> The Uniswap Labs framing is equivalent: proposers
are known for the current epoch and the next, so at least 6 minutes 24 seconds and at most 12 minutes
48 seconds of schedule is visible.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" />

Both carried a caveat: proposers for epoch `N` were guaranteed, but validator balance changes could
in rare cases still alter the proposers for epoch `N+1`.<Cite src="chainsecurity-multiblock-mev" />

That caveat is gone. EIP-7917, *Deterministic proposer lookahead*,<Cite src="eip-7917" /> shipped in
the Fulu consensus specification, which activated on mainnet with
Fusaka.<Cite src="fusaka-mainnet-announcement" /> The `BeaconState` now carries a
`proposer_lookahead` field: a list of validator indices covering the full lookahead period, starting
at the beginning of the current epoch, of length `(MIN_SEED_LOOKAHEAD + 1) * SLOTS_PER_EPOCH` — that
is, 64 slots.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-beacon-chain" /> `get_beacon_proposer_index` now reads
that pre-calculated list rather than computing on
demand,<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-beacon-chain" /> and at the start of epoch `N` the lookahead
for epoch `N+1` is computed and written into the
state.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-beacon-chain" />

Read what that does to the threat model rather than to the protocol. Before, an attacker planning a
two-block manipulation in the next epoch was working from a projection that could, rarely, change.
Now the schedule is a committed field in the state. The lookahead was always the enabling condition;
it is now a guarantee rather than an inference.

## The honest framing: a probability, not a capability

None of the above says TWAPs are broken. It says a specific opportunity exists, and the opportunity
arrives at a rate.

**Derive the rate.** Proposer selection samples the active set with acceptance probability
proportional to effective balance,<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" /> so treat each
slot as an independent trial with success probability `p` equal to your share of effective stake. The
expected number of trials until two consecutive successes is the standard result
`(1 − p²) / (p²(1 − p))`, which is exactly the expression ChainSecurity gives as
`(p⁻² − 1) / (1 − p)`.<Cite src="chainsecurity-multiblock-mev" /> Multiply by 12 seconds per slot to
get calendar time.

Their worked case: an attacker controlling 0.15% of stake — a capital cost of roughly $35 million as
of August 2022 — will on average propose two blocks in a row every 62 days, **with no opportunity
cost compared to normal staking**, because they are simply waiting for a schedule they would have
received anyway.<Cite src="chainsecurity-multiblock-mev" /> Reproduce it: `p = 0.0015` gives 445,111
slots, which at 12 seconds is 61.8 days.

The shape of that function is the real finding. Stake share enters quadratically, so the waiting time
collapses fast:

Longer runs are rarer but not rare enough to dismiss. Citing Revuelta, the Uniswap Labs study reports
that a proposer with around 1% share should expect three blocks in a row about 0.19 times per month —
once every five months — while a validator with 10% share should expect three in a row 181 times a
month.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" /> And because the schedule for two epochs is visible,
multiple *disjoint* sets of manipulations can be executed as though they were one longer set: two
two-block sets cross the same number of ticks as one three-block set, with equivalent impact and only
more fees paid.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" /> The study is explicit that its findings are
therefore a lower bound on likelihood, and the real probability is
higher.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" />

Two counterweights belong in the same paragraph, because leaving them out produces the slogan version
of this result. The attacker's second block **can be reorged**, and if it is, the arbitrage
opportunity gets captured and the cost reverts to that of a single-block attack without the multi-block
advantage.<Cite src="chainsecurity-multiblock-mev" /> And a large validator has a reason not to do
this that has nothing to do with cost: the Uniswap Labs authors argue validators with enough market
share to make an attack likely are unlikely to attempt it, because doing so would undermine their
position in the market and users' confidence in them as neutral
parties.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" />

Pointing in the other direction: with access to a way of submitting transactions privately to the
first block's proposer, an attacker confident it will not leak needs to control only the **second**
block, not both.<Cite src="chainsecurity-multiblock-mev" /> Private orderflow, which the MEV lessons
treat as a mitigation for users, is here a reduction in the attacker's requirement.

### What the 2023 numbers do and do not tell you

The Uniswap Labs study's headline conclusions are worth knowing and dangerous to quote as current.

Its finding was that two-block manipulation of top pairs remained prohibitively expensive: moving a
30-minute TWAP by 20% with a single manipulated block would require shifting the spot price by
71,721,000,000,000%, which needs an infeasible quantity of assets and loses a massive amount of it to
fees.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" /> Three-block and longer manipulations are more feasible, but
require large validator market share to be likely.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" />

The tick arithmetic behind that is derivable, and deriving it shows you exactly where the leverage
sits. For a 30-minute window of 150 twelve-second blocks, a target distortion `δ`, and `k`
manipulated blocks, the required tick is<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" />

```text
τ = 150 · (ln(1 + δ) / ln(1.0001)) / k + TWAP₍₀,₁₅₀₋ₖ₎
```

The first term is the tick shift needed in the average — `ln(1.2)/ln(1.0001) ≈ 1823.3` ticks for a
20% move — scaled up by 150 because it must be delivered in `k` blocks instead of spread over the
window. At `k = 1` that is `150 × 1823.3 ≈ 273,496` ticks, matching the study's
273,497.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" /> At `k = 10` it is 27,350, matching
theirs.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" /> The cost decreases exponentially with the number of
controllable blocks, because crossing fewer total ticks is exponentially
cheaper.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" />

<Aside kind="warn">
**Do not present that study's dollar figures as current.** Its samples were taken on 4 September 2022,
and every one of its cost numbers depends on that date's pool liquidity and that period's stake
distribution.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" /> Liquidity has moved between protocol versions and
venues since; staking concentration has changed. The *formulas* transfer, the *shape* transfers, and
the conclusions were explicitly conditional even when written. If you need a number for a decision,
recompute it from live pool state and current stake shares. A curriculum that repeats "two-block
manipulation costs $710 billion" in 2026 is quoting a measurement of a pool that no longer has those
reserves.
</Aside>

The study's own mitigations are worth knowing as design levers rather than as figures. Wide-range
liquidity is the strongest available: liquidity far from the current tick is what makes an extreme
manipulation expensive, and only liquidity within the tick range the manipulation must cross
contributes at all.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" /> Truncation is the cheapest structural fix —
capping how many ticks an oracle update may move relative to the previous one, so that a large
distortion requires many updates and therefore many blocks.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" /> A
time-weighted **median** price would require the pool to be manipulated for over half the blocks in
the period, which is a qualitatively different requirement; winsorised oracles retain most of a
TWAP's properties with added resistance.<Cite src="uniswap-v3-pos-oracles" /> Reference
implementations of median and winsorised variants exist to be read rather than
invented.<Cite src="winsorized-uniswap-oracle" />

## Aggregating several sources: the weights, not the count

If one pool is manipulable at a price, combine several. The 2026 analysis solves the attacker-designer
game for this and the result is sharper than "use more sources".

For independent constant-product pools, **liquidity weights maximise the minimum cost of manipulation
within the class of weighted medians, at any distortion level**; for weighted means the same holds
only locally, as the distortion tends to zero.<Cite src="arxiv-manipulation-cost-2026" /> For larger
distortions weighted means become more fragile: the optimal weights can depend on the target
distortion, and no single choice is uniformly optimal across distortion
levels.<Cite src="arxiv-manipulation-cost-2026" /> The framework extends to multi-asset star
architectures with liquidity weights remaining optimal in the same sense, and — this one is
counter-intuitive — in a frictionless model with cross-pool arbitrage, manipulation cost depends only
on the **total quote depth** and coincides across symmetric
aggregators.<Cite src="arxiv-manipulation-cost-2026" />

The practical readings, in order of how often they are violated:

**Equal weighting is a design choice, and a weak one.** An unweighted median or mean across venues of
very different depth hands the attacker the thinnest venue as the cheapest path.

**Weight by depth, and re-derive the weights when depth moves.** A weight set fixed at deployment
encodes a liquidity distribution that will not survive a migration.

**Independence is about the underlying, not the interface.** Two sources that both bottom out in the
same thin pool are one source with two addresses, and no weighting scheme repairs that.

**This is one paper's result, not settled consensus.** Treat it as a reason to model your aggregation
explicitly rather than as a rule to apply without checking its assumptions against your setup — it is
derived for independent constant-product pools, and the frictionless cross-pool result is a separate
model.<Cite src="arxiv-manipulation-cost-2026" />

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson** takes the 2026 attack surface, where none of this arithmetic is needed because the
price source is not a market at all — it is a function on a contract that somebody can move with a
single permissionless call.

**The hardening lesson** implements the levers named here: bounds, per-update movement caps, and the
divergence policy that decides what a second source is actually for.

**The MEV module** and its [searchers](/c/mev-searcher) pick up the other side of the proposer's power — the same schedule, used for value
capture rather than for distortion.

**Doing this for real.** Take one protocol you have read, find its price source, and run the pipeline:
distortion from the liquidation threshold, `q` from the window, `Δx` from live reserves, `c` from the
difference valued at the true price, `C` from the block count, `P` from current stake shares. If you
cannot obtain one of those inputs, that is itself the finding.
