A whitelisted user sends about a hundred dollars of USDT and receives about a hundred newly minted
USDe, atomically, less the gas and execution cost of putting on the hedge. The protocol opens a short
perpetual futures position of approximately the same notional on a derivatives exchange. The backing
assets go to an off-exchange settlement provider — they stay on chain, custodied by a third party,
and are delegated to the exchange as margin without ever being deposited
there.<Cite src="ethena-how-usde-works" />

Every previous design in this module answers *what is the redemption asset?* with a noun. Dollars in
a bank. Collateral in a vault. Newly minted governance tokens. This one answers with a **position**:
a long spot leg and a short derivatives leg, held open, on venues, by counterparties, marked
continuously.

That is not a rhetorical distinction. It changes which questions have answers. "How much collateral
is there" has an answer and it is a good one. "Where is the collateral" has an answer with four
company names in it. "What happens if the hedge is closed without our consent" has an answer that
does not appear anywhere in a collateral ratio.

This lesson builds the design from its primitive — the funding rate — and then works the risk
checklist that actually applies, which shares almost nothing with the one you have been using.

## The funding rate, derived rather than described

A perpetual future has no expiry. Nothing forces its price to converge on spot, because there is no
settlement date at which the two must meet. So venues manufacture convergence with a periodic
payment between the two sides of the contract.

**It is peer-to-peer.** One venue's documentation states it plainly: funding is purely peer-to-peer
and the exchange collects no fees on the payments.<Cite src="hyperliquid-funding-docs" /> When the
contract trades above the underlying, longs pay shorts; when it trades below, shorts pay
longs.<Cite src="hyperliquid-funding-docs" /> Nobody is obliged to make the rate positive, and no
venue is on the other side of it.

**Subgoal 1 — write down the formula.** For that venue the eight-hour funding rate is

```text
F = P_avg + clamp(interest_rate - P, -0.0005, 0.0005)
```

where `P` is the premium index, sampled every five seconds and averaged over the hour, and funding
is charged every hour at one eighth of the computed
rate.<Cite src="hyperliquid-funding-docs" />

**Subgoal 2 — unpack the premium.** The premium is not "perp price minus spot". It is built from
*impact* prices — the average execution price for a fixed notional on each side of the book:

```text
premium = impact_price_difference / oracle_price

impact_price_difference = max(impact_bid - oracle, 0) - max(oracle - impact_ask, 0)
```

with the oracle price computed by validators as a liquidity-weighted median of spot prices across
centralised venues.<Cite src="hyperliquid-funding-docs" /> Two consequences worth noting: the
premium is zero whenever the oracle sits *inside* the book's impact spread, so a wide book produces
no funding signal; and the number that matters is executable depth, not the last print.

**Subgoal 3 — work the venue's own numerical example.** Interest rate 0.01 per cent. Impact bid at
10,100 against a spot price of 10,000, so the premium is `(10100 - 10000) / 10000 = 0.01`, one per
cent. The clamped term is `clamp(0.01% - 1%, -0.05%, 0.05%)`, which pins to `-0.05%`. So
`F = 1% - 0.05% = 0.95%`.<Cite src="hyperliquid-funding-docs" />

**Subgoal 4 — read what the clamp is for.** The interest component is a fixed 0.01 per cent every
eight hours, representing the difference in cost between borrowing dollars and borrowing spot
crypto.<Cite src="hyperliquid-funding-docs" /> The clamp bounds how far the funding rate may be
pushed away from the raw premium by that component. When the premium is large the clamp saturates
and funding is essentially the premium; when the premium is near zero, funding is essentially the
interest rate. So the interest component sets the **floor of the ordinary regime** and the premium
sets everything else.

**Subgoal 5 — annualise the floor, and check the venue's figure.** The interest component is 0.01
per cent per eight hours, which the documentation also states as 0.00125 per cent per hour and 11.6
per cent APR paid to the short side.<Cite src="hyperliquid-funding-docs" /> Verify it: compounding
hourly, `(1 + 0.0000125) ^ 8760 - 1`, and since the exponent times the rate is
`8760 * 1.25e-5 = 0.1095`, the result is `e^0.1095 - 1`, about 11.6 per cent. The published figure
is the compounded one, not the simple one — a five-second check that tells you which convention the
venue uses, which matters when you compare it against a rate quoted elsewhere.

**Subgoal 6 — establish the size of the tail.** Funding on that venue is capped at four per cent per
hour, which the documentation notes is far less aggressive capping than its centralised
counterparts, and the payment at each interval is `position_size * oracle_price * funding_rate`,
using the spot oracle price rather than the mark price to convert size into
notional.<Cite src="hyperliquid-funding-docs" /> Four per cent an hour is an extraordinary number.
It exists because the mechanism has to be able to force convergence in a dislocated market, and it
is the reason "funding is usually small" is a statement about the ordinary regime only.

## Building a dollar out of two legs

Now assemble the instrument.

**The construction.** Hold a volatile asset spot. Hold a short perpetual on the same asset at
approximately the same notional. A move in the asset's price changes the spot leg by some amount and
the hedge by approximately the same amount with the opposite sign, so the combined position is worth
roughly a constant number of dollars across most market
conditions.<Cite src="ethena-how-usde-works" /> Stable assets in the portfolio — liquid stablecoins,
short-duration real-world assets — already hold a dollar value and need no
hedge.<Cite src="ethena-how-usde-works" />

**Where the yield comes from.** The short leg collects funding in the positive regime, and the spot
leg may earn a staking yield. The staked form of the token is where that accrues: as rewards flow to
the staking contract, the value of the staked token rises, and the issuer states that the staked form
accrues only positive or flat rewards, with periods of negative protocol revenue absorbed by a
reserve fund during which no rewards are typically
paid.<Cite src="ethena-how-usde-works" /><Cite src="ethena-funding-risk-docs" />

**Why this is not the previous lesson.** In a seigniorage design, redemption creates the asset that
backs redemption. Here the backing is bought before the token is minted and marked continuously.
There is no reflexive loop between the token's price and its own backing, because the backing is
somebody else's asset. Apply lesson five's threshold test and it does not bind: there is no `M` that
is a claim on this system.

**Why it is also not lesson two.** The backing is not in custody as an inert asset either. Half of it
is an obligation on a derivatives venue, whose value depends on that venue continuing to exist,
continuing to be reachable, and continuing to honour the position.

## The peg, in this design's own terms

Run lesson one's five questions and the answers are unusually well documented, which is itself worth
noting: an issuer that publishes its arbitrage path is telling you it wants the loop to run.

**The trade.** If the token is worth less on an external market than through the protocol, an
approved user buys it there and redeems it into the protocol at par; if it is worth more, they mint
at par and sell.<Cite src="ethena-peg-arbitrage-docs" /> The issuer states it earns no profit from
minting or redeeming, with slippage and execution fees included in the
price.<Cite src="ethena-how-usde-works" />

**Eligibility.** Minting and redemption are available to authorised, whitelisted
users.<Cite src="ethena-peg-arbitrage-docs" /> So the loop's speed is the whitelist's speed.

**Size, and this is the number to write down.** The issuer states that about 0.50 per cent of backing
assets are typically held in stablecoin form inside the minting contract to enable on-demand
redemptions, replenished from around 4 per cent of backing held in stablecoins across multiple
custodians.<Cite src="ethena-peg-arbitrage-docs" /> Treat those as issuer-published figures that
move rather than as constants. What they tell you structurally is that the **instantly available**
redemption capacity is a small fraction of supply, with a larger tranche reachable on an operational
timescale, and the remainder reachable only by unwinding positions. That is a redemption right with
three tiers of latency, and lesson one's band applies to each tier separately.

**Latency and refusability** follow from the above: the fast tier is fast, the second tier depends on
custodians during working hours, and the third depends on venues accepting a large unwind.

Notice what this framing buys you. The commonly asked question — "is it really backed?" — has a good
answer here and it is the wrong question. The better question is "how much of the backing can be
converted to dollars in the next hour, and at what price", and that is answerable from published
figures for anyone who thinks to ask it.

## What actually breaks a delta-neutral dollar

The issuer publishes a risk taxonomy, and it is a good one to argue with rather than around: funding
risk, liquidation risk, custodial risk, exchange failure risk, backing assets risk, stablecoin-related
risk and margin collateral risk.<Cite src="ethena-risks-docs" /> Take the three that determine the
design's character.

### Funding risk, and how long a reserve lasts

The issuer's position is that negative funding periods tend not to persist and revert to a positive
mean, and it publishes a supporting figure: BTC and ETH funding rates averaged an annualised 7.8 to
9 per cent over the preceding three years on an open-interest- or volume-weighted basis, including
the 2022 bear market, with the data stated as of 31 December
2024.<Cite src="ethena-funding-risk-docs" /> It also states, in the same place, that this historical
data predates the protocol's own effect on the market and that updated dynamics are being
monitored.<Cite src="ethena-funding-risk-docs" />

Take that at face value and then do the thing a historical average cannot do for you, which is to
price the bad regime.

**Subgoal 1 — write the drag.** Let `f` be the annualised funding rate on the hedged notional
(negative in the bad regime), `s` the staking or short-duration yield on the spot leg, and `h` the
hedged fraction of the book. The annual change in backing per unit is approximately

```text
drag = -( f * h + s )
```

**Subgoal 2 — put a plausible bad regime through it.** Take `f = -20%`, `h = 0.9`, `s = 3%`. Then
`drag = -(-0.18 + 0.03) = 0.15`, fifteen per cent of the hedged book per year, or about **4.1 basis
points a day**.

**Subgoal 3 — convert a reserve into a duration.** The reserve fund absorbs negative revenue instead
of passing it to stakers.<Cite src="ethena-funding-risk-docs" /> If it holds `R` as a fraction of
supply, it lasts

```text
days = R / (drag / 365)
```

At `R = 1%` and a 15 per cent drag, that is `0.01 / 0.000411`, about **24 days**. At `R = 3%`, about
73 days. This is the arithmetic that turns "the reserve fund covers negative funding" into a
statement with a number in it.

**Subgoal 4 — apply the mitigation the issuer names.** In periods of low or negative funding, more of
the backing is shifted into liquid stablecoins earning approximately the Treasury rate, which reduces
exposure to negative funding.<Cite src="ethena-funding-risk-docs" /> In the model that is `h`
falling. Halving `h` to 0.45 gives `drag = -(-0.09 + 0.03) = 0.06`, six per cent, which at `R = 1%`
buys 61 days instead of 24. The mitigation is real and it is quantifiable, and it also reduces the
yield in the good regime, which is the trade being made.

**Subgoal 5 — name what the model does not capture.** Unwinding hedges to reduce `h` is itself a
trade, executed into whatever market conditions caused the funding to go negative. The model treats
`h` as a dial. It is a dial that costs slippage to turn, and the cost rises exactly when you most
want to turn it.

**Subgoal 6 — state the monitoring consequence.** The metric is not the funding rate and not the
reserve fund's dollar size. It is **reserve fund as a fraction of supply, divided by the current
drag** — a duration in days. Publish that on a dashboard and a bad regime becomes a countdown you can
act on rather than a mood.

### Exchange failure, and what off-exchange settlement does and does not fix

The hedge lives on centralised derivatives venues. The issuer's mitigation is that backing is never
deposited to an exchange and always sits with off-exchange settlement providers, so exposure to a
single exchange's failure is limited to the outstanding profit and loss between settlement cycles —
one provider's cycle being daily.<Cite src="ethena-exchange-failure-docs" /> In the event of a
failure, the position is treated as closed and the backing is delegated to another venue to re-hedge
the exposed delta.<Cite src="ethena-exchange-failure-docs" />

That is a genuinely strong structure and it is worth being precise about what it converts. It
converts *custody risk at the venue* — the FTX failure mode, where the assets themselves were gone —
into *replacement risk*: for a window, the position is unhedged and must be rebuilt somewhere else at
whatever price is then available. The residual is the settlement gap plus the re-hedge.

The issuer also publishes its venue allocation, which at the time of writing it gives as 7 per cent
liquid cash, 3 per cent deliverable futures and 90 per cent perpetual futures spread across five
named venues in stated proportions, each with its own settlement cadence and funding
cycle.<Cite src="ethena-exchange-failure-docs" /> Read that as a concentration figure with a date on
it rather than as a constant: the largest single venue share is the size of the re-hedge you would
have to do in an afternoon.

### Forced deleveraging, which is the one that has no analogue

Here is the risk with no counterpart anywhere else in this module.

The short leg is a position at a venue, subject to that venue's risk engine. In a violent move the
engine can reduce or close positions that are not in default — auto-deleveraging exists precisely to
close the loop when liquidations cannot be filled. If the short is reduced without the holder's
consent, the book is no longer delta-neutral, and it becomes un-neutral **during exactly the
conditions that made the hedge valuable**. The issuer lists liquidation risk and margin collateral
risk as separate named categories for this reason.<Cite src="ethena-risks-docs" />

The market has provided a calibration point. The 10 October 2025 cascade is widely reported as
roughly nineteen billion dollars of liquidations, and it should be carried as a **reported floor
rather than a measurement**: exchange liquidation feeds are known to under-report, since many venues
publish only one liquidation per second per symbol, and analysts differ on the true
figure.<Cite src="coindesk-19b-liquidation" /> The magnitude is what matters here, not the decimals.
A day on which that much notional is force-closed across venues is a day on which a large short book
is at risk of being managed by somebody else's risk engine, and it is
[a cross-venue cascade](/c/liq-cascade-cross-venue) rather than an event at any one exchange.

<Aside kind="warn">
There is one more inherited risk that is easy to miss because it is boring: the stable portion of the
backing is other issuers' stablecoins, and the minting contract's redemption buffer is held in them.
So every conclusion from lesson two — reserve composition, banking calendar, freeze authority —
applies to that slice, in full, without modification.<Cite src="ethena-how-usde-works" /> The issuer
lists stablecoin-related risk as its own category.<Cite src="ethena-risks-docs" /> A synthetic dollar
does not escape fiat-backed risk; it holds a smaller amount of it and adds new risks on top.
</Aside>

operating cash. It is a yield position, and it should be sized and reported as one.

counterparty whose failure mode is legible. Yield positions want return per unit of risk. This
instrument is unambiguously the second. The honest structure is a bucket policy — enough in a
maximally liquid instrument to cover the payroll horizon, the rest wherever the risk-adjusted return
justifies — and the argument the team is making is really an argument for the size of the second
bucket, not for merging the two.

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** looks at products marketed with this design's vocabulary and built on something
else entirely — where the "hedged position" is a discretionary trading book at an external manager,
with no on-chain verification, and where the November 2025 failures happened. The distinction you
just learned is the one that separates the two, and it is the reason the lesson exists.

**Lesson nine** returns to the regulatory boundary, where this design has a specific and instructive
problem: the statutory payment-stablecoin category requires reserves drawn from an enumerated list of
government and bank instruments and forbids paying holders interest or yield, and a hedged
derivatives book is on neither list.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> The observed response is a
split product line — a compliant, non-yield-bearing token alongside the synthetic
one.<Cite src="forbes-ethena-genius" />

**In the oracles and liquidations tracks**, you now have the missing half of the funding story. You
knew perpetual funding as a number a venue reports. You can now derive it from impact prices and an
interest component, which means you can reason about when the signal disappears — a wide book — and
when it saturates.<Cite src="hyperliquid-funding-docs" />

**And operationally**, this design converts diligence from a document exercise into a monitoring one.
Reserve duration in days at current funding. Largest single venue share. Instantly redeemable buffer
as a fraction of supply. Depth on the deepest secondary venue at your own exit size. Four numbers,
all derivable from published data, all of which move before a price does.
