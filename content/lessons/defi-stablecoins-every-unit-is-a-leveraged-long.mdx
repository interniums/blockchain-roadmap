Somebody wants three times exposure to ETH without leaving the chain. They lock ETH in a vault, mint
stablecoin against it as debt, buy more ETH with the proceeds, lock that too, and repeat until the
collateral ratio stops permitting it.

Follow the stablecoin they minted. It got sold on a venue. It is now in somebody else's wallet,
paying a contractor, sitting in a treasury, or providing liquidity in a pool. To that holder it is a
dollar. It behaves like a dollar, it is denominated like a dollar, and its provenance is completely
invisible.

But it exists because a stranger wanted leverage. And when that stranger closes the trade — sells
the ETH, repays the debt — those particular dollars are destroyed. Not redeemed, not transferred:
burned out of existence, because they were never anything other than the liability side of somebody
else's position.

That is the entire character of a collateralised-debt-position stablecoin, and almost everything
surprising about this design follows from it.

## Supply is a demand curve, not a decision

For a [fiat-backed token](/c/stable-fiat-backed), supply is an issuer decision constrained by
inflows: somebody wires dollars, the issuer mints. For a CDP stablecoin there is no issuer in that
sense. Supply is the aggregate of every open vault's debt, and vaults open when borrowing is
attractive and close when it is not.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-overview" />

Three consequences, none of them obvious from the ratio dashboards.

**Supply is procyclical, in the wrong direction.** Leverage demand rises when collateral prices are
rising and collapses when they fall. So the stablecoin's supply expands into an ebullient market and
contracts into a frightened one — contracts, that is, exactly when holders most want dollar
balances. A dollar whose quantity shrinks during a crisis is a strange dollar, and it is a
structural feature rather than a management failing.

**There is no fundraising path.** An issuer of a fiat-backed token that wants more supply asks
customers to wire more money. A CDP system that wants more supply has to make borrowing cheaper, and
then wait for borrowers to respond — which is why these systems ship an explicit rate lever, and is
the whole of lesson four.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-overview" />

**Solvency is a lending problem wearing a currency's clothes.** Every mechanism from the lending and
liquidation modules applies here unchanged: collateral factors, liquidation thresholds, oracle
quality, keeper liveness, [cascades](/c/liq-cascade), [bad debt](/c/lend-bad-debt). The system is a
lending market. The only twist is that the borrowed asset is the one whose price is supposed to be
stable — so a liquidation, which normally means selling collateral to buy back a borrowed asset, now
means buying the stablecoin, which pushes the stablecoin's own price *up* at the moment the system
is under stress. Keep that in your pocket; it is the mechanism behind the upward depeg later in this
module.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" />

## What the mint-side arbitrage actually costs

Lesson one established that the peg is whatever the arbitrage band allows. For a CDP design the two
sides of that band are asymmetric in a way worth deriving, because the asymmetry explains why these
tokens sit slightly under par for long stretches and then spike above it violently.

**The below-peg side is cheap.** Trading under a dollar, an existing borrower buys the stablecoin at
a discount and repays their own debt at par. They needed to buy it anyway. The friction is a swap
fee. No new capital, no new risk.

**The above-peg side is expensive**, and here is the derivation.

**Subgoal 1 — enumerate what the arbitrageur must commit.** To mint one unit they must lock `c`
dollars of collateral, where `c` is the reciprocal of the maximum loan-to-value — 1.5 at a
sixty-six per cent LTV. They sell the minted unit at the market price `p`. Later they buy one unit
back at `p'` and repay, freeing the collateral.

**Subgoal 2 — write the profit and loss over a holding period `T` years.** Let `s` be the annual
stability fee on the debt and `ρ` the return the arbitrageur forgoes on the capital they had to lock
beyond the one dollar of value they received.

```text
PnL  =  p  -  p' * (1 + s*T)  -  (c - 1) * ρ * T
```

**Subgoal 3 — set the break-even condition.** Assume the trade works, meaning the price returns to
par, so `p' = 1`. Then the minimum premium that makes minting worthwhile is

```text
p  >  1 + s*T + (c - 1) * ρ * T
```

**Subgoal 4 — put illustrative numbers through it.** These are not any protocol's live parameters;
they are round figures chosen to expose the shape. Take `c = 1.5`, `s = 8%`, `ρ = 4%` and a holding
period of thirty days, so `T = 30/365 = 0.0822`. Then `s*T = 0.658%` and
`(c - 1) * ρ * T = 0.5 * 4% * 0.0822 = 0.164%`. Break-even premium: about **0.82 per cent**.

**Subgoal 5 — add the term that is not in the formula.** While the vault is open the arbitrageur is
short the stablecoin and long the collateral, at leverage. A collateral drawdown liquidates them.
That is not a cost you can express as a rate; it is a tail, and rational actors price tails
generously. Call it another fifty to a hundred basis points of required premium and the honest
answer is that nobody mints into a premium below roughly one per cent.

**Subgoal 6 — read the asymmetry off the result.** Below par, the correcting trade costs a swap fee
and needs no view. Above par, it costs interest, locked capital and liquidation risk, and needs a
view that the premium closes. So the band is *narrow below and wide above*. Which is exactly what
the record shows: the crvUSD incident of June 2024 was an **upward** depeg that persisted across
many blocks, and the mechanism that was supposed to correct it was automated precisely because
waiting for a human to accept that cost is not a peg
defence.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" /><Cite src="curve-crvusd-overview" />

<Aside kind="note">
Notice what this derivation does to a common piece of advice. "If it trades above a dollar, just
mint and sell" is true and useless, in the same way that "if it trades below a dollar, just redeem"
was true and useless in lesson one. Both sentences hide a capital commitment and a risk position.
Every time you catch yourself saying arbitrage will fix something, write down the arbitrageur's
balance sheet for the duration of the trade. If you cannot, you do not yet know whether the trade
exists.
</Aside>

## The peg stability module, read as code

Given how expensive the mint side is, the obvious move is to build a facility where minting is free,
instant and riskless — by accepting an asset already worth a dollar.<Cite src="dss-psm-source" />

That is a peg stability module, and it is short enough to read in full. The canonical implementation
exposes two functions. `sellGem` takes `gemAmt` of a whitelisted asset, computes a fee as
`gemAmt * tin`, and hands back the amount minus that fee in stablecoin. `buyGem` does the reverse,
charging `gemAmt * tout` on the way out. Both fees are governance parameters set through the same
`file` mechanism as everything else in the system, so the band this facility offers is a value in
storage rather than a property of the design.<Cite src="makerdao-psm-docs" /><Cite src="dss-psm-source" />

Now the line that tells you what the module really is. Inside `sellGem`, after joining the gem, the
contract calls the core vault engine with the collateral delta and the debt delta set to *the same
number* — it locks `gemAmt` of the asset and draws `gemAmt` of stablecoin against
it.<Cite src="makerdao-psm-docs" />

Read that as an engineer and three facts fall out at once.

**A PSM is a vault, not a swap.** There is no counterparty. The whitelisted asset becomes collateral
in the system, and new stablecoin is created against it.<Cite src="dss-psm-source" />

**It is a vault at exactly one hundred per cent collateralisation.** Equal ink and equal art. There
is no buffer, no liquidation threshold, no room for the collateral to fall.

**Which is only permissible because the system prices that collateral at par by assumption.** A
vault with no over-collateralisation passes the engine's safety check only if the collateral is
valued at or above the debt. The asset in the PSM is not marked to market; it is *defined* as a
dollar. Hold that thought until lesson seven, where a different set of protocols made the same
assumption about a different asset and it was not true.

**And the only bound is the ceiling.** Since the module is a vault, the amount that can pass through
it is capped by the debt ceiling on that collateral type.<Cite src="makerdao-psm-docs" /> That ceiling is the sole limit on how much
of the stablecoin's backing can become the whitelisted asset.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" />

## What the ceiling did in March 2023

The peg-stability design works, in the narrow sense that it does what it says. The band collapses to
`tin` plus `tout`, which can be zero, and the peg gets very tight.<Cite src="makerdao-psm-docs" />
That is why these modules became ubiquitous.

The Federal Reserve's study of the March 2023 episode is the best available account of what else
they do, and it is worth following in sequence because every step is a mechanism you now
understand.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" />

**Step one: the module became an exit, not a stabiliser.** With the fiat-backed token's own primary
redemption suspended over the weekend and exchanges no longer taking it at par, its holders needed a
door. The PSM was a hard-coded, zero-haircut facility that would take that token at one for one and
hand back a different stablecoin, and it kept doing so because that is what the code
said.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" />

**Step two: the ceiling bound within hours.** The only safeguard was a 950 million dollar daily cap,
and minting hit it on Friday 10 March and again on Saturday after the facility
reopened.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> Around a billion dollars of the distressed asset
was deposited on each of those two days, minting an equivalent amount of the CDP
stablecoin.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" />

**Step three: the price of the healthy token was dragged to the price of the distressed one.** Both
fell in tandem while the module was open, and a differential opened up only once the cap was reached
and the arbitrage stopped.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> This is lesson one's band running
in the direction nobody plans for: a tight band transmits *both* ways, and if you pin your asset to
something that breaks, you have pinned yourself to the break.

**Step four: the collateral pool rebalanced toward the distressed asset.** The Fed's account is
blunt about the composition shift — during the crisis the pool moved from higher-quality collateral
such as ETH, over-collateralised at 150 per cent, toward the distressed fiat-backed token, which was
now under-collateralised at less than 100 per cent because it had itself
depegged.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> The CDP system's own defence — borrowers buying the
discounted stablecoin to repay loans and burn supply — was vastly outweighed by the volume minted
through the module.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" />

**Step five: the contagion spread to assets with no exposure at all.** Two further modules accepted
two smaller fiat-backed stablecoins. As the CDP token fell, traders bought it at a discount and
exchanged it through those modules for the smaller tokens, which then sold off. Over 400 million of
one of them was withdrawn — more than half its total outstanding supply — and it traded down to
around 91 cents; the other reached about 96.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> Neither had any
connection to the failed bank. They were connected to a contract that promised them at par.

**Step six: governance could not act inside the incident window.** Emergency parameter changes were
proposed on the Saturday morning — cut the daily cap to 250 million, raise the fee to one per cent —
and passed in a little over two hours. Existing governance rules imposed a 48-hour delay, so they
executed on Monday 13 March, by which time the public intervention had already resolved the
turmoil.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" />

### The governance latency is the real parameter

Step six deserves separating out, because it generalises past this incident and past this design.

The module's exposure is bounded by a number that governance sets. Governance operates on a
timescale of days: proposal, discussion, vote, timelock. The exposure fills on a timescale of hours,
because it is filled by automated arbitrage responding to a price. Whenever a parameter that bounds
risk is adjustable more slowly than the risk accumulates, that parameter is effectively fixed during
any incident, and its value during calm weather is the value you will have during the crisis.

The practical form of the question is: **what is the worst outcome reachable at current parameters
before anyone can change them?** Not what the parameters are, and not what governance intends to do.
The reachable set, at today's settings, over the length of your slowest approval path. For a
peg-stability module that is the entire ceiling, and the answer to "should the ceiling be this large"
becomes a question you can actually decide.

<Aside kind="warn">
This is also where the two halves of this module collide with the law. The regulated payment
stablecoin category requires reserves drawn from an enumerated list of government and bank
instruments.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> A CDP system with a large peg-stability balance is,
in economic substance, part reserve-backed — but the reserve is somebody else's token, held as
collateral in a vault, and the CDP token itself has no path into the regulated category regardless.
Lesson nine takes that up properly. The point here is that "how much of our backing is the
whitelisted asset" is simultaneously a risk question, a decentralisation question and a regulatory
one, and it is answered by a single integer in a governance parameter.
</Aside>

<Answer>
**The module's balance goes to its ceiling.** Buying the distressed token at 0.94 and swapping it
through the module at par for a token trading near one dollar is a six-cent arbitrage at zero fee
with no capital lock-up and no liquidation risk — by an enormous margin the cheapest trade available
anywhere in this system. It runs until the module holds 400 million, which is the ceiling, and it
runs in minutes, not hours.

**The CDP token's price is dragged toward 0.94, and how close depends on relative size.** The
module mints 100 million of new supply into a market that did not ask for it, while the arbitrageurs
sell that supply to realise the profit. This is the tandem move the Fed's account
documents.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> Once the ceiling binds, the two prices are free to
separate again — and note the perverse timing: the peg improves at the moment the arbitrage *stops*,
which will read on a dashboard as the crisis abating.

**Collateral composition moves from 500/300 to 400/400 in nominal terms and worse in real terms.**
The module's holding is marked at par by assumption but worth 0.94, so the real backing is 376
million, and total real collateral behind 900 million of supply is 500 million of ETH — itself
volatile — plus 376. The system is not insolvent; it is materially less over-collateralised than
its own dashboard reports, and the gap is invisible because the oracle is a constant.

**The bounding parameter is the ceiling** — it is the only quantity that caps the exchange, since
the fees are zero and eligibility is open. A fee rise widens the band and slows the drain; a ceiling
cut stops it. In March 2023 governance chose both.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" />

**It will not be made in time because the drain is minutes and the process is days.** Proposal,
vote, timelock. The historical precedent had a two-hour vote and a 48-hour delay, executing after
the event had already resolved by other

the incident is the one that was set during calm — which means the ceiling must be sized as a
standing risk decision, not as a crisis lever.

**What a designer should take from this.** Either give the ceiling a fast path — a guardian able to
cut it inside one block, with the accountability that implies — or set it at a level you are willing
to be fully drawn on any given morning. What you may not do is set a large ceiling and hold the
intention of reducing it if something happens, because that intention is not implemented anywhere.

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** is the other lever: an interest rate that changes how many vaults are open at
all. It is slower than a peg-stability module and structurally different, because it works on the
supply of debt rather than on the composition of collateral — and one design automates the
arbitrage itself with contracts that act on pool imbalance.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-overview" />

**Lesson five** takes the same "where does the redemption asset come from" question to a design that
answers "we print it", and shows why that answer converts negative feedback into positive.

**Lesson seven** returns to the sentence you should now find alarming — *the collateral is priced at
par by assumption* — and shows what happened in November 2025 when several lending protocols made
that assumption about an asset whose value had already fallen.

**And when reviewing any protocol**, the transferable move is the composition question. For every
asset a system holds or accepts, ask what fraction of the total it may become at current parameters,
how quickly it can get there, and who can change the bound before it does. That is three queries and
one governance-config read, and it would have flagged every incident in this lesson in advance.
