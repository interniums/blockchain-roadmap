Open the contract for a large fiat-backed stablecoin and there is nothing to review.

An access-controlled `mint(to, amount)`. A `burn`. An allowance-based `transferFrom` you could
recite from memory. A pause. A blacklist. Behind a proxy, so an upgrade is possible, which you know
how to check. You could audit the whole thing before lunch and your findings would be about decimals
and event indexing.

That emptiness is easy to read as reassurance. The reflex from six tracks of threat modelling is
that risk lives where the code is complicated. Invert it here. **The contract is simple because the
product is not on chain.** Every property
that determines whether this token is worth a dollar — which assets the reserve holds, at which
institutions, in which jurisdiction, redeemable on which calendar — sits somewhere your tools do not
reach and your invariants cannot express.

A fiat-backed stablecoin is an IOU. The chain records who holds the IOU. It records nothing about
the debtor.

## What chain data actually tells you

Be precise about the boundary, because "off chain" is often used loosely and the split here is
sharp.

**Visible, cheaply and continuously.** Total supply and its history. Every mint and burn, with
timestamps, which together give you the primary market's activity — the same series the Federal
Reserve's study of March 2023 used to show that redemptions surged before the issuer's public
statement and collapsed to almost nothing over the weekend.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" />
Holder distribution. The addresses that hold minting authority. Whether the proxy admin is a
multisig and who signs it. Whether a given address is blacklisted.

**Invisible, always.** Whether the reserve exists. What it is composed of. Whether it is pledged to
somebody else — a question the statute now answers with a rehypothecation
prohibition.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> Whether the bank holding it opens on Monday.

That asymmetry produces a specific failure of intuition among people who work on chain: the search
for an on-chain cause. In March 2023 there was none — with one exception worth internalising. The
mint-and-burn series *did* move first, because informed parties redeemed ahead of the public
announcement.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> Primary-market flow is the only window from the
chain into the reserve.

## The reserve is not one asset, and the differences are the whole story

"Backed one for one" is a ratio. It says nothing about what the numerator is made of, and the
numerator is where the behaviour lives.

The clearest available taxonomy of reserve quality is, of all things, statutory. The United States
law governing payment stablecoins enumerates what a permitted issuer's reserves may consist of, and
reading the list as an engineer rather than as a lawyer is instructive because it is a ranking by
how an asset behaves in a crisis.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> The permitted categories are
coins and currency or money in an account at a Federal Reserve Bank; demand deposits or insured
shares at an insured depository institution, subject to limitations the banking regulators may set
for safety and soundness; Treasury bills, notes or bonds with a remaining maturity of ninety-three
days or less; overnight repurchase agreements backed by such Treasury bills; overnight reverse
repurchase agreements collateralised by Treasuries and overcollateralised on standard market terms;
shares in a registered government money market fund invested solely in the preceding categories; any
similarly liquid federal-government-issued asset the regulator approves; and tokenised forms of
several of the above.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" />

Three engineering observations fall straight out of that list.

**Maturity is capped, and the cap is short.** Ninety-three days is not a yield decision, it is a
mark-to-market decision: a short bill's price barely moves when rates do, so selling it in a hurry
does not crystallise a loss.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> Any reserve holding long-duration
paper is solvent on an accrual basis and potentially insolvent on a liquidation basis, and a run
forces the liquidation basis.

**A bank deposit is a different kind of object from a bill.** A Treasury bill is a claim on the
sovereign, held in custody, saleable to anybody, priced continuously. An uninsured deposit is an
unsecured claim on one company, available only while that company is open and solvent. The statute
puts both on the list, and then hedges the deposit category alone with a power for regulators to
limit it for safety-and-soundness reasons — which is legislative drafting doing exactly what a risk
register would do.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" />

**Rehypothecation is prohibited, with narrow exceptions.** Reserves may not be pledged, rehypothecated
or reused, except for margin on the permitted repo categories, standard custodial obligations, or
creating liquidity to meet expected redemptions — the last specifically contemplating selling bills
into repo of ninety-three days or less.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> The exception is the
interesting part: it concedes that a reserve must be *convertible on demand*, not merely *owned*.

<Aside kind="warn">
Dates matter here and are easy to garble. The Act was approved on 18 July 2025; it requires the
relevant regulators and the Treasury to promulgate implementing regulations not later than one year
after enactment, and it takes effect on the earlier of eighteen months after enactment or a hundred
and twenty days after final regulations are issued.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> Whether
those rulemakings were completed on schedule is a separate fact to check against the regulators, not
to infer from the statute. Read the list above as the standard the category is being held to, and
verify the timetable before asserting that any particular obligation binds today.
</Aside>

## Worked example: pricing an eight-per-cent hole

Here is the exercise that separates reading a depeg from watching one, and it takes six steps and
arithmetic you can do in your head.

**Subgoal 1 — establish the accounting identity.** Let a token be backed one for one, so reserves
`R` divided by tokens outstanding `N` equals 1.00. Suppose a fraction `s` of `R` becomes impaired,
with an eventual recovery rate `r` on the impaired part. Backing per token afterwards is

```text
B(r) = (1 - s) + s * r
```

**Subgoal 2 — put in the real numbers and bound the worst case.** On the evening of Friday 10 March
2023 the issuer of USDC disclosed that 3.3 billion dollars of reserves — around eight per cent of
the total at the time — were held as uninsured deposits at a bank that had entered receivership that
morning.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> So `s = 0.08`, and the worst case, total loss of
that tranche, is `B(0) = 0.92`. Ninety-two cents. Note that this bound holds without knowing anything
about the recovery process, which is what makes it useful in the first hour of an incident.

**Subgoal 3 — add the issuer's own capital as a junior buffer.** Shareholders absorb losses before
holders do. A later filing with the securities regulator put the issuer's total stockholders' equity
at 0.34 billion dollars as of the end of 2023, a little over a tenth of the 3.3 billion at
risk.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> Back out the reserve base from the two cited figures —
3.3 billion at roughly eight per cent implies a total near forty-one billion — and that cushion is
under one cent per token. So the buffer improves the worst case from about 0.920 to about 0.928 and
no further. Small buffers are worth computing precisely because computing them is how you
learn they are small.

**Subgoal 4 — compare with what the market actually printed.** The token traded down to
eighty-six cents.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> Set `B(r) = 0.86` and solve: `r = -0.75`.
A recovery rate cannot be negative. Depositors do not owe the bank money.

**Subgoal 5 — draw the conclusion the algebra forces.** The market price was not an estimate of
backing. It could not have been, because no assumption about the impaired tranche produces that
number. Something else was being priced.

**Subgoal 6 — name the something else, and be honest about the ambiguity.** Two candidates, and the
evidence does not cleanly separate them. The first is a **liquidity discount**: with primary
redemption suspended over a weekend, holding the token meant waiting an unannounced length of time
for an exit, while selling meant exiting now, and the gap between those is a price. The second is
**contagion beyond the disclosed tranche**: a second bank entered receivership that same weekend and
another stablecoin holding reserves there also broke its peg, so a rational holder was pricing some
probability that the disclosed eight per cent was not the whole of the
exposure.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> Both were live. What the algebra establishes is
only the negative: whatever the eighty-six cents was, it was not the solvency arithmetic.

**The falsification test settles which one dominated, after the fact.** If the price had been a
solvency estimate, reopening redemption would not have repaired it — the hole would still be there.
Redemptions resumed on Monday 13 March and the price returned to par, having already recovered
sharply on the Sunday-evening announcement that depositors would be
protected.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> A gap that closes the moment the exit reopens was
a gap about the exit. That distinction is the subject of the last lesson in this module, and this is
the cleanest instance of it in the record.

## The banking calendar is a protocol parameter

Take the previous lesson's five terms — eligibility, size, fee, latency, refusability — and notice
that for a fiat-backed token the whole of *latency* is set by an institution that has never heard of
your protocol.

Wire settlement runs on business days. A weekend is a two-day pause in the mint-and-burn loop by
default, and a bank holiday is another. During that pause the token continues to trade every second,
on venues that never close, at a price nobody can arbitrage back. The issuer's own explanation in
March 2023 was exactly this — that issuance and redemption were constrained by the working hours of
the US banking system.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" />

The engineering consequence is that a fiat-backed token has a **duty cycle**.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> Roughly five days in
seven its price is tethered by an arbitrage loop; the rest of the time it is a free-floating asset
whose behaviour is governed entirely by venue depth. Any system of yours that treats such a token as
a dollar-denominated numeraire assumes the loop is closed, and that assumption is false about a
third of the calendar even in good weather.

Statute has since put a floor under one part of this. A permitted issuer must publicly disclose its
redemption policy with clear procedures for timely redemption, and discretionary limitations on
timely redemption may be imposed only by the named regulators rather than at the issuer's own
choosing; fees may be changed only on at least seven days' notice to
consumers.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> That converts *refusability* from an unbounded
discretion into a supervised one for tokens inside the category — and leaves it exactly where it
was for tokens outside it.

## The freeze function is a property of the asset

Now the part self-custody does not solve.

A blacklist or freeze function means a balance you control with a key you alone hold can be rendered
untransferable by somebody else's transaction. No amount of hardware wallet discipline, no
[delegation](/c/evm-accounts-delegation-is-not-an-allowance) arrangement, no multisig changes it,
because the check runs inside the token contract on the path your transfer must take.

This is not an overreach to be designed away. It is a requirement of the category. The statute
defines a lawful order as one requiring a person to seize, freeze, burn or prevent the transfer of
the payment stablecoins they issued, identifying the tokens or accounts with reasonable
particularity and subject to review or appeal.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> It then makes the
capability a condition of market access for foreign issuers: a service provider may not make a
foreign-issued payment stablecoin available in the United States unless that issuer has the
technological capability to comply, and will comply, with such
orders.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" />

So "does this token have a freeze function" is the wrong question — inside the regulated category it
must. The right questions are the ones you would ask of any privileged capability in a threat model:
who holds the key, what process gates its use, is the action observable on chain, and what is your
product's behaviour when a user's balance becomes untransferable mid-flow. That last one is a
product decision most teams discover during an incident.

## An examination is not an audit

One more distinction, because the vocabulary is deliberately close and the difference is real.

The statute requires a permitted issuer to publish the composition of its reserves monthly on its
website, including the total tokens outstanding and, for each category of reserve instrument, the
amount, the average tenor and the geographic location of custody.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" />
It further requires that the previous month-end report be *examined* by a registered public
accounting firm each month, and that the chief executive and chief financial officers certify the
report's accuracy, with criminal penalties for a knowingly false
certification.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" />

An examination of a month-end report is a point-in-time procedure over a management-prepared
statement, backed here by personal criminal liability. It is a much stronger signal than nothing,
and it is still not a continuous proof of reserves. The reserve is verified periodically by a
professional third party under a legal duty — a good arrangement, and a different kind of guarantee
from the one you get when you can query the backing yourself. That distinction becomes the
diagnostic question for the fourth category in lesson seven, where the reported backing had no
examiner behind it at all.<Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" />

<Answer>
**What it gets right.** Duration risk is genuinely handled. Bills at a 45-day average tenor and
overnight reverse repo both mark close to par and can be converted quickly, which is the property
the ninety-three-day maturity cap in statute exists to
produce.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> Eighty-five per cent of this reserve behaves well in a
forced sale.

**What it misses, first: the fifteen per cent is a set of unsecured claims on four companies.**
Applying the identity from the worked example with `s = 0.15` gives a worst case of `B(0) = 0.85`
before any issuer capital — and the 2023 precedent had a far smaller
fraction.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> Spread across four banks the realistic single-name exposure is nearer
`s = 0.0375` each, giving `B(0) = 0.9625` per bank failure — unless the four are correlated, which
banks under stress are. The undisclosed identities are the problem: without them you cannot tell
whether this is four independent exposures or one exposure written four times, and that distinction
changes the answer by more than ten cents.

**What it misses, second: the redemption terms make the peg institutional.** Verified counterparties
only, next-business-day settlement. From the previous lesson's band derivation, latency `T` of one
business day — three days across a weekend — adds funding and risk carry to the friction, so the
band is materially wider than the zero fee suggests. Retail holders do not have a redemption; they
have a venue.

**What it misses, third: over-reservation of 0.2 per cent is not a buffer.** Two basis points of
excess covers rounding and accrued interest. It does not cover the loss of one uninsured deposit. Do
not let a number above 100 per cent do the work of a capital ratio.

**The dashboard quantity.** Not the reserve ratio, which will read 100.2 per cent right up to the
moment it does not. Track **uninsured single-name banking exposure as a percentage of reserves**,
and next to it **redemption capacity available today** — the amount that can actually be settled
before the next banking close. The first is the size of the hole you could take; the second is the
width of the door while you are taking it. Both are disclosable, and an issuer that will not
disclose the first has answered the question.
</Answer>

## Where this pays off

**The next two lessons** invert every property of this design. Collateral becomes volatile and
on chain, so the reserve is continuously verifiable and continuously at risk; supply stops being an
issuer decision and becomes the aggregate of user leverage; and the mechanism you had nothing to
review becomes the whole subject.

**Lesson three in particular** shows the two designs collide: the peg-stability module that pins a
crypto-backed stablecoin to a fiat-backed one imports everything in this lesson wholesale — the
banking calendar, the freeze authority, the uninsured deposit — into a system built specifically to
avoid them.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" />

**Lesson nine** returns to the statute and shows that the requirements catalogued here are not a
compliance checklist bolted onto a design space but a boundary drawn through it, with most of the
designs in this module on the far side.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" />

**And in production**, the takeaway is a monitoring change with a one-line justification. Alerting on
the price of a fiat-backed stablecoin tells you about the venue. Alerting on primary-market
mint-and-burn flow tells you about the reserve, roughly a day earlier, and it is a log query you
already know how to write.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" />
