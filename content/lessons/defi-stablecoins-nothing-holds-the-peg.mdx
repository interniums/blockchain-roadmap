At 11:37 in the morning, Eastern time, on Friday 10 March 2023, the Federal Deposit Insurance
Corporation took Silicon Valley Bank into receivership. The bank had absorbed more than forty
billion dollars of withdrawals in a single day.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> About twelve
hours later, at ten that evening, Circle disclosed that 3.3 billion dollars of USDC reserves —
around eight per cent of the total at the time — sat in that bank and could not be
withdrawn.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" />

Now put the on-chain view next to that.

The USDC contract did not pause. It did not revert. Transfers settled at the same gas cost they had
settled at on Thursday. `totalSupply` moved only as mint and burn instructions arrived, and over the
weekend almost none arrived, because the issuer's own statement was that issuance and redemption
were constrained by the working hours of the US banking system.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" />
The deployed bytecode was identical to Thursday's. Every function did exactly what it had always
done.

By Saturday the token traded at eighty-six cents.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" />

Nothing in the code broke, because nothing in the code had been holding the price up.

That sentence is the whole module in miniature, and it is worth resisting for a moment, because the
reflex you have built over six tracks is to look for the defect. There is none here. A stablecoin
contract is a ledger: it maps addresses to balances and enforces conservation. It has no opcode that
reaches into a Curve pool and sets a marginal price. It cannot. Price is a property of a market, and
a market is other people.

So if the contract does not hold the peg, what does?

## A peg is an equilibrium, and equilibria have arbitrageurs

Here is the frame this module runs on, and every design in it is an instance.

A stablecoin design does not set a price. It creates a **right** — a right to redeem the token for
something, or a right to mint the token by supplying something — and it prices that right at par.
When the market price of the token drifts away from par, exercising the right becomes profitable.
Somebody exercises it. Exercising it moves the market price back toward par, because redemption
takes tokens out of circulation and minting puts them in.

The peg is the fixed point of that loop. It is produced by traders, continuously, for
money.<Cite src="ethena-peg-arbitrage-docs" />

Which means every question you will ever ask about a peg reduces to three:

1. **What trade does a deviation create?** Concretely: buy where, redeem or mint what, sell what,
   for how much.
2. **Who is permitted to make it?** Anyone with an address, or a whitelist of institutions that
   passed onboarding.
3. **What does it cost them?** In fees, in capital tied up, in time, and in the risk they carry
   while the round trip is open.

Curve's own developer documentation for crvUSD is written in this register rather than the
promotional one: the peg is described as something the protocol's mechanisms *work toward*, with
PegKeeper contracts and a rate lever as the instruments, not as a property the token
has.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-overview" /><Cite src="curve-pegkeepers-docs" /> Ethena's documentation
is more explicit still, and names the arbitrage directly: if USDe is worth less on an external
market than through the protocol, an approved user buys it there and redeems it at par; if it is
worth more, they mint at par and sell.<Cite src="ethena-peg-arbitrage-docs" /> That is the loop,
written down by the issuer.

## Sizing the trade, because the size is the answer

"Arbitrage will close the gap" is the sentence everyone repeats and almost nobody sizes. Size it
once and the rest of the module gets easier, because you will stop treating arbitrage as a force and
start treating it as a trade with a profit-and-loss statement.

You already have the tools. A [constant-product pool](/c/constant-product) holds reserves and
enforces that their product is non-decreasing, and larger trades walk further along the curve, so
the [marginal price worsens with size](/c/price-impact). Real stable pools use the StableSwap
invariant instead, which is deliberately flatter near the balance point and only degenerates toward
constant product as the pool becomes lopsided.<Cite src="curve-stableswap-paper" /> That changes the
numbers a great deal and the shape of the argument not at all, so derive it on the curve you already
know and treat StableSwap as a depth multiplier.

**Subgoal 1 — write down what the arbitrageur actually receives.** Suppose a pool holds `x` units of
the stablecoin and `y` units of a reference dollar asset, so the pre-trade price of the stablecoin
is `p0 = y / x`. Redemption pays one dollar per token minus a fee `f`. Buying `dx` tokens out of the
pool costs, by the invariant,

```text
dy = y * dx / (x - dx)
```

**Subgoal 2 — turn that into an average price, not a marginal one.** The arbitrageur does not pay
`p0`; they pay the average over the whole trade, which is

```text
p_avg = dy / dx = y / (x - dx)
```

This is the step people skip, and skipping it is what makes arbitrage look unbounded. The average
price rises as `dx` grows, so profitability is self-limiting.

**Subgoal 3 — state the profitability condition and solve it for size.** The trade profits while the
average price paid is below the net redemption value, `p_avg` below `1 - f`. Substituting and
rearranging:

```text
y / (x - dx)  <  1 - f
x - dx        >  y / (1 - f)
dx            <  x * ( 1 - p0 / (1 - f) )
```

**Subgoal 4 — put numbers in and read what they mean.** Take a pool with ten million stablecoin
against 9.8 million dollars of the reference asset, so `p0 = 0.98`, and a redemption fee of ten
basis points. Then `dx` must stay below `10,000,000 * (1 - 0.98 / 0.999)`, which is about 190,190
tokens — a little under two per cent of the pool. Deploy half of that, 95,095 tokens: the pool
charges about 94,088 dollars, redemption returns about 94,999, and the profit is roughly 912
dollars, near 0.97 per cent on capital deployed for one round trip.

**Subgoal 5 — notice which quantity dominates.** The maximum profitable size is proportional to `x`,
the depth of the pool. It is *not* proportional to how much stablecoin exists, or to the size of the
reserve, or to anything on the issuer's balance sheet. A deviation in a thin pool is closed by a
small trade; the same deviation in a deep pool is worth closing at scale. This is why "the peg held"
and "the venue was liquid" are so often the same observation wearing different clothes.

**Subgoal 6 — check the boundary case.** At exactly `dx = 190,190` the profit computes to
approximately zero. It should: that is the size at which the average price paid equals the net
redemption value, which is the definition of the boundary. A derivation that does not reproduce its
own boundary condition is a derivation with an algebra error in it.

<Aside kind="note">
Run this once with your own numbers before continuing. Set `f` to zero and watch the maximum size
converge on `x * (1 - p0)`; set `f` to one per cent with `p0` at 0.995 and watch the expression go
negative, which is the algebra telling you there is no profitable trade at all — the deviation is
smaller than the friction. That negative result is the single most useful output of the formula,
because it defines the band inside which a peg is allowed to wander unmolested.
</Aside>

## The band is the friction, and the friction has five terms

The derivation gives a clean result: the market price can sit anywhere inside a band around
redemption value, and the width of the band is the total cost of the round trip. Below the band,
buying and redeeming pays. Above it, minting and selling pays. Inside it, nothing pays, and the
price does whatever the order flow does.

So the strength of a peg is the *narrowness of that band*, and the band is set by the terms of the
redemption right. Five terms, and you should learn to ask for all five by name, because issuers
routinely publish one and omit four.

**Eligibility.** Who may exercise the right. This is the term that most often turns out to be
"nobody you know". Ethena's mint and redeem contract is available to authorised, whitelisted
users.<Cite src="ethena-peg-arbitrage-docs" /><Cite src="ethena-how-usde-works" /> That is not a
criticism of the design — a whitelist is how an issuer meets its own compliance obligations — but it
determines who the arbitrageur can be, and therefore how quickly and at what size the loop runs.

**Size.** Minimum and maximum per exercise, and any aggregate ceiling. A ceiling is a hard stop on
the loop: past it, the arbitrage does not merely get less profitable, it becomes impossible. The
peg-stability module you will meet in lesson three is exactly this — a one-for-one swap facility
bounded by a debt ceiling — and in March 2023 that ceiling bound within
hours.<Cite src="dss-psm-source" /><Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" />

**Fee.** The `f` in the derivation, in both directions. The dss peg-stability module carries two of
them, `tin` on the way in and `tout` on the way out, each applied as a fraction of the swapped
amount.<Cite src="makerdao-psm-docs" /><Cite src="dss-psm-source" /> Raising a fee widens the band
by exactly that fee, which is
a lever an issuer can pull in a crisis and a fact an integrator should monitor.

**Latency.** How long between committing capital and receiving the proceeds. Same-block redemption
and next-business-day redemption are different products. Latency does not merely delay the profit;
it adds a risk premium, because the arbitrageur is exposed to further deviation while the round trip
is open, and either eats that risk or pays to hedge it. Both show up as a wider band.

**Refusability.** Whether the issuer may pause, suspend or decline. This is the term with no
continuous cost — it is fine, fine, fine, and then it is infinite. A right that can be suspended is
not a floor under the price. It is a floor under the price *until the moment you need it*, which is
a different thing, and the price chart will not show you the difference in advance.

### Adding latency to the band, quantitatively

The five terms are not equally hard to price. Fees are printed. Latency has to be converted, and the
conversion is worth doing once so that "T plus one settlement" stops sounding like an
administrative detail.

An arbitrageur buying at `p_avg` and redeeming at `1 - f` after a delay `T` earns the gross spread
once, but must fund the position across `T` and carry the risk that the deviation deepens. Write the
funding cost as `c` per unit per day and the hedging or risk charge as `h`. The trade clears only if

```text
(1 - f) - p_avg  >  (c + h) * T
```

The right-hand side is the part that does not exist for an instant, atomic redemption. At `T = 0`
the band collapses to the fee. At `T` of one business day, with a weekend in it, the band widens by
three days of carry and — this is the term that dominates — by whatever the arbitrageur charges
themselves for the possibility that the discount goes from two per cent to fifteen while they are
committed. In March 2023 that risk was not hypothetical, and the honest reading of the eighty-six
cent print is that it was the market's price for holding a claim across a weekend with an
unquantified hole in it.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" />

## Almost nobody redeems

Now the second half, and the part that makes depegs confusing to read.

The redemption right is exercised by a small number of parties in size. Everyone else exits the way
you would exit any token: they sell it on a venue, into another stablecoin or into whatever the pool
holds. So the price on your screen is a **secondary-market price**, produced by holders selling to
buyers, and it is connected to redemption value only through the arbitrageurs who move between the
two.

That connection is exactly as strong as the band. The Federal Reserve note on the March 2023 episode
makes the causal chain explicit in both directions: it was not until the primary market shut down
that USDC hit its low, and it was not until Circle resumed processing redemptions on Monday that the
token returned to par.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> The turn came at 6:15 in the evening
Eastern on Sunday 12 March, when the Treasury, the Federal Reserve Board and the FDIC jointly
announced that depositors would be protected; the secondary price recovered sharply on that
announcement and fully once redemptions resumed, with Circle reporting 3.8 billion dollars redeemed
and 0.8 billion minted in the days that followed.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> Meanwhile
secondary volume did the
opposite of drying up — hourly trading reached nearly two billion dollars on 11 March, with
decentralised venues carrying most of the activity, which is unusual for that market and tells you
where the pent-up redemption pressure went when the primary door was
shut.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" />

Sit with that asymmetry, because it has a direct engineering consequence. Suspending redemption is
the standard crisis response for a bank, and for a bank it works, because a closed branch cannot be
run on. A token trades whether or not the issuer is answering the phone. The note states this in one
line: unlike bank deposits, stablecoins are actively traded on secondary markets, so suspending
primary redemption cannot arrest a run the way temporarily closing a bank
can.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> The suspension removes the arbitrage that was
*narrowing* the deviation, and leaves the selling in place.

### What the price means once the link is cut

While arbitrage is live, the secondary price is an estimate of redemption value plus or minus the
band. When redemption is gated or suspended, it stops being that and becomes something else
entirely: the market's estimate of eventual recovery value, discounted for time and for the chance
of zero.

Those two quantities can be very far apart, and nothing on the chart distinguishes them. Analyses of
the November 2025 Stream Finance episode describe exactly this discontinuity — a token holding near
par right up to a withdrawal freeze, then repricing to a fraction of it within the day, with the
reported intraday low near twenty-six cents and roughly a hundred and sixty million dollars of
deposits stranded behind the freeze.<Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" /><Cite src="blockeden-stream-contagion" />
Those figures come from secondary reconstructions of the same few analyses rather than from
independently audited disclosure, so carry them as reported magnitudes rather than as measurements.
The structural point does not depend on the decimals: the price was near par because arbitrage was
still connecting it to a redemption right, and it was not near par one day later because that right
had been withdrawn.

## What to ask, in order

The whole of this lesson compresses into a short interrogation you can run against any token that
calls itself a dollar, and you will run it four more times in this module against four different
designs.

**One — what is the redemption asset?** Dollars in a bank, a basket of crypto collateral, newly
minted governance tokens, or a claim on a trading position. The answer decides which risk checklist
applies, and using the wrong checklist is the most common analytical failure in this area.

**Two — who may redeem?** If the answer is a whitelist, write down that the peg is defended by those
parties' willingness to warehouse risk, not by an impersonal
mechanism.<Cite src="ethena-peg-arbitrage-docs" />

**Three — at what fee, size and latency?** These three multiply into the band. If you cannot get
numbers, you cannot state the band, and you should say so rather than assume it is tight.

**Four — can it be refused?** Look for the pause authority in the contract, and for the discretion
in the terms of service. Both count.

**Five — where is the deep venue, and how deep is it?** From the derivation: the maximum profitable
arbitrage scales with pool depth, which is why protocols that take their own peg seriously build
instruments that act on pool state directly rather than waiting for a
trader.<Cite src="curve-pegkeepers-docs" /> A token with a strong redemption right and no liquid venue will
still print alarming deviations, because the trade that fixes them is too small to be worth anyone's
operational attention.

Note what is *not* on the list. Collateral ratio is not on it. Attestation frequency is not on it.
Those matter, and they are the subject of the next lesson, but they answer a solvency question,
whereas everything above answers a price question — and the two come apart precisely when it
matters, which is the subject of the last lesson in this module.

<Aside kind="warn">
One term on this list is law rather than design, and it is worth knowing early. The United States
statute governing payment stablecoins requires an issuer in that category to publicly disclose its
redemption policy, including procedures for timely redemption of outstanding
tokens.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> That is a disclosure obligation on issuers inside the
regulated category, and it tells you nothing about a token outside it. When the terms are not
published, that absence is itself the answer to question four.
</Aside>

<Check type="explain" concept="stable-peg-is-not-a-property">
A protocol ships a stablecoin with these properties. Redemption is one-for-one into USDC, available
to anyone, with no fee. The redemption contract holds two per cent of outstanding supply in USDC at
any time and is topped up by a keeper on a best-effort basis. The only venue is a StableSwap pool
holding about 1.5 per cent of supply. The token has traded between 0.9985 and 1.0012 for four
months.

The team argues the peg is strong: permissionless redemption, zero fee, and four months of a tight
range as evidence.

Say what the four-month range is and is not evidence of, and name the specific condition under which
this design deviates far.

<Answer>
**The range is evidence about the band, and the band is genuinely narrow — while the loop runs.**
Permissionless eligibility and a zero fee are the two strongest terms on the list, and with instant
settlement the friction is close to gas plus pool fee. A range of about twenty-seven basis points is
what that friction predicts. So the team's inference is correct as far as it goes.

**It is not evidence about size, and size is the unfunded term here.** The redemption contract holds
two per cent of supply. That is the *maximum aggregate* exercise of the right before the loop stops,
regardless of eligibility or fee, and the keeper top-up is best-effort — which is to say it is a
liveness assumption dressed as an implementation detail. Four calm months exercise the loop at
volumes far below the ceiling, so they test the fee and the eligibility and say nothing at all about
the ceiling.

**The condition under which it deviates far is redemption demand above two per cent of supply
arriving faster than the keeper replenishes.** At that point the arbitrage of the previous
paragraphs is not merely less profitable — the term `1 - f` disappears from the inequality, because
there is nothing to redeem into. The price stops being an estimate of redemption value and becomes
an estimate of recovery value, and the only remaining bid is the pool, which holds about 1.5 per
cent of supply.

**Sizing the damage takes one substitution.** With redemption unavailable, the marginal seller walks
the pool's curve alone. Using the constant-product approximation from earlier as a floor on the
damage — real StableSwap is flatter near balance and then steepens toward constant product as the
pool goes lopsided<Cite src="curve-stableswap-paper" /> — selling a further one
per cent of supply into a pool holding 1.5 per cent moves the price by tens of per cent, not by
basis points. The deviation is not proportional to the news; it is proportional to the ratio of flow
to depth.

**What to change, in order.** Publish the ceiling and monitor headroom as a first-class metric, not
the price. Replace best-effort replenishment with a committed source and an alarm on its failure.
Then either deepen the venue or accept, out loud, that the token's price is only defined for exit
sizes below a stated fraction of supply — which is a defensible product decision, and an
indefensible surprise.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** takes the simplest possible redemption asset — dollars in a bank — and shows
that the analysis moves entirely off chain, into reserve composition and banking hours, where none
of your tools reach and where the March 2023 episode actually happened.

**Lessons three and four** put the same interrogation to a design whose redemption asset is volatile
crypto collateral, where the mint side is a loan and the peg gets two extra instruments: a
one-for-one swap facility and an interest-rate lever.

**Lesson five** runs it against a design whose redemption asset is a token the protocol prints,
which makes the loop reflexive and turns the defence into the accelerant.

**Lesson six** runs it against a design whose backing is a live hedged position rather than an asset
in custody, and the derivation you just did is the reason its risk checklist is unrecognisable to
anyone carrying a collateral-ratio habit.

**And in production**, the immediate application is a monitor. Most teams alert on price and are
therefore alerted last. The band derivation tells you what to watch instead: redemption headroom
against its ceiling, venue depth against typical exit size, and whether the primary door is open at
all. Each of those moves before the price does, and each of them is a query you can already write.
