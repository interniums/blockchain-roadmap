Two candles, both red, both steep.

One is a dollar token falling to eighty-six cents on a Saturday in March 2023. It is back at par by
Monday afternoon, and the holders who did nothing lose nothing.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" />

The other is a dollar token falling to about twenty-six cents on a Tuesday in November 2025. It never
recovers, and the holders who did nothing lose almost everything.<Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" />

At the moment each chart was being drawn, they looked the same. This lesson is the procedure for
telling them apart in the hour when it matters, and it reduces to one distinction with a derivable
consequence: **is this a liquidity gap or a solvency gap?** In the first, waiting is correct and
arbitrage closes the deviation. In the second, waiting is the losing move and arbitrage is the
mechanism that drains what is left.

## Below and above are different questions

Start with the sign, because it changes the class of the problem entirely.

**A token trading below par is holders paying to exit.** That is what a solvency question looks like
when it is expressed as a price. It may not *be* a solvency problem — the March 2023 case was not —
but the shape of the behaviour is people accepting a loss for immediacy, and that is the behaviour a
run is made of. The Federal Reserve study of that episode frames stablecoins exactly this way: a
run-able liability of their issuer, susceptible like money market funds and bank deposits to crises
of confidence, contagion and self-reinforcing runs.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" />

**A token trading above par is buyers unable to acquire it fast enough.** That is a capacity or
access problem: minting is gated, slow, expensive, or somebody needs the token urgently for reasons
unrelated to its value. There are two quite different reasons for a premium and they are worth
separating.

**The first is a flight to quality.** During the March 2023 episode, while four stablecoins were
selling off, two others appreciated and traded marginally above their dollar peg as capital moved
into them.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> That premium is a compliment. It closes when the
panic does, and nobody is harmed.

**The second is forced demand, and it bites.** In June 2024 a liquidation cascade created enormous
demand for a CDP stablecoin, because liquidators must buy the borrowed asset to close positions and
the borrowed asset *was* the stablecoin. Its price spiked above par and stayed there across many
blocks, because the automated keepers that would normally sell into the premium were blocked by their
own anti-manipulation guard — the first successful update came roughly 525 blocks, about 45 minutes,
after the deviation began.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" />

And then the premium did damage nobody had modelled. The affected lending markets denominated
collateral against the stablecoin rather than against dollars, so the stablecoin trading *up* was
arithmetically identical to every collateral asset trading *down*. Users in an unrelated market —
one built on the assumption its collateral would hold near a dollar — were liquidated by a move in
the denominator.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" />

## The three tests, in the order you can run them

Now the downward case, which is where the money is. Three tests, ordered by how fast you can answer
them.

### Test one: is the primary door open?

This is the fastest and it is nearly decisive.

If redemption is operating, the market price is tethered to redemption value by the band from lesson
one, and any deviation larger than the band is a statement about the *friction*, not about the
backing. If redemption is suspended, gated, or has hit a ceiling, the tether is cut and the price is
the market's estimate of eventual recovery value.

The historical record makes the link explicit in both directions. The token did not hit its low until
the primary market shut down, and it did not return to par until the issuer resumed processing
redemptions.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> The other case froze deposits and redemptions at
the moment of disclosure, which removed the exact mechanism that could have stabilised the
price.<Cite src="blockeden-stream-contagion" />

So: check the redemption contract, the issuer's status page, and the ceiling — a ceiling counts as a
closed door once it binds, which in March 2023 took
hours.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> Two minutes of work.

### Test two: does the worst case reach this price?

This is lesson two's arithmetic, run under time pressure.

**Subgoal 1 — bound the impairment from what has been disclosed.** With a fraction `s` of backing at
risk and a recovery rate `r` on that fraction, backing per unit is `B(r) = (1 - s) + s * r`, and the
floor is `B(0) = 1 - s`.

**Subgoal 2 — compare the floor with the market price.** In March 2023 the disclosed exposure was
around eight per cent of reserves, giving a floor of 0.92, and the token traded at
0.86.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> The price was **below the worst case**. No assumption
about the impaired assets produces it.

**Subgoal 3 — read the verdict.** A price below the solvency floor cannot be a solvency estimate. It
is a time discount, a fear that the disclosure is incomplete, or both — and either way there is an
arbitrage: buy below the floor, wait for the door to reopen.

**Subgoal 4 — run the same test on the other case and watch it fail.** In November 2025 the disclosed
loss was around 93 million against roughly 160 million of user deposits — a floor somewhere near
zero-point-four before accounting for leverage, and reported leverage was above four with backing
around 170 million against roughly 530 million of borrowing.<Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" /><Cite src="blockeden-stream-contagion" />
At a price of 0.26, the market was inside the plausible range rather than below it. There was no
arbitrage, because there was nothing to arbitrage against.

**Subgoal 5 — note when this test is unavailable, and treat that as an answer.** The test needs a
denominator: total backing, verifiable. If the backing is a figure the issuer publishes and the
issuer is the entity under question, you cannot run it. That is not a gap in your analysis. It is the
finding.

### Test three: what does the price respond to?

A **liquidity gap** responds to news about the *door*: redemption resuming, a ceiling rising, a
backstop announced. In March 2023 the price recovered sharply on the Sunday evening announcement that
depositors would be protected — an announcement about access, not about the reserve's
composition.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> A **solvency gap** responds to news about the
*backing*, and mostly downward, because the first disclosure is rarely the last. Successive revisions
are the signature.

## Why waiting is the losing move in a solvency gap

Here is the derivation that makes the asymmetry concrete rather than a slogan, and it is the reason
"arbitrage will close the gap" needs its condition attached.

**Subgoal 1 — model sequential service.** A redemption facility pays par, first come first served,
until the reserve is gone. Let `R` be the redeemable reserve and `S` the supply, with `R` less than
`S`. The first `R` of supply redeems at 1.00; the remaining `S - R` receives whatever is left, which
in the limiting case is nothing. The payout is a step function of your position, not an average.

**Subgoal 2 — compute the expected value of queueing.** With no information about your position,
your expected recovery is `R / S` — the coverage ratio — with enormous variance, because the actual
outcomes are 1.00 and 0.

**Subgoal 3 — compare against selling.** Selling realises the market price `p` with certainty, so a
holder should queue rather than sell only when `R / S` — adjusted for their honest belief about how
fast they are relative to everyone else — exceeds `p`.

**Subgoal 4 — read what that does to everybody's incentives at once.** As `p` falls, queueing looks
better, which sounds stabilising. But every unit redeemed reduces `R` while reducing `S` by the same
amount, and when `R` is less than `S` that lowers the coverage ratio `R / S` for everyone still
holding. Each redemption makes the next holder's expected recovery worse. That is the run, expressed
as arithmetic, and note that **the redeemers are behaving perfectly rationally and are doing the
system no favours**.

**Subgoal 5 — state the consequence for the arbitrage advice.** In a liquidity gap, arbitrage closes
the deviation and transfers a fair profit to whoever supplied the immediacy. In a solvency gap, the
identical trade drains the remaining reserve to the fastest actors, and the holders who wait for a
recovery are the ones funding it — which is why the November 2025 freeze produced no recovery for
anyone who waited.<Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" /> The advice "arbitrage will close the gap" is not wrong; it is
missing its precondition, which is that there is enough behind the door for everyone.

<Aside kind="note">
The queue derivation also explains a behaviour that looks irrational and is not. Sophisticated
holders exit *before* they have finished analysing, because in a step-function payout the value of
being early exceeds the value of being right. The empirical work on the 2022 collapse found exactly
this stratification: wealthier and more sophisticated participants ran first and lost much less,
while later participants lost more and a significant fraction attempted to buy the
dip.<Cite src="terra-postmortems" /> If your process requires certainty before acting, your
process has decided to be in the second group.
</Aside>

## A runbook, with timeboxes

Put the three tests in an order somebody can follow at three in the morning.

**Minute zero to two — check the door.** Minting open? Redemption open? Ceiling hit? Who is eligible,
and are you? If the door is shut, skip to the last step: the price is now a recovery estimate and
your sizing decision is a credit decision.

**Minute two to ten — run the floor.** Disclosed exposure, worst-case backing, compare with market.
Price below the floor means liquidity. Price inside the plausible range means solvency, or means the
disclosure is incomplete, which you treat the same way.

**Minute ten to twenty — enumerate your indirect exposure.** The step teams skip, and usually the
largest number. Where is this asset collateral in positions you hold, what is denominated in it, and
which of your products quote it as a dollar? The November 2025 contagion travelled through collateral
listings, not through anybody's direct holding.<Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" />

**Ongoing — watch the right series.** Not the price. Primary-market mint and burn, which moved before
the price in March 2023 because informed parties redeemed ahead of the
announcement.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> Redemption-contract balance against supply,
since the instantly available tier is often a small fraction of
backing.<Cite src="ethena-peg-arbitrage-docs" /> And the protocol's own price aggregate against the
venue price, since a protocol that believes a stale number keeps acting on
it.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-overview" />

**The decision rule.** Liquidity gap: hold or buy, sized to your tolerance for the door staying shut
longer than expected — the historical recovery came within
days.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> Solvency gap: exit at the market price and stop
analysing, because in a step payout the expected value of further analysis is dominated by the cost
of the delay.<Cite src="blockeden-stream-contagion" />

<Check type="explain" concept="stable-depeg-asymmetry">
A dollar token you hold in treasury is at 0.968 and falling slowly over four hours. The issuer has
made no announcement. Redemption is open, whitelisted, with a published buffer of about one per cent
of supply in the redemption contract and a larger tranche held at custodians. Secondary depth at your
exit size would cost you about 40 basis points. Nothing else in the market is stressed.

Walk the three tests, state your read, and say what would flip it.

<Answer>
**Test one — the door is open, which is the strongest single signal available.** Redemption
operating means the price is tethered to redemption value by the friction band, and a 3.2 per cent
deviation is far wider than any plausible fee — so something is impeding the loop.

**And the buffer tells you which.** One per cent of supply is instantly redeemable; the rest is
reachable on an operational timescale.<Cite src="ethena-peg-arbitrage-docs" /> If today's redemption
demand exceeds that one per cent, whitelisted arbitrageurs are queueing behind custodian operations
and the latency term in the band has gone from minutes to hours. A wider band during elevated
redemption is the *expected* behaviour of a healthy system. This is the likeliest read.

**Test two — the floor test cannot run, and that is informative rather than alarming.** With no
disclosure there is no `s`, so there is no worst case to compute. The absence of an announcement
alongside a slow, orderly four-hour drift is much more consistent with flow than with news; solvency
news produces gaps and successive revisions, not
drifts.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /><Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" />

**Test three — watch what it responds to, and you have a clean experiment available.** If the price
firms when the custodian tranche is drawn down and redemptions clear, it was capacity. If it keeps
sliding through that event, the constraint is not capacity.

**The read: liquidity gap, hold.** Your exit costs 40 basis points against a 320 basis point
deviation, so selling crystallises most of the loss to avoid a risk you have not shown exists. The
asymmetry favours waiting *because the door is open*, which is the precondition the queue derivation
demands.

**What flips it, in order of how fast it should flip you.** Redemption pauses, is gated, or the
buffer stops replenishing — that is test one failing, and it converts the price from a friction
estimate into a recovery estimate in one step. Any disclosure of impairment, at which point run the
floor immediately and act on the arithmetic rather than on the announcement's tone. The deviation
gapping rather than drifting, which is the signature of information rather than flow. Or the discovery
that this asset is collateral in positions you hold elsewhere, which changes the size of the decision
even if it does not change the read.<Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" />

**One more thing to do while waiting, since it costs nothing.** Write down, now, the price at which
you would exit and the event that would trigger it. In a step-payout regime the failure mode is
deliberation, and a pre-committed rule is the only known defence against it.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The last lesson** in this module takes the same designs to a regulator, and it turns out the
liquidity-versus-solvency distinction is embedded in the statute too: the reserve list, the
redemption-policy disclosure and the limits on who may restrict timely redemption are all rules about
keeping the door open.

**In the incident-response module's terms**, this is a detection-to-action ladder for one class of
event: the three tests are cheap enough to run before you page anyone, and the decision rule tells
you which containment option is relevant.

**In treasury and product terms**, the artefact is a written policy: for every token you hold or
quote as a dollar, one page recording the redemption terms, the instantly available buffer, your own
exit depth, and the pre-committed rule. Written calmly it takes twenty minutes per token. Written
during an incident, it does not get written.
