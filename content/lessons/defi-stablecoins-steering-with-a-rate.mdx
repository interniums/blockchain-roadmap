Most protocols set interest rates by governance vote. One writes the rate down as a closed-form
function of the peg deviation and evaluates it on chain, so that nobody decides anything.

crvUSD's monetary policy contract computes the borrow rate as

```text
r = rate0 * exp(power)

power = (price_peg - price_crvusd) / sigma  -  DebtFraction / TargetFraction

DebtFraction = PegKeeperDebt / TotalDebt
```

where `rate0` is the baseline rate that applies when the PegKeepers carry no debt and the price is
exactly at par, `price_peg` is 1.00, `price_crvusd` comes from an aggregated oracle across several
Curve StableSwap pools, and `sigma` and `TargetFraction` are governance
parameters.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-monetary-policy" /> Both `r` and `rate0` are expressed per second
with eighteen decimals of precision, so the annualised figure is

```text
annualRate = (1 + rate / 1e18) ^ (365 * 24 * 60 * 60) - 1
```

which is compounding by the second.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-monetary-policy" />

That is the whole monetary policy. Four inputs, one exponential, no meeting. This lesson takes it
apart, because working the algebra tells you something a description cannot: exactly how hard this
lever pulls, and exactly how slowly.

## Reading the exponential

**Subgoal 1 — establish the sign convention and check it points the right way.** Below peg,
`price_crvusd` is under 1.00, so `price_peg - price_crvusd` is positive, `power` rises and the rate
rises. Expensive debt makes borrowers repay; repayment burns supply; less supply against unchanged
demand lifts the price. Above peg, everything reverses. It is negative feedback, and the sign is
worth verifying rather than assuming, because a monetary policy with the sign backwards is a
resonator.

**Subgoal 2 — find out what `sigma` actually does.** `sigma` divides the deviation, so it converts a
price gap into a dimensionless exponent. Take an illustrative `sigma` of 0.01 — this is a governance
parameter and not a constant, so treat it as a worked value rather than a live one. A price of 0.99
gives a first term of `(1.00 - 0.99) / 0.01 = 1`, and `exp(1)` is about 2.718. A price of 1.01 gives
`exp(-1)`, about 0.368.

So with that `sigma`, **a one-cent deviation multiplies or divides the per-second rate by `e`**.
`sigma` is the deviation that costs one factor of `e`. That single sentence is more useful than any
prose description of the contract, and you got it by substituting one number.

**Subgoal 3 — convert to something a borrower would recognise.** Per-second rates are unreadable.
Suppose `rate0` corresponds to five per cent annualised. From the compounding formula,
`rate0 / 1e18 = 1.05 ^ (1 / 31,536,000) - 1`, which is about `4.879e-8`.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-monetary-policy" />

Now multiply the per-second rate by `e` and re-annualise. Because annualisation is exponentiation,
multiplying the per-second rate by a factor `k` raises the annual gross rate to the power `k`:

```text
annual(k) ≈ 1.05 ^ k  -  1
```

**Subgoal 4 — read the numbers.** At `k = e = 2.718`, the annual rate goes from 5 per cent to
`1.05 ^ 2.718 - 1`, about **14.2 per cent**. At `k = 1/e = 0.368`, it goes to `1.05 ^ 0.368 - 1`,
about **1.8 per cent**. A one-cent depeg roughly triples the cost of borrowing, and a one-cent
premium roughly cuts it to a third.

**Subgoal 5 — check the boundary and the tails.** At `price_crvusd = 1.00` and no PegKeeper debt,
`power` is zero, `exp(0)` is one, and the rate is exactly `rate0`. That is the definition of the
baseline and it falls out of the algebra, which is the check that the algebra is right. In the tails
the exponential is doing something worth noticing: a five-cent depeg at this `sigma` is `exp(5)`,
about 148 times the per-second rate, which annualises to a number no borrower will ever pay. The
function does not saturate. That is a design choice, and it means the rate lever's *strength* is
never the binding constraint — its speed is.

**Subgoal 6 — account for the second term.** `DebtFraction` is PegKeeper debt over total debt,
divided by a target, and it *subtracts*.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-monetary-policy" /> PegKeeper debt
rises when the keepers have been depositing crvUSD into pools, which happens when crvUSD is trading
above par. So above the peg, both terms push the rate down at once: the price term because the price
is high, the debt term because the keepers have already had to act. Cheaper borrowing means more
minting means more supply means a lower price. Two instruments, one direction, no contradiction —
which is what you want and is not automatic. If you ever design a system with two peg levers, check
that they cannot fight.

<Aside kind="note">
Compare this with the [utilisation kink](/c/lend-rate-kink) from the lending module. Both are rate
curves that steepen; the difference is entirely in the input. A kinked curve reads *utilisation* and
is defending the pool's ability to honour withdrawals. This curve reads *price* and is defending a
peg. Same instrument, different controlled variable — and a system that needs both has to decide
which one wins when they disagree, because a stablecoin's borrow market has both a withdrawal
problem and a peg problem.
</Aside>

## Why the strongest lever is the wrong one in a crisis

The rate is unbounded above and instantaneous to compute. It still cannot defend against a run, and
the reason is transmission, not strength.

Trace the causal chain and put a timescale on each link. The oracle updates — an aggregated EMA
across several pools, so it is smoothed by construction, which is deliberate and also a
lag.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-overview" /> The rate updates. A borrower notices the rate. The borrower
decides whether the new cost of carry is worth it. The borrower acquires stablecoin to repay, which
means buying it on a venue — pushing the price up, which is the point. The debt is repaid and the
supply burns.

The slow link is the human in the middle, and it is slow in a specific way: borrowers are comparing
an annualised carrying cost against the expected return on a leveraged position. Tripling a five per
cent rate costs a borrower nine extra percentage points a year, which is about 2.5 basis points a
day. That is a real signal over weeks. It is nothing at all over the two hours in which a run
happens.

So the honest characterisation is: **the rate lever corrects drift, and does not touch a run.** It is
an excellent instrument for the condition it addresses — a persistent, modest deviation caused by
supply and demand being mismatched — and it is the wrong instrument for a fast dislocation, which is
precisely why systems that use it also keep a fast instrument alongside: a
[peg-stability module](/c/stable-psm), a reserve, or automated keepers.<Cite src="dss-psm-source" /><Cite src="curve-pegkeepers-docs" />

## PegKeepers: automating the arbitrageur

If the problem is that nobody performs the correcting trade fast enough, the fix is to pre-authorise
a contract to perform it.

That is what a PegKeeper is. Each holds a pre-minted allocation of crvUSD — out of circulation, and
correctly counted as such, until it is deposited — and is paired with one StableSwap pool containing
crvUSD and another fiat-redeemable dollar stablecoin. Its behaviour is restricted to exactly two
actions: depositing into that pool and withdrawing from it.<Cite src="curve-pegkeepers-docs" />

**Above par**, the keeper deposits crvUSD into the pool and receives LP tokens. More crvUSD in the
pool pushes its price down toward parity.<Cite src="curve-pegkeepers-docs" /> **Below par**, it
burns LP tokens to withdraw crvUSD, reducing the pool balance and pushing the price back up — and
this direction is only available if the keeper deposited earlier, because it needs LP tokens to
burn.<Cite src="curve-pegkeepers-docs" /> That constraint matters: the downward defence is bounded
by the upward defence that preceded it.

The trigger is pool state, and the `update` function is callable by any account, with a share of the
proceeds paid to the caller.<Cite src="curve-pegkeepers-docs" /> So this is not a bot the protocol
runs. It is an arbitrage the protocol has pre-funded and left on the table, which is a much more
robust arrangement — you are relying on somebody wanting money rather than on somebody's
infrastructure staying up.

Two design details are worth carrying, because both are the kind of thing you would get wrong.

**Why the price signal is an aggregate, not the pool.** The keeper acts on pool imbalance, but the
protocol's notion of the crvUSD price is an exponential moving average aggregated across several
Curve pools.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-overview" /> If the trigger and the signal were the same single
pool's instantaneous state, moving that pool would move the thing that decides whether to act — an
oracle you can push, with a pre-funded contract on the other side of it.

**Why V1 needed replacing.** The first implementation had a fifteen-minute cooldown between updates,
and an attacker could sandwich the update: balance the pool with a flash loan, call `update` so that
the keeper's action is minimised, then withdraw and repay — burning the keeper's next fifteen minutes
of capacity for the cost of the round trip.<Cite src="curve-pegkeepers-docs" /> A second failure mode
was worse: if the keeper had deposited and the *paired* stablecoin then depegged, the keeper could
not withdraw its crvUSD, leaving unbacked crvUSD in
circulation.<Cite src="curve-pegkeepers-docs" /> Both were addressed in V2 by splitting the
mechanism into a keeper that acts and a `PegKeeperRegulator` that grants or refuses
permission.<Cite src="curve-pegkeepers-docs" />

## When the safety check is the failure

The regulator's anti-spam defence compares the pool's current AMM state price against the pool's EMA
oracle price and refuses to let the keeper act if the two diverge beyond a threshold, on the
reasoning that a large divergence is what manipulation looks
like.<Cite src="curve-pegkeepers-docs" /><Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" />

On 12 June 2024, a liquidation cascade in a Curve Lend market created enormous demand for crvUSD —
liquidators must buy the borrowed asset to close positions, and the borrowed asset here is the
stablecoin itself. crvUSD spiked **above** par. That is the exact condition PegKeepers exist to
correct.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" />

They did not act. A large, genuine, fast price move produces exactly the same signature as a
manipulation: spot far from EMA. The deviation check fired and updates reverted. A risk analysis of
the incident found that the first successful `update` came roughly 525 blocks — about 45 minutes —
after the depeg began, once the deviation intermittently fell back inside
tolerance.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" />

Then the consequence propagated somewhere nobody had drawn on the diagram. Curve Lend markets
denominate collateral against crvUSD rather than against dollars. So crvUSD trading *up* is
arithmetically identical, from those markets' point of view, to every collateral asset trading
*down*. Users in an unrelated market — a staked-synthetic-dollar market advertising high leverage on
the assumption that its collateral would stay near a dollar — were liquidated by a move in the
denominator.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" /> The report's conclusion is the general lesson:
siloed markets designed to contain risk turned out to have generalised dependencies producing
knock-on effects between silos.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" />

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="stable-pegkeeper">
You are reviewing a new stablecoin that copies this architecture: an exponential rate function on
peg deviation, plus keeper contracts that add and remove liquidity from a paired StableSwap pool on
imbalance. The team has made three changes they consider improvements.

1. The price signal is the spot price of the single deepest pool, "so the keeper reacts faster than
   an EMA would".
2. The keeper's anti-manipulation guard is tightened from the reference implementation's threshold to
   a much narrower one, "because we would rather it did nothing than be exploited".
3. The keeper may withdraw from the pool at any time up to its full authorised allocation, without
   having deposited first, "so the downward defence is not limited by prior deposits".

Assess each. One of them is more dangerous than it looks.

<Answer>
**Change 1 is the dangerous one, and it is dangerous in a way that is familiar from the oracles
module.** The keeper is a pre-funded counterparty whose action is triggered by a price. Making that
price the spot state of one pool means an attacker who moves that pool decides when a contract with
a large allocation will trade, and in which direction. Above par, the keeper mints and deposits; so
push the pool up, let the keeper deposit into the imbalance you created, and take the other side.
This is why the reference design separates the trigger from the signal and uses an EMA aggregated
across several pools.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-overview" /> The latency the team is trying to remove
is the manipulation cost they are also removing. Reject.

**Change 2 is a real trade-off presented as a free one, and the historical record is against it.**
Tightening the divergence guard reduces the window for sandwiching the update and simultaneously
widens the set of *genuine* fast moves during which the keeper refuses to act. Those are the same
condition measured by the same statistic; no threshold separates them, because the signature is
identical. In June 2024 the reference threshold already produced roughly forty-five minutes of
inaction during a legitimate cascade.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" /> A narrower one
produces more of that, and the failure it prevents is one an attacker pays for while the failure it
causes is one your users pay for. At minimum, instrument how often the guard blocks action and
alert on it — a guard that blocks is indistinguishable from a guard that is idle unless you measure
it.

**Change 3 is not an improvement at all — it is a different mechanism wearing the same name.** In
the reference design the keeper can only withdraw crvUSD it previously deposited, because it must
burn LP tokens to do so.<Cite src="curve-pegkeepers-docs" /> That constraint is what makes the
keeper's balance sheet self-limiting: it can only take out what it put in. Remove it and the keeper
becomes a permanent bid that removes liquidity from a pool it never supplied, which drains the
paired asset and leaves the pool one-sided in exactly the condition where depth matters most. The
StableSwap curve is flat near balance and steepens sharply as it goes
lopsided,<Cite src="curve-stableswap-paper" /> so each withdrawal makes the next one more damaging
to price.

**The one thing they got right and did not mention.** Keeping the rate function exponential and
unbounded above is fine, because in this architecture the rate is not the crisis instrument. Its job
is drift. If they had instead tried to make the rate lever faster — shortening the oracle window,
say — they would have made the rate more manipulable to fix a problem the keepers already solve.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** removes the collateral entirely. Every mechanism in this one — a rate that
changes borrowing incentives, a keeper with a finite allocation, a pool that has to be deep — is
negative feedback with a bounded state. Take away the collateral and the feedback flips sign, and
you get a failure measured in days rather than in basis points.

**Lesson eight** uses this lesson's June 2024 incident as a case study in reading a deviation: an
upward depeg is a capacity signal, it is usually benign, and here it liquidated people in a market
that had nothing to do with it.<Cite src="llamarisk-crvusd-incident" />

**In the lending track's terms**, you now have a second reason to care about the numéraire a market
quotes in. The first was oracle correctness. The second is that the numéraire's own deviations are
distributed to every position in the market with the sign reversed.

**And as a reviewing habit**, the transferable move is to work every controller's transfer function
by hand once: substitute a deviation, get a rate, annualise it, and ask over what horizon anyone
would change their behaviour in response. Controllers are usually documented by their formula and
judged by their intent. The gap between the two is where the incidents live.
