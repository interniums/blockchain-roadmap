The swap procedure is five steps long, and the two in the middle are the whole design.

Terra's market module received a swap request, computed an exchange rate and a spread, transferred
the offered coins from the user to the module — and then **burned them**, minted the asked
denomination, and sent the newly minted coins to the trader.<Cite src="terra-classic-market-module" />
No pool of assets was drawn down. No counterparty took the other side. The protocol destroyed one
token and created another, at an exchange rate its own oracle reported.

Run the previous lessons' first question against that. *What is the redemption asset?* For a
fiat-backed token it is a bank balance. For a CDP token it is the collateral in your own vault. Here
it is a variable quantity of the protocol's own governance and staking token, minted for the
purpose. The module's own specification says as much in its opening line: price stability is
achieved through Terra-to-Luna arbitrage against the protocol's algorithmic market maker, which
expands and contracts the stablecoin's supply.<Cite src="terra-classic-market-module" />

The economists' framing is the cleanest one available: UST was, in economic terms, infinite-maturity
convertible debt with a face value of one dollar, backed by LUNA.<Cite src="terra-postmortems" />
Convertible into a security the issuer prints.

This is the design people describe as under-collateralised. It is not under-collateralised. It is
**un-collateralised by construction**, and the difference is not pedantry — it decides whether the
correcting trade adds backing or removes it.

## The mechanism, as specified

Terra's peg was defended by an on-chain market maker rather than by a reserve, and the spec is
precise enough to work with.<Cite src="terra-classic-market-module" />

The chain maintained two **virtual** liquidity pools — one representing all Terra stablecoin
denominations, one representing LUNA — using a constant-product rule where the Luna side is measured
by its fiat value rather than its unit count.<Cite src="terra-classic-market-module" /> Rather than
storing two balances, it stored a single number `δ`, the deviation of the Terra pool from its base
size, from which both pool sizes are derived:

```text
Pool_Terra = BasePool + δ
Pool_Luna  = BasePool^2 / Pool_Terra
```

Every Terra-to-Luna swap increased `δ` by the offered amount, and every Luna-to-Terra swap decreased
it by the asked amount.<Cite src="terra-classic-market-module" /> At the end of every block the
module replenished the pools, shrinking `δ` back toward zero at a rate set by
`PoolRecoveryPeriod`.<Cite src="terra-classic-market-module" />

The fees are documented with their default values: a `MinSpread` of 0.5 per cent on Terra-to-Luna
swaps, a `TobinTax` of 0.35 per cent on Terra-to-Terra swaps, a `BasePool` default of 250,000 SDR
and a `PoolRecoveryPeriod` defaulting to one day's worth of blocks. Terra-to-Luna swaps charged
either the minimum spread or the constant-product spread, **whichever was
greater**.<Cite src="terra-classic-market-module" /> These are the spec's defaults; the live chain's
parameters were set by governance and are not asserted here.

<Aside kind="note">
Notice what the replenishment is for. The spec says it lets the network sharply increase spread fees
during acute price fluctuations, after which the spread automatically returns to normal for
long-term price changes.<Cite src="terra-classic-market-module" /> That is a low-pass filter on the
exit door: it makes fast exits expensive and slow exits cheap. As a defence against front-running an
oracle it is sound. As a component of a run it is a queue with a surge price, and every holder can
see the price rising.
</Aside>

## Derivation one: the exit is capacity-limited, and the limit tightens as it is used

**Subgoal 1 — write the constant-product output.** With the pools balanced at `δ = 0`, both sides
equal `B = BasePool`. Offering `x` of Terra returns Luna worth

```text
ask = B * x / (B + x)
```

which is the standard constant-product result you derived in the AMM module, with the Luna side
denominated in fiat value.<Cite src="terra-classic-market-module" />

**Subgoal 2 — turn that into the spread the redeemer actually pays.** A frictionless conversion
would return `x`. So the constant-product spread is

```text
spread = 1 - ask / x = x / (B + x)
```

**Subgoal 3 — evaluate it at increasing sizes.** At `x` equal to one per cent of `B`, the spread is
about 0.99 per cent. At ten per cent of `B`, about 9.1 per cent. At `B` itself, fifty per cent.

**Subgoal 4 — state the property this exposes.** The redemption right's *fee* term — one of the five
from lesson one — is not a constant here. It is an increasing function of how much of the pool you
and everyone before you in this block have already used. The right gets worse precisely as more
people exercise it.

**Subgoal 5 — add the replenishment and read off the capacity.** Because `δ` decays toward zero over
`PoolRecoveryPeriod`, the pool is a bucket that refills over roughly a day. So there is a **daily
redemption capacity** at any given spread, set by `BasePool` and the recovery period, and it is a
governance parameter rather than a property of the backing. A protocol with a hundred per cent
reserve and a tiny `BasePool` would still gate its own exit.

**Subgoal 6 — check this against what holders actually did.** If the native swap is size-gated and
its execution price uncertain, most holders will not use it; they will sell on a venue instead,
which is lesson one's secondary-market peg. That is exactly what the record shows. The analysis of
the run finds that swap fees and uncertainty about the execution price of LUNA on exchanges appear
to have discouraged Anchor depositors from using the native swap contract as an exit, while one
trading firm with preferential exchange access conducted the largest volume of UST-to-LUNA
swaps.<Cite src="terra-postmortems" /> The redemption right existed and was, in practice,
concentrated in the hands of whoever could bear its friction.

## Derivation two: the threshold that decides whether redemption helps

Now the part that makes this design categorically different from the previous two lessons, and it
takes four steps.

**Subgoal 1 — define the backing ratio.** Let `S` be the stablecoin supply and `M` the total market
value of the governance token — its price times its supply. Define

```text
B = M / S
```

`B` is dollars of backing per dollar of stablecoin. Nothing about this ratio is enforced anywhere; it
is an observation about two markets — which is why no contract reverts as it deteriorates, and why
the quantitative post-mortems had to reconstruct it from market data after the
fact.<Cite src="arxiv-terra-luna-anatomy" />

**Subgoal 2 — model one redemption honestly.** A holder redeems `x` of stablecoin and immediately
sells the newly minted governance tokens. Two effects. Supply falls: `S → S - x`. And the value the
market assigns to the governance token falls by roughly the value extracted, because the redeemer
took `x` dollars out of it: `M → M - x`. This assumes the market prices the *aggregate* claim rather
than the per-unit price, which is the standard reflexive model. The alternative assumption — that
price stays fixed and only supply inflates — gives the same sign with a different magnitude, so the
conclusion below is robust to which you prefer.

**Subgoal 3 — compare the ratio before and after.**

```text
B' = (M - x) / (S - x)      versus      B = M / S
```

Cross-multiplying, `B'` exceeds `B` exactly when `S*(M - x)` exceeds `M*(S - x)`, which reduces to
`-S*x` exceeding `-M*x`, which holds exactly when `M` exceeds `S`.

**Subgoal 4 — state the result, because it is the whole lesson.**

- When `M > S` — the governance token is worth more than the stablecoin supply — **redemption raises
  the backing ratio.** The mechanism is self-correcting. Every exit leaves the remaining holders
  better backed than before. This is why the design works, and works convincingly, for as long as it
  works.
- When `M < S`, **redemption lowers the backing ratio.** Every exit leaves the remaining holders
  worse backed, which raises their incentive to exit, which lowers it further.
- `M = S` is the fixed point, and it is **unstable**: perturb it upward and the system converges
  back to health; perturb it downward and it accelerates away.

**Subgoal 5 — do the same for expansion, and find the trap.** Minting stablecoin burns the governance
token offered and mints the stablecoin asked for,<Cite src="terra-classic-market-module" /> so `S`
rises by `x` while `M` is, at best, unchanged. So `B = M / S` falls with every unit of
growth. Under the constant-aggregate-value assumption it falls faster still. **Growth in the
stablecoin's supply moves the system toward the unstable threshold**, and the faster the growth the
faster the approach.

That is the trap in one line: the metric everyone celebrated — supply going up — was the metric
moving the system toward the point where its defence mechanism reverses sign.

## What the record shows

Now put dates and magnitudes on it, from the detailed on-chain study of the run.

**The demand was bought.** The Anchor protocol offered UST depositors a yield of 19.5 per cent, which
drove large inflows and a large increase in UST issuance; both deposit and lending rates were
heavily subsidised, and by April 2022 the daily subsidy had reached six million
dollars.<Cite src="terra-postmortems" /> Read that against subgoal 5: the subsidy was buying supply
growth, and supply growth was lowering `B`.

**The ratio deteriorated from the other side too.** LUNA peaked at 119.18 dollars on 5 April 2022 and
then declined with the broader market, diminishing its valuation relative to
UST.<Cite src="terra-postmortems" /> `M` falling and `S` rising are the two ways to approach the
threshold, and both were happening.

**The run began as an ordinary withdrawal.** The first signs appeared on 7 May 2022, when two large
addresses withdrew 375 million UST from Anchor.<Cite src="terra-postmortems" /> The analysis
concludes it was not targeted manipulation by a single entity but growing concern about the
system's sustainability.<Cite src="terra-postmortems" />

**Then the loop ran to completion.** Over roughly three days the LUNA supply increased from one
billion to six trillion and its price fell from about eighty dollars to almost
zero.<Cite src="terra-postmortems" /> A six-thousand-fold supply increase is what subgoal 4's
below-threshold branch looks like when it is allowed to run: each redemption mints tokens at a lower
price, so the next redemption mints more of them.<Cite src="terra-postmortems" />

**And the losses were distributed by sophistication, not by exposure.** Wealthier and more
sophisticated investors ran first and lost much less; poorer and less sophisticated ones ran later,
lost more, and a significant fraction attempted to buy the dip.<Cite src="terra-postmortems" />
Independent quantitative work on the same collapse traced the trigger events through hourly and
transaction-level data and documented how the dependency structure across the broader market shifted
during the downturn.<Cite src="arxiv-terra-luna-anatomy" />

## Positive feedback is a different category of problem

Set this next to lesson four and the contrast is exact.

The rate lever is **negative feedback with a bounded state**. The controlled variable is a price, the
actuator is a rate, and the loop's effect is to remove the deviation that produced it. It is slow,
and slowness in a negative-feedback loop causes overshoot and oscillation — annoying, survivable,
correctable by tuning.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-monetary-policy" />

The seigniorage loop below its threshold is **positive feedback with no bounded state**. The actuator
is minting, minting reduces the backing, reduced backing increases the incentive to exercise the
actuator. Nothing in the loop opposes it. There is no saturation term, no cap on how many governance
tokens may be minted, and the parameter that does bound throughput — the virtual pool — bounds the
*rate* of the collapse while making the exit price worse for everyone still in the
queue.<Cite src="terra-classic-market-module" />

That difference in category is why the interventions people propose after the fact do not work.
Slowing the loop redistributes losses toward whoever is slowest; it does not change the fixed point.
Adding a reserve changes the design into a partially collateralised one, which is a different
system — a useful one, but the reflexive loop still governs whatever fraction remains
uncollateralised. And a keeper contract with a finite allocation, which is a real defence against
ordinary imbalance,<Cite src="curve-pegkeepers-docs" /> is a bid of bounded size against a supply
that is unbounded by construction.

<Check type="explain" concept="stable-death-spiral">
A team proposes a "hybrid" stablecoin. Each unit is redeemable for eighty cents of USDC from a
reserve contract plus twenty cents' worth of their governance token, minted at an oracle price. They
argue this is safe because it is eighty per cent collateralised, and that the twenty per cent tail
is too small to spiral.

Evaluate the claim. Identify the quantity that decides whether they are right, and say what you
would need to see to sign off.

<Answer>
**The eighty per cent is real and does exactly what they say.** A redeemer receives eighty cents of
a liquid, marked asset — an actual reserve, of the kind the algorithmic design had
none of.<Cite src="terra-postmortems" /> The reserve depletes one-for-one with redemptions and cannot be inflated
away. For that fraction the analysis is lesson two's: custody, freeze authority, and the redemption
capacity of the reserve contract.

**The twenty per cent is not a small version of the same thing — it is the whole of lesson five,
scaled.** Apply the threshold derivation to the uncollateralised part alone. Let `S_u = 0.2 * S` be
the uncollateralised claim and `M` the governance token's market value. The unstable fixed point is
at `M = S_u`, so the quantity that decides everything is

```text
M / (0.2 * S)
```

Above one, redemptions of the tail raise the tail's backing. Below one, they destroy it. The
collateralised fraction does not enter this ratio at all. So "eighty per cent collateralised" is not
an answer to the question; it only relocates the threshold to a fifth of supply.

**Now check whether the tail can stay small.** It cannot, in the state that matters, and this is the
part the proposal misses. Redemptions consume the USDC reserve first, in proportion, so after enough
redemptions the reserve is a smaller share of the remaining claim unless it is replenished. Worse,
if the reserve is drawn down by exactly eighty cents per redeemed unit, the *ratio* is preserved
while the absolute buffer shrinks — and if governance ever pauses reserve redemptions to preserve
them, the entire claim becomes the tail overnight.

**What I would need to see to sign off.** First, a live, published value of `M / (0.2 * S)` with an
alert well above one — not a launch-day number, a monitored one. Second, an explicit rule for what
happens when the reserve is exhausted, written in the contract rather than left to governance, since
governance operates on days and this operates on hours. Third, a cap on the governance tokens
mintable per period, with a stated and accepted consequence: past the cap redemption fails, which is
honest, rather than succeeding at a price that destroys the next redeemer. Fourth, a stress run in
which the governance token falls seventy per cent for reasons unrelated to this product — which is
the ordinary case, since it is a crypto asset — and the ratio is still above one.

**And the framing to give them.** They have not built one stablecoin that is eighty per cent safe.
They have built two instruments in a trench coat: a claim on a reserve, and a reflexive claim on
their own equity. Price and monitor them separately, because they fail on different clocks and only
one of them can be fixed by adding money.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** takes a design that is constantly mistaken for this one and is not: a synthetic
dollar whose backing is spot crypto plus an offsetting short. It holds real assets, so the reflexive
loop above does not apply — and its actual risks are so different that carrying this lesson's
checklist into it produces confident, wrong conclusions in both directions.

**Lesson seven** covers the category where the 2025 failures actually happened, and one thing that
episode shares with this one is the structure of the information problem: the loss existed for weeks
before the price reflected it, and the participants who understood the system first got out
first.<Cite src="terra-postmortems" />

**In design review**, the transferable artefact is the threshold. Any time a system's remedy involves
issuing more of an asset whose value is a claim on that same system, write down the ratio of that
asset's market value to the liability it backs, and find the fixed point. If the fixed point is
unstable, the design has a cliff, and the only remaining questions are how far away it is and how
fast the system is moving toward it.
