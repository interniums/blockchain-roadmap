On 28 October 2025 an on-chain analyst published a ratio. A yield-bearing token calling itself a
dollar showed backing assets of about 170 million dollars supporting roughly 530 million of
borrowing — a leverage ratio a little above four. Another researcher published an analysis of
circular minting between that protocol and a second one. Both were public. Both were
reconstructable from chain data by anyone who thought to look.<Cite src="blockeden-stream-contagion" />

Nothing happened for a week.

On 4 November the protocol disclosed roughly 93 million dollars of losses at an external fund
manager and suspended deposits and withdrawals. Within a day the token fell about 77 per cent from
one dollar toward an intraday low near 26 cents, stranding on the order of 160 million dollars of
deposits.<Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" /><Cite src="blockeden-stream-contagion" /> Every figure in
those two paragraphs comes from secondary reconstructions of the same small set of analyses rather
than from audited disclosure, and should be carried as a reported magnitude rather than a
measurement.

Here is the part that matters for your model of the world. Classify that token using the taxonomy
everyone teaches — fiat-backed, crypto-backed, algorithmic — and you cannot. It was not an IOU on a
bank account. It was not minted as over-collateralised debt against on-chain collateral. It was
certainly not seigniorage; there was no governance token being printed. It had real assets behind
it, deployed in real strategies, and it went to twenty-six cents in an afternoon.

The taxonomy predates the product. This lesson is about the category it has no slot for, because
that is where the failures of 2025 actually happened.

## What the fourth category is

Strip the marketing and the defining property is a single sentence: **its price is an accounting
output, not an arbitrage equilibrium.**

For every design in lessons two through six, the quoted value of the token is defended by somebody
being able to profitably move between the market and a redemption. The band is the friction. Take
that away and the number comes from somewhere else — the issuer computes a net asset value for a
portfolio it manages and publishes it, and the token is quoted at that.

Three consequences follow immediately, and each one inverts an intuition you have been building.

**A deviation is not an opportunity.** When an arbitrage-pegged token trades at 0.98, that is a trade.
When a net-asset-value token is marked down, that is an announcement. There is no counterparty on the
other side of a valuation.

**A loss is permanent, not temporary.** A depeg in the earlier sense is a dislocation that closes when
the loop reopens — the March 2023 episode returned to par the moment redemptions
resumed.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> A write-down does not close, because there is nothing
to close it against. The claim is simply smaller than it was.

**The diligence question changes.** "What is the collateral ratio" is the wrong question, because the
ratio is computed by the party whose competence is under examination. The right questions are: where
does the yield come from, and can I verify the backing with a query rather than with a report?

Applied to the November 2025 case, the answers were available. The yield was produced by an external
manager's off-chain trading book, with the marketing leaning on transparency and composability while
the engine sat inside a single manager's positions with no published custody and no real-time
attestation of what backed each token.<Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" /> And the reported returns
were flat — a steady headline yield, which is a signature of a manually set number rather than an
organic one, since real strategy returns are noisy.<Cite src="blockeden-stream-contagion" />

## Why a write-down produces bad debt instantly, with no liquidation

The mechanism that converted one fund's loss into a system-wide event is worth deriving, because it
is a property of the collateral type rather than a bug in any protocol.

Recall the three regions of a borrower's health from the lending module, stated here in the form the
Morpho documentation uses, with `LTV` the loan-to-value, `LLTV` the liquidation loan-to-value
parameter, and `LIF` the liquidation incentive factor:<Cite src="morpho-bad-debt-docs" />

That third region is the definition of bad debt: past it, no actor has an incentive to repay the
remainder.<Cite src="morpho-bad-debt-docs" />

**Subgoal 1 — establish the normal case, so the abnormal one is visible.** Volatile collateral moves
continuously. A position drifts up through `LLTV`, enters the liquidatable band, and liquidators
compete to close it because doing so is profitable. The band between `LLTV` and `1/LIF` is the
system's working margin — with `LLTV` at 0.86 and `LIF` at 1.15, `1/LIF` is about 0.87, so the band
is roughly one percentage point of loan-to-value wide. In price terms it is wider than it sounds,
and it lasts as long as the price takes to traverse it.

**Subgoal 2 — apply a net-asset-value write-down instead.** Take 100 units of collateral marked at
one dollar with `LLTV` at 0.86, so the borrower holds 86 of debt at `LTV = 0.86`. The manager
discloses a loss and the token reprices to 0.26. Collateral value is now 26.

```text
LTV = 86 / 26 = 3.31
```

**Subgoal 3 — locate that on the ladder.** 3.31 is not near `1/LIF` of 0.87. It is nearly four times
it. The position went from *healthy* to *deeply bad debt* without ever passing through the
liquidatable band in a way anyone could act on — because the price did not traverse; it
**gapped**.

**Subgoal 4 — compute the recovery.** A liquidator seizing the entire 100 units of collateral
recovers 26 against 86 of debt. The shortfall is 60 per 86, about 70 per cent of the loan, and no
liquidation discount changes it — there is no price at which selling this collateral repays this
debt. What actually happens is that the borrower stops caring: their debt exceeds their collateral,
so the rational move is to walk away with the borrowed asset. Analyses of the episode describe
exactly that outcome, with borrowers draining the supplied stablecoin and lenders left holding bad
debt denominated in a near-worthless token.<Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" />

**Subgoal 5 — add the oracle, which made it worse.** In the reported case, lending vaults priced the
token at a hard-coded one dollar rather than at its collapsing market price, so the liquidations that
should have unwound the loans never triggered at all.<Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" /> Put that in
the arithmetic: with the oracle pinned, `LTV` stays at 0.86 forever. The health check passes. The
dashboard is green. Every position in the market is by definition healthy while the collateral behind
it is worth a quarter of face.

**Subgoal 6 — state the general rule.** *Any collateral whose price is an issuer-published figure
rather than a market price has no liquidatable band.* It is either at par or written down, and the
gap between those states is instantaneous. Every parameter in a lending market — `LLTV`, `LIF`,
close factor, auction duration — is tuned for a price that moves continuously. Against a step
function they are all inoperative.

<Aside kind="warn">
The curator documentation for one of the affected protocols now says this in as many words. Supplying
into a market with an oracle that overestimates the collateral's price leads to loss of assets
because health computations and liquidations do not work correctly — and the guidance singles out
net-asset-value oracles updated by the token issuer as a case to be especially cautious with, since
they can diverge from market prices during exploit
scenarios.<Cite src="morpho-security-considerations" /> That is a canonical warning written after the
fact, which makes it a good thing to read *before* the next one.
</Aside>

## Isolation contains the accounting, not the cause

The second half of the episode is the part that surprised people who thought they had solved this.

Isolated markets exist so that a bad asset harms only its own market. That property held. Each
market's losses stayed inside that market's ledger. And the system-wide damage happened anyway,
because several protocols had independently listed the same asset as
collateral.<Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" /><Cite src="blockeden-stream-contagion" />

**Isolation is per market. Risk is per asset.** Ten isolated markets accepting the same collateral are
one exposure with ten sets of books. Isolation changes who bears the loss and stops it propagating
through a shared pool; it does nothing at all about a common cause, because a common cause does not
propagate — it arrives everywhere at once.

The reported contagion path shows both mechanisms at work. A second synthetic dollar had lent roughly
65 per cent of its own backing — about 68 million dollars — to the failed protocol through private
lending vaults, which made it a claim on that protocol's solvency in disguise; when the collateral
was impaired it fell about 98 per cent, to around 0.015, and was wound down with a claims process for
pre-event holders.<Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" /> A third token with overlapping collateral broke
below 0.60 days later.<Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" /> Independent analysis tallied roughly 285
million dollars of interconnected debt across the affected vaults, naming individual creditors in
the tens and hundreds of millions, and roughly a billion dollars left DeFi yield products within a
week.<Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" /><Cite src="blockeden-stream-contagion" /> That 285 million
figure is one researcher's construction of a debt graph, and it should be attributed rather than
stated as measured.

### The listing decision is the risk decision

If isolation does not bound the risk, something has to, and the answer is the same one the security
track keeps arriving at: the control has to sit where the decision is made.

In a permissionless lending system, the decision that creates this exposure is a **listing** — a
curator or a market creator choosing an oracle and a collateral asset. Two documented properties of
that decision are worth carrying.

**Supply caps do not do what people think.** In one protocol's vault design, caps limit reallocations
by curators and allocators, and do not limit direct supply on behalf of the vault, so a market with a
faulty oracle and a cap of zero can still be fully vulnerable and the potential loss is not bounded
by the cap.<Cite src="morpho-security-considerations" /> The documented remedy is removing the market
from the withdrawal queue entirely rather than capping it at
zero.<Cite src="morpho-security-considerations" />

**The most effective automated mitigation is a kill switch on oracle divergence.** The same guidance
recommends deploying monitoring that detects when oracle prices diverge from market prices, and
building or adopting an automated kill switch that empties the supply queue when a significant
deviation is detected — described as the single most effective automated
mitigation.<Cite src="morpho-security-considerations" /> Its response playbook is equally concrete:
pause deposits first so nobody can increase their share, then remove the affected market from the
withdrawal queue, using forced removal and a timelock if the market is
illiquid.<Cite src="morpho-security-considerations" />

Notice what that kill switch actually is. It is a monitor comparing **the price the protocol
believes** against **the price the market believes**, with an automated action on divergence. In the
November 2025 case those two numbers were one dollar and twenty-six cents for a full day. The signal
was not subtle; nothing was watching for it.

The bounded-rate idea from the oracles module is the other half of the same defence: adapters that
cap the rate at which a yield-bearing asset's reported price may grow exist precisely so that a
rate published by an issuer cannot move a lending market
arbitrarily.<Cite src="aave-price-cap-adapters" /> A cap constrains the upside error. A divergence
kill switch catches the downside one. A market that lists an issuer-reported price and has neither is
trusting a counterparty it has never named in its risk documentation.

## What you could have seen, in order

Turn all of the above into a procedure, because the whole point of the 28 October date at the top is
that this one was legible in advance.

**Ask where the yield comes from, and require a mechanism.** "Lending arbitrage, market making,
liquidity provision" is a list of activities, not a mechanism. A mechanism tells you who pays the
yield and why they are willing to. Funding on a short perpetual is a mechanism — a counterparty pays
it, and you can go and read the rate.<Cite src="ethena-how-usde-works" /> An external manager's
returns are not.

**Reconstruct the backing yourself, or record that you cannot.** This is the binary. If total backing
is a query against public state, run it. If it is a figure in a report, then the token's price is
that report's accuracy, and you should size the position as an unsecured credit exposure to the
reporting entity. In the reported case the reconstruction was possible and was published a week
early: backing around 170 million against borrowing around 530 million.<Cite src="blockeden-stream-contagion" />

**Check the shape of the yield curve over time.** A flat headline yield through changing market
conditions is a strong signal that the number is administered rather than
earned.<Cite src="blockeden-stream-contagion" />

**Look for the same asset in more than one place.** For any collateral you are exposed to, enumerate
every market that has listed it. That count is your correlation, and it is not visible from inside
any one market.<Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" />

**Watch the oracle against the market, always.** One number, one comparison, one
alert.<Cite src="morpho-security-considerations" />

<Answer>
**Defect one: the oracle is the counterparty.** `convertToAssets` on the issuer's own vault is a
figure the issuer computes, so the collateral's price in this market is whatever the issuer says it
is. That is the net-asset-value oracle case the curator documentation warns about
specifically.<Cite src="morpho-security-considerations" /> It has no liquidatable band: from the
derivation above, an issuer write-down takes `LTV` from 0.915 straight past `1/LIF` to a multiple of
it in one step, so every position goes from healthy to unrecoverable without an intervening state in
which a liquidator can act.<Cite src="morpho-bad-debt-docs" /> At `LLTV` 0.915 the shortfall is worse
than the worked example, because more was lent against each unit.

**Defect two: "isolated so the worst case is bounded by the cap" is two wrong claims in one clause.**
Isolation bounds *propagation*, not *exposure* — if three other protocols list the same asset, this
market is one ledger of a single shared risk, and nobody inside it can see the other
two.<Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" /> And the cap does not bound the loss in the way assumed: in
this vault design caps limit curator and allocator reallocations rather than direct supply on behalf
of the vault, and a faulty-oracle market with a cap of zero can still be fully
vulnerable.<Cite src="morpho-security-considerations" />

**Defect three: there is no divergence monitor and no kill switch.** The one mitigation described as
the single most effective automated control is absent, along with the response playbook that goes
with it — empty the supply queue first, then remove the market from the withdrawal
queue.<Cite src="morpho-security-considerations" />

**Which one turns 25 million into more: defect two, and specifically the correlated-listing half.**
The oracle defect determines that the loss is total when it happens; the cap determines the size of
*this* market's contribution; but the systemic figure comes from the same asset appearing across
several markets at once, which is what turned a fund's loss into a reported 285 million dollars of
interconnected debt in November 2025.<Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" /> A curator can only see their
own market. Someone has to be counting the asset across all of them, and if nobody in your
organisation is, then your exposure is unknown rather than capped.

**What I would require before approval.** A market oracle rather than an issuer view function, or a
growth-capped adapter if a rate must be used.<Cite src="aave-price-cap-adapters" /> An automated
divergence kill switch, tested. A written enumeration of every other venue listing this collateral,
refreshed. And an explicit statement, signed off by whoever owns the risk, of the loss if the
collateral reprices to zero overnight — because that is the actual distribution here, not a
continuous one.
</Answer>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** turns the central skill of this module into a procedure: telling a liquidity gap
from a solvency gap while it is happening, when both look identical on a chart. The two cases in this
module — a token that returned to par the day redemptions reopened, and a token that never
recovered — are the two answers.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /><Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" />

**Lesson nine** shows that the regulatory boundary cuts straight through this category, and that a
product whose value is a managed portfolio's net asset value has a name in securities law that is not
"stablecoin".

**Back in the lending and oracles tracks**, this lesson adds a collateral class those modules'
parameters were not designed for. Everything you tuned assumed continuous prices. Add a question to
every listing review: *does this asset's price move, or does it get announced?*

**And as a standing practice**, keep the 28 October fact in view. The information that mattered was
public a week before the loss was disclosed, in a form anyone could reconstruct, and the people who
acted on it were the people who had made reconstructing it part of their
routine.<Cite src="blockeden-stream-contagion" />
