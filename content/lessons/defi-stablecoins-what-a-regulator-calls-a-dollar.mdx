Read a statutory definition the way you would read an interface, and it stops being law and starts
being a type signature.

The United States GENIUS Act — Public Law 119-27, approved 18 July 2025 — defines a **payment
stablecoin** as a digital asset that is, or is designed to be, used as a means of payment or
settlement, whose issuer is obligated to convert, redeem or repurchase it for a fixed amount of
monetary value, and which represents (or creates a reasonable expectation) that it will maintain a
stable value relative to that fixed amount.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> The definition
excludes national currencies, deposits, and securities.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" />

One clause inside that is worth pausing on, because it is the kind of precision engineers usually
assume legislation lacks. The redemption obligation is for a fixed amount of monetary value **not
including a digital asset denominated in a fixed amount of monetary value**.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" />
Redeemable into dollars, yes. Redeemable into another stablecoin, no — that does not satisfy the
definition. Every peg-stability module in lesson three exists precisely to make one token redeemable
into another, and the statute declines to count that as redemption.

This lesson runs each design in this module against the statute's requirements and finds that most of
them have no route in. Not a difficult route. No route — which makes this a structural boundary rather
than a compliance burden, and that distinction changes what a team should build.

## Three requirements, read as constraints

Strip section 4 to the parts that bind a design rather than an entity.

**Reserves, one for one, from an enumerated list.** A permitted issuer must maintain identifiable
reserves backing outstanding tokens on an at-least-one-to-one basis, drawn from a closed list:
currency and Federal Reserve balances; demand deposits or insured shares at insured depository
institutions; Treasury bills, notes or bonds with 93 days or less remaining; overnight repurchase
agreements backed by such bills; overnight reverse repurchase agreements collateralised by
Treasuries; shares in registered government money market funds invested solely in those categories;
other similarly liquid federal-government assets the regulator approves; and tokenised forms of
several of those.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> Reserves may not be pledged, rehypothecated or
reused, with narrow exceptions.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" />

**No yield to holders.** No permitted or foreign payment stablecoin issuer may pay the holder of any
payment stablecoin any form of interest or yield — in cash, tokens, or other consideration — solely
in connection with the holding, use or retention of that
token.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" />

**Only permitted issuers, and eventually only permitted issuers get distributed.** It is unlawful for
anyone other than a permitted payment stablecoin issuer to issue a payment stablecoin in the United
States; and beginning three years after enactment it is unlawful for a digital asset service provider
to offer or sell a payment stablecoin to a person in the United States unless it was issued by a
permitted issuer.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> Foreign issuers additionally need the
technological capability to comply with lawful orders to seize, freeze, burn or block
transfers.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" />

## Running the designs through the type check

**Subgoal 1 — fiat-backed.** Reserves are already cash, bills and deposits. The list was written
around this design. The work is entity-level — becoming a permitted issuer, monthly published reserve
composition including average tenor and custody location, monthly examination of the month-end report
by a registered public accounting firm, and officer certification with criminal penalties for a
knowingly false one.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> Real work, and none of it changes the
mechanism. **Fits.**

**Subgoal 2 — CDP.** The reserve is volatile crypto collateral locked in user vaults. Nothing on the
enumerated list resembles it, and there is no issuer holding it — it belongs to the borrowers who
posted it.<Cite src="curve-crvusd-overview" /> The redemption obligation also fails: closing a vault
returns collateral, not a fixed amount of monetary value. And where such a system does offer
one-for-one redemption, it is into another stablecoin, which the definition explicitly does not
count.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> **No route.**

**Subgoal 3 — algorithmic.** There is no reserve. Redemption pays newly minted governance tokens,
which are neither on the list nor a fixed amount of monetary value. **No route**, and the exclusion is
about as unambiguous as statutory drafting gets.

**Subgoal 4 — delta-neutral synthetic.** The backing is spot crypto plus a short derivatives position
held at trading venues through off-exchange settlement.<Cite src="ethena-how-usde-works" /> A hedged
derivatives book is not on the enumerated list, and the yield — funding collected on the short — is
the product's reason to exist, which collides with the interest
prohibition.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> **No route, twice over.**

**Subgoal 5 — the fourth category.** A token whose price is a net asset value computed by a manager
running a discretionary strategy fails the reserve list and, more fundamentally, runs into the
definitional exclusion of securities.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> The classification argument
the November 2025 failures should have prompted is not "which kind of stablecoin was this" but
"which kind of instrument was this".<Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" />

**Subgoal 6 — read the pattern.** Four of the five designs in this module fail, and they fail on the
*reserve list*, which is the one requirement no amount of disclosure satisfies. You cannot document
your way onto an enumerated list. That is why this is a boundary and not a burden.

## The yield prohibition is the sharpest edge

Of the three requirements, the one that reshapes product design most is the ban on paying holders
yield, because for several designs the yield *is* the product.

Read the text precisely, since the precision is where the industry response lives. The prohibition
binds the **issuer**, applies to any form of consideration, and attaches to yield paid *solely in
connection with the holding, use or retention* of the payment
stablecoin.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> Each of those three qualifiers is a seam, and
commentary has focused on exactly that: one 2026 analysis argues that a synthetic dollar's yield
mechanism sits outside what the Act addresses, and describes the resulting industry pattern as a
split product line — a compliant, non-yield-bearing token for the regulated category alongside a
separate yield-bearing synthetic one.<Cite src="forbes-ethena-genius" />

Treat that as reporting on an unsettled question rather than as a conclusion. Whether a given
structure sits inside or outside the prohibition depends on rulemaking and on facts, and it is a
question for counsel rather than for a curriculum. What is not unsettled is the **engineering**
consequence, which holds either way: a team that wants both regulatory-category distribution and a
yield-bearing product is building two tokens with two sets of contracts, two risk profiles, two sets
of disclosures and, in practice, a shared brand. Everything you learned about designs in lessons two
through six applies to one of them; everything about the other is unchanged by any of this.

<Aside kind="warn">
Dates, stated exactly, because this is the part that gets garbled. Approved 18 July
2025.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> Implementing regulations required from the federal
regulators, the Treasury and state regulators **not later than one year after
enactment**.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> The Act takes effect on the **earlier** of eighteen
months after enactment or 120 days after final implementing regulations are
issued.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> The distribution prohibition on non-permitted payment
stablecoins begins **three years after enactment**.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> Whether the
rulemakings were in fact completed by their deadline is a separate fact that must be checked against
the regulators — this lesson does not assert it either way.
</Aside>

<Check type="explain" concept="stable-regulatory-boundary">
A team is designing a dollar-denominated token for consumer payments in the United States, and wants
to pass the reserve yield through to holders because "that is what makes it competitive with a
savings account".

Say what they can and cannot build, and give them the two decisions that actually matter.

<Answer>
**What they can build.** A payment stablecoin backed one for one by instruments from the enumerated
list, issued by a permitted issuer, with published redemption procedures, monthly reserve composition
including tenor and custody location, and monthly examination by a registered public accounting
firm.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> The reserve earns the yield. The issuer keeps it.

**What they cannot build inside that category.** Anything that pays the holder interest or yield in
any form solely for holding, using or retaining the token.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> "Pass
the reserve yield through" is a direct description of the prohibited act, and no wrapper that is
clearly the same economics with a different label should be assumed to survive.

**Decision one: which side of the boundary is this product on?** Inside the category they get lawful
issuance and, after the three-year clock, the ability to be offered by US service providers when
non-permitted tokens cannot.<Cite src="genius-act-public-law" /> Outside it they get the yield and
lose the distribution channel that makes a consumer payments product viable in the first place. For a
*payments* product this is not a close call, and the pressure to make it close comes from comparing
against savings accounts — a comparison that imports the wrong competitor.

**Decision two: if they still want a yield product, is it a second product or a different company?**
The observed pattern is a split line: a compliant non-yield token and a separate yield-bearing
one.<Cite src="forbes-ethena-genius" /> That is two codebases, two reserve models, two risk
registers, and two incident runbooks — and the trap is a shared brand implying shared guarantees, so
the disclosures have to work harder than the marketing.

**What I would tell them before they talk to counsel.** Everything above is the shape of the
constraint, not legal advice, and the seams in the yield prohibition are genuinely
contested.<Cite src="forbes-ethena-genius" /> But the two decisions do not depend on how those seams
resolve. Also: pick the reserve conservatively even inside the list. Uninsured deposits are on the
list and are an unsecured claim on one company, and the mechanism that broke in March 2023 was the
reserve and the banking calendar, not the
contract.<Cite src="fed-svb-stablecoins-note" /> Compliance is a floor, not a risk model.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this leaves you

That is the module. Nine lessons, one question asked repeatedly: *what trade does a deviation create,
who may make it, and what does it cost them?* Every design answered differently, and every failure in
the record was that answer failing rather than a contract failing.

**The practice exercise** builds a peg-stability module and demonstrates the band closing to the fee —
which is lesson one's derivation and lesson three's code, executable.

**The reflection prompt** asks you to take a token you actually hold and write down who may redeem
it, at what size, at what fee, on what timescale, whether the issuer may refuse, and whether you can
verify the backing on chain. Do it before you forget the vocabulary. The whole module exists to make
that page take twenty minutes instead of a week.

**Into the rest of the DeFi track**, three things now compose differently. Lending markets have a
collateral class whose price is announced rather than
traded.<Cite src="pharos-stream-elixir" /> Oracles have a consumer whose failure mode is a constant
rather than a manipulation. And liquidations have a case with no liquidatable band at all.

**And into whatever you build**, the durable habit is smaller than any of the mechanisms here. When
somebody tells you a number is stable, ask what makes it so, who is paid to keep it so, and what
happens to them on the day it stops paying. That question has answered every episode in this module,
and it required no legal training and no new tooling — only the discipline to keep asking after the
first reassuring answer.
