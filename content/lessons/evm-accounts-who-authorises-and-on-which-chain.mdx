A relayer's accounting for one afternoon, reconstructed after the fact.

It received a signed authorization from a user, verified the signature, verified the nonce against
the chain, built a type-4 transaction around it, and broadcast. The transaction was included four
blocks later. It reverted. The user was not delegated. The relayer paid.

Nobody attacked anything. No signature was forged, no rule was broken, and the user's wallet did
nothing a wallet is not supposed to do. The relayer had verified a fact about the world and then
paid for a transaction that would execute in a *different* world, several seconds later.

This lesson is about the object at the centre of that story — the authorization tuple — and about
the two of its fields that decide who can be hurt by it: the nonce and the chain id.

## Six fields, one signature

An authorization is a tuple of six values.<Cite src="eip-7702" />

```text
[chain_id, address, nonce, y_parity, r, s]
```

The last three are a secp256k1 signature. The first three are what is being signed: which chain
this is valid on, which address to delegate to, and at which nonce of the signing account.

Notice what is *not* in the tuple. There is no expiry, no spending limit, no list of permitted
functions, no fee, no recipient, and — crucially — nothing that says who submits it. The tuple
commits to *what* and *when*. It says nothing about *by whom*, and that omission is the entire
sponsorship story.

## The digest, and why it cannot be a transaction

The signer is recovered as:<Cite src="eip-7702" />

```text
authority = ecrecover(keccak256(MAGIC ‖ rlp([chain_id, address, nonce])), y_parity, r, s)
```

with `MAGIC = 0x05`.<Cite src="eip-7702" />

That one prefix byte is doing the work you already know from
[EIP-712 typed structured data](/c/eip-712-typed-data): domain separation.<Cite src="eip-712" /> An
authorization's preimage is
a three-element RLP list sitting behind the byte `0x05`, which is not the shape of anything a
wallet signs as a transaction and not the shape of anything it signs as a typed message. The
authorization gets its own signing domain, so a signature collected in one context cannot be
presented as authority in another.

This is the same defensive pattern, for the same reason, as every other prefix you have met.
[Signature recovery](/c/ecrecover) does not know what it is recovering. It returns an address for
any digest at all. The only thing standing between "a signature over 32 bytes" and "authority to
act" is that the 32 bytes could not have come from anywhere else.

The signature also has to satisfy the low-`s` rule: `s` must be less than or equal to half the
curve order, as required by EIP-2.<Cite src="eip-7702" /><Cite src="eip-2" /> If you remember
[malleability](/c/signature-malleability), you know why — without the rule, every valid
authorization has a second, equally valid byte encoding, and any system keying deduplication or
replay protection off the signature bytes has two identities for one authorization.

## What the client checks, step by step

The authorization list is processed after the sender's nonce is incremented and before execution
begins. For each tuple, in order:<Cite src="eip-7702" />

**Subgoal: confirm this authorization is meant for this chain.** Verify `chain_id` is zero or the
current chain's id. Anything else, and the tuple is abandoned.

**Subgoal: confirm the nonce is representable.** Verify the nonce is below `2**64 - 1`. The tuple
must leave room to increment.

**Subgoal: find out who signed.** Recover `authority` from the digest above, enforcing the low-`s`
rule.

**Subgoal: charge for the state you are about to touch.** Add `authority` to the accessed-addresses
set, per EIP-2929.<Cite src="eip-2929" /> Subsequent access is at warm prices.

**Subgoal: confirm this account may be delegated at all.** Verify the code of `authority` is empty
or already a delegation. A contract account fails here.

**Subgoal: confirm the authorization is current.** Verify the nonce of `authority` equals the tuple
nonce exactly.

**Subgoal: settle the cost.** If `authority` is not empty, credit the difference between
`PER_EMPTY_ACCOUNT_COST` and `PER_AUTH_BASE_COST` to the global refund counter.

**Subgoal: install, then consume.** Set the code of `authority` to `0xef0100` followed by the
address — or, when the address is zero, clear the code instead. Then increase the nonce of
`authority` by one.

Two properties of this loop are worth carrying.

**The nonce increment is last.** Any earlier failure abandons the tuple and moves to the next one,
so an authorization that fails validation does not consume the authority's nonce.<Cite
src="eip-7702" /> It does still cost the transaction sender, because the charge was levied upfront.

**Multiple tuples from one authority are allowed, and the last valid one wins.**<Cite
src="eip-7702" /> Each valid tuple consumes a nonce, so the second must be signed at the
incremented value. A list is not a set.

## A signed authorization is a live round, not a document

The nonce turns the tuple into something with a short and unpredictable shelf life.

An authorization is valid at exactly one nonce of one account. Anything that moves that account's
nonce invalidates it: a transaction the user sends themselves, another authorization they signed,
a contract creation performed by their own delegate. The user does not have to intend it. Sending
one ordinary transaction while a sponsored authorization is in flight is enough.

That is why an authorization behaves less like a permission slip and more like a single round
chambered in one specific position. It fires once, in one place, or not at all.

<Aside kind="warn">
There is a nonce-accounting consequence that has produced real wallet bugs. Since Pectra, a signed
authorization consumes the authority's nonce, so transactions are no longer the only thing that
moves it.<Cite src="eip-7702" /> Any component that derives "next nonce" by counting transactions
sent — rather than by asking the node for the account's nonce, including pending state — will
compute a value that is too low and produce a stuck transaction behind a
[nonce gap](/c/nonce-ordering).
</Aside>

## Signer and payer are different roles

Nothing in the tuple names a submitter. Any party may place a valid authorization into their own
transaction and pay for it.<Cite src="eip-7702" />

This is the point of the design. Sponsorship — account X paying for account Y — is one of the three
motivating use cases the EIP names, alongside batching and privilege de-escalation.<Cite
src="eip-7702" /> A user with zero ETH can be delegated to a smart wallet by an application that
picks up the gas, which is the on-ramp that plain EOAs never had.

## Chain id: the field with a wildcard

`chain_id` may be the current chain's id, or zero. Zero means the authorization is valid on
**every** chain.<Cite src="eip-7702" />

That is not an oversight. The EIP's rationale states the trade directly: what code an address holds
on another chain is a real question, deterministic deployment is possible but "verifying such a
deployment may not always be desirable", so the chain id exists to narrow the scope — and when
universal deployment *is* what you want, you set it to zero.<Cite src="eip-7702" />

The practical use is a user delegating one address to the same wallet implementation across every
chain they will ever touch, with one signature instead of a dozen. The practical risk is the
mirror image: a zero-chain-id authorization is a commitment to whatever code happens to sit at that
address on chains the signer has never used, deployed by people they have never heard of.

This inverts the habit that [EIP-155](/c/eip-155-chain-id-replay) trained into
everyone.<Cite src="eip-155" /> For a decade, chain binding has been the default for transactions
and opting out has not been an available mistake. Here, opting out is a field value — and it is the
value that makes the nicest onboarding flow.

A December 2025 measurement study of EIP-7702 usage names the chain-agnostic mode as one of two
extended attack surfaces it identifies, alongside repeated remote activation of delegated code
through ERC-4337's EntryPoint pipeline.<Cite src="arxiv-7702-phishing" /> The same study reports
analysing over 150,000 authorization and execution events involving around 26,000 addresses, and
finds authorizations heavily concentrated in a small number of contract families linked to
criminal activity.<Cite src="arxiv-7702-phishing" /> Treat that as one team's measurement rather
than a settled figure — but the direction is the point: the wildcard is used, and not only by
wallets.

## Griefing the sponsor

Now back to the relayer at the top.

The relayer's problem is structural, not a bug in its code. It verifies a fact — this signature is
valid, this nonce matches, this account holds a balance I expect to be compensated from — and then
pays for a transaction that will execute later, in a state the authority controls.

The EIP names both moves. The authorized account can cause a sponsored relayer to spend gas without
reimbursement by **invalidating the authorization**, which means increasing its own nonce, or by
**sweeping the relevant assets out of the account**.<Cite src="eip-7702" /> OpenZeppelin repeats
the warning in its own delegation guidance and points at the same two mitigations the EIP
suggests: requiring a bond, or running a reputation system.<Cite src="oz-eoa-delegation" />

Neither mitigation is cryptographic, and that is the lesson. There is no signature the user can
produce that commits them to *not* invalidating their own authorization, because the invalidating
action is an ordinary transaction they are always entitled to send. A relayer's exposure is priced,
not proved.

  why="Signature validity feels like the whole check because it is the only part that is cryptographic, and the cryptographic part is the part that is hard."

## What this does to the mempool

There is a second-order effect the spec spends a whole section on, and it reaches further than
relayers.

Historically a node could statically determine whether a pending transaction was still valid,
because an EOA's balance could only fall through a transaction that EOA originated. One
transaction could invalidate only transactions from the same sender.

Once an account is delegated, **anyone can call it at any point in any transaction**, and that call
can move its balance. A single transaction from a stranger can therefore invalidate pending
transactions from other accounts, and no static analysis of the pool can see it coming.<Cite
src="eip-7702" />

The spec's recommendation to clients is blunt: do not accept more than one pending transaction for
any account with a non-zero delegation indicator.<Cite src="eip-7702" /> If you have internalised
that [the mempool is not a queue](/c/mempool-is-not-a-queue), this is one more reason the pool's
acceptance policy is a node-local decision that your product cannot assume.

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="sponsored-relay-griefing">
A sponsorship service works like this. A user signs an authorization delegating to the service's
audited wallet contract, and also signs a token permit letting the service take 5 USDC from the
user's account as a fee. The service verifies both signatures, checks on-chain that the user's
nonce matches and their USDC balance is at least 5, and broadcasts a type-4 transaction carrying
the authorization plus a call that collects the fee.

Name the two independent ways this loses money, and say why adding more verification before
broadcast does not fix either.

<Answer>
**Loss one: the nonce moves.** Between the service's check and inclusion, the user sends any
ordinary transaction. Their nonce increments, the authorization no longer matches, and the tuple is
abandoned during processing. The service still paid the upfront authorization charge and the
transaction's gas.

**Loss two: the balance moves.** The user transfers their USDC away in that same window. The fee
collection reverts, or collects nothing. The delegation may still have been installed — recall that
processed delegations are not rolled back when execution fails — so the user got the thing they
wanted and the service got the bill.

**Why more pre-broadcast verification does not help.** Every check the service performs describes
the chain as of some block. The transaction executes at a later block, and the state it depends on
is controlled by the counterparty. This is not a race that a faster check wins; it is a check that
is about the wrong point in time. The gap is not closable by observation.

**What actually addresses it.** Change what is at stake rather than what is known: hold a bond, or
carry reputation, which are exactly the two mitigations the EIP suggests. Or move the fee inside
the same call frame so that failing to collect it reverts the work you were paying for — noting
that this still does not recover the authorization charge, because that was levied before
execution.

The general form is one you have met on the networking track: a signature proves something about
the past. Inclusion happens in the future. Any design that treats the two as the same instant has a
window, and someone will stand in it.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Working with tuples in practice

Two toolchain entry points, so the practice exercises are not a scavenger hunt.

In **viem**, `signAuthorization` produces the tuple from a wallet client, and the result is attached
to a transaction's authorization list.<Cite src="viem-sign-authorization" /> In **Foundry**, three
cheatcodes cover the flow: `signDelegation` produces a signed delegation for an implementation
address and a private key, `attachDelegation` attaches one to the next transaction, and
`signAndAttachDelegation` does both.<Cite src="foundry-cheatcode-sign-delegation" /> The signed
struct carries the authority's nonce at signing time, which is exactly the field that makes a test
of the stale-authorization case straightforward to write.<Cite
src="foundry-cheatcode-sign-delegation" />

## Where this pays off

**Delegation is not an allowance** is the next lesson, and it starts where this one ends: you now
know that one signature installs the pointer. That lesson is about what the pointer can do, which
is everything.

**Account abstraction proper** — ERC-4337 and the wallet standards built above it — reuses this
separation of signer from payer, with a paymaster in the relayer's seat and the same
you-cannot-prove-the-future problem to solve.<Cite src="erc-4337-docs" />

**Cross-chain replay** joins the chain-id wildcard here to the transaction-level story from
EIP-155 and the message-level story from typed data.<Cite src="eip-155" /> Three layers, three separate decisions about scope,
and only one of them defaults to safe.
