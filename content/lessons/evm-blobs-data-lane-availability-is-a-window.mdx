An L2 stops producing blocks on a Tuesday. It is a small chain — a few thousand users, a sequencer
run by four people, a treasury nobody is going to bail out. The team goes quiet. Two months later
somebody funds a recovery effort, and the plan is the obvious one: everything the chain ever did was
posted to Ethereum as blob data, so reconstruct the state from L1 and stand up a new sequencer.

The plan fails in the first hour. The commitments are all still there, in blocks that are still
canonical, exactly where the batcher put them. The data behind them is not. No node on the network is
under any obligation to serve it, most stopped weeks ago, and the recovery effort's second hour is
spent asking on a forum whether anyone happens to have kept 40 GB of blobs from a chain nobody was
watching.

Everything about that outcome is working as designed. The design's promise was never "we will keep
this". It was "we will make sure everyone *could* have taken a copy". This lesson is about the
distance between those two sentences, and about the fee market that exists because of it.

## The window, exactly

EIP-4844 sets `MIN_EPOCHS_FOR_BLOB_SIDECARS_REQUESTS` to **4,096 epochs**, and describes it in its
own security considerations as **around 18 days**.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> The mainnet consensus
configuration carries the same constant, and since Fulu it carries a companion,
`MIN_EPOCHS_FOR_DATA_COLUMN_SIDECARS_REQUESTS`, also **4,096
epochs**.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config" /> An independent measurement paper on DAS
networking states the same figure — 4,096 epochs, roughly 18 days — which is a useful
cross-check.<Cite src="pandas-das-paper" />

Now read what the constant's own description says it means: it is the **minimum epoch range over
which a node must serve** data column sidecars.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> The
specification puts the obligation in exactly that shape. Clients must keep a record of sidecars seen
within a serving range anchored to the current wall-clock epoch, must support serving requests over
that range, and peers unable to answer within it should return `ResourceUnavailable` and may be
descored or disconnected.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" />

Two errors live on either side of that sentence, and engineers make both.

**The obvious error: treating the window as permanent.** Nothing serves the data afterwards. The
commitment in the chain remains, so the chain will happily tell you a blob with that versioned hash
was published — and will be no help whatsoever in obtaining it. Every recovery, dispute or audit that
needs blob bytes has a clock on it, and the clock is not 18 days from *now*, it is 18 days from
*publication*.

**The subtler error: treating the window as deletion.** The specification imposes a floor on serving,
not a ceiling on keeping. A node that wants to keep every blob it ever saw is free to, archival
services exist precisely to do that, and the bytes were broadcast to a public network in the first
place. "We will publish it as a blob and it will disappear in eighteen days" is not a retention
policy, not a privacy control and not a deletion mechanism. It is a statement about what strangers
are obliged to do, made about data you already gave to strangers.

<Aside kind="warn">
A concrete way the boundary bites tooling rather than products. PeerDAS changed *what* is retained:
nodes now custody columns of the extended data matrix rather than whole blobs, and the request
protocols follow. `BlobSidecarsByRange` and `BlobSidecarsByRoot` are deprecated as of
`FULU_FORK_EPOCH + MIN_EPOCHS_FOR_BLOB_SIDECARS_REQUESTS`, with a defined transition period during
which clients answer only for the pre-Fulu interval; retrieval after that runs over the data-column
request protocols instead.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> Retrieval code written against the
Dencun-era protocols does not degrade gracefully — it gets empty responses that look exactly like
"the blob is gone".
</Aside>

## Why the window is short on purpose

It would be easy to read 18 days as a compromise someone will eventually improve on. It is the
opposite: it is the concession that makes the whole resource cheap, and EIP-4844 argues it explicitly.

The security-considerations section makes the load argument. Blob data raises per-block bandwidth
requirements, but the *sustained* load is much lower than alternatives that reduce calldata
costs, because there is no expectation that blobs be stored for as long as an execution payload. That
is what makes a "keep for at least a certain period" policy implementable at all, and the EIP
contrasts its 18 days against proposed — and at the time unimplemented — one-year rotation times for
execution payload history.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

And the rationale states the requirement blobs were actually built against, which is narrower than
people assume. Rollups need data to be available **once**, long enough to ensure honest actors can
construct the rollup state — but not forever.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Optimistic rollups need the
underlying data only when fraud proofs are being submitted; ZK rollups prove equivalence between the
blob commitment and their own, and are not reading the blob back from the chain
either.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

Hold that next to what you know about [state storage](/c/storage-is-the-expensive-one) and the design
snaps into focus. A storage slot is expensive because writing one imposes an unbounded obligation:
every full node carries it, indefinitely, and nobody has ever agreed on how to stop. A blob is cheap
because the obligation terminates. **The retention window and the price are not two facts about
blobs. They are the same fact, stated once as a policy and once as a
number.**<Cite src="eip-4844" /><Cite src="eip-4844-site" />

That reframing is the practical takeaway of this lesson, because it tells you what question to ask
about any data-availability offering you are ever quoted a price for: not "how cheap", but "for how
long, enforced by whom, and what happens at the boundary".

## The second market, and why it is second

Blob data is metered and priced by a mechanism that runs in parallel to the execution fee market and
shares none of its inputs.

Each blob consumes `GAS_PER_BLOB` blob gas, defined as `2**17` — 131,072 — so a transaction's blob
gas is that constant times the number of versioned hashes it
carries.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> The price per unit is derived from the header's `excess_blob_gas`, a
running total of blob gas consumed above target, and the base fee per blob gas is an exponential in
that accumulator floored at `MIN_BASE_FEE_PER_BLOB_GAS` of **1
wei**.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> EIP-4844 set `BLOB_BASE_FEE_UPDATE_FRACTION` to **3,338,477**, chosen so
the maximum per-block rate of change at target is about **1.125**;<Cite src="eip-4844" /> that
constant is now a per-fork entry in the `blobSchedule` in client configuration rather than a fixed
number.<Cite src="eip-7840" />

Three properties of this market have consequences that the execution market does not prepare you for.

**The blob fee is burned in full and is not refunded if the transaction fails.** It is deducted from
the sender's balance before execution.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> A batcher whose transaction reverts for
an execution-layer reason has still paid for its data.

**There is no priority component.** The envelope has `max_fee_per_blob_gas` and no tip
counterpart,<Cite src="eip-4844" /> so no proposer earns anything from your blob gas and you cannot
outbid a competitor on the blob side. You compete on the execution tip, or you wait.

**Quantisation is charged, not rounded.** Blob gas is a per-blob constant, so 4 KB and 128 KiB cost
the same.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Any cost model expressed in price-per-byte is wrong for small
payloads by however much of the blob you left empty.

The independence is not total, and the exception is the previous module's material rather than this
one's: EIP-7918 assigns blobs a reserve price denominated in execution gas, so that when the blob base
fee bottoms out near 1 wei the price signal is restored.<Cite src="eip-7918" /> The direction matters
— execution constrains blob at the floor, never the reverse — and it binds only at the bottom of the
range.

## Capacity, and a number this lesson will not give you

The last piece is how much blob space exists per block, and the honest answer is that it is no longer
a constant anyone should memorise.

EIP-7840 moved target, maximum and the base-fee update fraction into a per-fork `blobSchedule` object
in client configuration.<Cite src="eip-7840" /> EIP-7892 then introduced **blob-parameter-only
hardforks** — lightweight upgrades that change nothing but those blob parameters, so capacity can be
stepped on telemetry rather than on a release train.<Cite src="eip-7892" /> Since Fusaka went live on
**3 December 2025**, that is how blob capacity moves.<Cite src="ethereum-org-fusaka" /><Cite src="eip-7892" />

The sources retrieved for this curriculum do not agree on where the pair stands as of August 2026.
ethereum.org's PeerDAS page describes a plan in which the count begins doubling every few weeks after
Fusaka until it reaches a **maximum of 48**, explicitly staged so developers can watch for adverse
effects.<Cite src="ethereum-org-peerdas" /> Other material in this curriculum's research sweep places
the post-Fusaka maximum at **15** after the first parameter fork and **21** after the second, with no
confirmed step beyond that, and describes talk of higher figures as speculative. **Sources disagree,
none of them settles the live value, and no target/max pair is stated here as current.**

The engineering habit that follows is the same one the fee-market lesson reached: **read the schedule
out of your client's configuration for the fork you are on**, and treat any pair printed in any
document — this one included — as a snapshot with an expiry date. If a cost model depends on the
number, the model should query it.

<Aside kind="note">
One capacity limit that is not on the schedule and is easy to trip: EIP-7594 introduces a limit of
**6 blobs per transaction**, which clients must enforce at submission, on receipt from the network,
and during block production and processing.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> A batcher that sizes its
submission against the per-*block* maximum rather than the per-*transaction* limit produces
transactions that are rejected before they are ever priced. Client pool policy adds further
constraints on top: go-ethereum keeps blob transactions in a dedicated pool whose design notes treat
replacement as something to discourage aggressively, since propagating a replacement blob transaction
wastes real bandwidth.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" />
</Aside>

<Check type="predict" concept="blob-pruning">
A regulated client needs to publish daily disclosure documents, roughly 90 KB each, such that any
member of the public can verify at any future date that a given document is the one that was
published on a given day. Retention obligation: seven years. A colleague proposes blobs, on the
grounds that the cost is trivial and "it's on Ethereum, so it's permanent and auditable".

Say what works, what fails, and what the corrected architecture looks like. Then answer the question
the colleague will ask next: whether posting the document as calldata instead fixes it.

<Answer>
**What works is the auditability half, and it works well.** A blob transaction puts a versioned hash
into a canonical block. Seven years later that hash is still there, still in a block every node
agrees on, and anyone holding a copy of the document can recompute the commitment and check
it.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> As a *timestamped commitment*, this is exactly right and costs almost
nothing.

**What fails is the retrieval half.** The obligation to serve blob data is 4,096 epochs, around 18
days, after which no node owes anyone a copy.<Cite src="eip-4844" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" />
At seven years the protocol will confirm that *a* blob with that hash was published and will not
help you obtain it. A member of the public who does not already have the document cannot get it from
the chain, so the verification story collapses for exactly the audience it was built for.

**Note also what the design does not buy: the 90 KB is public forever in the sense that matters
legally.** It was broadcast to a public network and anyone could have archived it. Pruning is a
statement about serving obligations, not a redaction.

**The corrected architecture separates the two guarantees.** Publish the blob for the commitment and
the timestamp. Separately and durably store the document yourself, and treat any third-party archive
as a convenience rather than a control, since none of them are obliged to you either. The chain's
job is to make the commitment unforgeable and time-stamped; retention is your job, and now it is
explicitly on your architecture diagram instead of implicitly assumed.

**On switching to calldata: it changes the answer, and it changes the price by more than people
expect.** Calldata is part of the execution payload, so it is retained on the terms execution history
is retained on — which EIP-4844 itself contrasts against the 18-day blob window when explaining why
blobs can be cheap.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> But it is priced in execution gas, in the market that
NFT mints and DeFi congestion move, and 90 KB of it is a large transaction bought at whatever the
execution base fee happens to be. You would be paying a congestion-linked price for a durability
property that history-expiry work is separately trying to bound. For one document a day it may well
be the right call; the point is that it is a *different trade*, not a strictly better one, and the
deciding question is the one this lesson keeps returning to — for how long, enforced by whom.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

The final lesson in this module explains the other half of why blob data is cheap: since PeerDAS, no
node holds all of it, so adding capacity no longer adds proportional work to every
participant.<Cite src="eip-7594" /><Cite src="ethereum-org-peerdas" /> Retention and distribution are
the two levers, and this lesson covered the first.

On the scaling track, the retention window becomes a design constraint you reason with rather than a
fact you recall. It sets the outer bound on a fraud-proof challenge period, it decides whether a
rollup needs its own archive, and it is the reason "the data is on Ethereum" is a claim that has to
be qualified with a date before it means anything.

And the two-market structure is the foundation of every rollup cost model you will build there: one
component of a batch is denominated in a resource the rest of the chain competes for, and the other
is not.<Cite src="eip-7918" /> Knowing which is which is the difference between a model that explains
a bad month and one that merely records it.
