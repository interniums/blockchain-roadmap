For two years after Dencun, every argument about raising Ethereum's blob capacity ended in the same
place, and it was not a cryptography argument. It was an upload-bandwidth argument about somebody's
home connection.

The reasoning was short enough to fit on one slide. Every full node downloaded every blob, so the
data each node had to move was the blob count times 128 KiB, every twelve seconds, forever. Doubling
the blob count doubled that. Ethereum's own explainer names the constraint without euphemism: upload
bandwidth limits in ordinary residential connections — in Germany, Belgium, Australia, the United
States — are low enough that careless requirements would push node operation into data
centres.<Cite src="ethereum-org-peerdas" /> Capacity was therefore bounded by the weakest node the
network wanted to keep, and every proposal to raise it was a proposal to lose some of them.

PeerDAS is the change that broke that particular argument, and it went live with Fusaka on
**3 December 2025**.<Cite src="ethereum-org-fusaka" /><Cite src="eip-7594" /> This lesson is about
what replaced the argument, and about being precise on what the replacement does and does not
promise — because the popular summary overstates it in a way that matters the next time somebody asks
how far this scales.

## The problem sampling has to solve

Start from why "just download less" is not a plan.

A node that downloads a fraction of the data cannot verify the rest by looking at it. And it cannot
distinguish the two situations it most needs to distinguish: data that is being **withheld** and data
that is merely **late**. This is the same indistinguishability that produces the
[availability–finality dilemma](/c/availability-finality-dilemma) one layer down, and it has the same
consequence — silence is silence, and a rule that acts on silence acts identically in both worlds.

EIP-7594 names it as the design's primary threat: the failure mode of a sampling scheme is a data
withholding attack, in which a block producer tries to convince the network that some data is
available while failing to provide it.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> Everything below is machinery for
making that attack detectable by nodes that never see most of the data.

## Turning "some is missing" into "most is missing"

The move that makes sampling work is erasure coding, and the reason it works is the property from the
previous lesson: a commitment to a polynomial binds evaluations nobody has written down yet.

Each blob is extended one-dimensionally. `FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_EXT_BLOB` is `2 * FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_BLOB`,
which is **8,192**; `FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_CELL` is **64**; and `CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB` is therefore
**128**, which is also `NUMBER_OF_COLUMNS`.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-preset" /> Arrange the
block's blobs as rows: the matrix has at most `MAX_BLOBS_PER_BLOCK` rows and a fixed 128
columns, each row being a blob plus its extension.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> A
**column** is a cross-section — cell *i* of every blob in the block — and each column has its own
gossip subnet.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

Now the threshold that does all the work: **a node can reconstruct the entire data matrix if it
acquires at least 50% of all the columns.**<Cite src="eip-7594" />

Read that as a constraint on the attacker rather than as a convenience for the honest node. To make
the data genuinely unrecoverable, a publisher cannot withhold a little. It must withhold *more than
half* of the columns — because anything less leaves enough for anyone to reconstruct the rest. So the
question "is this data available" has been converted from "are all the bytes here", which requires
downloading all the bytes, into "is more than half of it missing", which a handful of random probes
answers.

The intuition from there is one line: if more than half the columns are missing, a single random
sample lands in a hole with better than even odds, and the chance that a node's *k* independent
samples all miss the holes falls off like 2<sup>−k</sup>. That intuition is directionally right and
it is not the actual guarantee. The real bound is more careful, and this lesson comes back to it
after the mechanics, because the difference is where the overclaiming happens.

<Aside kind="note">
Why columns rather than rows, given that a row is a whole blob and would be the obvious unit? The
EIP's rationale gives two reasons. Reconstruction over columns is far more tractable, because nodes
can be assumed to already hold much of the complete data — most blobs are in the public mempool —
whereas row sampling would require reconstruction to work over extension blobs that do not exist on
the network a priori. And sampling over rows would require *column* extensions computed at block
construction time, on the critical path, while sampling over columns needs row extensions that can be
computed in advance and, in practice, outsourced to the senders of blob
transactions.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> The consensus spec's own FAQ adds the practical objection: a
rollup cannot know in advance which row its blob will occupy, so row custody would not even give
rollups the natural distribution it appears to promise.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />
</Aside>

## Who holds what, and who decides

Custody is assigned rather than volunteered, and the assignment is public.

The configuration sets `NUMBER_OF_CUSTODY_GROUPS` to **128**, `CUSTODY_REQUIREMENT` — the minimum an
honest node custodies and serves from — to **4**, and `SAMPLES_PER_SLOT`, the minimum number of
samples an honest node downloads, to **8**.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config" />
Nodes running validators carry more: `VALIDATOR_CUSTODY_REQUIREMENT` is **8**, and
`BALANCE_PER_ADDITIONAL_CUSTODY_GROUP` is **32 ETH**, so custody scales with the stake behind a
node.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config" /> At the top end, a node backing validators with a
combined balance of at least **4,096 ETH** is a *supernode* and subscribes to every column subnet,
custodying all of them.<Cite src="ethereum-org-peerdas" />

Three properties of the assignment mechanism repay attention.

**It is a public, deterministic function of the node id.** Custody groups are selected
pseudo-randomly by `get_custody_groups`, whose inputs — node id and custody size — are all public, so
any party can compute what any node should be holding.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />
That is what makes targeted sampling possible: a node looking for column 37 can find peers that
should have it through discovery rather than by asking everyone.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

**Custody grows by extension, not reshuffle.** Increasing the custody size for a given node id
*extends* the returned list rather than producing a new shuffle, so a node that voluntarily custodies
more remains a superset of what a peer would compute for it under the default. A node advertises the
larger figure in its ENR as `custody_group_count`, up to the full 128 for a
supernode.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> The design deliberately allows nodes to hold
more than the minimum, and the EIP calls this "transparent" scaling that alternative schemes do not
readily support.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

**Sampling is at least custody.** A node downloads
`sampling_size = max(SAMPLES_PER_SLOT, custody_group_count)` groups each slot, so what it custodies
is always a subset of what it samples, and sampling succeeds only if it retrieves all selected
columns.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />

And a caveat the specification states about itself, which is worth carrying because most explainers
omit it: **custody assignments are not rotated.** The current design deliberately starts with a
simple, stable backbone and does not shuffle assignments over time, while noting that staggered
rotation likely needs to happen on the order of the pruning
period.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> A stable, publicly computable map of who holds
what is exactly as useful to an adversary choosing targets as it is to a peer choosing whom to ask.

### The fraction, and the one piece of arithmetic worth doing

A default node subscribes to 8 of 128 column subnets. ethereum.org works the consequence through:
receiving 8 of 128 subnets is **1/16 of all data**, but because the data was extended to twice its
original size, that is **1/8 of the original blob data** — which it describes as a new theoretical
scaling limit of **8×** relative to the everyone-downloads-everything
scheme.<Cite src="ethereum-org-peerdas" /> EIP-7594's rationale states the same fraction and, more
usefully, states where it goes next: 1/8 today, reducible to 1/16 or even 1/32 in future **by
reducing the size of samples — that is, by increasing the number of
columns**.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

That last clause is the sentence to remember, and the next section explains why.

## What the cell proofs cost, and who pays

A node holding one column and nothing else must be able to verify it. Cell KZG proofs are what make
that possible: a cell proof proves that a blob's KZG commitment opens to a particular cell at a
particular index, which is what allows downloading specific cells while preserving integrity against
the commitment.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

Computing those proofs is expensive; verifying them is cheap. The specification resolves the
asymmetry by moving the work to the party that has the most reason to do it once: **blob transaction
senders compute the cell proofs themselves** and include them in the pool wrapper, rather than having
every block producer recompute them for every blob.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> The wrapper carries
`CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB` proofs per blob — 128 — and the blobs travel *unextended*, at half that many
cells each, because the receiver can recompute the extension cheaply and sending them would be
redundant.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> Verification is batched.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

This is the design pattern to extract: when a scheme has an expensive half and a cheap half, the
protocol pushed the expensive half onto the sender, who does it once, and left the cheap half with
the thousands of parties who each do it once per block.

<Aside kind="note">
There is a hard timing budget behind all of this that explains why the mechanism is peer requests
rather than something more elaborate. Blocks arrive every twelve seconds and a committee must
validate each new block within the first four seconds, so sampling has to complete inside that same
four-second budget if availability is to be attested alongside validity without changing the
consensus protocol.<Cite src="pandas-das-paper" /> Every design choice above — direct peer exchanges,
precomputed proofs, deterministic custody discoverable in advance — is downstream of a four-second
deadline over planetary-scale latencies.
</Aside>

## The guarantee, stated honestly

Now the part that gets summarised badly.

EIP-7594 formalises the security argument. With *n* sampling nodes, *m* possible samples and *k* the
minimum a node must download, it bounds the probability of convincing a fraction ε of the nodes that
withheld data is available. For mainnet parameters and an assumed 10,000-node network, the EIP
tabulates upper bounds: at ε = 0.02 the bound is 10<sup>−20.04</sup>, at ε = 0.03 it is
10<sup>−101.55</sup>, at ε = 0.05 it is 10<sup>−306.34</sup>. Its conclusion is that the chance of a
successful attack drops to a negligible amount, and PeerDAS is considered secure against
withholding.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

The row above those is the one worth reading carefully. **At ε = 0.01 — fooling 100 nodes out of
10,000 — the tabulated upper bound is 1.**<Cite src="eip-7594" /> The bound says nothing at that
scale. It is not a claim that fooling 1% of the network is easy; it is a statement that this argument
does not rule it out.

So the honest formulation has three parts, and dropping any of them produces a claim the specification
does not make:

- The guarantee is **probabilistic**, not a proof. It is a statement about the probability of an
  attacker satisfying enough sampling queries, not a demonstration that the data exists.
- It is a guarantee about **the network**, not about you. It bounds the chance of fooling a fraction
  of the nodes. An individual node's own successful sample is much weaker evidence than the aggregate
  bound suggests.
- It **strengthens with network size**, and the EIP says so directly: the sampling scheme decreases
  the probability of a successful attack as the network grows, for a sublinear amount of downloaded
  data.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> A small network is a materially weaker one.

There is a second half to the mechanism that is easy to miss and does a lot of practical work:
availability is **enforced**, not merely observed. Validators follow fork-choice rules under which
they accept and vote for a block only after verifying the availability of its
data.<Cite src="ethereum-org-peerdas" /> And the network heals: a node that obtains more than half the
columns should reconstruct the full matrix, and once it has a column by reconstruction it must expose
it as if it had received it over the network — sending it to its topic mesh neighbours if subscribed,
and otherwise still advertising availability — after which it may drop the column if it is not part of
its own custody.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /><Cite src="ethereum-org-peerdas" /> Gaps
close on their own.

## What actually scales, and by how much

Here is where the popular summary needs correcting, and where a product engineer's instinct for
asymptotics earns its keep.

"No node downloads everything, so blob count can grow without per-node cost growing" is not quite
true, and the arithmetic above shows why. The column count is **fixed at 128**.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-preset" />
Adding blobs adds *rows* to the matrix, not columns, so every column gets taller and a node
custodying 8 of 128 groups downloads 1/16 of a larger total. Per-node bandwidth still grows linearly
in the blob count.

What PeerDAS changed is the **constant**, and it changed it by a lot: each node moves roughly an
eighth of the original data instead of all of it, which is the 8× headroom ethereum.org
describes.<Cite src="ethereum-org-peerdas" /> Spend that headroom and you are back at the same wall,
which is precisely why the EIP names the next lever in the same breath as the current fraction:
reduce sample size, increase the number of columns, and the fraction each node carries falls to 1/16
or 1/32.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> Beyond that sits full Danksharding, which replaces the
one-dimensional extension of each blob with two-dimensional coding across the entire
matrix.<Cite src="ethereum-org-peerdas" />

So the accurate sentence is: **PeerDAS did not decouple blob capacity from per-node bandwidth. It
divided the coupling by a constant, and made the constant a parameter the protocol can keep
increasing.** That is a smaller claim than "unlimited scaling" and a much larger one than "a
one-time 8× win", because a coupling with an adjustable constant is an engineering problem rather
than a ceiling.

  why="It was exactly true from Dencun until Fusaka, so material written in that window says so correctly, and the reasoning it teaches is the reasoning that produced the old capacity ceiling."
  src="eip-7594"
/>

This is also the mechanism behind the change in rollup economics. Capacity that can be raised in
steps, on telemetry, by blob-parameter-only forks rather than by full network
upgrades<Cite src="eip-7892" /><Cite src="eip-7840" /> is capacity that can chase demand — and a data
market whose supply can chase demand prices very differently from one whose supply is a constant. The
previous lesson declined to give you the current target and maximum, and the reason is visible from
here: those are now dials, and dials move.

<Check type="explain" concept="data-availability-sampling">
A default node — no validators, custody at the minimum — completes its sampling for a slot: every
column it selected came back and every cell verified against the block's commitments.

State precisely what it may now conclude and with what confidence. Then answer three follow-ups: does
the conclusion get stronger if it samples twice as many columns; does it get stronger if the network
doubles in size; and what would change if a rollup that posted a blob in that slot could not obtain
its own data back an hour later.

<Answer>
**What it may conclude.** That it retrieved every column it selected, each verified against the
block's KZG commitments,<Cite src="eip-7594" /> and therefore that any publisher withholding enough
to make the data unrecoverable — more than half the columns, since 50% suffices to
reconstruct<Cite src="eip-7594" /> — happened to satisfy all of this node's queries. It may not
conclude that the data exists. The guarantee is a bound on an attacker's success probability, and the
specification's own table has a row, at ε = 0.01, where that upper bound is 1.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

**Does more sampling help?** Yes, and this is the lever the node itself controls. Sampling size is
`max(SAMPLES_PER_SLOT, custody_group_count)`, and a node may advertise a higher custody count, up to
all 128 groups.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> More samples means more queries an
attacker must satisfy simultaneously. It costs bandwidth in direct proportion, which is the whole
trade.

**Does a bigger network help?** Yes, and this one the node does not control. The EIP states that the
scheme decreases the probability of a successful attack as the network grows, for a sublinear amount
of data downloaded,<Cite src="eip-7594" /> and the tabulated bounds are computed for an assumed
10,000-node network.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> A node's own sampling result means less on a small
network than on a large one, and nothing the node does locally compensates for that.

**And the rollup that cannot fetch its own data an hour later?** That is not a contradiction, and
recognising why is the point of the exercise. Availability and retrievability are different claims.
Sampling attests that the data was published and reconstructible at the time of the block; it says
nothing about whether any particular party can obtain it later, over which protocol, from a peer that
still custodies the right columns. And the obligation to serve is bounded in any case, so retrieval
is a question with a clock on it.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /><Cite src="eip-4844" />

**The transferable rule:** treat a successful sample as evidence about *publication at a moment*,
priced against the size of the network doing the sampling. Anything you need to read later is a
retention question, and anything you need to be *correct* is a proof question. Three different
guarantees, three different mechanisms, and only one of them was checked here.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this unlocks the scaling track

This module ends where Track 09 begins, and the handover is unusually clean.

**The rollup cost model** is the two markets from the previous lesson plus the capacity schedule from
this one. A batcher decides between blobs and calldata on every batch, and the decision moves because
the blob market's supply is now stepped by parameter-only forks rather than
fixed.<Cite src="eip-7892" /><Cite src="eip-7840" />

**Rollups versus validiums** is this lesson's guarantee, moved off Ethereum. A validium keeps the
same commitments and the same proofs and replaces the availability mechanism with something else — a
committee, another chain, a different sampling network. Everything you now know about what sampling
does and does not prove is the toolkit for evaluating that substitution, and the question to ask
about any alternative is the one this lesson answered for PeerDAS: what does an attacker have to
withhold, how many parties have to be fooled, and what does the bound say at small fractions.

**The withholding attack** — a valid proof over data nobody has — is the failure mode those modules
build toward, and it is not exotic. It is the scenario in which every mechanism in this module works
correctly and the users still cannot exit, because availability was never a claim about the future.

And the honest asymptotics matter more there than anywhere else. A scaling roadmap whose next step is
"more columns, smaller samples"<Cite src="eip-7594" /> is a very different object from one whose next
step is unbounded, and telling those apart is most of what senior judgement about this space consists
of.
