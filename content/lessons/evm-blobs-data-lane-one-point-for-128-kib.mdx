An engineer reads the previous lesson, gets to the versioned hash, and asks the question everyone
asks: why is there a KZG commitment in the middle of this at all? A blob is 128 KiB of bytes. SHA-256
turns 128 KiB of bytes into 32 bytes. It is binding, it is standard, it has no ceremony, no elliptic
curve and no new failure mode, and the versioned hash runs it through `sha256` anyway.<Cite src="eip-4844" />
Why is there an extra layer underneath?

It is a good question, and it has a precise answer that is not "KZG is better". A hash is a perfectly
good [commitment](/c/commitment) — for the operation a hash supports. The blob design needs two
operations a hash does not support, and both of them are load-bearing rather than nice to have. This
lesson is about what those operations are and what had to be bought to get them.

## The two things a blob commitment has to do

Write down the jobs before comparing the tools.

**Job one: convince a contract of one value at one position, without publishing the rest.** This is
the optimistic-rollup fraud proof from the previous lesson. The challenger walks a disputed
transition in small steps and needs to establish, on-chain, that byte range *k* of the blob holds a
particular value — while the blob itself stays off-chain, because putting it on-chain is exactly the
cost the whole design exists to avoid.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

**Job two: let the data be extended, split, and checked piecewise against the same commitment.** This
is data availability sampling, and the requirement is stronger than it sounds. The blob gets
erasure-coded into an extension, the result is cut into cells, and a node that holds one cell and
nothing else must be able to verify that cell against the blob's commitment.<Cite src="eip-7594" />
Note what "the same commitment" is doing there: the extension cells are values nobody wrote down when
the commitment was made, and they must still be authenticated by it.

Now run the candidates.

### A hash fails job one immediately

To open a hash commitment you reveal the preimage. There is no partial opening; the only proof that
`sha256(blob)` corresponds to a particular byte at position *k* is the whole blob. That collapses job
one back into "publish 128 KiB in calldata", which is the cost you were escaping.

This is not a defect in SHA-256. It is what a [plain hash commitment](/c/hash-commitment) is: a
binding reference to one indivisible object.

### A Merkle tree fixes job one and stalls on job two

The obvious next move is a [Merkle tree](/c/merkle-tree) over the 4,096 field
elements.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Now an [inclusion proof](/c/merkle-inclusion-proof) for one element
is a path of sibling hashes — twelve levels for 4,096 leaves — and job one is solved with a proof
that grows only logarithmically. There is no ceremony, no pairing, and no new cryptographic
assumption beyond the hash you already trust. If job one were the whole requirement, this is what
Ethereum would have shipped, and the state trie is evidence that the ecosystem is entirely
comfortable with it.

Job two is where it stops.

Erasure-code the 4,096 elements into 8,192 and you have 4,096 values that were never in the original
tree. A Merkle root over the original data says nothing about them. You can build a second
tree over the extended data — but then a node checking one extension cell against that second root
learns only that *somebody published this cell under this root*, not that the extension was computed
correctly from the original blob. A publisher who wants to withhold data can extend incorrectly, and
the Merkle root cannot tell you. Recovering the guarantee means proving the extension was done
honestly, which is a proof about a computation over all 4,096 inputs — precisely the expensive thing
sampling exists to avoid.

The property the design actually needs is that **the commitment binds every evaluation, including
ones nobody has computed yet.** That is not something a hash tree offers. It is the defining feature
of a polynomial commitment.

## What a polynomial commitment is

The scheme comes from Kate, Zaverucha and Goldberg, and the abstract states the property in one
sentence: a polynomial commitment lets a committer commit to a polynomial with a short string that a
verifier can use to confirm claimed evaluations of it. Their commitments are constant size — single
elements — the overhead of opening one is also constant, and even opening multiple evaluations needs
only a constant amount of communication.<Cite src="kate-zaverucha-goldberg" /> The comparison they
draw is against the homomorphic commitment schemes already in the literature, whose commitment sizes
are *linear* in the degree of the committed polynomial.<Cite src="kate-zaverucha-goldberg" />

Map that onto a blob. Take its 4,096 field elements as describing a polynomial of degree 4,095. The
[commitment](/c/polynomial-commitment) to that polynomial is one elliptic-curve point — 48 bytes as
`KZGCommitment` in the specification.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> An opening at any chosen point is one
more group element, also 48 bytes, and this does not depend on the degree.<Cite src="eip-4844" /><Cite src="kate-zaverucha-goldberg" />

That is the whole shape of the trade: 128 KiB of data, one point to commit to it, one point to open
it anywhere. The project's own reference site compresses the reasoning into a single line worth
keeping: blobs are committed to with KZG, an efficient vector commitment scheme with fixed-size proof
data, forward-compatible with data-availability sampling.<Cite src="eip-4844-site" /> Three claims,
and the third is the one the next section is about.

### Price one concrete task against all three schemes

Abstract comparisons of commitment schemes are easy to nod along to. Run one job through each.

**The job.** A challenger must establish on-chain that field element 3,001 of a published blob holds
a specific value. The blob is public but off-chain; the chain holds only its 32-byte versioned
hash.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

**Subgoal — price the hash.** The only opening is the preimage, so the challenger publishes all
4,096 field elements as calldata and the contract rehashes them.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Proof size is
the whole blob. Verification is a hash over 128 KiB. The dispute now costs what the design was built
to avoid.

**Subgoal — price the Merkle tree.** The challenger publishes the element plus a sibling path,
twelve levels deep for 4,096 leaves, and the contract walks it. Proof size is a few hundred bytes and
verification is a dozen hashes. This is genuinely good, and for job one alone it is the cheaper
answer.

**Subgoal — price the polynomial commitment.** The challenger publishes the 48-byte commitment, the
position, the claimed value and a 48-byte proof, and calls the point-evaluation precompile, which
first checks that the commitment hashes to the stored versioned hash and then verifies the
opening.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Proof size is constant regardless of degree.<Cite src="kate-zaverucha-goldberg" />
Verification is a fixed 50,000 gas.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

**Subgoal — notice that job one did not decide this.** Merkle wins on gas here and loses the design
anyway. Keep that result in mind through the next section, because it means the choice was made
somewhere else entirely — and the place it was made is job two.

<Aside kind="note">
**Which polynomial, exactly — and why sources disagree.** There are two ways to read 4,096 numbers as
a polynomial of degree 4,095: as its *coefficients*, or as its *values* at 4,096 fixed points. They
give different polynomials, and material describing blobs uses both readings. ethereum.org's PeerDAS
page describes the blob as a polynomial whose coefficients encode the data.<Cite src="ethereum-org-peerdas" />
The evidence in the specification points the other way: the mainnet trusted setup ships 4,096 G1
points **in the Lagrange basis** alongside the monomial-basis vector, and a Lagrange-basis setup is
what lets a commitment be computed directly from a blob's 4,096 *values* with no interpolation
step.<Cite src="consensus-specs-trusted-setup-4096" />

Nothing in this lesson depends on which one you carry — constant-size openings and the extension
property hold either way — but it is worth knowing that the disagreement is real rather than sloppy
paraphrase, and that a basis change is not a free relabelling when you are writing an encoder.
</Aside>

### How the extension property falls out

Return to job two with the polynomial in hand.

A degree-4,095 polynomial is determined by any 4,096 of its evaluations. Evaluate it at 4,096
*additional* points and you have 8,192 values of which any 4,096 suffice to recover the whole thing —
which is exactly Reed–Solomon erasure coding, obtained for free by having chosen a polynomial in the
first place.<Cite src="ethereum-org-peerdas" /> The consensus preset makes the doubling explicit:
`FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_EXT_BLOB` is `2 * FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_BLOB`, giving
8,192.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-preset" />

And the commitment does not change. It was a commitment to the *polynomial*, not to a list of 4,096
numbers, so the extension values are things it already committed to. EIP-7594 states the consequence
directly: cells are the smallest units that can be authenticated against their blob's KZG
commitments, and a cell proof proves that a KZG commitment opens to a cell at a given
index — including the extension cells.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

This is why the fourth lesson in this module is possible at all. Without a commitment that binds
values nobody wrote down, there is no way to hand a node a fragment of erasure-coded data and let it
check the fragment on its own.

## What it cost: the ceremony

Nothing about this is free, and the price is the one property the whole scheme rests on that a hash
does not need.

KZG commits to a polynomial by evaluating it at a secret value, and the ceremony exists to construct
that secret so that no single person knows it — and so that many people remain convinced nobody knows
it years later.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" /> The public output is not the secret but its
**powers**: the ceremony's own documentation writes the requirement as
`[1, τ, τ², τ³, …, τ^(2¹²−1)]`.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" />

Read that exponent. 2¹² is 4,096 — the same 4,096 as `FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_BLOB`.<Cite src="eip-4844" />
The ceremony's degree bound and the blob's size are the same number, because they are the same
constraint: the [structured reference string](/c/structured-reference-string) has to be long enough
to commit to a degree-4,095 polynomial and there is no reason to make it longer. The shipped mainnet
setup file matches: 4,096 G1 points in the Lagrange basis, 4,096 in the monomial basis, and 65 G2
points.<Cite src="consensus-specs-trusted-setup-4096" />

The trust assumption is **1-of-N**: only a single participant in the entire ceremony needs to have
kept their secret input for the result to be secure, which means an attacker would have to compromise
the contribution software of every participant and collude with all of them.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" />
The documentation is candid that the more realistic failure mode is not collusion but a common bug
leaking randomness across many contributions, which is the argument for multiple independent
implementations rather than one audited one.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" />

  why="'Trusted setup' and 'secret' pattern-match to confidentiality. The ceremony's own FAQ states the consequence in the other register: an attacker who extracted the secret could make arbitrary claims about the data in EIP-4844, which would break every service depending on blob data."

The second cost is compute. Verifying KZG proofs is genuinely expensive work that nodes perform on
behalf of blob consumers — expensive enough that it is the stated justification for tying the blob
base fee's floor to the execution base fee, on the reasoning that the execution base fee is the
network's own live measure of how in demand that compute is.<Cite src="eip-7918" /> You can see the
same asymmetry inside the EVM: `BLOBHASH` costs 3 gas and the point-evaluation precompile costs
50,000.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

And there is a third cost that is easy to miss because it is structural rather than numeric: the
scheme is now a dependency with a version. That is precisely why the execution layer refers to blobs
by a *versioned* hash rather than by the commitment — so the scheme underneath can be replaced without
changing the 32-byte interface contracts already use.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> The cryptography was
chosen knowing it might have to be swapped.

<Check type="explain" concept="kzg-commitment">
A colleague proposes a simplification for a new data-availability layer you are designing: keep the
128 KiB chunk, keep the erasure coding, but replace KZG with a Merkle tree over the extended data.
Their argument: Merkle proofs are logarithmic rather than constant, which is close enough at this
size; hashing is far cheaper than pairings; and it removes the trusted setup entirely.

Say which of their three claims are true, identify the one thing the design loses, and describe the
concrete attack that becomes available.

<Answer>
**All three of their claims are true, and that is what makes the proposal worth taking seriously.**
Twelve sibling hashes for 4,096 leaves is a small proof; hashing genuinely is cheaper than
pairing-based verification, which is expensive enough to be cited as a justification for pricing
policy;<Cite src="eip-7918" /> and dropping the ceremony removes a real 1-of-N assumption along with
the operational burden of distributing and validating a setup
file.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-trusted-setup-4096" />

**What it loses is the binding between the original data and its extension.** A Merkle root over the
extended data commits to whatever 8,192 values the publisher put in the tree. It does not commit to
those values being a correct Reed–Solomon extension of the original 4,096. With KZG there is nothing
to check, because the extension values are evaluations of the polynomial the commitment already
committed to.<Cite src="eip-7594" /><Cite src="kate-zaverucha-goldberg" />

**The attack.** A publisher builds the tree over a deliberately inconsistent extension — the first
half is the real blob, the second half is noise. Every individual cell verifies against the root,
because each one really is in the tree. Sampling nodes draw random cells, every sample succeeds, and
they conclude the data is available. But no set of 4,096 cells reconstructs the blob, because the
values are not evaluations of a single polynomial. The data was never recoverable, and the sampling
scheme said it was. That is a data-withholding attack that passes every check the design
performs — and defeating withholding is the entire security goal of the sampling
scheme.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

**Repairing it costs more than KZG.** You would have to prove the extension was computed correctly,
which is a statement about a computation over all 4,096 inputs. Proving that cheaply enough for every
block puts you back in the business of succinct proofs, with a heavier dependency than the one you
removed.

**The transferable rule:** when comparing commitment schemes, do not compare proof sizes. Compare
what the commitment *binds*. A Merkle root binds a list. A polynomial commitment binds a function,
and a function has values at points you have not looked at yet.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

The next lesson leaves the cryptography and asks the question the commitment quietly raises: the
commitment is in the chain forever, so how long is the *data* around to be opened against it? The
answer is a bounded window, and it turns out to be the reason blob data is priced the way it
is.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

After that, the extension property does the heavy lifting. Every claim in the PeerDAS lesson — that a
node can hold one column of 128 and verify it, that any half the columns reconstruct the whole, that
a withholder must hide a large fraction rather than a little — is this lesson's polynomial, seen from
the networking layer.<Cite src="eip-7594" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />

On the ZK track, the same scheme reappears as one polynomial commitment among several, and the
comparison you will be asked to make — setup versus no setup, constant versus logarithmic openings,
what each scheme binds — is the one you just made against Merkle. The blob case is the easiest place
to have made it first, because here the requirement that decides it is concrete: somebody has to be
able to check a piece of data that was never written down.
