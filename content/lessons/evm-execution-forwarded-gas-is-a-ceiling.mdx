A relayer service takes signed user intents, wraps each one in a transaction it pays for, and submits
it. Estimation returns 184,000 gas. The relayer sends 184,000. The transaction lands, the receipt says
success, the relayer collects its fee.

The user's action did not happen. Somewhere three frames down, a sub-call ran out of gas, returned
false, and a caller two levels up treated the failure as an acceptable outcome and carried on.

Nothing malfunctioned. The gas limit on a transaction is not a promise about any frame except the
first, and the amount a caller hands to a callee is a *ceiling* the protocol may quietly lower. This
lesson is about the rule that makes that true, the attack it killed, and the attack it created.

## The rule, in one line of specification

Here is the entire 63/64 rule as the executable specification states it:<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-gas" />

```python
def max_message_call_gas(gas: Uint) -> Uint:
    return gas - (gas // Uint(64))
```

Whatever a caller holds, at least one sixty-fourth of it stays behind. This has been true since
EIP-150 in 2016.<Cite src="eip-150" />

The reason is stated in the EIP itself: before it, a contract could forward everything it had, so a
callee that consumed all available gas left its caller with nothing — no gas to inspect the result, no
gas to clean up, no gas to fail loudly. The caller could not survive the callee. The 63/64 rule makes
survival structural rather than a matter of care.<Cite src="eip-150" /><Cite src="rareskills-eip150" />

### The full computation, in order

The one-liner is only half of it. What actually reaches the callee comes out of
`calculate_message_call_gas`, and the order of operations matters:<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-gas" />

```python
call_stipend = Uint(0) if value == 0 else call_stipend
if gas_left < extra_gas + memory_cost:
    return MessageCallGas(gas + extra_gas, gas + call_stipend)

gas = min(gas, max_message_call_gas(gas_left - memory_cost - extra_gas))

return MessageCallGas(gas + extra_gas, gas + call_stipend)
```

Walk it as four steps.

**Subgoal — pay the fixed charges first.** `extra_gas` is the account-access charge plus, for a
value-bearing call, the value-transfer charge and possibly the new-account charge. `memory_cost` is
whatever expanding the input and output regions costs in the *caller's* frame. Both come off the top,
before anything is forwarded.

**Subgoal — apply the cap to what is left.** `max_message_call_gas` runs on
`gas_left - memory_cost - extra_gas`, not on the raw remaining gas. Warming a call target with an
access list therefore does two things at once: it lowers `extra_gas`, and it raises the base the
63/64 fraction is taken from.<Cite src="eip-2929" />

**Subgoal — take the minimum, and notice what is not there.** `gas = min(gas, ...)`. If the caller
asked for more than the cap allows, the request is silently trimmed. There is no error, no flag, and
nothing anywhere that tells the callee it received less than the caller intended, or tells the caller
that its request was reduced.

**Subgoal — add the stipend, if any.** The gas made available to the sub-call is
`gas + call_stipend`, and `call_stipend` is zero unless the call carries value, in which case the
specification's `GasCosts.CALL_STIPEND` is 2,300.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-gas" /> That is a
small allowance added on top so a plain ether transfer to a contract can at least run a trivial
receive handler.

## The decay, and the attack it killed

If each frame keeps a sixty-fourth, forwarded gas shrinks geometrically with depth. After `d` nested
calls the innermost frame holds at most `G × (63/64)^d`.

Take the largest budget a single transaction can have — since the Fusaka upgrade a transaction is
capped at 16,777,216 gas, which is 2^24, regardless of how much room the block
has.<Cite src="eip-7825" /><Cite src="eip-7607" />
Run the arithmetic on that ceiling:

| depth | gas that could still reach the innermost frame |
|---|---|
| 10 | ~14,300,000 |
| 100 | ~3,470,000 |
| 200 | ~719,000 |
| 500 | ~6,380 |
| 1000 | ~2.4 |
| 1024 | ~1.7 |

This is arithmetic on the rule, not a measurement: it is `16777216 × (63/64)^d` with the integer
flooring ignored, and it assumes each frame spends *nothing* except what the rule withholds. Real
frames also pay for the call instruction itself, so the reachable depth is lower still. Simulating the
same decay with a fixed per-frame consumption gives depth 500 at 100 gas per frame, 474 at 150, and
293 at 2,600 — again arithmetic, not observation, and again worth reproducing yourself with a
recursive contract and a `gasleft()` probe rather than believed.

The point does not depend on the exact figure. **The 1024-frame depth limit is not reachable through
gas-consuming calls.** You run out of gas hundreds of frames earlier, every time.

The depth limit still exists and still fails the way the previous lesson described — gas returned, 0
pushed, execution continues.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-system" /> It is simply no longer a
lever anyone can pull.

## The attack it created

Removing one attack created another, and this one is live.

Because forwarded gas is a ceiling chosen by the caller, a caller can supply *just enough* gas for its
own logic to complete and *not enough* for a sub-call to finish. The sub-call halts exceptionally.
The outer frame sees false. If the outer code ignores that boolean, or treats failure as an acceptable
outcome, the transaction succeeds having skipped the work it was supposed to do.

That is **gas griefing**, and it is the opening scene. It has three ingredients, all of which must be
present:

1. Somebody other than the beneficiary chooses the gas limit — a relayer, a keeper, a batching
   contract, an account-abstraction bundler.
2. The outer logic can complete on less gas than the whole operation needs.
3. The result of the inner call is not enforced.

Remove any one and the attack disappears. Which is the useful way to hold it, because the third
ingredient is the only one you usually control.

### What does not fix it

**Checking `gasleft()` before the call.** The check passes, the call is made with 63/64 of what you
had, and the callee runs out anyway because 63/64 of a small number is still a small number.

**A larger fixed gas argument.** `call{gas: 100000}` is a maximum. When the caller holds less than
that, the call proceeds with less, silently, by the `min` above.

**Trusting the receipt.** A transaction's status flag reflects the outermost frame only. An inner
frame that reverted or halted contributes nothing to it.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-interpreter" />

### What does

**Enforce the boolean.** `require(ok)` converts a silent skip into a loud failure, which pushes the
cost of griefing back onto the griefer: the transaction now fails and they have wasted their own gas
for no state change.

**Measure gas across the call, not before it.** Record `gasleft()` immediately before and immediately
after, and require the difference to be at least what the operation was supposed to consume. This is
the only construction that detects "the callee was starved" as distinct from "the callee failed on its
own merits", and it is what relayer contracts that take griefing seriously actually do.

**Make the outer logic depend on the inner result.** If the payment to the relayer is computed from
state that only the inner call can produce, a starved inner call stops being profitable to arrange.

<Aside kind="warn">
The 2,300-gas stipend is not a defence and has not been one for years. It is a fixed number priced
against opcode costs that have been repriced repeatedly since it was chosen, so "the recipient can
only log an event with 2,300 gas" is an assumption about a 2016 gas schedule.<Cite src="eip-1884" />
The compiler now agrees: since 0.8.31, solc warns about the deprecation of `send` and `transfer` on
`address`.<Cite src="solidity-changelog" /> Use `call` with an explicit reentrancy guard, and treat
the gas you forward as a decision rather than a default.
</Aside>

### So how much should you forward?

Three answers, in descending order of how often they are right.

**Forward everything the rule allows, and check the result.** Omitting the gas argument entirely —
`addr.call(data)` with no `{gas: ...}` — forwards the maximum the rule permits, which is what you want
whenever the callee is trusted or the whole operation is meant to succeed or fail together. The 63/64
reserve means you are still alive afterwards to branch on the boolean. This is the default for a
reason.

**Cap it deliberately when the callee is hostile and the failure is acceptable.** A notification hook,
an optional callback, a best-effort refund: these are cases where you genuinely want a bound, because
an unbounded callee could otherwise consume the whole transaction. Pick the bound from measurement of
the honest path plus headroom, and document what happens when it is hit.

**Never cap it to a number you read somewhere.** A fixed gas argument is an assertion about a gas
schedule, and gas schedules change. EIP-1884's own backwards-compatibility section says it plainly:
contracts that assume a fixed gas cost for calls, or for internal sections, may cease to
function — and it names the 2,300 stipend as the case most likely to
break.<Cite src="eip-1884" />

The asymmetry worth internalising: **too much forwarded gas costs you nothing when the callee behaves,
because unspent gas comes back. Too little costs you a silent skip.** The failure modes are not
symmetric, so the default should not be either.

## Why estimation cannot save you

`eth_estimateGas` is where these two properties collide, and the failure mode is specific enough to
be worth naming.

Estimation is a dry run followed by a binary search: execute the transaction at some limit, see
whether it succeeds, narrow the range. The search is only correct if success is monotonic in the gas
limit — if a transaction that works at N also works at N+1. That assumption breaks whenever behaviour
depends on the gas *available* rather than on the gas *needed*, and the 63/64 rule is exactly such a
dependency. A transaction can be estimated to N, sent with N, and fail deep in the call tree, because
at N the innermost frame receives fractionally less than it did during the run that the estimator
sampled.

Two practical consequences.

**An estimate is a floor with no margin, not a budget.** Tooling adds headroom for this reason, and
the appropriate headroom is a property of your call graph's depth, not a universal percentage.

**An estimation failure is not a revert.** A node reporting that gas required exceeds allowance is
making a balance-and-allowance statement, not a claim about the transition function. Reading
estimation errors as revert reasons produces confidently wrong diagnoses.

## The other way a caller gets hurt

There is a symmetric hazard worth mentioning here because it is also about gas at a frame boundary,
and it runs in the opposite direction.

A caller that copies a sub-call's return data into memory pays memory-expansion gas proportional to
the size of that data — and the callee chooses the size. A malicious callee can return megabytes,
forcing the caller to pay a quadratic expansion cost it never agreed to, which is a denial of service
against the caller rather than the callee. The mitigation is to bound how much return data you copy,
using `RETURNDATASIZE` to decide rather than copying blindly; the pattern is well enough known to have
a widely used library implementation.<Cite src="excessively-safe-call" />

Both hazards have the same root. **Gas at a frame boundary is a negotiation with no protocol.** The
caller picks how much to hand over and cannot be sure it was enough; the callee picks how much to hand
back and the caller pays for it.

<Check type="predict" concept="gas-griefing">
A payments contract lets a relayer execute pre-signed user operations and take a fee:

```solidity
function execute(UserOp calldata op, bytes calldata sig) external {
    require(_verify(op, sig), "bad signature");
    nonces[op.sender]++;
    (bool ok, ) = op.target.call{gas: op.callGas}(op.callData);
    emit Executed(op.sender, ok);
    _payRelayer(msg.sender, FLAT_FEE);
}
```

The team's position: the boolean is not ignored, it is recorded in an event, and the user consented to
`op.callGas` when they signed. Predict what a hostile relayer does, and what it costs them.

<Answer>
**The relayer sends the transaction with a total gas limit chosen so that `op.callGas` cannot be
honoured.**

`op.callGas` is a maximum, not a requirement. `min(gas, max_message_call_gas(...))` will silently
trim it to 63/64 of whatever remains in the frame at that moment.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-gas" />
The relayer controls the transaction-level gas limit, so they control what remains. They pick a limit
generous enough for `_verify`, the nonce write, the event and `_payRelayer`, and too small for the
sub-call.

**What the relayer gains.** The nonce increments, so the user's signed operation is consumed and
cannot be replayed. The event is emitted with `ok == false`, which almost nothing reads. `_payRelayer`
pays out. The relayer is paid the flat fee for having done nothing, and can repeat it against every
operation in the queue.

**What it costs them.** Slightly *less* than executing honestly, because they skipped the expensive
part. That is the whole problem: the griefing is not merely possible, it is the profit-maximising
strategy. Any design where the fee does not depend on the work is a design that pays for not doing
the work.

**Three defects, ranked.**

1. **The fee is unconditional.** `_payRelayer` runs whether `ok` is true or false. Gate it, or compute
   it from something only a successful call produces.
2. **The nonce is consumed before the call and never restored.** A user's operation is destroyed by a
   failed attempt, which turns a griefing attack into a denial of service against the user as well as
   a theft from the protocol.
3. **`op.callGas` is treated as a guarantee.** The only way to establish that the sub-call actually
   received what the user signed for is to measure it: read `gasleft()` before and after and require
   the observed consumption, or the observed availability, to meet the signed figure. Nothing in the
   call instruction reports it.

**The one that is not a defect.** Recording the boolean in an event is genuinely better than
discarding it — it makes the attack visible in hindsight. It is monitoring, not enforcement, and the
team's mistake was counting it as the latter.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**Three ways out of a frame** is next, and it completes this picture from the callee's side. You now
know why a caller is always alive to observe a failure; the next lesson is about what the caller can
actually tell about it, which is less than you would like.

**Silent call failure** as a practice exercise is this lesson made concrete: build the two-contract
case, prove in a test that the outer transaction succeeds with state changed and the inner work
skipped, then fix it and prove the fix.

**Account abstraction** in the app track inherits every ingredient of gas griefing at once — a bundler
choosing gas limits on behalf of a user, a validation phase that must complete, and an execution phase
that may not. The gas-accounting rules in that design exist because of exactly this
mechanism.<Cite src="eip-150" />

**Gas estimation and CI gas gates** in the toolchain track need the monotonicity caveat above. A gas
snapshot taken in a test harness with different warmth and different depth is not the number your
users pay, and the 63/64 rule is one of the reasons the two diverge.<Cite src="eip-2929" />

Finally, the failure taxonomy this lesson keeps gesturing at — halted, reverted, returned false —
stops being three overlapping words in the next lesson and becomes three distinct machine states with
three distinct fingerprints.<Cite src="solidity-error-handling" />
