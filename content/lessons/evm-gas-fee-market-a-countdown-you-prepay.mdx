Two support tickets arrive on the same morning.

The first: *"The swap failed and it still took money."* The transaction is on-chain. Its receipt says
status zero. The nonce was consumed, the state changes were undone, and a fee was charged — partly
burned, partly paid to whoever proposed the block. Nothing about the failure reversed the payment,
because the payment was not for the swap. It was for the work the network did discovering that the
swap would not complete.

The second is stranger. *"Our relayer stopped sending. It reports insufficient funds and the wallet
has plenty."* The team had been seeing occasional out-of-gas failures, so they raised the relayer's
gas limit from 300,000 to 5,000,000. Nothing else changed. The relayer's transactions still consume
about 180,000 gas each. Its cost per transaction has not moved by one wei. And it can now barely send
anything at all.

Both tickets are produced by the same short mechanism, and neither has an answer inside the sentence
"gas is the fee you pay for computation." That sentence is where most gas explanations start and it
is wrong in a way that generates support tickets. This lesson replaces it.

## Two properties, one counter

Strip away the economics and gas is a decrementing integer attached to a transaction. Before each
operation takes effect, the operation's cost is subtracted. If the remaining balance is not enough,
the operation does not run and the frame halts.<Cite src="execution-specs" /> That is the whole
machine. Two properties fall out of it and nothing else does.

**Halting is guaranteed.** A shared computer that anyone can submit programs to has no way to decide
in advance whether a submitted program terminates, and no way to charge for a program that never
does. Gas sidesteps the question entirely: every program terminates, because the counter reaches
zero. An infinite loop on Ethereum is not a hang. It is a transaction that costs its gas limit and
reverts.

**Resource use is priced.** The schedule is not an arbitrary tariff. Each cost is an attempt to track
what the operation actually costs the network to execute and to carry — CPU, memory, the bytes that
must propagate to every node, and above all permanent state that every node must keep forever. When
those estimates drift out of line with reality, the schedule is repriced, and it has been repriced
repeatedly.<Cite src="eip-1884" /><Cite src="eip-2929" /> Two of those repricings landed after most
of the gas material on the internet was written; the last lesson in this module is about them.

Notice what gas is *not* in either property. It is a unit of work, like a CPU-second. What a unit
costs in ether is decided somewhere else entirely — by the fee market you met in Track 01, where the
base fee moves with demand and is [burned rather than paid to anyone](/c/base-fee-burn), and a
priority fee is what you add on top.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> The two halves are independent: a
transaction's gas cost is a fact about the code it runs, and its ether cost is that fact multiplied
by a market price you did not set.

  why="Every wallet collapses the two into one number in ether before showing it to you, because that is the number you are being asked to approve. The decomposition only becomes visible when one half moves and the other does not."

## The prepay is a solvency claim, not a price

Now the second ticket, which is the more interesting one.

Before a transaction executes, the protocol debits the sender's balance for the *entire* gas limit at
the price the sender is willing to pay, and asserts that the balance covered it. The EIP-1559
reference implementation states the check directly: after subtracting the attached value, the signer's
balance must be at least `gas_limit * max_fee_per_gas`.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> Execution then runs,
`gas_used` comes out the other side, and the difference — `gas_limit - gas_used`, priced at the
effective gas price — is credited straight back.<Cite src="eip-1559" />

So the limit is not a price. It is the maximum exposure you are authorising, and the protocol makes
you prove you could cover it before it will begin. This is also why the sender's own account is
already loaded and warm when execution starts: it had to be read to perform exactly this
check.<Cite src="eip-3651" />

Run the relayer's arithmetic. Suppose it signs with `maxFeePerGas` at 50 gwei.

| | gas limit | reserved per transaction |
|---|---|---|
| before | 300,000 | 0.015 ETH |
| after | 5,000,000 | 0.25 ETH |

Its actual spend per transaction did not change: about 180,000 gas either way, and the unused
4,820,000 is returned in full. But a relayer keeps several transactions in flight, and each one in
the pool holds a reservation against the same balance. At 0.015 ETH of reservation apiece a 1 ETH
float supports sixty-six in flight. At 0.25 ETH it supports four.

The team did not make their transactions more expensive. They made their working capital sixteen times
less efficient, and the node rejected the fifth pending transaction with the only error it has for
that condition.

<Misconception
  belief="Setting a high gas limit costs more."
  reality="You are charged for gas used; the unused remainder is credited back at the same price. The limit prices nothing — it reserves balance and caps how much a runaway or late-reverting transaction can take from you."
  why="Every other limit you set on a purchase is a commitment to spend up to it. This one is a solvency assertion, and the only place its size shows up is in how much of your balance is unavailable while the transaction is pending."
  src="eip-1559"
/>

The honest version of the advice, then, is not "limits are free." It is: a high limit costs you
liquidity and worst-case exposure, not price. Set it with headroom for state that may have changed
between estimation and inclusion, and no more.

## Intrinsic gas: the bill before the first opcode

Some gas is charged before the interpreter starts. This is **intrinsic gas**, and it exists because a
transaction imposes costs on the network merely by existing — it must be gossiped, stored in a block,
signature-checked, and kept in history — whether or not it executes anything.

The formula is inherited down the transaction-type chain, and EIP-1559 restates it: 21,000 as the
base, plus 16 gas per non-zero calldata byte and 4 per zero byte, plus 1,900 per access-list storage
key and 2,400 per access-list address.<Cite src="eip-1559" /><Cite src="eip-2028" /><Cite src="eip-2930" />
Contract creation adds 32,000 plus 2 gas per 32-byte word of initcode.<Cite src="eip-7623" /><Cite src="eip-3860" />
Newer transaction types add their own terms: an EIP-7702 set-code transaction is charged 25,000 per
authorization tuple up front, with 12,500 of that returned through the refund counter when the
authority account already exists.<Cite src="eip-7702" />

That is five independent terms, and the reason a plain ETH transfer costs 21,000 gas exactly is that
every other term is zero: no calldata, no list, no creation, no opcodes.<Cite src="eip-1559" />

<Aside kind="warn">
The 4-and-16 calldata rates are the *standard* branch of a two-branch formula since Pectra. A
transaction whose gas is dominated by its data pays a different, higher rate.<Cite src="eip-7623" />
Everything in this section is correct for ordinary application transactions and incomplete for
data-heavy ones; the third lesson in this module is entirely about that branch.
</Aside>

**Subgoal — apply the rates.** `41 × 4 + 27 × 16 = 164 + 432 = 596` gas of calldata, on top of the
21,000 base: an intrinsic cost of 21,596 before a single opcode runs.<Cite src="eip-1559" />

**Subgoal — change one argument and re-price.** Send the maximum `uint256` instead. The amount word
becomes 32 non-zero bytes: `12 × 4 + 56 × 16 = 48 + 896 = 944`, an intrinsic cost of 21,944. Same
function, same 68 bytes, 348 gas more.<Cite src="eip-2028" />

That gap is why "gas golfing" spends effort producing zero bytes — mined function selectors with
leading zeros, addresses with zero prefixes, amounts rounded to fewer significant bytes. It is also
why the number is not stable across your own test data: a fuzz run with random `uint256` amounts
prices calldata differently from a suite that always transfers 1 ether.

<Misconception
  belief="Calldata is charged when your contract reads it."
  reality="The per-byte charge is levied once, at intrinsic-gas time, before execution begins. Inside execution, reading calldata is an ordinary cheap opcode with no per-byte state cost."
  why="The two charges are almost always taught together as 'the cost of calldata', which hides the fact that one is paid by the sender for bandwidth and history and the other is paid for by an interpreter step."
  src="eip-2028"
/>

There is one more consequence of intrinsic gas being charged first. A transaction whose gas limit is
below its own intrinsic cost is **invalid** — not failed, invalid. It cannot be included in a block
at all, so it costs nothing and produces no receipt.<Cite src="eip-7623" /> That is a completely
different outcome from running out of gas, and confusing the two is the source of a lot of wasted
debugging.

## Three ways to hit the ceiling, and only two of them cost you

| outcome | included? | what you pay |
|---|---|---|
| limit below intrinsic gas | no — invalid | nothing |
| explicit `REVERT` | yes | gas consumed up to that point |
| out of gas / invalid opcode | yes | all gas allotted to the frame |

The second and third rows are the distinction most people miss. `REVERT` was introduced precisely so
a contract could abort, undo its state changes, return data explaining why, **and hand the remaining
gas back**.<Cite src="eip-140" /> A `require` that fails deep in a call is cheap in exactly this
sense: you pay for what ran.

An exceptional halt is not like that. When the counter cannot cover the next operation, the frame's
entire remaining allotment is consumed, and the frame cannot observe or recover from
it.<Cite src="execution-specs" /><Cite src="yellow-paper" /> There is no `catch` for out of gas
inside the frame that ran out.

<Misconception
  belief="A failed transaction is free."
  reality="A transaction that reverts is included in a block, consumes its nonce, and is charged for everything executed before the failure. Only genuinely unused gas comes back."
  why="Failed card payments and failed API calls are reversed in full, so 'failed' and 'no charge' are the same event everywhere else in a product engineer's experience. On-chain, inclusion and success are separate facts, and you are paying for inclusion."
  src="eip-1559"
/>

The product consequence is that your error taxonomy needs three buckets, not two. *Rejected before
inclusion* is a client-side bug you can fix and retry for free. *Reverted* is a business-logic outcome
you should surface with the revert reason. *Out of gas* is a budgeting failure and the most expensive
of the three, because it burns the whole allotment for nothing.

## The same shape, one frame down

Everything above describes the transaction's allowance. Inside execution the identical shape recurs
per call, with one twist that catches people.

When a frame makes a call it names a gas allowance for the callee. Since EIP-150 the protocol will
not hand over everything: the callee receives at most all-but-one-sixty-fourth of what the caller
holds, defined as `N - floor(N / 64)`.<Cite src="eip-150" /> The caller therefore always keeps a
slice, which is what lets it observe the callee's outcome and react rather than dying with it. Note
the ordering: the cold-or-warm charge for touching the callee's account is applied *before* the
63/64 share is computed, so a cold target both costs more and shrinks what is left to
forward.<Cite src="eip-2929" />

The twist: if a frame asks for more gas than the rule allows, the call is **not** rejected. It
proceeds with the smaller amount.<Cite src="eip-150" /><Cite src="rareskills-eip150" /> The gas
argument to a call is a ceiling, not a demand. Nothing tells the callee it was shortchanged and
nothing tells the caller its request was trimmed.

That is the mechanism behind **gas griefing**: a caller supplies just enough gas for its own logic to
finish and too little for a subcall, the subcall halts exceptionally, the outer frame sees a boolean
`false`, and if the outer code ignores that boolean the transaction succeeds having skipped the work
it was supposed to do.<Cite src="evm-opcodes-gas" /> Relayers, callback hooks and any contract that
pays for someone else's execution are the standard victims. The full treatment is in
[call gas is a maximum](/c/call-gas-is-a-maximum) and [gas griefing](/c/gas-griefing); what matters
here is that it is the same idea as the transaction limit — an authorised ceiling, not a promise —
applied one level down.

It also explains a failure that looks impossible. An exactly-estimated gas limit can still run out,
because estimation measures the total while the 63/64 rule governs how much of that total can reach
the deepest frame.<Cite src="arkis-estimategas" /> Headroom on the limit is not superstition.

<Answer>

a client-side error to fix, not an on-chain event to investigate.

**2 — included, fails, costs the full 90,000.** Intrinsic gas is affordable, so execution begins, and

the three outcomes and produces the least diagnostic information.

sender's balance was reserved against `900,000 × maxFeePerGas`, and any other transaction it wanted
to send had to fit in what was left.

The ranking people expect is 1 cheapest, 3 most expensive. The real ranking is 1 free, 3 correct, and
2 — the one that looks like a sensible tight budget — the only one that burned money for nothing.
</Answer>

## What this unlocks

**Cold and warm access**, next lesson, takes the "resource use is priced" half of the counter and
shows that the price is not a constant. The same `SLOAD` costs 2,100 or 100 gas depending on what ran
before it in the same transaction, which means the gas figure in your test report is a function of
your test's history.<Cite src="eip-2929" />

**The two new ceilings**, the lesson after, revisits the intrinsic-gas formula above and adds the two
terms that arrived in 2025 and 2026: a floor price on calldata for data-dominated transactions, and a
hard per-transaction cap of 2^24 gas that exists independently of the block limit.<Cite src="eip-7623" /><Cite src="eip-7825" />

Further out, **gas profiling** is where the limit-versus-used distinction becomes a CI gate, and
**transaction UX** is where it becomes an error taxonomy — the three-bucket split above is the
difference between an interface that says "transaction failed" and one that says which of three
different things went wrong and what the user should do next.
