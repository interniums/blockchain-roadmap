Two pieces of correct code stopped being correct without anyone editing them.

The first is a cost estimator inside a data-posting service. It multiplies non-zero calldata bytes by
16 and zero bytes by 4, adds 21,000, and quoted its customers accurately for years. Since Pectra it has
been under-quoting the exact transactions it exists to price, by a factor approaching two and a half.

The second is a deployment script with a line that reads, in effect, "set the gas limit to the block
gas limit and let the refund sort it out." Since Fusaka the node it submits to rejects the transaction
outright. Not out of gas — rejected, before the mempool, with an error most tooling surfaces as
something unhelpful.

Nothing about either program changed. Two ceilings appeared underneath them.

## Ceiling one: calldata now has a floor price

Calldata pricing was stable for a long time. EIP-2028 set it in 2019, cutting the non-zero byte from
68 gas to 16 and leaving the zero byte unchanged at 4, on the argument that cheaper data meant more
layer-two throughput.<Cite src="eip-2028" /><Cite src="eip-1559" /> That worked. It worked so well that by 2024 rollups posting data
had become the dominant consumer of block space, and the pricing that invited them in had a problem
nobody had needed to care about before.

The problem is not the average. EIP-7623 states it plainly: under the old pricing a single execution
payload could reach about 7.15 MB, while the average was around 100 KB.<Cite src="eip-7623" /> That
gap of roughly seventy times is what every node's hardware, bandwidth and validation budget had to be
sized against. The worst case sets the safety margin for everyone, and almost nobody was near it.

EIP-7623 shipped in Pectra, which activated on 7 May 2025.<Cite src="eip-7600" /><Cite src="ethereum-org-history" />
It does not raise the price of calldata. It adds a second branch and takes the larger of the two.

### The formula, and the one thing to notice about it

Define `tokens_in_calldata` as the zero-byte count plus four times the non-zero-byte count. Then:

```text
tx.gasUsed = 21000 + max(
    4  * tokens_in_calldata + execution_gas_used + creation_terms,   # standard branch
    10 * tokens_in_calldata                                          # floor branch
)
```

with `STANDARD_TOKEN_COST` 4 and `TOTAL_COST_FLOOR_PER_TOKEN` 10.<Cite src="eip-7623" /> Because a
non-zero byte is four tokens, the standard branch is the familiar 4-and-16 per byte and the floor
branch is **10 gas per zero byte and 40 per non-zero byte**.<Cite src="eip-7623" />

Now the thing to notice, which most summaries of this EIP omit: **the floor branch does not contain
`execution_gas_used`.** It is a max, not a sum. A transaction in the floor regime pays for its data
and nothing at all for its computation, right up until the computation grows large enough to make the
standard branch win.

That gives a break-even you can carry in your head. The floor binds whenever
`10t > 4t + e`, which is `e < 6t`. For calldata that is entirely non-zero, `t = 4 × bytes`, so:

> The floor applies unless the transaction spends more than **24 gas of execution per non-zero
> calldata byte** — or 6 gas per zero byte.

<Aside kind="note">
`execution_gas_used` is defined as execution gas *with the refund subtracted*.<Cite src="eip-7623" />
A large storage-clearing refund therefore lowers the standard branch and can tip a transaction into
the floor regime, where the refund then buys nothing because execution gas is not in the branch being
paid. The [refund cap](/c/refund-cap) from the previous lesson and this floor interact, and the
interaction runs against intuition in both directions.
</Aside>

### Worked example: pricing a batch post

**Subgoal — convert bytes to tokens.** Take 100 KiB of compressed batch data, 102,400 bytes, of which
5% are zero: 5,120 zero and 97,280 non-zero.

```text
tokens = 5,120 + (97,280 × 4) = 5,120 + 389,120 = 394,240
```

**Subgoal — evaluate both branches.** The standard branch charges `4 × 394,240 = 1,576,960` plus
whatever the transaction executes. The floor branch charges `10 × 394,240 = 3,942,400` and stops
there.<Cite src="eip-7623" />

**Subgoal — decide which one binds.** The floor wins unless execution exceeds
`6 × 394,240 = 2,365,440` gas. A batch-posting transaction typically executes a signature check and a
handful of storage writes — nowhere near that. So this transaction pays the floor: 21,000 + 3,942,400,
against the 21,000 + 1,576,960 + execution that the old estimator predicts.

**Subgoal — state the error in the estimator's own terms.** The estimator quoted 1,576,960 gas of
calldata and the transaction is charged 3,942,400. Its quote is 40% of the real cost. That is the
first program at the top of this lesson.

The floor also raises the *validity* threshold, which is a separate failure. A transaction's gas limit
must now clear `21000 + TOTAL_COST_FLOOR_PER_TOKEN × tokens_in_calldata` as well as its ordinary
intrinsic cost — whichever is greater.<Cite src="eip-7623" /> A limit computed from the old model can
therefore be rejected as invalid rather than merely running out.

### The proposal that is not coming yet

EIP-7976 would replace the 10-and-40 floor with a flat 64 gas per byte, zero and non-zero alike, and
its authors estimate that this cuts the worst-case block size by about 37%; the same document notes
that a 10 MiB payload would cost roughly 671M gas under 64/64 against roughly 105M under
10/40.<Cite src="eip-7976" /> It is in peer review, and it is not assigned to any named
fork.<Cite src="eip-7976" />

<Misconception
  belief="Calldata is about to get much more expensive again — 64 gas per byte is coming."
  reality="EIP-7976 proposes exactly that and is in Review status with no fork assignment. Its numbers are proposal numbers, not scheduled ones."
  why="Draft EIPs circulate in roadmap posts and conference talks with the same confidence as shipped ones, and the distinction between 'Final and in a hardfork meta' and 'Review' is not visible in a screenshot of a specification."
  src="eip-7976"
/>

Read the direction rather than the number. Calldata is being pushed out of the data-availability
business on purpose: EIP-7623's own motivation names blobs as the preferred path for data
availability and calls the transition a reason to reprice calldata.<Cite src="eip-7623" /> If your
product posts bulk data, the durable answer is not to model the floor precisely. It is to be in
[the blob lane](/c/blob), which has [its own fee market](/c/separate-fee-markets) and does not compete
with execution gas.

## Ceiling two: a transaction cannot be as large as a block

For a decade there was exactly one ceiling on a transaction's size: it had to fit in a block. That
stopped being true on 3 December 2025.<Cite src="ethereum-org-history" />

EIP-7825, shipped in Fusaka, caps any single transaction at **16,777,216 gas — 2^24**, and enforces it
in two places: a transaction declaring a higher `gasLimit` is invalid and never enters the
transaction pool, and a block containing such a transaction is itself invalid and
rejected.<Cite src="eip-7825" /><Cite src="eip-7607" />

The word that matters is `gasLimit`. The cap is on the limit you *declare*, not on the gas you
consume. A transaction that would have used 200,000 gas is rejected outright if it asks for a limit
of 20,000,000 — and asking for far more than you need is exactly what the "set it to the block limit"
habit does. That is the second program at the top of this lesson, and the failure it produces is a
node-side rejection rather than an on-chain receipt, so it does not appear in any block explorer for
the team to look at.

Meanwhile the block itself got larger, not smaller. EIP-7935 recommends that execution clients set the
default block gas limit to 60M, up from the 36M it describes as the mainnet value at the time of
writing.<Cite src="eip-7935" /> It is worth being precise about what that document is: it is
Informational, and it changes client *default configurations* rather than a protocol constant. The
block gas limit remains something validators adjust; the EIP coordinates where they start from and
ties the change to a fork so that the testing happens.<Cite src="eip-7935" />

Put the two numbers together and the shape of a block changed:

```text
60,000,000 / 16,777,216  =  3.58
```

At most three maximum-sized transactions fit in a block, and a block is no longer something a single
transaction can fill. That was the point. EIP-7825's motivation lists the risks of one transaction
consuming most of a block: uneven load, longer validation times, and larger single-transaction state
changes feeding state growth.<Cite src="eip-7825" />

<Misconception
  belief="If it fits in a block, it can be one transaction."
  reality="Since Fusaka a transaction is capped at 2^24 gas regardless of the block limit, which is currently defaulted several times higher."
  why="For ten years the block gas limit genuinely was the only ceiling, so 'will it fit in a block' was the complete question. The two limits also moved in opposite directions in the same upgrade, which makes the intuition 'blocks got bigger, so transactions can be bigger' exactly backwards."

### Who this actually bites

Not ordinary application transactions, which are two orders of magnitude below the cap. It bites the
things that grew by looping:

- **Monolithic deployments.** Deploying a large contract pays intrinsic creation gas, a per-word
  charge on initcode, and a per-byte deposit for the code that ends up in state.<Cite src="eip-7623" /><Cite src="eip-3860" />
  A deployment that was already uncomfortably large now has a hard rejection point rather than a
  merely expensive one. The [code size and deposit limits](/c/code-deposit-and-size-limits) were
  always the first constraint; the cap is a second one, further out and less forgiving.
- **Batch operations that scale with a list.** Airdrop distributors, multi-send contracts, migration
  scripts and anything with `for (uint i; i < recipients.length; ++i)` inside a single transaction.
  These fail by growing, so they pass every test written against a short list.
- **Anything that allocates large memory.** Memory is priced with a
  [quadratic term](/c/memory-expansion-quadratic), so the per-transaction cap now puts a hard bound on
  how much memory one transaction can ever afford to touch. The exact ceiling is arithmetic on the
  expansion formula and is worth deriving yourself rather than reading — it is a number that moves if
  the memory pricing is ever changed, and there is a live proposal to change it.

The operational response is the same in all three cases and it is not "raise the limit". It is to
split the work across transactions, which means your contract needs to be resumable — a cursor, a
checkpoint, an idempotent step — rather than an all-or-nothing loop.

## What the two ceilings have in common

Take a step back from both, because the pattern is more useful than either number.

Every significant gas change of the last decade has moved in the same direction: away from pricing
the average case and toward bounding the worst case.

| change | what was fine on average | what was catastrophic adversarially |
|---|---|---|
| EIP-150 | state-reading opcodes | transactions that only read state |
| EIP-1884 | `SLOAD` at 200 gas | a trie that had grown underneath the price |
| EIP-2929 | account and slot access | touching thousands of accounts in one transaction |
| EIP-3529 | storage refunds | gas tokens, and real block gas near twice the nominal limit |
| EIP-7623 | around 100 KB of calldata | a payload of roughly 7.15 MB |
| EIP-7825 | transactions far below the block limit | one transaction filling a block |

Each row is stated by the EIP that changed it: EIP-150 after the 2016 denial-of-service
attacks,<Cite src="eip-150" /> EIP-1884 raising `SLOAD` from 200 to 800 as the trie
grew,<Cite src="eip-1884" /> EIP-2929 citing malicious transactions that took 20 to 80 seconds to
execute,<Cite src="eip-2929" /> EIP-3529 citing gas tokens and a block's real gas reaching nearly
twice its nominal limit,<Cite src="eip-3529" /> EIP-7623 citing a 7.15 MB worst case against a
roughly 100 KB average,<Cite src="eip-7623" /> and EIP-7825 citing uneven load and validation time
from single-transaction gas hogs.<Cite src="eip-7825" />

Six repricings, one argument. In every row the average user was unaffected and the tail was the
problem, and in every row the fix made a previously-correct number conditional rather than replacing
it. That is why stale gas knowledge does not fail loudly: the old figure remains correct in the
ordinary case, which is the case you test.

It also tells you where the next one lands. Anything currently cheap on average and unbounded in the
worst case is a candidate, and both proposals in this lesson — the 64-gas floor and a linear
replacement for quadratic memory pricing — are exactly that shape.

### Three questions for any gas number

Since every number in this module turned out to be conditional, carry the conditions with it.

1. **Which fork was this measured under?** A number without a fork is a number with an expiry date you
   cannot read. Both ceilings here are younger than most of the material that would teach you gas.
2. **Is it a price or a bound?** A price is what you pay per unit. A bound is a ceiling that rejects
   you. The estimator at the top of this lesson got a price wrong; the deployment script hit a bound.
   They fail in completely different places — one in your invoice, one before the mempool.
3. **Does it depend on transaction history?** If the answer is yes, as it is for every state access,
   then the number is a property of a call path and not of an opcode, and a benchmark that does not
   reproduce the path is not measuring your cost.

<Answer>
The floor branch charges `10 × 200,000 = 2,000,000` in all three cases. The standard branch charges
`4 × 200,000 = 800,000` plus execution.<Cite src="eip-7623" /> The crossover is at
`6 × 200,000 = 1,200,000` gas of execution.

**1 — floor branch. Charged 21,000 + 2,000,000.** Execution of 300,000 is well below the crossover,
so the standard branch comes to 1,100,000 and loses. Note what this means at the margin: this
transaction could do another 899,999 gas of computation for free, because execution gas does not
appear in the branch it is paying.

**2 — standard branch. Charged 21,000 + 800,000 + 2,000,000 = 21,000 + 2,800,000.** Execution is above
the crossover, so the sum exceeds the floor and the floor does not bind. This transaction pays for
both its data and its work, at the old calldata rates.

**3 — floor branch, and the refund is worth nothing.** `execution_gas_used` is defined net of the gas
refund, and the refund itself is capped at a fifth of the gas the transaction
used.<Cite src="eip-7623" /><Cite src="eip-3529" /> Whatever the netted figure works out to, it is
below 300,000 and therefore far below the 1,200,000 crossover, so the floor still binds and the
transaction is charged 21,000 + 2,000,000. The refund reduced a branch that was not being paid. A team optimising this transaction by clearing more storage would
measure zero improvement and have no obvious explanation for it.

**The fourth transaction is rejected before the mempool.** 20,000,000 exceeds the per-transaction cap
of 16,777,216, and EIP-7825 invalidates on the declared `gasLimit` rather than on gas
used.<Cite src="eip-7825" /> It does not matter that this transaction would only have consumed about
2 million. There is no receipt, no revert reason and no block explorer entry to inspect — which is
why this failure is so much harder to diagnose than the other three.
</Answer>

## What this unlocks

**Calldata cost modelling** on the scaling track is this lesson applied to the question rollups
actually face: for a given payload, is calldata or a blob cheaper today, and how does the answer move
with each fee market independently.<Cite src="eip-7623" />

**Gas profiling** in the toolchain track needs both ceilings to interpret its own output. A gas
snapshot taken locally has no calldata floor in it unless the harness sends realistic calldata, and a
test that passes at 20M gas tells you nothing about a transaction that cannot be submitted at 20M
gas.

**Transaction UX** in the application track inherits the failure mode that has no receipt. Pool-level
rejection is a category your error handling has to carry, alongside revert and out-of-gas, and it is
the only one of the three where retrying with the same parameters can never work.

And the reflection this module asks for comes out of the same place. Every number in these three
lessons was either measured on a pinned toolchain or read out of a specification with a fork attached
to it, because the alternative — a number remembered from a table — was wrong for the cold storage
read, wrong for the reentrancy guard, and wrong for calldata. The habit is not scepticism about other
people's numbers. It is attaching the conditions to your own.
