The pull request is one line per modifier and the description writes itself: *same behaviour, roughly
eleven times less gas.*

The first half of that sentence is the problem. It is not the same behaviour. Transient storage
differs from persistent storage in two specified ways that have nothing to do with price, and both of
them are load-bearing for security. One of them was published as a concrete attack five months before
the fork that shipped the feature.<Cite src="chainsecurity-tstore" />

This lesson is about those two differences, and then about the economics question underneath them:
what does making a defence cheap actually buy, and what does it not?

## Difference one: the stipend rule does not carry over

`SSTORE` refuses to run when the gas remaining is at or below 2,300 — the stipend check EIP-2200
introduced. EIP-1153 specifies that `TSTORE` carries no such requirement: its behaviour differs from
`SSTORE` in that it does not require `gasleft` to be less than or equal to the gas
stipend.<Cite src="eip-1153" /><Cite src="eip-2200" />

That looks like a footnote. It invalidates a security assumption that a decade of Solidity and Vyper
code was built on.

The assumption is this: `address.transfer()` and Vyper's `send()` forward exactly 2,300 gas, and 2,300
gas was, by construction, too little to change state. A recipient's fallback could log something and
that was all. Many contracts therefore skipped a reentrancy guard around an ETH transfer, reasoning —
correctly, at the time — that the callee could not reach back in and do anything.

With transient storage, 2,300 gas is enough. `TSTORE` costs 100 and there is no minimum-gas check
standing in front of it.<Cite src="eip-1153" /><Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /> An audit firm
laid this out in November 2023, before Dencun activated, and its summary is worth carrying whole:
reentrancy with 2,300 gas is now possible when transient storage is used, so Solidity's `transfer` and
Vyper's `send` are no longer reentrancy-safe.<Cite src="chainsecurity-tstore" />

### The drain, step by step

The firm's realistic example is a wrapped-ETH contract offering a `temporaryApprove` — one of the use
cases EIP-1153 itself suggested.<Cite src="eip-1153" /> The allowance lives in transient storage. The
withdrawal function checks the allowance, sends the ETH, decrements the balance, and finally clears
the allowance.<Cite src="chainsecurity-tstore" />

**Subgoal — establish the ordering.** Note that the send happens *before* the transient allowance is
cleared. With persistent storage this ordering was safe, because a 2,300-gas fallback could not have
called back in and changed the allowance.

**Subgoal — set up.** The attacker deposits 10 ETH from address A1, then calls
`temporaryApprove(A2, 10 ETH)`.<Cite src="chainsecurity-tstore" />

**Subgoal — trigger the send.** The attacker calls the withdrawal for `(A1, A2)`. The check passes and
10 ETH is sent to A2.

**Subgoal — reenter inside the 2,300-gas window.** In A2's fallback, A2 calls A1, and A1 calls
`temporaryApprove(A2, 0)`. That is a `TSTORE`, and it executes, because there is no stipend check to
stop it.<Cite src="eip-1153" />

**Subgoal — collect.** Back in the withdrawal function, the allowance is now read as zero from
transient storage. The balance decrement subtracts zero. A1 keeps its full wrapped balance and A2 has
the ETH.<Cite src="chainsecurity-tstore" />

**Subgoal — notice what the fix is not.** Adding a reentrancy guard to the withdrawal function does
close this particular hole, and it is the right immediate fix. But the general lesson is not "add a
guard here". It is that the *reason* the ordering was safe has been removed everywhere at once, in
every contract that adopts transient storage, without any of those contracts changing.

### The part that reaches contracts you do not control

The firm makes a second point that is easy to skip and is arguably more important: the new reentrancy
can affect a contract that uses transient storage even when it contains no `transfer` call itself,
because the transfer may live in a *different* contract it interacts with — an exchange, a payout
splitter.<Cite src="chainsecurity-tstore" />

Their examples are ordinary, widely used infrastructure: a `disperseEther` function that pays multiple
recipients using `transfer`, which at the time of writing had been called over 150,000 times, and an
exchange's `orderBuy` that forwards ETH to a seller the same way, called over 1,300 times in the
preceding month.<Cite src="chainsecurity-tstore" /> Callers of those functions previously did not have
to think about reentrancy. Callers that use transient storage now do.

Existing deployed contracts are not themselves affected — nothing about their bytecode changed — but
they must be treated carefully by anything new that integrates with
them.<Cite src="chainsecurity-tstore" />

<Aside kind="stop">
The practical instruction: if your contract uses transient storage anywhere, every integration that
relies on a 2,300-gas ceiling to bound what a callee can do must be re-examined. That includes
integrations you inherited, integrations inside dependencies, and the general habit of treating
`transfer` as the safe way to send ETH — which was already obsolete for repricing reasons and is now
obsolete for a second, independent reason.
</Aside>

## Difference two: composability stops being free

The second difference is subtler, has no exploit attached, and will cost more teams more time.

Composability, in the sense the Solidity team uses it, means each component behaves as an independent
transaction that does not share state with previous components. The EVM has largely guaranteed this:
several calls into a contract inside one complex transaction are virtually indistinguishable from the
same calls spread over several transactions. Transient storage allows that principle to be
violated.<Cite src="solidity-transient-storage-blog" />

Their example is three lines and it is the whole hazard:

```solidity
contract MulService {
    function setMultiplier(uint multiplier) external {
        assembly { tstore(0, multiplier) }
    }
    function multiply(uint value) external view returns (uint) {
        uint m;
        assembly { m := tload(0) }
        return value * m;
    }
}
```

Now run `setMultiplier(42)`, `multiply(1)`, `multiply(2)`.

Sent as one batched transaction, the answers are 42 and 84. Sent as three separate transactions, the
multiplier is discarded between them and the answers are 0 and 0. With memory or storage the results
would be identical either way; grouping calls would not matter.<Cite src="solidity-transient-storage-blog" />

That is a contract whose observable behaviour depends on how a caller chose to batch. Every batching
relayer, every account-abstraction bundler, every multicall router is now a variable in your
contract's semantics.

<Aside kind="note">
The announcement notes this is not inherent to the idea. Clearing transient storage for a contract as
soon as no function of that contract remained on the call stack — possibly several times per
transaction — would remove the problem. The Solidity team advocated for exactly that scoping change
during standardisation; the concern was ultimately not adopted, so responsible use now sits with the
contract author rather than with the EVM.<Cite src="solidity-transient-storage-blog" />
</Aside>

The operational rule that follows is narrow and mechanical: **set the value, run the protected body,
clear the value, all within one call into your contract.** Never leave a transient value set when a
call into your contract returns.

And do not economise on the clearing write. Skipping it saves 100 gas and restricts your contract to a
single call per transaction, which prevents its use in the composed transactions complex applications
depend on.<Cite src="solidity-transient-storage-blog" /> A lock you did not clear is a lock against
your own users.

## Locks stop at the contract boundary

One more limit, because it is the thing people reach for next.

Transient storage is private to the contract that owns it, exactly as persistent storage
is.<Cite src="eip-1153" /> A lock set by contract A is invisible to contract B. If your protocol's
safety depends on B honouring or forwarding A's lock state, it depends on B's cooperation rather than
on the machine — and if B is untrusted, you have no argument at all.

It is worth being precise about what EIP-1153 did and did not fix here, because the motivation is easy
to over-read. The problem it solves is that *frames of the same contract* could previously communicate
only through call inputs and outputs, which an untrusted intermediate frame is under no obligation to
relay, or through costly storage.<Cite src="eip-1153" /> Transient storage gives those frames a
private channel that routes around the intermediate frame entirely.

It does nothing for coordination *between* mutually distrusting contracts. That still requires
explicit arguments, explicit return values, and a trust argument about the party in the middle.

The safe pattern is therefore the narrow one: lock, body, unlock, one contract. Anything that spans a
trust boundary needs a safety argument that does not rest on a transient value.

## What cheap defences actually change

Now the economics, and this section is an argument rather than a citation, so it is worth marking as
one. The inputs are measured; the conclusion is reasoning, and you should check the reasoning rather
than take it.

The measured inputs: a storage guard costs roughly 3,100 gas warm and somewhere up to roughly 7,100
cold; a transient guard costs about 284, flat.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /><Cite src="eip-2929" />

**On the defender's side**, that changes which decisions get made. At thousands of gas, "guard every
external function" is a proposal someone will price and reject, and the rejection produces a set of
functions judged safe by argument rather than by mechanism. At hundreds of gas, guarding everything is
below the threshold at which anyone bothers to object. The gain is not that guarded functions became
safer. It is that the *category* of unguarded-because-expensive functions can stop existing.

**On the attacker's side**, the ledger moves too, and this half is usually omitted. The general
principle is that lowering the price of a defence also lowers the price of *testing whether it is
present*: probing a protocol for an unguarded path costs gas per attempt, and the same cheapening of
inter-frame state that lets a defender guard forty functions lets an attacker construct and rehearse
more multi-step paths for the same budget. Nothing here makes the individual probe more expensive.
And, as the first half of this lesson established, the feature opened a reentrancy vector that did not
previously exist — a change in attack surface, not merely in attack cost.

**The honest conclusion** is therefore narrower than "cheaper defences improve security":

> Transient storage changed the price of both sides of the same game. Its security value is that it
> removes the excuse for leaving a function unguarded — not that it made reentrancy harder to attempt
> or harder to succeed at.

  reality="It changes relative costs on both sides. Here the concrete gain is that guarding everything becomes affordable; the concrete loss is a new reentrancy vector through 2,300-gas sends. Both are consequences of the same feature."
  why="Defence improvements are almost always reported without the attacker's side of the ledger, because the defender's side is the one with a number attached. A gas saving is measurable and a change in attack surface is not, so the measurable half becomes the whole story."

## A migration checklist

If you are converting storage guards to transient ones, these are the questions that are not in the
diff:

1. **Does anything in the contract or its dependencies rely on a 2,300-gas ceiling to bound a
   callee?** Every `transfer`, every `send`, and every integration that inherits one. That assumption
   is void.<Cite src="chainsecurity-tstore" />
2. **Is every transient value cleared before every return path out of the outermost call into the
   contract?** Including revert paths handled with `try`/`catch`, where the frame did not revert and so
   nothing was rolled back for you.
3. **Does the contract behave identically whether its functions are called in one transaction or
   several?** Run the batched and unbatched sequences and compare.<Cite src="solidity-transient-storage-blog" />
4. **Is the compiler at 0.8.34 or later?** The transient-storage clearing bug, rated high severity, was
   introduced in 0.8.28 and fixed in 0.8.34 — and its symptom is a clearing operation silently not
   happening, which is indistinguishable from item 2 being done wrong.<Cite src="solidity-known-bugs" />
5. **Does every target chain implement the Dencun opcodes?** The compiler emits `TSTORE` for whatever
   EVM version you target, not for whatever chain you deploy to.
6. **Did you re-measure, on your own toolchain, with cold and warm cases separated?** The circulating
   numbers for this comparison were all wrong for this configuration.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" />

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="transient-composability-hazard">
A vault adopts transient storage in two places. Both changes were reviewed and approved.

```solidity
// (a) a reentrancy guard, cleared on the way out
modifier nonReentrant() {
    assembly { if tload(LOCK) { revert(0, 0) } tstore(LOCK, 1) }
    _;
    assembly { tstore(LOCK, 0) }
}

// (b) a cache, so repeated price lookups in one transaction hit the oracle once
function _price() internal returns (uint256 p) {
    assembly { p := tload(PRICE_SLOT) }
    if (p == 0) {
        p = oracle.latest();
        assembly { tstore(PRICE_SLOT, p) }
    }
}

function withdraw(uint256 amt) external nonReentrant {
    uint256 owed = amt * _price();
    balances[msg.sender] -= amt;
    payable(msg.sender).transfer(owed);   // 2300-gas stipend
}
```

Find the defects. There are three, and they are of three different kinds.

<Answer>
**Defect one — the cache is never cleared, so the contract is not composable.** `PRICE_SLOT` is
written on the first call into the vault and survives every later call in the same transaction,
because transient storage is discarded only at the transaction boundary and not when a call
returns.<Cite src="eip-1153" /> A batching relayer that groups four user operations into one
transaction gives all four the price fetched by the first. Split across four transactions, each gets
its own price. The contract's answers now depend on how someone else batched, which is exactly the
violation the Solidity team's announcement describes.<Cite src="solidity-transient-storage-blog" />
Worse, the vault's exposure to a stale price is now a function of transaction composition rather than
of anything the vault controls. The rule the announcement gives is to clear transient storage
completely at the end of a call into the contract; the guard in (a) follows it and the cache in (b)
does not.<Cite src="solidity-transient-storage-blog" />

**Defect two — `transfer` is no longer a bound on what the recipient can do.** The 2,300-gas stipend
stopped a callee from writing storage because `SSTORE` has a minimum-gas check. `TSTORE` does
not.<Cite src="eip-1153" /><Cite src="eip-2200" /> A recipient's fallback can therefore write this
vault's transient state within the stipend — and the cache slot is a `TSTORE`-writable value that
changes what subsequent calls compute with.<Cite src="chainsecurity-tstore" /> The guard in (a) does
block re-entry into `withdraw` itself, which limits the immediate damage, but it does not block
re-entry into any *other* function of the vault, nor into a different contract that then calls back
in through a path the guard does not cover.

**Defect three — the ordering inside `withdraw` is stale-safe only by accident.** `owed` is computed
before the balance decrement, and the decrement happens before the transfer, which is
checks-effects-interactions and is correct. But `_price()` is now an *external call* to the oracle
made inside a guarded body, and its result is cached in a slot the recipient may be able to influence
per defect two. The safe shape is to read the price into a local, clear the transient cache before
returning, and never let a value that survives a call boundary participate in a solvency calculation.

**The three kinds, named.** The first is a composability defect — no exploit, wrong answers, discovered
by a partner integration. The second is a security defect created by a specified semantic difference
between two opcodes. The third is a design defect, in which a caching optimisation quietly widened the
trust surface of a calculation. Only the second has an attacker in it, and it is the one a
gas-focused migration review is least likely to look for.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**Reentrancy and vulnerability classes** in the security track pick this up as one instance of a
general pattern: a mitigation whose safety rested on a numeric assumption — 2,300 gas — survives right
up until the numbers move. The same shape explains why gas-based defences age badly in general.

**Checks-effects-interactions** is the control that did not depend on a gas number and therefore did
not break. It remains the primary defence; guards are a backstop, and this lesson is a demonstration
of why the ordering discipline is worth more than the cheap
lock.<Cite src="chainsecurity-tstore" />

**Audit scoping** in the security track inherits the third-party point directly. "Does this dependency
use transient storage" is now a question with security consequences for *your* contract, and it is not
visible in an interface.

**Uniswap v4 hook development** is where all of this lands in production. Hooks run inside someone
else's unlock window, with someone else's transient deltas live, and the discipline of clearing what
you set and never depending on what you did not set is the difference between a hook that composes and
one that does not.

That closes the module. Seven regions, three orders of magnitude of price between them, and one
consistent rule underneath: **choose the location by lifetime, and let the price follow.**
