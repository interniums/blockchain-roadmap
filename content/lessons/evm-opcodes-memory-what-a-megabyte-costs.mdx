Ask what a megabyte of memory costs and the answer on any machine you have ever programmed is: it is
not a question. Memory is the resource you do not price. You allocate a buffer, you use it, the
runtime reclaims it, and nobody writes a design document about a megabyte.

On the EVM, a megabyte costs **2,195,456 gas** — and since December 2025 there is a per-transaction
ceiling that puts a hard stop somewhere under three megabytes, at any price you are willing to pay.

The interesting thing is not that memory costs something. It is *how* it costs: the curve is shaped
so that small buffers feel free and large ones are impossible, with a surprisingly sharp corner
between the two. This lesson locates that corner, prices the copies that fill the buffer, and names
the two ceilings that now bound the whole question.

## The formula, and where it comes from

The total cost of having expanded a frame's memory to `a` 32-byte words is:

```text
C_mem(a) = 3 * a + floor(a**2 / 512)
```

An instruction that raises the [high-water mark](/c/memory-highwater-never-shrinks) pays the
*difference*: if memory was already `b` words long, the charge is `C_mem(a) - C_mem(b)`. And `a` is
the number of words needed to cover the range from address 0 to the last word that has been read or
written.<Cite src="eip-3336" />

That statement comes from EIP-3336, which quotes the live rule while proposing to replace it — a
useful property in a source, because a proposal has to state the status quo accurately in order to
argue against it. EIP-1153 uses the same formula in its own security analysis, which is a second
independent appearance of it in a normative document.<Cite src="eip-1153" />

The two terms do very different jobs.

`3 * a` is the linear term: three gas per 32-byte word. This is what people mean when they say memory
is cheap, and at the sizes ordinary contracts use, it is the only term you can see.

`floor(a**2 / 512)` is the quadratic term. It is negligible, then it is not.

### Where the corner is

Setting the two terms equal gives `a**2 / 512 = 3 * a`, so `a = 1536` words — exactly **48 KiB**. At
that size each term contributes 4,608 gas and they are precisely equal. Below it the linear term
dominates; above it the quadratic term does, and it keeps pulling away.

That derivation is arithmetic on the formula above rather than a figure quoted from a source, and the
same is true of every number in the table that follows. They are reproducible in three lines of
Python, and reproducing them is a better use of ten minutes than trusting this page.

The marginal view makes the shape even clearer. Adding one more 32-byte word costs 3 gas when your
buffer is small, 7 gas at 32 KiB, 11 gas at 64 KiB, 35 gas at 256 KiB, and 131 gas once you are a
megabyte in. The word does not change. The price of the word changes, because you are paying for the
span rather than the byte.

## Why the curve is shaped that way

The quadratic term is not a fee-extraction device. It is a bound on how much memory one execution can
force every node on the network to allocate, and the surrounding literature treats it as exactly that.

EIP-3336, proposing a paged model, is explicit that its replacement curve was designed to
asymptotically approach the existing charges specifically so that programs cannot allocate
substantially more memory than they can today.<Cite src="eip-3336" /> Keeping the bound was the
constraint the redesign had to satisfy.

EIP-7686, proposing to drop the quadratic term entirely in favour of a flat linear price plus a hard
limit, states the problem the current model creates for implementers: between quadratic expansion and
the 63/64 rule for sub-call gas, it is extremely hard to compute the maximum memory an execution can
require.<Cite src="eip-7686" /> Its replacement rule is that an execution with N gas may use at most N
bytes of memory — a clean invariant that matters most for implementations in constrained
environments such as ZK provers.<Cite src="eip-7686" />

<Aside kind="warn">
Neither proposal has shipped. Both are marked **Stagnant** on eips.ethereum.org — EIP-3336 was created
in March 2021 and EIP-7686 in April 2024, and neither has moved since.<Cite src="eip-3336" /><Cite src="eip-7686" />
Quadratic expansion is the live pricing as of August 2026. Treat any material that presents linear memory limits as the current
model, or as imminent, as wrong on both counts.
</Aside>

## The second ceiling, which is newer than most gas material

Until recently the only bound on how much memory one transaction could touch was the block gas limit
and your willingness to pay. That changed twice in quick succession.

The block gas limit default rose to 60 million.<Cite src="eip-7935" /> And EIP-7825 introduced a
protocol-level cap of **16,777,216 gas** — two to the twenty-fourth — on any single transaction,
enforced both at transaction-pool admission and at block validation, regardless of what the block
limit happens to be.<Cite src="eip-7825" />

Put those two facts next to the expansion formula and a new constraint appears that did not exist
before. Solving `3a + a²/512 = 16,777,216` gives roughly 91,900 words, which is a little under **2.8
MiB**. That is the absolute upper bound on how much memory a single call frame can ever expand to,
now, at any gas price, having spent its *entire* transaction budget on nothing but expansion. A real
transaction that also does work has far less.

That figure is again arithmetic on the two cited constants rather than a number from a source. It is
worth deriving yourself once, because it is the kind of bound that changes an architecture decision:
anything whose design assumes it can assemble multi-megabyte structures in EVM memory is not merely
expensive, it is impossible.

For comparison, EIP-1153's own security analysis ran the same arithmetic against the older
30-million-gas figure and got roughly 3.75 MB in a single context, or about 20 MB spread across many
contexts that each reset the expansion charge.<Cite src="eip-1153" /> The per-transaction cap now
binds tighter than the block limit ever did, and the cross-context trick is bounded by it too, since
the cap applies to the transaction as a whole.

## Copies are priced by the word

Expansion is only half of what a large buffer costs. The other half is filling it.

Every bulk operation over a byte range is priced by length: a small base cost, plus a charge per
32-byte word touched, plus whatever memory expansion the destination causes. The copy family —
`CALLDATACOPY`, `CODECOPY`, `RETURNDATACOPY` and, since Cancun, `MCOPY` — shares one calculation,
which the Yellow Paper defines for the group.<Cite src="yellow-paper" />

`MCOPY` is the member whose numbers are stated most plainly. EIP-5656 gives the reference
calculation as a base of 3 gas plus 3 gas per word copied plus memory expansion, and says explicitly
that the instruction belongs to the same copy group as `CALLDATACOPY` and `RETURNDATACOPY` and
follows the same Yellow Paper calculation.<Cite src="eip-5656" /> That rate has an independent
confirmation from the other end of the family: EIP-211 specifies `RETURNDATACOPY` as
`3 + 3 * ceil(amount / 32)` and states that this is the same as `CALLDATACOPY`.<Cite src="eip-211" />
Both EIPs flag the Yellow Paper as the final word on the arithmetic, which is a good instinct to
copy: for anything gas-critical, check the executable spec rather than an EIP's convenience
restatement.<Cite src="execution-specs" />

<Aside kind="note">
`KECCAK256` is in the same shape — a base plus a per-word charge over the input it reads from memory,
plus expansion for the region it reads. This corpus has **not** re-verified its constants against a
current client, so no figure appears here. That absence is the point: "just hash the whole struct" is
a cost that scales with the struct, and if you need the number, measure it on your own toolchain with
a probe whose result is consumed so the compiler cannot eliminate it.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" />
</Aside>

The interaction between the two charges is what people get wrong. A copy's true price is not the copy
opcode's own cost. It is:

```text
base + per-word rate * ceil(length / 32) + (C_mem(new_highwater) - C_mem(old_highwater))
```

For a small destination near the start of memory the third term is a handful of gas and the copy
looks linear in length. For a destination that pushes the high-water mark past the 48 KiB corner, the
third term becomes the whole cost, and doubling the length roughly quadruples it.

<Aside kind="note">
The execution-spec tests carry concrete `MCOPY` expansion vectors, which are the right artefact to
check an implementation or a mental model against rather than any prose description of the
rule.<Cite src="eest-mcopy" />
</Aside>

### Why `MCOPY` existed to be added

The historical detour is worth one paragraph, because it explains code you will still meet.

Before Cancun there was no memory-to-memory copy instruction. The two available techniques were
calling the identity precompile — whose cost EIP-5656 gives as 15 + 3 per word plus call overhead,
and which was made uneconomic when `CALL` was repriced — and emitting `MLOAD`/`MSTORE` pairs, at at
least 12 gas per word, plus the arithmetic to advance offsets in a loop.<Cite src="eip-5656" /> The
EIP's worked example: copying 256 bytes cost at least 96 gas with unrolled load-store pairs, against
27 gas with `MCOPY`.<Cite src="eip-5656" />

The awkward part is partial words. Copying a length that is not a multiple of 32 means the final word
must be loaded from both source and destination, masked, combined, and stored back — which is where
hand-written copy loops acquire their bugs.<Cite src="eip-5656" /> `MCOPY` also handles overlapping
ranges correctly, behaving as if an intermediate buffer were used.<Cite src="eip-5656" />

emitted assembly for your settings rather than assuming.

## The design rule that falls out

Three facts now compose into something you can use before writing code.

The charge is against the **high-water mark**, so the peak matters and the average does not. The
charge is **superlinear past 48 KiB**, so splitting one large buffer into several small ones within
the same frame saves nothing — it is one contiguous span either way. And the charge is **per frame**,
so a sub-call genuinely resets it.

Therefore: streaming beats buffering. Processing a large input in chunks that each fit comfortably in
the linear region, rather than materialising the whole thing in memory, is not a micro-optimisation
here. It is the difference between a function that costs a few thousand gas and one that cannot run.

And the corollary for interfaces: a function that takes `bytes memory` forces the whole array into
memory and pays expansion for it. A function that takes `bytes calldata` reads it in place and pays
none. That is the subject of the fifth lesson, and it is the single largest memory-related decision
most contracts make.

<Check type="predict" concept="memory-expansion-quadratic">
A contract assembles a report in memory before hashing it. Each entry adds 256 bytes. The team
benchmarks at 100 entries (25 KiB) and measures the memory expansion at 3,650 gas, which they round
to "about 37 gas per entry" and put in a capacity plan: 1,000 entries should be about 37,000 gas of
memory, comfortably affordable.

What will 1,000 entries actually cost in expansion, and what is the largest report the contract can
ever produce?

<Answer>
**1,000 entries is 256,000 bytes, which is 8,000 words.** Applying the formula: `3 * 8000 = 24,000`
for the linear term, and `8000**2 / 512 = 125,000` for the quadratic term, giving **149,000 gas** —
not 37,000.<Cite src="eip-3336" /> The plan is out by roughly a factor of four, and it is out in the
direction that gets discovered in production.

**The error is extrapolating from inside the linear region.** At 25 KiB the buffer is 800 words, so
the quadratic term contributes `800**2 / 512 = 1,250` against a linear term of 2,400 — a third of the
total, and easy to mistake for measurement noise. Per-entry cost is not a constant. It rises with the
square of the report size, so any "cost per item" measured at one size is wrong at every other size.

**The ceiling is set by two things.** Memory expansion alone hits the per-transaction cap of
16,777,216 gas<Cite src="eip-7825" /> at roughly 91,900 words, a little under 2.8 MiB — about 11,000
entries, if the transaction did nothing else at all. In practice the report also has to be built and
hashed, so the real limit is well below that.

**What to do instead.** Hash incrementally over chunks rather than materialising the whole report, so
the high-water mark stays in the linear region regardless of entry count; or reuse one fixed-size
buffer per chunk, since [the mark never falls](/c/memory-highwater-never-shrinks) and reuse is the
only available remedy;<Cite src="solidity-layout-memory" /> or, if the entries arrive as transaction
input, keep them in calldata and never copy them to memory at all.

**And re-benchmark at the size you actually intend to run.** A single measurement in a superlinear
region is not a rate. Three measurements at three sizes would have shown the curve bending and
prevented the whole plan.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**Solidity's memory map**, next lesson, shows where the allocator puts things and therefore what your
high-water mark actually looks like in a compiled contract — including the reserved region below
`0x80` that no allocation may use.

**Calldata versus memory** is the practical application of everything here. The two locations have
completely different cost structures, and a parameter's declared location decides which one you pay.

**Gas profiling** in the toolchain track is where "measure the curve, do not extrapolate a rate"
becomes a workflow. Memory-heavy functions are the ones where a single-point benchmark misleads most,
because the shape of the cost is the thing being measured.

**ZK circuits and alternative VMs**, much later, are where EIP-7686's motivation lands: a machine
whose memory bound is hard to compute is a machine that is hard to prove things about, which is why
the linear-limit proposal exists at all even though it has stalled.<Cite src="eip-7686" />
