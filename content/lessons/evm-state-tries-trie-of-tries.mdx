A user sends 100 units of an ERC-20 token. From the contract's point of view almost nothing happens:
two numbers in a mapping change. From your product's point of view it is one line of Solidity and one
event.

From the node's point of view, that transaction obliges it to recompute two chains of hashes, produce
a new 32-byte value for the token contract's account, and then recompute a third chain of hashes to
carry that change up to a root every other node on the network will independently arrive at or reject
the block.

That asymmetry — one line of source, three chains of keccak — is the whole subject of this lesson. You
already know [what an MPT is](/c/mpt-four-node-types) and how its
[branch](/c/mpt-branch-node), [extension](/c/mpt-extension-node) and [leaf](/c/mpt-leaf-node) nodes are
shaped. What you have not yet seen is the specific way Ethereum arranges them, why that arrangement
is nested rather than flat, and which of your engineering intuitions it invalidates.

## Storage is not in the state trie's key space

Start with the fact that surprises people, because everything else follows from it.

There is one global state trie. Its path is always `keccak256(address)` and its value is always the
RLP-encoded account record.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mpt" /> Contract storage is not in that trie. Each
account with storage has **its own separate trie**, and the root of that trie is the `storageRoot`
field sitting inside the account's leaf in the state trie.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mpt" /><Cite src="execution-specs" />

So the state is not one map of size (accounts + slots). It is a map of accounts, in which some values
contain the root of another map.

An account with no storage carries the **empty-trie root** — the hash of the RLP encoding of the empty
string. On this project's toolchain, `cast keccak 0x80` returns
`0x56e81f171bcc55a6ff8345e692c0f86e5b48e01b996cadc001622fb5e363b421`, and every storage-free account on
mainnet carries that identical value.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /> That is not a special case
in the code; it is just what the recursive definition produces when the map is empty.

### Why nest instead of flattening

A flat design is easy to imagine: one trie, keys of the form `address ‖ slot`. It would make proofs a
single walk. Four things fall out of the nested choice instead, and three of them are useful.

**A storage proof is two proofs.** One walk from `stateRoot` to the account leaf, a decode, then a
second walk from the extracted `storageRoot` to the slot. `eth_getProof` returns exactly that pair,
and lesson 5 is about what it does and does not establish.<Cite src="eip-1186" />

**A contract has a single 32-byte summary of all its own state.** Two nodes can settle "did anything
in this contract change between block N and block M" by comparing two `storageRoot` values, with no
knowledge of what the contract does. That is a genuinely cheap capability and the flat design does not
have it.

**Per-account subtrees can be handled independently.** A client can fetch, verify, prune or heal one
account's storage without touching anyone else's, because that account's storage is a self-contained
trie under a root it can check.

**And every storage write costs two trie updates, not one.** Change one slot and the specification's
model is: rehash the path up the storage trie, obtain a new `storageRoot`, write it into the account's
leaf, rehash the path up the state trie. Two structures, two chains of keccak, for one number.

<Aside kind="note">
The two-update model is the *semantics*, not the execution strategy. No production client recomputes a
root after every SSTORE — they accumulate the block's writes and compute roots once at the end, which
is why a block's state root is defined as the root *after* all of its transactions rather than as
something a single opcode produces.<Cite src="execution-specs" /> Hold both pictures: the logical one
tells you what a proof will look like, the implementation one tells you why per-opcode gas does not
scale with trie depth.
</Aside>

## Hexary: shallow, and therefore wide

Ethereum's trie branches sixteen ways. A branch node has sixteen child slots indexed by the next
nibble, plus a seventeenth for a key that terminates exactly there.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mpt" />

You met the depth-versus-width trade in Track 01. What matters here is where the bill actually lands,
because it is not where most people expect.

Sixteen-way branching keeps the structure **shallow**: expected depth is roughly the base-16 logarithm
of the number of entries, so even very large maps are a handful of levels deep. Traversal is cheap.
Disk seeks per lookup are few.

The cost is paid by **proofs**, because a proof must ship whole nodes. To prove one leaf you send each
node along the path, and a branch node carries all sixteen of its child references whether or not you
care about them. EIP-7864 does this arithmetic against the current design and puts the expected cost of
proving a single branch in a `2^32`-entry tree at `15 * 32 * log_16(2^32) = 3840`
bytes.<Cite src="eip-7864" /> Roughly four kilobytes, for one value.

Two consequences that show up in real systems:

- **Verifying proofs on-chain is expensive**, because you pay calldata for every one of those bytes and
  keccak for every node. Bridges that verify storage proofs in a contract are paying this per message.
- **Statelessness is hard for exactly this reason.** A block's witness is a bundle of these proofs, and
  the bundle is large enough that [witness size](/c/witness-size-is-the-blocker) became the headline
  objection to the current format before the objection was reframed.

## Four tries, and only two of them are maps

Ethereum runs four Merkle Patricia Tries, and lumping them together is the source of a lot of
confusion. Two are persistent maps; two are per-block scratch structures.

| Trie | Key | Lifetime |
|---|---|---|
| State | `keccak256(address)` | Persistent; carried forward and mutated block to block |
| Storage (one per account) | `keccak256(slot)` | Persistent, nested inside the state trie |
| Transactions | RLP of the index in this block | One block, then discarded |
| Receipts | RLP of the index in this block | One block, then discarded |

Notice that the persistent two hash their keys and the throwaway two do not. That is not an
inconsistency — hashing exists to bound depth against an adversary who chooses keys, and nobody chooses
the index of a transaction in a block. Lesson 3 makes the argument properly; lesson 4 is about the
throwaway pair.

## The trie is a commitment. It is not the database.

This is the correction most likely to change how you read client documentation and debug sync problems.

It is natural to read "the state trie" as a description of what a node has on disk. It is not. The trie
defines *a function from the state to a 32-byte value*, and it defines the proof format. What clients
actually store is up to them, and they have all moved away from storing the literal structure.

Geth exposes two **state schemes**. Its own pruning documentation notes that offline pruning applies
only to the hash-based scheme, with a path-based scheme that prunes by default alongside
it.<Cite src="geth-dbpruning" /> Pruning itself is described in terms of a **state snapshot** — a flat
layer that the pruner iterates to decide which trie nodes are still reachable and which are
stale.<Cite src="geth-dbpruning" /> A flat snapshot exists precisely because answering "what is in slot
X" by walking a trie on disk is too slow to serve.

Three things follow that you will meet in practice:

**Archive sizes and sync behaviour differ wildly between clients**, because they are storing different
things. Geth's own hardware page, last edited September 2023, puts a snap-synced full node above 650 GB
growing by about 14 GB per week, and a full archive node above 12 TB.<Cite src="geth-hardware" /> Those
are one client's numbers with a date attached; another client's layout produces different ones, and
quoting any of them without the client and the date is how stale infrastructure estimates propagate.

**"Rebuild the trie" is not a recovery step you can reason about generically.** What a client does to
recover depends on which representation it keeps.

**The trie is still authoritative.** However the bytes are laid out, the root the client computes must
match, or its block is rejected. The commitment is the contract; the storage layout is an
implementation detail that can and does change between releases.

  why="Every explainer describes the trie as 'where the state lives', and the specification does define state in terms of it. The specification is defining a function, not a storage engine — but nothing in the prose flags the difference."

## Worked example: what one token transfer actually touches

Take `token.transfer(bob, 100)` and follow the state changes. The token contract holds
`mapping(address => uint256) balances` declared at slot 0.

**Subgoal 1 — turn the mapping key into a slot number.** Solidity computes the slot for
`balances[alice]` as `keccak256(abi.encode(alice, 0))`, where 0 is the declaration
slot.<Cite src="solidity-layout-storage" /> This keccak is *inside the contract's own address space*.
It has nothing to do with the trie yet.

**Subgoal 2 — turn the slot number into a trie path.** The storage trie's path for that slot is
`keccak256(slotNumber)`.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mpt" /> Second keccak, different purpose: this one is
the trie's, and lesson 3 explains why it is there.

**Subgoal 3 — pay for the read.** The first SLOAD of a slot in a transaction costs 2,100 gas and every
later one costs 100, as measured on this project's toolchain.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /> That
21-fold gap is EIP-2929's pricing of cold versus warm state access, and lesson 6 is about what it is
really charging for.<Cite src="eip-2929" />

**Subgoal 4 — pay for the writes.** Two slots change. Alice's balance goes from one non-zero value to
another: 2,900 gas. Bob's, if he held none before, goes from zero to non-zero: 20,000 gas. Each carries
an additional 2,100 when the slot is cold.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /> The asymmetry is not
arbitrary — one of those writes adds an entry to a structure every node must keep forever, and the
other does not.

**Subgoal 5 — propagate the roots.** Both slots live in the same account's storage trie, so both
rehash paths that converge. The token contract's `storageRoot` changes once. That changes the token
contract's account leaf, which changes the path up the state trie to `stateRoot`.

**Subgoal 6 — notice what did not change.** Alice's and Bob's own accounts are untouched by the token
transfer. They hold no token balance; the token contract does. The only account whose storage moved is
the token's. This is the point at which "my tokens are in my wallet" stops being a useful description
of anything.

<Check type="predict" concept="per-account-storage-trie">
A single transaction does three things: sends 1 ETH from account A to account B, and calls contract C,
which writes to two of its own storage slots and reads one slot of contract D without writing it.

How many storage tries change? How many account leaves change? And what would happen to `stateRoot` if
the two writes to C happened in the opposite order?

<Answer>
**One storage trie changes** — contract C's. D was only read, and a read changes nothing. A and B have
no storage involved.

**Four account leaves change.** A's balance drops and A's nonce increments (it sent the transaction).
B's balance rises. C's `storageRoot` field changes because its storage trie moved. And the fee
recipient's balance changes, which is the one people forget — every transaction pays a priority fee to
whoever proposed the block, so their account is written on essentially every block.

D's leaf does not change. Reading a slot is free of state effects, however much gas it costs.

**The order of the two writes to C makes no difference to `stateRoot`.** This is the important half of
the question. A trie is a commitment to a *set* of key-value pairs, not to a sequence of operations:
the same final map yields the same root by construction, no matter what route the writes took to get
there. That property is what makes the root a usable consensus check at all — two clients that execute
the same block with different internal ordering, batching or caching must still land on the same 32
bytes.

The place where order *does* matter is gas, not the root. If both writes touch the same slot, the
second one is warm and the transition it is priced on may differ.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" />
Same final state, same root, different cost — which is a good illustration of the fact that gas prices
the work of getting there and the root prices only the destination.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**Lesson 3** answers the question this lesson deliberately left open: why is there a keccak between the
slot number and the trie path at all, and what does the design give up by putting it there.

**Lesson 5** cashes in the nesting. The two-walk proof is a direct consequence of `storageRoot` being a
field rather than a key prefix, and it is what lets a device with a header verify one slot of one
contract.

Further out: the per-account storage trie is why [clearing storage is refundable](/c/refund-cap) —
you are shrinking something every node keeps<Cite src="eip-3529" /> — and why
[storage is priced two to three orders of magnitude above memory](/c/storage-is-the-expensive-one).
And the four-kilobyte proof is the number that motivates the replacement designs in
[the binary state tree](/c/binary-state-tree) work, which lesson 6 puts in its proper, unscheduled
place.
