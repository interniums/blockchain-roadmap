Two loops, same shape. One sums an array in memory; one sums the same values out of storage. The
storage version costs orders of magnitude more gas, and the explanation everyone reaches for is
"persistence is expensive".

That explanation is not wrong so much as unhelpful. It suggests the price is a fee for durability, as
though the protocol were charging rent. What the price is actually denominated in is **time to process
a block**, and what makes storage access slow is a specific, structural consequence of everything in
the previous five lessons: hashed keys, a nested trie, and a map that only ever gets bigger.

This lesson is that argument, and what a product engineer does about it.

## Gas is a time estimate, and state access broke the estimate

EIP-2929 states the model outright: the main function of an opcode's gas cost is to estimate the time
needed to process that opcode, so that a gas limit corresponds to a limit on the time to process a
block. It then says that storage-accessing opcodes — SLOAD, the CALL family, BALANCE and the EXT
family — had historically been underpriced.<Cite src="eip-2929" />

The evidence it gives is not theoretical. In the 2016 Shanghai denial-of-service attacks, once the
worst client bugs were patched, one of the more durably successful strategies was simply to send
transactions that access or call a large number of accounts. The EIP quotes measurements showing that
EXTCODESIZE forces the client to search for the contract on disk, and that such malicious transactions
took around 20 to 80 seconds to execute where an average transaction took a few
milliseconds.<Cite src="eip-2929" />

Four orders of magnitude, from an opcode that reads one integer.

The repricing that followed is the cold/warm split you meet in every gas table. Measured on this
project's toolchain: an SLOAD costs 2,100 the first time a slot is touched in a transaction and 100
thereafter; BALANCE and EXTCODESIZE cost 2,600 cold and 100
warm.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /><Cite src="eip-2929" />

**Read the ratio, not the numbers.** A twenty-one-fold difference between the first and second read of
the same slot is not a pricing preference. It is the protocol telling you that the expensive part
already happened, on a disk, and everything after it is a cache hit.

## Why the access is random

Now connect it to lesson 3.

Trie paths are `keccak256` of the key. That was the right decision — it denies an attacker control over
trie shape — and its unavoidable side effect is that **logically adjacent keys are physically
unrelated**. Two slots your contract writes in the same line of code land in unrelated parts of the
structure. An array's elements, which a database would store contiguously, do not benefit from any
locality at all.

So state access is a random-access pattern over a large on-disk structure. There is no prefetch that
helps, no scan that amortises, no ordering to exploit. That is the physical fact the cold price is
denominated in, and it is why the same fact drives the fix everyone is reaching for: if you knew
*ahead of time* which slots a block would touch, you could issue those reads in parallel instead of
discovering them one at a time as execution proceeds.

## And the structure only grows

The second half of the problem is that nothing shrinks the state on its own.

Entries leave only when something writes a default value — clearing a slot, or emptying an account. The
protocol pays for that with a refund, and EIP-3529 deliberately reduced the payment: the clearing
refund became `SSTORE_RESET_GAS + ACCESS_LIST_STORAGE_KEY_COST`, 4,800 gas, and the total refundable
after a transaction was capped at `gas_used // 5` rather than half.<Cite src="eip-3529" /> The EIP's
own reasoning is that a large refund lets a block be inflated far past its nominal gas limit, which is
its own denial-of-service surface.<Cite src="eip-3529" /> The incentive to shrink state exists; it is
deliberately not strong enough to be gamed.

The result is monotone growth, and the operational numbers are public. Geth's hardware page, last
edited September 2023, puts a snap-synced full node above 650 GB, growing by roughly 14 GB per week,
with a full archive node above 12 TB.<Cite src="geth-hardware" /> Attach the date and the client to
those figures whenever you repeat them: they are one implementation's measurements at one moment, and
different clients store genuinely different things.

<Aside kind="warn">
Which is the deeper point. The trie is a commitment, not a storage engine — Geth's own pruning
documentation describes a **state snapshot** layer that the pruner iterates to decide which trie nodes
are still reachable, and distinguishes a hash-based state scheme from a path-based
one.<Cite src="geth-dbpruning" /> Clients moved away from storing the literal trie years ago precisely
because random-access lookups over it are too slow to serve. Any disk-size or sync-time figure you
quote is therefore a statement about one client's layout, never about "Ethereum's state".
</Aside>

## Three responses, and only one of them is a redesign

The pressure has produced work in three distinct places. Keeping them separate is what stops roadmap
reading from turning into vibes.

**Storage layout, already shipped.** Flat tables and snapshot layers with the trie derived rather than
stored. Invisible over JSON-RPC, and the reason archive sizes and sync strategies differ so much
between implementations.<Cite src="geth-dbpruning" />

**Pricing and pre-declaration, partly shipped.** EIP-2929's cold/warm split makes the first touch pay
for the seek.<Cite src="eip-2929" /> EIP-2930 lets a transaction pre-declare addresses and slots to warm
them up front.<Cite src="eip-2930" /> EIP-7928 would go further and record, at the *block* level, every
account and storage location accessed during execution along with post-execution values — explicitly in
order to enable parallel disk reads, parallel validation and parallel state-root computation. Its own
motivation notes that execution cannot be parallelised without knowing the access set in advance, and
that EIP-2930's transaction-level lists are optional and unenforced.<Cite src="eip-7928" /> It is in
peer review, created in 2025.<Cite src="eip-7928" />

**A replacement commitment, proposed.** EIP-7864 specifies a unified binary tree that merges accounts,
storage and code into one structure, drops RLP, and would freeze the existing trie while new writes
accumulate in the new one.<Cite src="eip-7864" /> If you read
[the binary state tree](/c/binary-state-tree) lesson in Track 01 you have the design; what belongs here
is its status. It is a Draft. Its hash function is explicitly undecided.<Cite src="eip-7864" /> This
project found it attached to no named fork, and the direction of travel is evidenced by a draft
specification arguing against the alternative rather than by any dated cancellation
announcement.<Cite src="stateless-binary-tree" />

## What to do with this today

The structure will not change under you this year. Five things follow from it that are actionable now.

**Budget in slots, not instructions.** Arithmetic is nearly free; a cold slot is not. A design review
that counts storage reads and writes will find more savings than one that counts operations.

**Warm what you know you will touch.** An access list moves the cold charge to the front of the
transaction where you can pay it once, deliberately, rather than discovering it inside a
loop.<Cite src="eip-2930" />

**Never plan to enumerate.** There is no way to list an account's non-zero slots over the standard
API, and there will not be one, because the structure has no order to enumerate along. The index is
yours to build.

**Treat unbounded per-user state as a cost you are imposing on others.** A contract that adds a
permanent entry per user is adding a row every node on the network carries forever, subsidised by a gas
schedule that prices the write once. That is not a reason never to do it; it is a reason to know you
are doing it, and to give the entry a way to be cleared.

**Be careful about verifying historical state on-chain.** EIP-7864 names this among its breaking
changes: a change of tree structure means in-EVM proofs of historical state stop
working.<Cite src="eip-7864" /> If you are building a contract that verifies storage proofs, that is a
dependency on a format, and the format has a live proposal to replace it.

<Check type="explain" concept="state-growth-problem">
A colleague looks at the measured numbers — SLOAD 2,100 cold, 100 warm — and proposes an optimisation:
"Our contract reads the same three config slots on every call. Let's pack them into one slot. Then it's
one cold read instead of three, so we save 4,200 gas per call."

Is the reasoning sound? What is it right about, what is it wrong about, and what does the answer tell
you about what gas is actually pricing?

<Answer>
**The conclusion is roughly right and the reasoning is wrong in a way that will mislead the next
optimisation.**

*Right about:* three separate slots mean three separate paths through the storage trie, hence three
separate cold accesses at 2,100 each. Packing three small values into one 32-byte slot means one path,
one cold access.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /> The saving is real, and it is real because of
locality that the packing created *inside a single slot* — the only locality the trie offers, since
[hashed paths destroy every other kind](/c/secure-trie-hashed-keys).

*Wrong about:* the arithmetic assumes each read was cold. Cold is a per-transaction property, not a
per-call one.<Cite src="eip-2929" /> If the contract is called twice in the same transaction, the second
call's reads are warm at 100 each and the packing saves almost nothing on that call. If a caller
supplies an access list, the slots may be warm before execution even starts.<Cite src="eip-2930" /> The
honest claim is "up to 4,200 on a cold path", not "4,200 per call".

*Also missing:* packing has a cost on the write side. Updating one packed field is a read-modify-write
of the whole slot, so a struct that is written field-by-field can end up more expensive than the
unpacked version even while reads got cheaper. And packing helps in storage only —
[on the stack and in memory everything occupies a full word regardless](/c/packing-helps-storage-only).

**What it tells you about gas.** The cold price is not a fee for the *value* being read; the same value
costs 100 the second time. It is a fee for the work of locating it — a random seek into a large on-disk
structure — which is why EIP-2929 frames opcode pricing as an estimate of processing
time.<Cite src="eip-2929" /> Once you hold that, the whole schedule reads differently. Warm access is
cheap because the client already has the node. Clearing is refunded because the structure gets
smaller.<Cite src="eip-3529" /> Storage is expensive because every node on the network will carry the
result indefinitely. **Gas prices work and footprint, not durability.**
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

This is the last lesson in the module, so the payoff is mostly outward.

The cold/warm model reappears everywhere gas is optimised, and it is the reason
[access lists](/c/access-list) exist as a transaction feature rather than a client one. The
"compute is cheap, storage is not" hierarchy is the single most reliable heuristic for
[where a contract's gas actually goes](/c/compute-is-cheap-storage-is-not).

The growth problem is upstream of a whole cluster of topics you will meet later:
[statelessness](/c/statelessness), [witness size](/c/witness-size-is-the-blocker),
[state expiry](/c/state-expiry), and the sync-mode choices node operators argue about. All of them are
the same sentence in different words — the state is large, growing, randomly accessed, and every node
carries it.

And the habit worth taking with you is smaller than any of that. When you meet a gas number, ask what
physical work it is estimating. The number will change at the next repricing; the work it stands for
usually will not.
