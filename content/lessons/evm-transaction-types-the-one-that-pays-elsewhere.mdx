An L2 team is watching a mint. The execution base fee has gone up by a factor of ten in nine minutes,
their alerting is lit, and someone asks the obvious question: should we pause the batcher until this
passes?

The answer is no, and the reason is not "it will be over soon". The reason is that the batcher's cost
is denominated in a resource whose price the mint does not touch. Blob gas and execution gas are
priced by two separate mechanisms reading two separate header fields, and neither one is an input to
the other.<Cite src="eip-4844" /><Cite src="eip-1559" />

That is the headline of this lesson, and it is the correct instinct to build. It also has exactly one
deliberate exception, added later and now Final, which sets a floor under blob prices as a function of
the execution base fee.<Cite src="eip-7918" /> Getting the exception right is the difference between
knowing the design and repeating a slogan.

## The envelope, and the thing it does not contain

Type `0x03` is the 1559 field set plus two:

`rlp([chain_id, nonce, max_priority_fee_per_gas, max_fee_per_gas, gas_limit, to, value, data, access_list, max_fee_per_blob_gas, blob_versioned_hashes, y_parity, r, s])`<Cite src="eip-4844" />

Three constraints on that list are not visible in it:

- **`to` MUST NOT be nil.** Every other field follows 1559 semantics, but this one deviates: a blob
  transaction must always name a 20-byte address, which means **a blob transaction can never be a
  contract creation**.<Cite src="eip-4844" />
- **`blob_versioned_hashes` must be non-empty,** and every entry must begin with
  `VERSIONED_HASH_VERSION_KZG`, the byte `0x01`.<Cite src="eip-4844" />
- **The blobs are not in the payload.** `blob_versioned_hashes` is a list of hashes. The blob bytes
  themselves are somewhere else entirely.

A versioned hash is `VERSIONED_HASH_VERSION_KZG || sha256(commitment)[1:]` — the version byte followed
by all but the first byte of the SHA-256 hash of the blob's KZG
commitment.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> The EVM's access to a blob is exactly this and nothing more: the
`BLOBHASH` opcode, byte `0x49`, costs 3 gas, takes an index from the stack, and returns
`tx.blob_versioned_hashes[index]` — or a zeroed 32 bytes if the index is out of
range.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

That misconception has a corollary worth stating separately: **blobs are not retained forever.**
EIP-4844 sets `MIN_EPOCHS_FOR_BLOB_SIDECARS_REQUESTS` to 4096 epochs, a little over eighteen days, as
the window during which nodes are required to serve them.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> What you are buying
is a guarantee that the data *was* available long enough for anyone who cared to fetch it — long
enough to run a fraud proof or reconstruct a state — not a guarantee that it will still be there next
year.

## Two network representations, and why your library broke

This is the part that surprises people who have only read the payload definition.

A blob transaction has two forms on the wire. In `BlockBodies` responses — the canonical, consensus
form — it is the plain [EIP-2718](/c/eip-2718-envelope) payload above.<Cite src="eip-2718" /> In
`PooledTransactions` responses, during transaction gossip, it is *wrapped* to carry the blobs
alongside.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Under EIP-4844 that
wrapper was `rlp([tx_payload_body, blobs, commitments, proofs])`, with one KZG proof per
blob.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

PeerDAS changed it. The current wrapper is
`rlp([tx_payload_body, wrapper_version, blobs, commitments, cell_proofs])`, where `wrapper_version` is
a single byte currently set to 1, and `cell_proofs` holds `CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB` proofs **per blob**
rather than one.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> The reason is a straight cost transfer: computing cell proofs
is expensive and verifying them is cheap, so rather than have every block producer recompute them for
every blob, the specification requires the **sender** to compute them and ship them in the
wrapper.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

If you have ever seen a blob-submission library fail after a network upgrade with an error about proof
counts, this is why. Any tooling written against the EIP-4844-era one-proof-per-blob shape produces a
wrapper that current nodes reject.

One more networking rule, easy to miss and operationally significant: **nodes must not automatically
broadcast blob transactions to their peers.** They are announced by hash via
`NewPooledTransactionHashes` and must then be explicitly requested with
`GetPooledTransactions`.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> A blob transaction does not flood outward the way an
ordinary one does; it advertises and waits to be pulled.

## The second market

Now the fee mechanics, which are a deliberate near-copy of EIP-1559 operating on a different resource.

Each blob consumes `GAS_PER_BLOB` blob gas, defined as `2**17` — 131,072 — so a transaction's blob
gas is `GAS_PER_BLOB * len(tx.blob_versioned_hashes)`.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> The price per unit comes
from a header field: `excess_blob_gas` is a running total of blob gas consumed in excess of the target,
increased by above-target blocks and decreased by below-target ones, floored at
zero.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> The base fee per blob gas is then an exponential in that running total,
approximating

```text
base_fee_per_blob_gas = MIN_BASE_FEE_PER_BLOB_GAS
                      * e ** (excess_blob_gas / BLOB_BASE_FEE_UPDATE_FRACTION)
```

with `MIN_BASE_FEE_PER_BLOB_GAS` of 1 wei and a `BLOB_BASE_FEE_UPDATE_FRACTION` that EIP-4844 set at
3,338,477 — chosen so the maximum per-block rate of change at target is about
1.125.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Treat that constant the way you treat the target and the maximum: it is
now a per-fork entry in the `blobSchedule` alongside them, not a fixed number.<Cite src="eip-7840" />

Three differences from the execution market matter more than the similarities.

**There is no blob tip.** The envelope carries `max_fee_per_blob_gas` and no priority counterpart.
The blob fee is deducted from the sender's balance before execution and **burned in full**, and it is
**not refunded if the transaction fails**.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> A proposer earns nothing from your
blob gas, which means you cannot bid your way past a competitor on the blob side. You compete on the
execution tip, or you wait.

**Your `max_fee_per_blob_gas` is a hard inclusion gate.** A block is invalid if it contains a blob
transaction whose ceiling is below that block's blob base fee.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> This is the same
shape as the 1559 ceiling, applied to a second resource, and it fails the same way: below the base
fee, the transaction is not slow, it is ineligible.

**The capacity numbers are not constants any more.** EIP-4844 shipped with a target of 3 blobs and a
maximum of 6 per block, described in the EIP itself as small initial limits expected to be increased
as the network demonstrated reliability.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> They have been. Target and maximum now
live in a per-fork `blobSchedule` in client configuration rather than being hard-coded
constants,<Cite src="eip-7840" /> and are stepped by *blob-parameter-only* forks — lightweight
upgrades that change nothing but these parameters.<Cite src="eip-7892" /> **This lesson deliberately
does not tell you the current target and maximum.** Sources retrieved for this curriculum disagree
about which BPO forks have activated as of August 2026, and no primary source was found that settles
it; the honest answer is to read the pair from your client's configured schedule for the current fork
rather than from any document, including this one.

<Aside kind="note">
Note what the split into two exponential mechanisms buys structurally. Execution gas and blob gas
measure different scarce things — one is CPU and state access on every executing node, the other is
bandwidth and temporary storage across the peer set. Pricing them with a single number would force
those two costs to be traded off at a fixed exchange rate that nobody chose. Two mechanisms let each
resource discover its own price, and let each be scaled on its own schedule, which is precisely what
the BPO forks have been doing to blob capacity without touching the execution gas limit.<Cite src="eip-7892" />
</Aside>

## The independence, stated precisely

Here is the claim in the form that survives contact with the specification.

**Demand-side independence holds, and it is the point.** The execution base fee is updated from the
parent block's execution gas used.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> The blob base fee is updated from
`excess_blob_gas`, which accumulates from blob gas used.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> A block full of
NFT mints raises the first and leaves the second alone; a block full of rollup batches does the
reverse. Rollups buy data capacity without bidding against ordinary users for execution capacity, and
a spike in one market is not evidence about the other.

<Misconception
  belief="Blob transactions compete with ordinary transactions and make them more expensive."
  reality="They are metered and priced separately, in different units, from different header fields. Blob demand does not enter the execution base-fee update at all."
  why="Both are called 'gas', both appear in the same block, and block explorers show them in the same fee column. The naming collision does most of the damage."
  src="eip-4844"
/>

**And there is one coupling, in one direction, at the floor.** EIP-7918 — Final — observed that when
the blob base fee bottoms out near its 1-wei minimum, the mechanism stops functioning as a market: a
10% change in the blob base fee can shift a blob consumer's total cost by a negligible fraction,
so the price carries almost no signal and demand becomes inelastic.<Cite src="eip-7918" /> Its fix is
a reserve price. An `if`-clause is added to `calc_excess_blob_gas`: when
`BLOB_BASE_COST * base_fee_per_gas` exceeds `GAS_PER_BLOB * base_fee_per_blob_gas`, the update no
longer subtracts the target, so `excess_blob_gas` climbs rather than
decaying.<Cite src="eip-7918" /> With `BLOB_BASE_COST` set to `2**13`, the ratio between the reserve
blob base fee and the execution base fee is fixed at `BLOB_BASE_COST / GAS_PER_BLOB`, or
one-sixteenth.<Cite src="eip-7918" />

The second stated purpose is worth carrying because it explains why the coupling points this way and
not the other: verifying KZG proofs is genuinely expensive compute that nodes perform on behalf of
blob consumers, and the execution base fee is the network's own live measure of how in-demand that
compute is.<Cite src="eip-7918" />

So the accurate sentence is: **the two markets do not compete for the same capacity and blob demand
never moves the execution price, but the execution base fee places a floor under the blob base fee.**
The exception runs execution → blob, at the bottom of the range only, at a fixed ratio. If your
blob costs are already meaningfully above 1 wei per blob gas, the floor is not binding and full
independence is what you observe.

## A different pool, with different rules

Because blob transactions are large and the pool is not consensus, clients treat them separately.
go-ethereum keeps them in a dedicated blob pool with its own configuration, and its design notes give
the reasoning: replacements are expensive because propagating a replacement blob transaction wastes
real bandwidth, so replacement should be aggressively discouraged; evicting an already-propagated blob
transaction is a denial-of-service vector; and the presence of blob transactions from an account
excludes non-blob transactions from that same account in the pool.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" />

The consequence for an L2 batcher is that the pool behaviours you learned on ordinary transactions do
not transfer. The next lesson takes that apart properly, because all of it is downstream of one rule
about nonces.

<Check type="explain" concept="separate-fee-markets">
Your rollup's batcher posts blobs continuously. Over one hour the execution base fee rises by a factor
of ten because of an unrelated mint, while blob demand is unchanged.

Work out what happens to (a) the blob gas your batcher consumes, (b) the blob base fee it pays, and
(c) the total cost of a batch. Say under what condition your answer to (b) changes.

<Answer>
**(a) Blob gas consumed does not change at all.** It is `GAS_PER_BLOB * len(blob_versioned_hashes)`,
a pure function of how many blobs you attach — `2**17` per blob.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Nothing about
execution demand enters it.

**(b) The blob base fee is unaffected — in the ordinary case.** It is derived from `excess_blob_gas`,
which accumulates only from blob gas used relative to the target, and blob demand is stated as
unchanged.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

**(c) The total cost still rises, and this is the part people get wrong in the other direction.** A
blob transaction pays *both* markets. Its calldata, its call into the inbox contract and its 21000
intrinsic cost are all ordinary execution gas, charged at the execution base fee plus tip — so that
component multiplies by roughly ten along with everyone else's.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> "The markets
are independent" means the blob component is unaffected, not that the transaction is.

**The condition that changes (b): the EIP-7918 reserve price.** If the blob base fee is low enough
that `BLOB_BASE_COST * base_fee_per_gas` exceeds `GAS_PER_BLOB * base_fee_per_blob_gas` — that is, if
the blob base fee sits below one-sixteenth of the execution base fee — then `calc_excess_blob_gas`
stops subtracting the target, and `excess_blob_gas` rises even though blob demand did
not.<Cite src="eip-7918" /> A tenfold jump in the execution base fee raises that floor tenfold, so a
batcher that had been enjoying near-minimum blob prices will see them pulled up. A batcher already
paying well above the floor sees nothing.

**The rule to carry into an incident:** ask which of your costs is denominated in which resource
before you conclude that a price spike is yours.
</Answer>

## Where this pays off

The blobs module goes underneath this envelope: what a blob actually is as 4,096 field elements, what
a KZG commitment compresses, and how PeerDAS distributes the data so that no node has to hold all of
it.<Cite src="ethereum-org-peerdas" /> Everything there explains *why* the transaction carries a hash
rather than the data.

On the scaling track, the two-market structure is the economic foundation of the rollup cost model,
and the EIP-7918 floor is the reason a rollup's data cost is not independent of L1 congestion at the
bottom of the range — a subtlety that matters exactly when you are trying to explain why your unit
economics moved.<Cite src="eip-7918" />

And in the very next lesson, the blob pool's separate rules turn out to be one instance of the general
problem: a sender's transactions form a strict sequence, and every pool policy in existence is a
consequence of that.
