At 03:14 a settlement service signs transaction number 1,204 with a `maxFeePerGas` computed from a
base-fee estimate that was four blocks stale. Nothing else goes wrong. No process crashes, no key is
lost, no contract reverts.

By 09:00 the service has 1,800 signed transactions outstanding and not one of them has been included —
including the eleven it re-sent with correct fees, the seven it sent to a different RPC provider, and
the one an engineer sent by hand from a wallet marked "cancel".

Every one of those responses was aimed at the wrong object. The problem was never any of the 1,799
transactions after 1,204. It was 1,204, and until 1,204 resolves, nothing behind it is even *eligible*.

## One rule, and everything else is consequence

A transaction from an account is valid only when its nonce equals that account's current nonce
exactly. The nonce is a sequentially incrementing counter indicating the transaction number from the
account,<Cite src="ethereum-org-transactions" /> and the protocol increments it by exactly one per
included transaction.<Cite src="execution-specs" />

There is no "greater than or equal to". There is no reordering window, no priority override, no
skipping a slot that turned out to be a mistake. A transaction with nonce `n+1` is not a low-priority
transaction while `n` is outstanding — it is an **invalid** transaction, and it stays invalid until
`n` is included.

That single rule is the source of every stuck-transaction incident you will ever debug, and it
interacts badly with two things you already know from Track 01. The
[mempool is not a queue](/c/mempool-is-not-a-queue): pending transactions are a set, re-priced and
re-sorted every block by whoever is building. And a
[sender's transactions are totally ordered](/c/nonce-total-ordering): within one account, they are the
one thing in the mempool that *is* a queue. Both are true at once. Across senders there is no line;
within a sender there is nothing but line.

### Pending and queued are different sets

Clients implement the rule by splitting the pool. go-ethereum's legacy pool keeps a *pending* set of
transactions that are executable right now — nonce-contiguous from the account's current nonce — and
holds everything behind a gap separately; its admission path literally asks whether a new transaction
`isGapped`, and treats it as executable only if the transactions already held can fill the gap
up to it.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" />

The operational consequence is the one that makes incidents confusing: **a gapped transaction is not
gossiped and cannot be mined, and nothing in the interface says so.** Your wallet shows "pending". The
RPC returns an object for the hash. `eth_getTransactionReceipt` returns null, which it also does for a
transaction that is genuinely about to land. From the outside, structurally-ineligible and merely-slow
look identical.

## There is no cancel

The word is not in any specification, and the operation does not exist. What wallets label "cancel" is
a **replacement**: a different transaction signed with the *same* sender and the *same* nonce, usually
a zero-value self-transfer, broadcast in the hope that it is the one that gets
mined.<Cite src="ethereum-org-transactions" /> The two race. Whichever is included invalidates the
other, and if the original wins, you have paid for a self-transfer and achieved nothing.

Whether a node will even accept the replacement is **client policy, not consensus**. No EIP specifies
a minimum fee bump. go-ethereum's legacy pool carries a `PriceBump` in its default configuration,
documented as the minimum price bump percentage required to replace an existing nonce, currently set
to 10; the blob pool sets the same field to 100, with a design note arguing that replacing an
already-propagated blob transaction wastes real bandwidth and should be aggressively
discouraged.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" /><Cite src="geth-blobpool" /> Other figures circulate in the
ecosystem — 12.5% is commonly quoted — and other clients set their own values; the sources surveyed
for this curriculum do not agree on a single number, and none of them is normative anyway.

The engineering conclusion is not a number. It is: **the bump threshold is a property of the node you
are talking to, it differs between clients and between pools, and a replacement that one provider
accepts another may reject with `replacement transaction underpriced`.** Build the percentage as
configuration, and treat rejection as an expected branch rather than an error.

### And it may simply disappear

A pool is a bounded, local cache, not a durable queue. go-ethereum's legacy pool defaults cap an
account at 16 pending slots and 64 queued, cap the pool globally at 5,120 pending and 1,024 queued,
and drop transactions after a `Lifetime` of three hours.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" /> Those are
go-ethereum's current defaults, they are configurable, and every other client has its own.

Which gives four ways a transaction leaves every pool without ever being mined: it is **replaced**, it
is **evicted on slot pressure**, it is **evicted on lifetime**, or it is **invalidated** because the
account's nonce moved past it or its balance no longer covers `gas_limit * max_fee_per_gas`, which is
the amount the sender must be able to afford.<Cite src="eip-1559" /><Cite src="geth-legacypool" />
None of the four produces a receipt, an error, or a notification. Any product that treats "I
broadcast it" as "it will eventually land" is wrong on all four counts.

## The wrinkle that makes nonce tracking hard again

Computing the next nonce used to be a solved problem: ask the node, add one for each transaction you
have in flight. EIP-7702 broke the premise.

Processing an authorization tuple **increments the authorising account's nonce by one**, and this
happens whether or not that account sent the transaction.<Cite src="eip-7702" /> Nothing else changed
about nonces; what changed is that "the account's nonce" is no longer the same quantity as "the number
of transactions this account has sent". A relayer can sponsor a delegation for your user's address,
and your user's nonce moves without your user broadcasting anything.

For a wallet or a service that maintains its own nonce counter from its own send history, that is a
silent off-by-one that manifests as a gap — and a gap, per everything above, stalls the whole account.

Clients have added pool rules specifically to contain this, and they are strict enough to break
designs that work fine on undelegated accounts. In go-ethereum:

- An account that is delegated, **or merely has a pending authorization in the pool**, is limited to
  **at most one in-flight executable transaction**.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" />
- A gapped-nonce transaction from such an account is rejected outright, with an error named
  `gapped-nonce tx from delegated accounts`.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" />
- A transaction carrying an authorization signed by an address that already has in-flight transactions
  known to the pool is rejected as `authority already reserved`.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" />

Read that list as a product constraint rather than as trivia. **A batch sender that keeps five
transactions in flight from one EOA stops working the moment that EOA is delegated**, and the failure
appears at submission time against some providers and not others, depending on which client they run.

<Aside kind="warn">
The same asymmetry is what makes sponsored authorizations griefable. The sponsor pays intrinsic gas
for every tuple regardless of validity,<Cite src="eip-7702" /> and the authority can invalidate a
tuple before inclusion simply by getting any transaction of their own mined, which moves their nonce
past the one the tuple names.<Cite src="nethermind-7702-attack-surfaces" /> A relayer that does not
price this in is subsidising a denial-of-service.
</Aside>

Blob transactions add their own version of the constraint. go-ethereum's blob pool design holds that
nonce-gapped blob transactions are disallowed, and that the presence of blob transactions from an
account excludes non-blob transactions from that account in the pool — you cannot interleave an
ordinary transaction between two blob transactions from the same sender and expect the pool to hold
it.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" /> The current implementation keeps gapped blob transactions only in a
short-lived reorder buffer, capped in both duration and total count, explicitly as a DoS
guard.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" /> For an L2 batcher, that means the pool semantics you validated on
type-2 traffic do not carry over to type-3 traffic.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

## What a correct sender looks like

Everything above collapses into a small number of design commitments.

**One allocator per account, and it is the only thing that reads the nonce.** Concurrent workers each
calling `eth_getTransactionCount` produce collisions, because the answer does not account for what the
other workers are about to send. `pending` includes what the *node you asked* knows about, which is
not the same as what you sent through a different provider.<Cite src="execution-apis" />

**Retain the signed bytes and the nonce that was assigned.** A replacement requires re-signing at the
same nonce; if you cannot reconstruct which nonce a stuck transaction holds, you cannot free the
account.

**Have a stuck path, and have it act on the oldest unconfirmed nonce.** Not the newest, not the one
the user complained about. The blocked account has exactly one blocking transaction and it is the
lowest outstanding nonce.

**Cap in-flight depth, and cap it at one for delegated accounts.** Otherwise the pool rules above will
do it for you, at submission time, inconsistently across providers.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" />

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="nonce-ordering">
A payout worker runs four concurrent goroutines against one hot wallet. Each one does:

```text
nonce = eth_getTransactionCount(wallet, "pending")
sign and broadcast with that nonce
```

The wallet is an ordinary EOA. Describe the failure modes, then describe what changes if the wallet is
later delegated with an EIP-7702 authorization.

<Answer>
**Collision, from the race.** Two goroutines that call `eth_getTransactionCount` before either has
broadcast receive the same answer and sign two different transactions at the same nonce. They become
accidental replacements of each other: one is included, the other is invalid forever, and the payout it
carried never happens. The service sees a successful broadcast and no receipt.

**Gaps, from partial failure.** If a broadcast fails after a nonce was consumed locally — a timeout, a
rejected fee, a provider hiccup — the later transactions form a gap and sit ineligible, not slow.
Nothing downstream is includable.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" />

**Disagreement between providers.** `pending` is answered from the pool of the node you asked, and
pools are node-local. Round-robin across two providers and the two of them will legitimately give
different answers to the same question at the same instant.<Cite src="execution-apis" />

**The fix** is a single in-process allocator that owns the account: it reads the chain nonce once at
startup, hands out nonces itself, and reconciles against `latest` — never `pending` — when it needs to
resynchronise, because `latest` reflects what the chain has actually accepted rather than what one
node happens to be holding.

**Now delegate the wallet.** Two additional things break, and they break at a different layer.

First, the account's nonce can now advance without this service sending anything, because processing
an EIP-7702 authorization increments the authority's nonce.<Cite src="eip-7702" /> A local counter
that only counts its own sends will drift, and drift produces a gap.

Second, and more abruptly: go-ethereum limits a delegated account — or one with a pending
authorization in the pool — to a single in-flight executable transaction, and rejects gapped-nonce
transactions from it outright.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" /> The four-goroutine design does not
degrade here; three of the four submissions are refused. The correct shape for a delegated hot wallet
is depth-one and strictly serial, or a different account.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

The nonce rule is the reason several later mechanisms exist in the shape they do.

**Account abstraction and batching.** The whole appeal of getting several operations into one
transaction is that a sequence of separate transactions from one sender is fragile in precisely the
way this lesson describes — and the type-`0x04` envelope you met earlier is one of the answers.<Cite src="eip-7702" />

**Relayers and sponsored flows.** Once the payer and the actor are different accounts, they have
different nonce sequences, and which of the two can stall the other becomes a design question with a
real answer.<Cite src="nethermind-7702-attack-surfaces" />

**L2 batch posting.** A sequencer's inbox transactions come from one address, in order, forever. Every
throughput property of that pipeline is bounded by this rule, and the blob pool's stricter policy
tightens it further.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" />

**And the product surface.** "Pending" is the single most overloaded word in this domain: it can mean
executable and competing, ineligible behind a gap, or already dropped from every pool you can reach.
The module's reflection prompt asks you to look at code you have written and decide which of the five
envelopes it silently assumes. Ask the same question of your pending state — and of what your
interface offers a user at ten seconds, two minutes and ten minutes, given that all three of those
words are live possibilities.
