You are building a sealed-bid auction on a chain where every byte of every transaction is public and
permanent. Bidders must submit now and be held to what they submitted, but nobody may learn a bid
before the auction closes.

Two requirements, and they pull in opposite directions: **say nothing, and be bound by what you
said.**

## The two properties, and why they are separate

A commitment scheme lets you publish a value now and open it later. The Zcash protocol
specification gives the normative definition, and it is worth reading in its own words: a commitment
scheme takes a **commitment trapdoor generated at random** together with an input, such that no
information is revealed about the input without the trapdoor (*hiding*), and given the trapdoor and
the input the commitment can be verified to open to that input **and no other**
(*binding*).<Cite src="zcash-protocol-spec" />

The formal statements are sharper than the English:

- **Computational hiding** — for any two inputs `x` and `x'`, the distribution of commitments to `x`
  and the distribution of commitments to `x'` are computationally
  indistinguishable.<Cite src="zcash-protocol-spec" /> Note that the randomness is over the
  *trapdoor*, not over the input. A commitment to a fixed value with a fixed trapdoor hides nothing.
- **Computational binding** — it is infeasible to find `x ≠ x'` and trapdoors `r`, `r'` with
  `COMM_r(x) = COMM_r'(x')`.<Cite src="zcash-protocol-spec" /> That is a collision statement over the
  *pair*, not over the value alone.

The trapdoor is what a Solidity developer would call the salt. One more note from the same section
generalises well: the trapdoor generator need not produce a uniform distribution over the trapdoor
space, and where it does not, it is incorrect to draw a trapdoor from the uniform distribution
instead.<Cite src="zcash-protocol-spec" /> How you sample the salt is part of the scheme, not a
detail below it.

These properties are independent, and the interesting failures are the ones where a scheme has one
and not the other. A scheme that is perfectly binding and not hiding at all is the common case, and
it is the subject of most of this lesson: the hash did its job, and the secret was never secret.

<Aside kind="warn">
They can also be traded against each other by accident, and the canonical example is in the same
document. Zerocash's note commitment truncated an internal hash of two of its inputs to 128 bits,
**motivated by providing statistical hiding**. That truncation put the birthday bound within reach:
at a work factor on the order of 2⁶⁴ an attacker could find two distinct input pairs with the same
truncated hash, and therefore the same note commitment — breaking binding, and with it the balance
property, potentially minting arbitrary currency.<Cite src="zcash-protocol-spec" /> Zcash replaced
the nested construction with a single hash evaluation.<Cite src="zcash-protocol-spec" />

Two lessons in one failure. Strengthening hiding weakened binding, because the change was made to a
shared component. And the halving from lesson one showed up exactly where it always does: a
128-bit digest gives 64 bits against an attacker who chooses both sides.<Cite src="nist-sp-800-107" />
</Aside>

## The construction, and where each property comes from

`keccak256(value ‖ salt)`, where the salt is a fresh high-entropy value.<Cite src="yellow-paper" />
Open by publishing `value` and `salt`; anyone re-hashes and compares.

**Binding comes from collision resistance.** To open to a different value you would need a second
`(value, salt)` pair with the same digest, which is the collision game and therefore half the digest
length in bits — 128 for `keccak256`.<Cite src="nist-sp-800-107" /> Note that it is collision
resistance and not second-preimage resistance, because the committer chooses both pairs; they are
attacking their own commitment.

**Hiding comes entirely from the salt.** Not from the hash. This is the sentence to keep.

Preimage resistance is defined over a **randomly chosen** digest.<Cite src="nist-sp-800-107" /> It
says nobody can invert the function. It does not say nobody can guess your input and check. Bids in
whole ETH, US postcodes, phone numbers, an address drawn from a known allowlist, a boolean — every
one of those input spaces is small enough to enumerate on a laptop, and the digest you published is
the oracle that tells the attacker when they have guessed right.

<Check type="recall" concept="hash-commitment">
A team fixes the "sealed bid" problem by salting: `keccak256(bid ‖ salt)`. To keep the reveal step
simple and avoid storing anything client-side, they derive the salt deterministically as
`salt = keccak256(bidderAddress)`.

Is this hiding? Is it binding?

<Answer>
Binding, yes — collision resistance is untouched.

Hiding, no. The salt is a public function of a public value, so an attacker who knows the bidder's
address computes the same salt and is back to enumerating bids. Read it against the formal
definition and the failure is exact: hiding requires that commitments to two different bids be
indistinguishable *when the trapdoor is drawn at random*<Cite src="zcash-protocol-spec" />, and here
the trapdoor is not random at all — it is a constant, per bidder, that anyone can compute.

The salt has to be *secret* and *fresh*: drawn from a CSPRNG per commitment and kept by the bidder
until the reveal.

The tell is general: if the committer can recompute the salt from public data at reveal time without
having stored anything, so can everyone else.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Four failure modes worth knowing before you build one

**The encoding can break binding even when the hash is fine.** In Solidity,
`abi.encodePacked` concatenates arguments without length prefixes, so two dynamic arguments can be
re-cut in more than one way: `("aa", "b")` and `("a", "ab")` produce identical bytes and therefore an
identical commitment.<Cite src="nethermind-encodepacked-collisions" /> Nothing about `keccak256`
failed; the ambiguity was introduced before the hash ever ran, and the committer now has two openings
for one digest without solving anything. Use `abi.encode`, which pads each argument to 32-byte
boundaries and carries lengths, or keep at most one dynamic
argument.<Cite src="scsfg-abi-hash-collisions" />

**The commitment does not say who made it.** A commitment over the value alone is a bearer token:
anyone watching the mempool can copy the published digest and submit it as their own, then race you
at reveal time with the value they will learn from your reveal transaction. The fix is to hash the
committer into the commitment. ENS does exactly this — its registrar's `makeCommitment` hashes the
entire registration struct, which carries the `owner` address and a `bytes32 secret` alongside the
name being registered.<Cite src="ens-eth-registrar-controller" /> Copying the digest is then useless,
because opening it requires being the owner it names.

**Binding does not compel opening.** A committer who dislikes the outcome can simply never reveal.
Commit-reveal protocols therefore need a deadline plus a consequence: a forfeited bond, a default
value, or exclusion from the result. ENS bounds the window from both sides — a commitment is invalid
before `minCommitmentAge` and after `maxCommitmentAge`, both fixed at
deployment.<Cite src="ens-eth-registrar-controller" /> Without such a bound, "refuse to reveal" is a
free option, and options have value.

<Aside kind="note">
Ethereum itself pays for that option, and the price has been computed. The beacon chain's randomness
comes from proposers revealing a value each slot, so a proposer assigned to the end of an epoch can
withhold their block to bias the result. Modelling the optimal withholding strategy gives the gain
directly: an adversary with 5% of stake can propose a 5.048% fraction of rounds, 10% can propose
10.19%, and 20% can propose 20.68%.<Cite src="randao-manipulation-arxiv" />

Small numbers, and that is the point — the leak is a few basis points rather than a collapse, which
is why the design is considered acceptable rather than broken. But it is a permanent tax paid by
every commit-reveal scheme with a last mover, and no amount of cryptography removes it. It is a
protocol-design problem.
</Aside>

**Reveal order leaks.** In a naive two-phase auction the last bidder to reveal has seen everyone
else's value before deciding whether to reveal their own. The cryptography is intact; the *protocol*
gave one participant a strictly better information set. Randomised or simultaneous reveal windows,
or a forfeit large enough to price the option, are the usual answers — and note the shape is the same
one ERC-2612 meets with `deadline`, where a relayer holding a signed permit has a free option to
submit or withhold it.<Cite src="eip-2612" />

<Aside kind="note">
You have already met a commitment in this module without it being named. An Ethereum address is the
low 20 bytes of the `keccak256` of a public key — a binding commitment to that key, published long
before the key itself is.<Cite src="yellow-paper" /> It is hiding only by accident of input
distribution: public keys are drawn from a space far too large to enumerate. Change the input to
something guessable and the identical construction stops hiding anything.

It is also a reminder that binding here is 20 bytes, not 32. Against a committer who chooses both
keys that is 80 bits of collision resistance<Cite src="nist-sp-800-107" />, which is why address
collisions are a live consideration in designs where someone picks an address before anyone commits
to trusting it.
</Aside>

## Where this pays off

The same shape scales up and never changes: commit now, reveal later, with hiding and binding named
separately. Sealed-bid auctions and commit-reveal randomness are the small end, and ENS registration
is the version most people have used without noticing.<Cite src="ens-eth-registrar-controller" />
Encrypted mempools and order-flow auctions are the same idea applied to transactions, so that a
searcher cannot act on your intent before it is final. And polynomial commitments — the machinery
under proving systems and blob availability — are this same contract with a different mathematical
object supplying binding: Zcash's own note commitments already move from a plain SHA-256 hash in the
Sprout pool to Pedersen and Sinsemilla constructions in later pools, keeping the two properties and
changing only what provides them.<Cite src="zcash-protocol-spec" />

When you meet those later, the questions to ask are always the same four: what supplies hiding, what
supplies binding, what happens to the protocol if nobody ever opens, and who goes last.
