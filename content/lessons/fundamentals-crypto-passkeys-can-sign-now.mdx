The phone in your pocket already holds a hardware-backed signing key that a user unlocks with their
face. Every argument for using it to control an on-chain account is a product argument, and every
one of them has been obvious for years. So why did it not work until recently — and what changed?

The blocker was never policy. It was a curve.

## Two curves, no compatibility

Ethereum signs with ECDSA over secp256k1. Secure Enclave, Android Keystore, HSMs and every
FIDO2/WebAuthn authenticator sign with ECDSA over **secp256r1** — the NIST curve, also written P-256
or prime256v1.<Cite src="eip-7951" />

They are not near-misses of each other. SEC 2 publishes both, in adjacent sections, as different
kinds of object.<Cite src="sec2-curve-params" />

**secp256k1 is a Koblitz curve.** Its coefficients are `a = 0` and `b = 7`, and SEC 2 says the
Koblitz parameters were found by repeatedly selecting parameters that admit an efficiently
computable endomorphism until a prime-order curve appeared.<Cite src="sec2-curve-params" /> The
curve was chosen for speed.

**secp256r1 is verifiably random.** Its `a` equals `p − 3` and its `b` is derived from a published
seed `S` using SHA-1, per ANSI X9.62.<Cite src="sec2-curve-params" /> SEC 2 gives the reasoning: a
seed-derived curve cannot have been predetermined, so it is "extremely unlikely to be susceptible to
future special-purpose attacks, and no trapdoors can have been placed in the parameters during their
generation".<Cite src="sec2-curve-params" /> The curve was chosen for auditability of its own
provenance.

Two different design goals, and consequently a different prime field, different coefficients, a
different generator and a different group order.<Cite src="sec2-curve-params" /> Verification is
defined against those specific numbers<Cite src="sec1-ecc" />, so a P-256 signature handed to
`ecrecover` is not a weak signature or a wrong signature; it is not a signature at all as far as that
code is concerned. And you cannot ask a passkey authenticator to generate a secp256k1 key — the
hardware does not offer the curve.

Verifying P-256 in pure Solidity is possible, and was the pre-2026 workaround. It is also expensive
enough that nobody wanted it on a hot path. The ecosystem's directional claim is "hundreds of
thousands of gas"; no measured figure is settled here, so treat the exact cost as unknown until you
measure it on your own compiler and optimiser settings. That measurement is one of this module's
practices, and the point of doing it yourself is that the answer moves with the optimiser.

## What EIP-7951 added

EIP-7951 specifies a precompile, `P256VERIFY`, at address `0x100`, costing 6900 gas.<Cite src="eip-7951" />
The executable spec carries the same constant.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-gas" /> It shipped in
the Fulu-Osaka upgrade — "Fusaka" — which activated on mainnet on 3 December 2025 at block
23,935,694.<Cite src="ethereum-org-history" />

The interface is worth memorising because it is unforgiving:

- **Input: exactly 160 bytes**, as `h ‖ r ‖ s ‖ qx ‖ qy`, each 32 bytes — the message hash, the two
  signature components, and the public key's two coordinates.<Cite src="eip-7951" />
- **Success returns a 32-byte word equal to one.** Failure returns **zero bytes** — not a zero word,
  not a revert.<Cite src="eip-7951" />
- **It must not revert under any circumstance**, and it charges the full 6900 gas whether the
  signature was valid or not.<Cite src="eip-7951" />
- Every input check — length, `0 < r < n` and `0 < s < n`, coordinates below the field modulus, the
  point actually on the curve and not the point at infinity — fails by returning that same empty
  output.<Cite src="execution-specs-p256verify" />

<Aside kind="stop">
Read the failure convention again, then read this:

```solidity
(bool ok, bytes memory out) = address(0x100).staticcall(input);
require(ok, "bad signature");   // WRONG
```

`ok` is true. A precompile that returns zero bytes has not reverted, so the low-level call succeeded.
This code treats every invalid signature as valid. The check has to be on the *output*:
`out.length == 32` and the word equal to one.<Cite src="eip-7951" />

This is the `ecrecover` footgun wearing different clothes. There, failure was a 20-byte value that
compares equal to an uninitialised slot; here, failure is an empty return that compares equal to
success on the flag most people check. Both times the precompile is behaving exactly as specified,
and both times the caller asked a question whose failure answer was indistinguishable from a pass.
</Aside>

## Verification, not recovery — and what that forces

`ecrecover` recovers a public key from the signature. `P256VERIFY` verifies against a public key you
supply. EIP-7951 gives the reason plainly: FIPS 186-5 specifies verification rather than recovery,
and that is what hardware implementations expose.<Cite src="eip-7951" /><Cite src="nist-fips-186-5" />

The architectural consequence is the thing to carry away. Because the key must be passed in, a
passkey-controlled account has to have **registered its public key on chain in advance**. There is no
20-byte address that stands in for the key, and therefore no way for a plain EOA to be
passkey-controlled. A passkey account is necessarily a smart account holding a registered
`(qx, qy)` — which is exactly the shape ERC-1271 was written for: a contract that answers "is this a
valid signature for me?" on its own terms.<Cite src="eip-1271" />

## What the authenticator actually signs

The precompile takes a 32-byte hash. You do not get to choose which one.

WebAuthn defines the assertion signature as being over the **binary concatenation of
`authenticatorData` and the SHA-256 hash of `clientDataJSON`**.<Cite src="w3c-webauthn-3" /> Both
halves are produced by the browser and the authenticator, not by you, and both have structure a
contract has to walk.

`authenticatorData` is a byte array of 37 bytes or more, laid out as: a 32-byte `rpIdHash` (the
SHA-256 of the relying-party ID the credential is scoped to), one byte of flags, then a 4-byte
signature counter.<Cite src="w3c-webauthn-3" /> The flags byte is a bitfield: bit 0 is User Present,
bit 2 is User Verified, bit 3 is Backup Eligibility and bit 4 is Backup
State.<Cite src="w3c-webauthn-3" /> A contract that does not check bit 2 has accepted a signature the
user's face never authorised.

`clientDataJSON` carries the `type`, the `challenge` — **base64url-encoded**, not raw bytes — the
`origin`, and cross-origin information.<Cite src="w3c-webauthn-3" /> Your user-operation hash lives
inside that JSON, encoded.

So the on-chain sequence is not "hash the user operation and verify". It is:

**Subgoal — reconstruct the exact signed bytes.** Concatenate the supplied `authenticatorData` with
`SHA-256(clientDataJSON)`, then hash that to get the 32 bytes the precompile
wants.<Cite src="w3c-webauthn-3" />

**Subgoal — prove the JSON is about your request.** Base64url-encode the digest you meant, and check
that string appears as the `challenge` value in the reconstructed JSON. Skip this and the contract
verifies a genuine signature over somebody else's challenge.

**Subgoal — check the flags and the scope.** User Verified if your policy requires
it<Cite src="w3c-webauthn-3" />, and `rpIdHash` against the domain you
registered.<Cite src="w3c-webauthn-3" />

<Aside kind="note">
The 4-byte signature counter is a curiosity worth knowing about. WebAuthn relying parties are told to
store the last value seen and compare: a counter that does not advance is "a signal, but not proof,
that the authenticator may be cloned".<Cite src="w3c-webauthn-3" /> It is also frequently zero,
because many platform authenticators do not implement it. Treat it as telemetry, not as a nonce —
your replay protection still has to come from the challenge.
</Aside>

## Two sharp edges

**Malleability is back.** EIP-7951 does not enforce low-`s`, and says so directly in its security
considerations: secp256r1 signatures are not required to be non-malleable under FIPS 186-5, and
applications needing non-malleability must add the check
themselves.<Cite src="eip-7951" /><Cite src="nist-fips-186-5" /> Every passkey signature therefore
has a second valid form, exactly as in the earlier lesson — except that here there is no EIP-2 to
have partly cleaned up after you, and the threshold you compare against is P-256's order, a different
constant from secp256k1's.<Cite src="sec2-curve-params" />

**The signed message is not yours.** Everything in the previous section follows from this. A contract
cannot hand `0x100` the user operation hash directly; it must rebuild the WebAuthn byte string first,
which means the encoding contract between your client and your contract is now part of your security
model — the same class of agreement that EIP-712 formalises for typed data.<Cite src="eip-712" />

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="p256verify-precompile">
An account contract verifies a passkey signature, then records `keccak256(abi.encode(r, s))` in a
`usedSignatures` mapping to prevent replay. The public key is registered, the domain is checked, the
challenge is bound correctly. Two problems remain. Name them.

<Answer>
**One: the replay key is the signature again.** With no low-`s` enforcement on this
curve,<Cite src="eip-7951" /> the mirrored pair `(r, n − s)` is an equally valid signature over the
same message and hashes to a different key. Replay guard defeated. Derive the key from the message —
a nonce or the challenge digest — or reject high-`s` yourself.

**Two: whatever this contract does with the precompile's result must inspect the returned bytes.**
A failed verification returns zero-length data with `success` true, so any `require` on the call's
success flag alone authorises every invalid signature.<Cite src="eip-7951" />

Both are the same mistake in different clothing: trusting a signal that was never carrying the
information you assumed. And note that neither is caught by a test suite built from valid
signatures — the first needs an adversarial input, the second needs an invalid one, and the happy
path exercises neither.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Aside kind="warn">
Do not carry an L2 gas figure out of this lesson. The same interface at the same address existed on
several L2s before mainnet, and EIP-7951 states explicitly that its cost does *not* preserve
compatibility with those deployments.<Cite src="eip-7951" /> Whether individual L2s re-priced after
Fusaka is unsettled and sources disagree, so the only figure to carry forward is the L1 one:
6900 gas.<Cite src="eip-7951" /> The core-dev discussion around the proposal is the place to track
how that lands per chain.<Cite src="eip-7951-magicians" />
</Aside>

## Where this pays off

This is the primitive under passkey wallets. It is why a passkey needs a smart account rather than an
EOA, why key registration is a first-class step in onboarding rather than an implementation detail,
and why account-abstraction stacks now ship P-256 and WebAuthn signer modules as swappable
components behind ERC-1271.<Cite src="eip-1271" /> It also composes with delegation: since Fusaka, an
ordinary EOA that has delegated to contract code under EIP-7702 can route its authorisation through
a passkey verifier, which is a different route to the same product outcome.<Cite src="eip-7702" />

It is also the clearest example in this track of a fact with a timestamp. Every explanation of
account abstraction written before 2026 says passkeys cannot sign for Ethereum, and every one of them
was correct when it was written.<Cite src="eip-7951" /> When you meet a confident architectural
constraint in this space, the useful question is which upgrade it predates.
