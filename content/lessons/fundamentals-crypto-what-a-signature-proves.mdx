A wallet pops up, you approve, and something happens on chain. The interesting question is not how
the signature is computed. It is: **when a contract accepts that signature, what has actually been
proven — and about what?**

The answer is narrower than the mental model most people arrive with, and the gap between the two is
where a whole family of production bugs lives.

## The pair, and which direction is cheap

A keypair is a randomly chosen private scalar `d` and the public point `Q = d·G`, where `G` is a
fixed generator point on the curve. Computing `Q` from `d` is one multiplication. Recovering `d`
from `Q` is the discrete logarithm problem on secp256k1, and there is no known feasible method.

The curve is not a vague "elliptic curve" — it is a published sextuple. SEC 2 specifies secp256k1 as
`y² = x³ + ax + b` over the prime field `F_p` with `a = 0` and `b = 7`, a named generator `G`, a
group order `n` just under 2²⁵⁶, and cofactor 1.<Cite src="sec2-curve-params" /> Everything in this
lesson and the next three is arithmetic modulo that specific `n`. It is worth knowing it is a
constant with a number, not a parameter.

An Ethereum address is a third thing, derived from the second. The executable spec computes it as
the last 20 bytes of the `keccak256` of the 64-byte public key.<Cite src="execution-specs-ecrecover" />
That 64 bytes is the point's `x` and `y` coordinates concatenated, with the `0x04` "uncompressed
point" prefix that SEC 1 defines for the octet-string encoding stripped
off.<Cite src="sec1-ecc" /> So an address is a lossy commitment to a public
key.<Cite src="yellow-paper" />

<Misconception
  belief="An address is the public key."
  reality="It is the low 20 bytes of the keccak256 hash of the public key. The key cannot be recovered from the address."
  why="Both are hex blobs that identify the same account, and nothing in a wallet UI distinguishes them."
/>

That lossiness has two consequences worth holding onto. An account's public key is not on chain
until the account signs something — until then the network knows a 20-byte fingerprint and nothing
else. And the address is a truncated digest, which is a shape you have already met: 20 bytes is 160
bits, so an attacker who gets to choose *both* keys is playing the collision game at 80 bits rather
than the preimage game at 160.

## What you sign is a digest, not a message

ECDSA signs a 32-byte value. Not a struct, not a sentence, not an intent — a digest. Everything a
signature means is therefore determined by what got hashed into that digest, and by nothing else.

<Misconception
  belief="You sign a message."
  reality="You sign a 32-byte hash. The message matters only insofar as it determines the hash, and the wallet showing you a message is a rendering choice, not a cryptographic one."
  why="Every signing UI displays text, so the digest — the only thing the algorithm sees — is invisible."
/>

OpenZeppelin's ECDSA library states the sharper version of this in its own source: the hash *must*
be the output of a hash operation, because signatures can be crafted that recover to arbitrary
addresses over non-hashed data.<Cite src="oz-ecdsa-source" /> "Sign this 32-byte value for me" is
not a safe request; "sign the hash of this structure" is.

The reason that request is unsafe is worth spelling out, because it is not obvious. Recovery runs
*backwards*: pick any `r` and `s`, pick a recovery bit, and the arithmetic below hands you a public
key and therefore an address.<Cite src="sec1-ecc" /> The signature is valid; you simply had no
control over whose it appears to be. An attacker who can also choose the 32 bytes being "signed"
gets to search until the recovered address is one your contract trusts. Hashing first removes that
freedom, because the attacker cannot steer a `keccak256` output.

## The signature, and why it has three parts

A secp256k1 signature is the pair `(r, s)`. Producing it requires a per-signature secret nonce `k`.
SEC 1's signing operation computes the point `k·G`, takes its `x` coordinate modulo `n` as `r`, and
sets `s = k⁻¹(e + r·d) mod n`, where `e` is the digest interpreted as an
integer.<Cite src="sec1-ecc" /> Two things live in `s`: the private key, and the nonce that hides it.

Ethereum adds a small recovery identifier `v`.

<Aside kind="warn">
`k` must be unpredictable and must never repeat. Look at the `s` equation and the reason falls out:
two signatures by the same key over different digests with the same `k` share `r`, giving two linear
equations in the two unknowns `k` and `d`. Anyone can then solve for `d` with schoolbook algebra.
This is a real historical failure mode, and it is why deterministic nonce generation was
standardised: RFC 6979 derives `k` from the private key and the message so that a broken random
number generator cannot leak the key.<Cite src="rfc-6979" /> FIPS 186-5 likewise treats the
per-message secret as a value whose compromise compromises the key.<Cite src="nist-fips-186-5" />
</Aside>

## Recovery, and why Ethereum has it at all

Standard ECDSA verification takes a public key and answers yes or no. Ethereum instead **recovers**
the public key from the signature and the digest, which is why it never has to store or transmit
public keys — and `v` is the extra bit that says which candidate to take.

This is not an Ethereum invention. SEC 1 specifies a Public Key Recovery Operation and gives the
motivation directly: recovery is useful "in bandwidth constrained environments, when transmission of
public keys cannot be afforded", so a verifier can recover the key and check it against a directory
rather than receive it.<Cite src="sec1-ecc" /> Ethereum's directory is the address in the
transaction's destination field, or the `owner` slot in your contract.

The operation itself explains `v` in one pass:<Cite src="sec1-ecc" />

**Subgoal — turn `r` back into a point.** `r` is an `x` coordinate reduced modulo `n`, so the
candidate `x` values are `r`, `r + n`, and so on up to the cofactor. For secp256k1 the cofactor is
1<Cite src="sec2-curve-params" />, so in practice there is one realistic candidate and a vanishingly
rare second.

**Subgoal — pick a `y`.** Each valid `x` gives two points, `R` and `−R`, mirrored across the axis.
Nothing in `(r, s)` distinguishes them.

**Subgoal — compute the key.** With `R` fixed, `Q = r⁻¹(sR − eG)`.<Cite src="sec1-ecc" /> Different
choices of `R` yield different, equally valid `Q`.

So a bare `(r, s)` names up to four public keys and gives you no way to choose. `v` is the discriminator
that collapses the set to one — and note where the two-way ambiguity in step two comes from, because
the next lesson is entirely about the fact that it does not disappear when you fix `v`.

## `ecrecover`, precisely

`ecrecover` is a precompiled contract. Its input is 128 bytes — the 32-byte digest, then `v`, `r`
and `s` each padded to 32 bytes — and its output is the recovered address, left-padded to 32 bytes.
It costs 3000 gas.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-gas" />

Here is the whole of its validation, read off the executable spec: `v` must be exactly 27 or 28;
`r` must satisfy `0 < r < n`; `s` must satisfy `0 < s < n`, where `n` is the curve order. If any
check fails, or if the point cannot be recovered, the precompile **returns immediately with no
output at all**.<Cite src="execution-specs-ecrecover" />

Two absences in that list matter later. There is no upper-half check on `s` — the full range is
accepted, which is the next lesson. And there is nothing at all about what the digest means, which
is the lesson after that.

The failure mode explains the footgun. The precompile does not revert; it returns zero bytes. A
zero-length return leaves the caller nothing to read, and Solidity's `ecrecover` builtin surfaces
that as `address(0)` — a perfectly ordinary-looking address that happens to equal every
uninitialised `address` slot in storage.

<Misconception
  belief="ecrecover reverts on a bad signature."
  reality="It returns address(0). Compared against an owner or signer slot that was never set, that comparison succeeds."
  why="The precompile calling convention predates custom errors and reverting return paths, and the builtin's signature gives you no way to distinguish 'recovered the zero address' from 'failed'."
/>

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="ecrecover">
A minimal signature-gated admin function:

```solidity
function setFeeRecipient(address who, uint8 v, bytes32 r, bytes32 s) external {
    bytes32 digest = keccak256(abi.encode(who));
    require(ecrecover(digest, v, r, s) == admin, "not admin");
    feeRecipient = who;
}
```

`admin` is assigned in the constructor of the *implementation* contract, and this contract is
deployed behind a proxy. What can an attacker do, and what is the one-line fix?

<Answer>
Behind a proxy, `admin` lives in the proxy's storage and the implementation's constructor never ran
against it, so `admin` is `address(0)`. The attacker submits any malformed signature — for example
`v = 0` — which fails the precompile's `v in (27, 28)` check.<Cite src="execution-specs-ecrecover" />
The precompile returns no data, the builtin yields `address(0)`, and `address(0) == admin` passes.
They set the fee recipient to themselves.

The fix is a single guard: recover into a local, then `require(recovered != address(0) && recovered == admin)`.
Better still, use a library that returns an error rather than a sentinel — OpenZeppelin's ECDSA
promises never to return `address(0)` without also reporting an error.<Cite src="oz-ecdsa-source" />

Note what the bug is *not*: nothing about the cryptography failed. The precompile behaved exactly as
specified. The contract asked a question — "did this recover to `admin`?" — whose failure value and
whose uninitialised value are the same 20 bytes. The general form is worth naming, because it long
outlives this precompile: **a sentinel return value is only safe when the sentinel cannot also be a
legitimate answer.**
</Answer>
</Check>

## "Recovered a real address" is not "valid for my purpose"

Suppose the guard is in place and `ecrecover` returns a live, non-zero address that matches your
stored signer. What have you learned?

That the holder of that key signed *some* 32-byte value which happens to equal the digest you
computed. Nothing states that the two of you agreed on what those bytes meant. The precompile has no
notion of application context, and standard ECDSA does not carry one
either.<Cite src="nist-fips-186-5" />

<Misconception
  belief="If ecrecover returns a non-zero address matching my signer, the signature is valid for my purpose."
  reality="It is valid over some digest. Whether that digest is the one you meant — this contract, this chain, this action, not yet used — is entirely your job."
  why="The precompile answers the only question it can answer, and its answer is a 20-byte address that looks like an authorisation."
/>

That gap is why the signed bytes are given structure at all. EIP-191 fixes a prefix so a signed blob
can never also be a valid transaction<Cite src="eip-191" />, and EIP-712 hashes a declared schema
together with a domain so the digest is pinned to one contract on one chain.<Cite src="eip-712" />
Both are lessons of their own further into this module. What matters here is *why* they exist: not
to make the cryptography stronger, but to put meaning inside the only 32 bytes the algorithm ever
sees.

<Aside kind="note">
There is a second gap, and it is larger. Everything above assumes the signer *has* a private key.
Smart-contract accounts do not — ERC-1271 opens by saying exactly that, and defines
`isValidSignature(hash, signature)`, which returns the magic value `0x1626ba7e` on
success.<Cite src="eip-1271" /> The contract decides for itself what counts as a valid signature: a
multisig quorum, a passkey, a session key with an expiry.

The practical consequence is that `ecrecover(...) == expectedSigner` is not a general signature
check. It is the check for one kind of account. Code that only knows that form silently refuses
every smart account, which since EIP-7702 includes ordinary EOAs that have delegated to
code.<Cite src="eip-7702" />
</Aside>

## So what has been proven?

A successful `ecrecover` proves exactly one thing: **the holder of the key behind this address
produced a signature over this 32-byte digest, at some point, in some context.**

It does not prove when. It does not prove for which contract, which chain, or which deployment. It
does not prove the signature has not been used before. It does not prove the signature was submitted
by the signer — anyone holding the bytes can relay them. It does not prove the signer understood the
digest. And it does not prove the *bytes* you were handed are the only bytes that would have
recovered this signer.

Each of those missing properties has to be added by you, on top. The next three lessons add them one
at a time: uniqueness (the bytes are not an identifier), context (the domain separator), and
legibility (what the human is shown before they approve).

## Where this pays off

Every externally owned account authorises its transactions this way, so this primitive sits under
literally every state change on the chain that a person initiated. Delegating an EOA to contract
code under EIP-7702 is authorised by a signature of exactly this shape.<Cite src="eip-7702" />
Gasless token approvals under EIP-2612 are this primitive plus a structure and a
nonce.<Cite src="eip-2612" /> Account-abstraction stacks verify user operations through ERC-1271
rather than `ecrecover`, which is why a signer module is a replaceable component in those
designs.<Cite src="eip-1271" />

The `address(0)` failure mode in particular is the oldest live footgun in Solidity, and it still
shows up in access-control findings. When you reach the security track's vulnerability classes, the
two entries to watch for — missing zero-address check and missing signer-context check — are both on
this page, and neither of them is a cryptography failure.
