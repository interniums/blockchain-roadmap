Your webhook fires. The payload says the transaction succeeded, there is a receipt, there is a block
number, and your handler is about to release a file, credit an account, or dispatch a parcel.

How much is that receipt worth?

The honest answer is a number, and the number depends on which of several distinct states the
transaction is currently in. Treating "confirmed" as a boolean is the most common way a competent
team ships an unrecoverable bug on this platform, and it happens because every API in sight returns
something that looks like a boolean.

## Four states, four prices

A transaction moves through a sequence, and each step changes what it would cost somebody to undo it.

**In a mempool.** Not on the chain at all. A node is holding it, has told its peers about it, and
nothing has happened. Reversal cost: nothing, because there is nothing to reverse. A transaction can
sit here for a long time, or be dropped and forgotten by every node without any announcement.

**In the head block.** Executed, receipted, visible in every explorer. This is where your webhook
fired. But the head is the output of a fork-choice computation over the messages a node has received
so far, and a node that receives different messages computes a different head. Reversal cost: close
to nothing. No attack is required, no rule is broken, and nobody is punished — an ordinary
reorganisation of the last block or two is normal operation of a working network, not an incident.

**Under a justified checkpoint.** A supermajority of stake has voted for the checkpoint that this
block sits beneath. Reversal is now expensive rather than incidental, but the checkpoint is not yet
finalised and the guarantee is weaker than the next one in a way that is easy to overstate.

**Under a finalised checkpoint.** Reversal requires an attack whose cost is at least a third of all
staked ETH being destroyed, and the attack leaves a signed, publishable proof of who did it.<Cite
src="casper-ffg-paper" />

Notice that this is a cost curve, not a switch. At no point does a transaction cross a line into
"cannot be undone". It crosses lines into "would cost this much to undo", and your job is to decide
which price is high enough for the thing you are about to do.

### What "justified" is actually worth

The middle rung deserves a paragraph, because it is the one most often either ignored or treated as
equivalent to the last one.

Justification is a supermajority of stake voting for a checkpoint. Finalisation requires that to
happen and then requires the *next* checkpoint to attract a supermajority too. So a justified
checkpoint is one step short: the votes exist, but the protocol has not yet obtained the second
piece of evidence that turns a reversal into a provable, punishable contradiction.<Cite
src="casper-ffg-paper" />

The practical reading is that justification is meaningfully stronger than the head and meaningfully
weaker than finalisation, and the gap between the second and third rungs is not latency — it is
whether a reversal is attributable. That difference is the entire reason the last rung is worth
waiting the extra epoch for. An unattributable reversal costs an attacker nothing but effort. An
attributable one costs them their stake.

### Your infrastructure hides all of this

One reason teams end up treating confirmation as a boolean is that everything between them and the
chain is working hard to make it look like one.

Recall that the head is a *local* computation over the messages a node happens to have received. Now
consider that your RPC endpoint is almost certainly not one node — it is a pool behind a load
balancer. Two consecutive calls can land on two nodes whose heads differ by a block, which means a
client can observe the block number going *backwards* without anything being wrong anywhere. Code
that assumes monotonic block numbers will misbehave, rarely, in a way that reproduces on nobody's
machine.

The same flattening happens further up. A webhook delivers an event, not a state; by the time your
handler runs, the block it describes may no longer be on the canonical chain. An indexing framework
will roll its own tables back to the common ancestor when that happens, which is the correct
behaviour and also the thing that makes the problem invisible: afterwards, the database is
consistent, and the only trace of the reorg is whatever your handler did to the outside world in the
meantime.

The rule that follows is worth adopting before you need it: **anything your handler does that leaves
your own database — an email, a payout, a file, a call to a partner's API — is not covered by the
rollback.** Those are the actions that belong further up the cost curve.

## The API you should actually be calling

The good news is that you do not have to compute any of this. The execution-layer JSON-RPC block
parameter accepts, alongside a block number, the tags `latest`, `safe` and `finalized`.<Cite
src="ethereum-org-json-rpc" /> Any read you can do against a block number you can do against a tag,
which means "has this transaction finalised" is a query rather than a calculation.

This is the single most useful practical takeaway in the module: **you do not count confirmations on
Ethereum, you ask the chain which tag your block is under.**

<Aside kind="warn">
Be careful with `safe` specifically. `latest` and `finalized` have stable meanings, but the rule that
determines which block a client reports as safe is defined in the consensus specification and has
been revised — the current specification derives it from a fast confirmation rule rather than
straightforwardly from the justified checkpoint.<Cite src="consensus-specs" /> A great deal of
material written before that change describes `safe` as "the justified checkpoint" and is now
describing something else. If your policy depends on the difference, read the current spec and your
client's release notes rather than an explainer.
</Aside>

## Confirmation rules come with assumptions attached

The fast confirmation rule is worth understanding even if you never use it directly, because it makes
explicit the thing the rest of the spectrum leaves implicit.

Finalisation tolerates asynchrony: it does not need the network to behave in order to be safe, which
is why it is slow. The fast confirmation rule buys a much shorter confirmation time by *assuming*
something instead — that attestations from honest validators arrive within the slot they were created
in — and it delivers a confirmation to users who are willing to believe that assumption.<Cite
src="fcr-paper" />

The specification states the consequence in the plainest possible terms, and it is worth reading
twice: if the synchrony assumption is broken, confirmed blocks can be reorganised *without any
adversarial behaviour and without anyone being slashed*.<Cite src="consensus-specs" />

That sentence is the shape of every point on this spectrum. A confirmation is not a fact about the
world. It is a conditional: *given these assumptions about the network and the adversary, reversal
costs at least this much.* Faster confirmations are not a free improvement; they are the same claim
with more assumptions loaded into it.

There is a second reason "irreversible" is the wrong word, and it is uncomfortable enough that it
tends to be left out. Finalisation is a rule enforced by the software that participants choose to
run. A finality-reverting attack does not end with the protocol shrugging; it ends with a
coordinated human response about which chain to continue, because the alternative is accepting the
attacker's history.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> Whether you find that reassuring or
alarming, it is not a mathematical property, and any explanation that presents finality as one is
selling you something.

## The habit to unlearn

<Misconception
  belief="Waiting N confirmations is the right policy on any chain."
  reality="Counting confirmations is a heuristic from chains with probabilistic finality, where depth is the only signal available. Here there is an explicit finalised marker to query instead."
  why="The habit predates proof-of-stake finality by a decade and survived it, largely because exchange documentation and integration guides carried the numbers forward unchanged."
/>

You will encounter specific confirmation counts presented as industry standard — figures in the range
of a dozen to a few dozen blocks circulate widely. None of the sources gathered for this curriculum
traces any of those numbers to a measurement, a specification, or a stated risk model. They are
folklore with a plausible shape.

That does not make depth-based policies useless. It makes an *inherited* depth-based policy useless,
because the number encodes assumptions — about reorg depths, about attacker budget, about the value
at stake — that were somebody else's and were probably never written down.

## How long the wait actually is

Two spec constants set the floor. An epoch is 32 slots, and a slot is 12 seconds, which makes an
epoch 6 minutes 24 seconds.<Cite src="consensus-specs" /> Finalisation happens at epoch boundaries
and takes two of them, so the shortest possible wait for a transaction is around 12.8 minutes.

<Aside kind="stop">
Do not carry that figure into a product decision as an expected time. It is a floor derived from
arithmetic on two constants, and the sources disagree about what actually happens.

A transaction that lands early in an epoch waits longer than one that lands late, so the best case
alone spans a range — one peer-reviewed treatment puts it at 13 to 19 minutes depending on
submission time.<Cite src="fcr-paper" /> Separately, at least one measurement study reported a median
noticeably above the mechanism's arithmetic. The research behind this module did not reconcile those
figures and neither should you: **measure the lag against the chain you are actually using, record
the date and the method, and re-measure.** That is what one of this module's practice exercises is
for.
</Aside>

There is a further caveat that a single number can never express: finalisation is not guaranteed to
arrive on any schedule at all. Under the conditions the previous lesson described, the gap between
the head and the finalised checkpoint can grow for a long time. A policy of "wait for finalised" is
therefore also, implicitly, a policy about what your product does while waiting — and "spin
indefinitely" is a choice, not a default.

## Writing a policy you could defend

Put the pieces together into something you would be willing to show a risk reviewer. A defensible
confirmation policy has four parts, and most policies in the wild have one.

**Subgoal: name the action, not the transaction.** Policies attach to irreversible acts, not to
event types. "Withdrawal processed" is not an action; "funds leave our custody" is. Different actions
triggered by the same event can sit at different points on the spectrum, and usually should.

**Subgoal: state the price you are buying.** Not "we wait for finality" but "we act only on blocks
under a finalised checkpoint, because reversal then requires roughly a third of the total stake to be
destroyed, which exceeds the value of any single action we take". The sentence should contain the
comparison. If it does not, you have chosen a latency, not a risk posture.

**Subgoal: state what happens during the wait.** Users see a state. Which one? Is there a timeout? Is
there a manual override, and who holds it? An unfinalised chain for an hour is a product problem
before it is a protocol problem.

**Subgoal: state what changes the answer.** The obvious trigger is value: an action worth a hundred
times more deserves a re-derivation, not the same number. The less obvious ones are chain-specific —
an L2 with a different reorg profile, or a chain with no finality marker at all, invalidates the
whole policy rather than adjusting it.

A marketplace runs three flows, all triggered by the same on-chain payment event:

1. Show the buyer an "order received" screen.
2. Release a downloadable digital file to the buyer.
3. Pay the seller out to their bank account.

The team currently acts on all three when the transaction appears in the head block. Assign each flow
a point on the spectrum and say what the failure looks like if you get it wrong.

<Answer>
**1 — the head block is correct.** The action is reversible by re-rendering a screen. If the block is
reorganised, the order disappears from the UI and the buyer is confused. Waiting minutes to show a
confirmation screen is a worse product for no security gain, since nothing was given away.

**2 — depends on the file, and the honest answer is usually "not the head block".** A digital file
cannot be recalled once downloaded. The reversal cost of the *action* is total, so pairing it with the
cheapest point on the chain's cost curve is the mismatch. If the value per file is small and the
volume is high, an intermediate confirmation with its assumptions written down is defensible. What is
not defensible is the head block plus no stated reasoning.

**3 — finalised, and probably with a human in the loop above some threshold.** A bank payout leaves
the system entirely and is the most expensive thing here to undo — the fiat leg has no relationship
to chain state at all. This is the flow that justifies waiting.

The failure mode if you get it wrong is specific and worth naming: a reorg drops the payment
transaction, your indexer correctly rolls its database back to the common ancestor, and the order
row disappears. What does not roll back is the file the buyer already downloaded and the money
already sent. Modern indexing frameworks handle the database side for you, which makes this failure
*less* visible rather than less real — the state looks consistent afterwards precisely because the
irreversible side effects live outside the state.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**Finality versus confirmations** in the proof-of-stake track is this lesson with the mechanism
underneath it: you will see exactly how a checkpoint gets justified and then finalised, and where the
one-third figure comes from.<Cite src="eth2book-consensus" />

**Reorg depth in practice** turns the head-block row of the table into a measured distribution rather
than a hand-wave, which is what any depth-based policy needs to rest on.

**Transaction confirmation policy as a product decision** is the applied version, and it is where the
four-part structure above gets exercised against real flows.
