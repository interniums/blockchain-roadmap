Your replication design already survives a replica that dies. Every system you have shipped assumed
that: a node answers correctly or it stops answering, and the cure is a health check and a failover.

What does the design do about a replica that stays up, answers every request promptly, and lies?

## The assumption you have been making without noticing

Most replication you have worked with — leader election, log replication, the consensus protocols
inside a database cluster — is built for **crash faults**. The fault model is fail-stop: a process
follows the protocol until it halts, and after it halts it says nothing. A slow reply and a dead
process are hard to tell apart, and a lot of engineering goes into that distinction, but the content
of any reply that does arrive is taken at face value.

That assumption is entirely reasonable when you own every machine. The replicas run your build, on
your hardware, behind your firewall. A replica that returns a wrong answer is a bug you will fix, not
an adversary you must defeat.

Now remove the ownership. The replicas are operated by thousands of strangers, some of whom stand to
profit from the network reaching a particular conclusion. The fail-stop assumption is no longer a
simplification. It is the hole.

## Byzantine means arbitrary

The fault model that drops the assumption is the **Byzantine** one, named for the paper that framed
it as generals besieging a city who must agree on a plan while some of them are traitors.<Cite
src="lamport-byzantine-generals" /> A Byzantine component is not defined by intent. It is defined by
the absence of any constraint on its behaviour: it may do anything at all, including things that
look correct.

The framing is worth unpacking, because the useful content is in what "anything" covers:

- **Silence,** which is the crash fault — Byzantine includes it as a special case.
- **Stale answers.** A node serving results from a state it has not updated in an hour, confidently.
- **Contradictory answers.** Telling peer A that it saw block X and peer B that it saw block Y.
- **Selective correctness.** Behaving properly for every peer that might be monitoring it, and not
  for the one it wants to disadvantage.
- **Correct until the moment deviation pays.** A node with a perfect record for a year is not
  evidence of anything about the next block.

The most common real instance of this on Ethereum is mundane and involves no attacker. An operator
runs a validator, migrates it to new hardware, and briefly has two instances live with the same
signing key. Both do their job. Both sign, and the two signatures conflict. Nobody intended anything;
the fault is Byzantine anyway, and the protocol treats it as such — which strikes operators as unfair
right up until they think about how a protocol would distinguish that case from a deliberate one. It
cannot. That is the whole point of the model.

## The answer that does not work here

The instinct at this point is to remove the problem rather than tolerate it: vet the operators,
issue them credentials, and expel anyone who misbehaves. That is a real design, it is what
permissioned consortium chains do, and it is a perfectly good fit for some problems.

It does not fit this one, for a reason worth naming explicitly rather than waving at.

Expulsion needs a body with the authority to expel, and that body becomes the thing everyone must
trust — the property the system was built to avoid. Worse, admission control is also a censorship
control: whoever decides who may participate decides whose transactions can be excluded with
impunity. A chain whose validator set is curated has a different security story to tell, and it is a
story about the curator.

There is also a mechanical problem. Identity in an open network is free to manufacture. A thousand
"independent" validators can be one machine with a thousand keys, and no amount of vetting fixes
that unless the vetting is itself an authority. This is why the fault budget is denominated in
**stake** rather than in participants: stake is the one thing an open network can count that cannot
be duplicated by creating more identities.

So the design keeps the open set and pays for it by assuming the worst about its members. Everything
in the rest of this module — the thresholds, the signatures, the punishments — is the bill for that
choice.

## What changes in the design

Three things, and each of them explains a piece of Ethereum you have probably already met without
knowing why it was there.

### Every message is authenticated

Under crash faults, "node 3 tells me that node 7 reported X" is usable information. Under Byzantine
faults it is worthless: node 3 may have invented it, and node 7 may not exist.

So every consensus message carries a signature from the participant that produced it, and relaying is
safe only because the relay cannot alter the payload without invalidating the signature. This is why
the consensus layer is saturated with signature verification, and why aggregate signature schemes
matter so much for throughput — the protocol cannot take anyone's word for anything.

### Decisions need thresholds, and the threshold is not a majority

Here is where the two-thirds number that runs through everything downstream actually comes from. The
argument is short enough to carry in your head, and knowing it will stop you from misquoting the
result later.

**Subgoal: bound how long you may wait.** Up to *f* participants may be faulty, and a faulty
participant may never answer. If you insist on hearing from all *n*, you can be blocked forever. So a
decision must be reachable after hearing from *n − f*.

**Subgoal: consider two decisions made independently.** Two groups, each of size *n − f*, might each
reach a decision without having heard from the other. Two subsets of that size must overlap in at
least *n − 2f* members — that is forced by counting.

**Subgoal: require an honest witness in the overlap.** If every member of the overlap were faulty,
they could have told the first group one thing and the second group another, and the two groups could
decide differently — a safety violation. To rule that out, the overlap must contain at least one
honest participant: *n − 2f* has to exceed *f*.

**Result:** *n* must be greater than *3f*. A quorum of *n − f* is then more than two thirds of the
participants, and the fault budget is less than one third. This bound is the one that holds in the
partially synchronous setting — where the network is eventually well behaved but you cannot say when
— and it is the setting a public blockchain lives in.<Cite src="dls-partial-synchrony" />

<Aside kind="note">
It is worth being precise about what signatures do and do not buy here. In a fully synchronous model
with unforgeable signatures, agreement is possible with far more than a third faulty — that is one of
the results in the original Byzantine generals paper.<Cite src="lamport-byzantine-generals" /> The
one-third bound above is not a consequence of lacking cryptography; it comes from partial synchrony.
Signatures are doing a different job: preventing impersonation, and making misbehaviour
*attributable*. Which is the next point.
</Aside>

Ethereum does this accounting in stake rather than in nodes, for the obvious reason that node counts
are free to manufacture and stake is not. Read every two-thirds threshold in later lessons as two
thirds *of total active stake*, and every one-third fault budget the same way.

### Misbehaviour is priced, not merely tolerated

Tolerating a fault means the protocol still works despite it. Ethereum goes further for one specific
class of Byzantine behaviour: contradiction that leaves evidence.

A validator's vote is a signed message. Two conflicting signed messages from the same key are a
self-contained proof of misbehaviour that anyone can verify and anyone can publish. The consensus
specification defines exactly which pairs count — casting two different votes for the same target
epoch, or casting a vote whose source-to-target span strictly surrounds an earlier one from the same
validator.<Cite src="consensus-specs" /> Either pattern is provable from the two messages alone, with
no need to establish who did what to whom or why.

Notice which faults this can and cannot reach. A validator that simply goes offline leaves no
contradiction to prove, so it is handled by ordinary penalties for absence rather than by this
mechanism. A validator that equivocates leaves a document. The protocol punishes what it can prove.

<Aside kind="warn">
The exact penalty arithmetic is one of the places where circulating explanations have not kept up
with the protocol, and where our sources disagree with each other about the current formula. The
structural point survives the disagreement and is the part worth learning: the penalty for a slashed
validator scales with **how much other stake was slashed in the same window**, so an isolated
operator mistake and a coordinated attack by a large fraction of the network are priced very
differently.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-rewards-penalties" /> Do not carry a specific number out of
any explainer, including this one, without checking it against the current specification.
</Aside>

That design choice — price correlation, not error — follows directly from the model. The protocol
cannot tell an attacker from a misconfiguration, so it stops trying. It measures the only thing it
can observe: how many participants contradicted themselves at the same time.

Classify each of these as a crash fault, a Byzantine fault, or neither, and say what the protocol can
prove in each case.

1. A validator's host loses power for six hours.
2. A validator runs an old client version that computes attestations from a state it derived with a
   buggy transition function, and signs them.
3. A validator is behind a saturated uplink and its messages arrive too late to be counted.

<Answer>
**1 — crash fault.** The node stops and says nothing. It is a special case of Byzantine behaviour, but
nothing about it is provable as misbehaviour: absence is indistinguishable from a partition or a slow
link, so the protocol handles it through penalties for missed duties rather than through slashing.

**2 — Byzantine.** The node is up, responsive, and emitting signed messages that do not follow the
protocol. Intent is absent and irrelevant. Whether anything is *provable* depends on what the
messages contain: if the bug makes it equivocate, the contradiction is a self-contained proof and it
gets slashed. If the bug merely makes it vote for a branch nobody else supports, there is no
contradiction to publish — its votes are ignored and it loses rewards.

**3 — neither, and this is the interesting one.** The node is following the protocol exactly. What
failed is the network, not the participant. This matters because a protocol cannot tell case 3 from
case 1 from a partition — all three look like silence — and that inability is precisely the pressure
the next lesson is about.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

The **slashing conditions** you will meet in the proof-of-stake track are not an arbitrary list of
punishable offences. They are the set of Byzantine behaviours that leave a signed proof, which is why
they are exactly two, and why "was offline" is not among them.<Cite src="eth2book-consensus" />

A **threat model document** for anything you build on this should now be written in the same
vocabulary. For each dependency: does it fail by stopping, or can it fail by lying? An RPC provider
can lie. An oracle can lie. A sequencer can lie. Each needs a different mitigation, and treating a
lying dependency as a crashing one is the single most common gap in these documents.

**Sequencer failure severity** on an L2 is the same question in a specific form: a sequencer that
halts and a sequencer that reorders or censors are different incidents with different blast radii,
and the protocol-level escape hatches address only one of them.<Cite src="bft-formal-verification" />
