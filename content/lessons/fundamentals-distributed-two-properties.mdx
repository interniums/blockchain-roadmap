Two incidents, both reported in the press as "the chain broke".

In the first, block production stops. For an hour, nothing is added. Wallets show stale balances,
transactions sit unconfirmed, exchanges pause deposits. Then it resumes and everything that was
pending goes through.

In the second, block production never stops. But when the dust settles, two different versions of the
last hour have both been declared final by different parts of the network, and each contains a
transfer of the same funds to a different recipient.

These are not two severities of the same failure. They are failures of two different properties, and
which one you can survive is a property of *your product*, not of the chain. This lesson makes the
distinction precise, and then shows why a network that partitions forces a choice between them.

## The distinction is formal, not rhetorical

"Nothing bad ever happens" and "something good eventually happens" are the usual one-liners. They are
memorable and they are true, but they are not what makes the distinction load-bearing. The sharper
version comes from the classical definitions, and it is worth carrying because it tells you
immediately which kind of failure you are looking at.

Think of an execution as an infinite sequence of steps the system takes. A property is the set of
sequences that satisfy it. Then:

- A **safety** property is one that, whenever a sequence violates it, is already violated by some
  *finite prefix* of that sequence. There is a first moment at which the bad thing happened, and
  nothing that comes afterwards can repair it.
- A **liveness** property is one where *every* finite prefix can still be extended into a sequence
  that satisfies it. No finite observation ever proves it violated. There is always, in principle, a
  continuation in which the good thing finally occurs.<Cite src="alpern-schneider-liveness" />

The same work established something that justifies why protocol requirements get split this way at
all: every property is the intersection of a safety property and a liveness property.<Cite
src="alpern-schneider-liveness" /> The two categories are not a convenient taxonomy someone invented
for slides. They are exhaustive.

### What that buys you operationally

**A safety violation is a permanent, pinpointable event.** You can point at the pair of finalised
checkpoints, publish them, and no future behaviour of the network makes them un-conflict. The damage
is done at the instant of violation.

**A liveness violation is never observable.** Watch a chain produce nothing for an hour. You have not
observed a liveness violation. You have observed a long finite prefix that is entirely consistent
with the chain resuming in the next second. This is not pedantry — it is the reason the two failures
feel so different in an incident channel. Someone can prove safety broke by pasting two signatures.
Nobody can prove liveness broke, ever; they can only report that it has not happened yet.

That asymmetry propagates all the way into how the protocol defends each one. Safety is defended with
thresholds and punishments, because violations are provable and can therefore be priced. Liveness is
defended with mechanisms that keep options open — alternative proposers, forced-inclusion paths,
timeouts — because there is nothing to punish, only progress to restore.

## Ethereum's two properties, concretely

Strip the generalities and name what each one is on this chain.

**Safety.** Two conflicting checkpoints are never both finalised. "Conflicting" means neither is an
ancestor of the other — two histories that cannot both be true. This is the property that makes
"finalised" mean anything at all.<Cite src="eth2book-consensus" />

**Liveness.** Blocks continue to be produced, transactions are eventually included, and checkpoints
are eventually finalised. Three separate guarantees, in increasing order of what they require from
the network.

Now re-read the two incidents at the top. The first is a liveness failure with safety intact: nothing
conflicting was finalised, and that is *why* everything pending went through cleanly on resumption.
The second is a safety failure. Nothing about it heals.

The second misconception has real product consequences. If your transaction is not landing, the
question is not "is the chain up" — the chain is up. The question is which of the three liveness
guarantees you are actually relying on, and inclusion of *your* transaction is the weakest of them
and the only one that depends on someone else choosing to include you.

## The forced choice

Here is where the two properties stop being independent.

Set up the situation carefully, because the whole result lives in one detail. A validator sits on the
network and, over the last several minutes, has heard from only a third of the other participants.
What does that mean?

It could mean the other two thirds crashed. It could mean they are fine and a fibre cut has split the
network in two, with this validator in the smaller piece. It could mean they are fine and their
messages are merely slow. It could mean an adversary is selectively dropping traffic.

**From inside, these are the same observation.** Silence is silence. There is no message that arrives
saying "you are partitioned", because the entity that would send it is on the other side of the
partition.

So the validator must act on an ambiguous signal, and it has exactly two options:

**Option one: keep going.** Treat the missing participants as absent and continue producing and
finalising on whatever is left. This preserves liveness — the chain keeps moving regardless of how
many peers are reachable. But if the cause was a partition rather than a crash, the participants on
the other side are doing the same thing on their side, and when the partition heals there are two
finalised histories. That is a safety violation.

**Option two: wait.** Refuse to finalise without hearing from a supermajority. This preserves safety —
by construction, only one side of any partition can hold a supermajority, so only one side can
finalise anything, and the histories cannot conflict. But if the cause was a partition, the chain
stops finalising until it heals, and you have no idea when that is. That is a liveness failure.

There is no third option, because there is no additional information to act on. This is the
**availability–finality dilemma**: a protocol cannot be both live when participation fluctuates and
safe when the network partitions, because individual nodes cannot tell the two situations apart.<Cite
src="ebb-and-flow-paper" />

### The scenario, walked through

Abstractions about partitions are easy to nod along to and hard to reason with. Run one concretely.

**Subgoal: split the participants.** A transatlantic link degrades. Sixty percent of the stake ends
up on one side, forty percent on the other. Nobody on either side knows the split exists, or what the
proportions are; each side observes only that a lot of peers went quiet.

**Subgoal: watch the safety-favouring rule play out.** Under a rule that requires more than two
thirds of stake to finalise, the sixty-percent side cannot finalise — sixty is not enough. The
forty-percent side cannot either. Both sides keep producing blocks, because block production has no
supermajority requirement, so both keep serving users a moving head. Nothing conflicting is
finalised, on either side, for as long as the split lasts. Safety holds. Finality stops.

**Subgoal: watch the liveness-favouring rule play out.** Under a rule that finalises on a majority of
whoever showed up, the sixty-percent side finalises its history. So does the forty-percent side,
against its own smaller denominator. Two conflicting finalised histories now exist. When the link
recovers, there is no mechanism that reconciles them, because both satisfied the rule they were
following. Someone's funds moved twice.

**Subgoal: notice what heals and what does not.** In the first case, the link recovers and the chain
finalises again, catching up through the backlog. Users waited. In the second case, the link recovers
and nothing recovers with it. The conflict is a fact about the past.

**Subgoal: notice what neither side could have done better.** No amount of extra care on either side
helps, because neither side had the information. This is the part engineers resist longest: the
failure is not a consequence of a bad implementation, and a more careful protocol does not avoid it.
The choice is structural.

## "Eventually" is doing a lot of work

Before leaving liveness, one warning about the word at its centre.

A liveness guarantee says the good thing happens eventually, and "eventually" is unbounded by
definition — that is what makes the property unfalsifiable by any finite observation. A protocol can
satisfy liveness perfectly while making a user wait a week.

Users do not experience "eventually". They experience a duration, and their tolerance for it is set
by the action, not by the protocol. So a liveness guarantee is necessary and never sufficient for a
product decision. What you need alongside it is a distribution: not "will this land" but "what does
the ninety-ninth percentile look like, and what does my interface do in that tail".

This is why the practice exercises in this module ask you to *measure* rather than to look up. A
number you measured has a date and a method attached. A number you inherited from a tutorial has
neither, and the tutorial's number was true about a different network under different load.

## The stall is not designed to last forever

Option two above leaves an uncomfortable gap: if the safety-favouring rule waits for a supermajority
that may never return, does the chain stay stalled indefinitely?

No, and the mechanism that prevents it is a good illustration of how liveness gets defended when
punishment is unavailable.

When finalisation falls behind by more than a few epochs — the specification's threshold is a delay
of more than four — the protocol enters an **inactivity leak**.<Cite src="consensus-specs" /> During
the leak, validators that are not attesting to the chain the rest of the participants see lose
balance steadily, while participating validators do not gain. Because the thresholds are denominated
in stake, draining the absent side's balance shrinks the denominator, and after enough epochs the
participating set once again holds more than two thirds of what remains. Finalisation resumes.

Sit with what that actually does. The protocol cannot force absent validators to return, so it
redefines the electorate instead: whoever is present is eventually all that counts. On one side of a
partition, the validators on the other side are the ones being drained; on the other side, the
reverse. Run the leak on both forks for long enough and both chains finalise their own
history.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

So the safety-favouring option does not hold out indefinitely. It buys time — a stall, during which
the partition is expected to heal — and if the partition outlasts that budget, liveness is restored
on each side at the cost of the safety guarantee that spanned them. There is no rule that resolves
the resulting conflict, which is why this scenario is discussed as the point at which a decision
stops being the protocol's and becomes the community's.

<Aside kind="note">
Note the shape of the argument. It is not "the network is unreliable, so guarantees are weaker". It
is an *indistinguishability* argument: two different worlds produce identical observations, so any
rule a node follows produces identical behaviour in both, and one of those worlds punishes that
behaviour. Indistinguishability is the engine behind almost every impossibility result in distributed
systems, and once you can spot it you can predict which guarantees are available before reading the
proof.
</Aside>

## Two theorems this lesson deliberately does not use

You have met impossibility results before, and two of them will be pattern-matching against the
paragraphs above right now. Both are being left out on purpose. The reasons are worth stating,
because misapplying them is the single most common defect in blockchain explanations and you will
meet it constantly.

**The CAP theorem.** The classical statement is about a web service replicating a single data object:
you cannot simultaneously guarantee atomic consistency for that object, availability in the sense
that every request to a non-failing node gets a response, and tolerance of arbitrary message loss
between nodes.<Cite src="gilbert-lynch-cap" /> Its model has a known, fixed set of nodes; its faults
are lost messages and crashes, not participants that lie; and its "C" is linearizability of one
register, not agreement on an ordered log among mutually distrusting strangers. Import the triad
wholesale and you import all of that quietly along with it.

The result used above is a different statement, proved in a different model — Byzantine participants,
an open set, and participation that fluctuates as nodes go offline and return.<Cite
src="ebb-and-flow-paper" /> It reaches a conclusion with a similar shape, which is exactly why the
confusion is durable.

/>

**FLP.** The other one says that in a fully asynchronous system, no deterministic protocol can
guarantee that all correct processes eventually decide, if even one process may fail by crashing.<Cite
src="flp-impossibility" /> It is a real and important result, and it is not the argument here.

FLP is about *termination in an asynchronous model*, and its standard escape is to stop assuming full
asynchrony: assume the network is eventually well behaved, without knowing when, and deterministic
consensus becomes possible again.<Cite src="dls-partial-synchrony" /> That is the model every
practical protocol including Ethereum's actually assumes, and it is why the protocol is allowed to
have a slot schedule and timeouts at all. Citing FLP as the reason a chain can stall confuses the
justification for having timing assumptions with the consequences of a partition.

<Aside kind="warn">
A practical filter for any consensus explanation you read, including ones written by people who
should know better: if it invokes CAP or FLP without naming the fault model and the participation
model, the invocation is decoration. Ask which of "nodes crash", "nodes lie" and "nodes leave and
come back" the cited result actually assumes.
</Aside>

## Which property your product depends on

The engineering payoff of all this is a question you can ask about any feature you ship.

**When the chain stalls, what happens to your users?** They wait. Balances are stale, actions queue,
support tickets arrive. Unpleasant, bounded, recoverable — you can build for it with a status banner
and idempotent retries.

**When safety breaks, what happens to your users?** An action you already took on the strength of an
on-chain event turns out to have been taken on the strength of a history that no longer exists. You
shipped the goods. You credited the account. You released the file. There is no retry for that.

So the mapping is: **liveness failures cost you time; safety failures cost you the thing you gave
away.** A product whose most irreversible action is "refresh a dashboard" can be aggressive. A
product whose most irreversible action is "wire fiat to a bank account" cannot, and the next lesson
is about the specific dial that sets where you sit.

### A field guide for the incident channel

When something looks wrong on-chain, three questions separate the cases faster than any dashboard.

**Is the head advancing?** If block numbers are increasing, block production is alive and you are not
in a halt. Most reports of "the chain is down" resolve here, at your own RPC provider rather than at
the chain.

**Is the gap between the head and the finalised checkpoint growing?** A steady gap is normal
operation. A growing gap is a finalisation stall — a liveness failure of the strongest guarantee,
with safety intact. Your correct response is to stop taking irreversible actions and keep serving
reads, not to page the whole team.

**Are two different providers reporting different blocks at the same height, and are both of them
claiming finality?** That is the only one of the three that is a safety question, and it is the only
one where continuing to operate makes things worse rather than merely later.

The reason to wire these three into a runbook separately is frequency. The first is routine, the
second is rare, and the third is the one that would be unrecoverable. Teams that have not separated
the categories tend to build a single alarm for "chain problem", which fires constantly for the first
case, gets muted for exactly that reason, and would therefore be silent for the third.

<Answer>
**1 — a liveness failure, and only of the third guarantee.** Blocks are being produced and
transactions are being included; what has stalled is finalisation. Safety is untouched: nothing
conflicting was finalised, because nothing was finalised. The evidence is the gap between the head
and the finalised checkpoint, which any node will report. Note that this is exactly the state option
two above puts you in, and it is the intended behaviour rather than a malfunction.

**2 — a liveness limitation, not a safety failure, and not even a protocol failure.** The affected
transactions are not being included, which is the weakest of the three liveness guarantees and the
one that depends on someone else's choice. Nothing conflicting is finalised. The evidence is
comparative: the same transactions land when other proposers get a turn. This is why the defences
against censorship are inclusion mechanisms rather than punishments — there is no signed
contradiction to slash.

**3 — a safety failure, and the most instructive of the three.** Two conflicting histories both
advanced, and if both sides finalised anything, both cannot be true. The evidence is a pair of
conflicting blocks each with a supermajority behind it on its own side.

The instructive part is the cause. No adversary was involved and no threshold was violated. The
partition was not in the network — it was in the transition function, which is the failure the first
lesson in this module was about. Safety depends on every honest participant computing the same thing;
a client bug removes the "same".
</Answer>

## Where this pays off

**Accountable safety** is the reason the safety half of this is worth so much: on Ethereum, a safety
violation is not only detectable but attributable, so the protocol can name and punish the
participants responsible. That is a stronger claim than "safety holds", and the next two lessons
build it.

**Finality versus confirmations** is this lesson with the numbers filled in. Once you know that
liveness and safety are the axes, "how long do I wait" becomes "how much safety am I buying, and at
what latency cost".

**Forced inclusion** and the **inactivity leak** are the two mechanisms that address the liveness
side directly — one for a chain that will not include you, one for a chain that will not finalise.
Both look strange until you have seen that liveness cannot be defended by punishment.<Cite
src="ethereum-org-pos" />

And on the scaling track, **rollup versus validium** and **data availability** turn out to be this
same dilemma one layer up, with the same indistinguishability argument at their heart: you cannot
tell "the data is withheld" from "the data is late".

The next lesson takes the safety axis and stops treating it as a boolean.
