The last lesson used three words — head, justified, finalised — as though they were three readings
from one instrument. They are not.

Ethereum's consensus is two protocols running at the same time, over the same blocks, designed to do
opposite jobs. One of them must produce an answer right now and is allowed to change its mind. The
other may take its time and must never change its mind. Neither is usable alone, and almost every
confusing thing about Ethereum consensus comes from reading a statement about one of them as though
it were about the other.

The Ethereum Foundation's own name for the arrangement, in the post that introduced it, was two
ghosts in a trench coat.<Cite src="two-ghosts-trench-coat" /> The combination has a formal name,
Gasper, and a paper that analyses it as a single object.<Cite src="gasper-paper" />

## The division of labour

Go back to the two properties.

**Liveness needs a component that always has an answer.** At every moment, a node has to be able to
say which block is the current tip — to serve a balance query, to build the next block on top of
something, to tell a wallet whether a transaction landed. This component can never stall, so it must
be able to decide from whatever messages it happens to have. That means it can be wrong, and it means
two honest nodes can briefly disagree.

**Safety needs a component that is allowed to stall.** Deciding that something is settled forever is
exactly the decision you must not make on partial information. This component needs a supermajority
and must refuse to proceed without one, which means it will sometimes produce nothing for a while.

Those requirements are contradictory in one component and complementary in two. So Ethereum has two:
a **fork-choice rule** called LMD-GHOST that always names a head, and a **finality gadget** called
Casper FFG that occasionally declares a checkpoint settled.<Cite src="ethereum-org-gasper" />

## LMD-GHOST: the component that always answers

The name unpacks into the whole algorithm: **L**atest **M**essage **D**riven **G**reediest
**H**eaviest **O**bserved **S**ub**T**ree.

Read it backwards. It walks a tree of blocks, choosing at each step the subtree that is heaviest by
some measure, using only the observations this node has actually made, and counting only each
validator's most recent message.

### What it computes

The specification's procedure is short enough to describe exactly.<Cite src="consensus-specs" />

**Subgoal: pick a starting point that cannot be reconsidered.** The walk begins at the block of the
current justified checkpoint, not at genesis. Everything below that point is settled enough that the
fork choice does not revisit it, which is the first place the two protocols touch: the finality
gadget's output is the fork choice's root.

**Subgoal: score each candidate branch.** For a given block, its weight is the sum of the effective
balances of every active, unslashed validator whose latest attestation supports that block or a
descendant of it. Validators known to have equivocated are excluded from the count entirely.

**Subgoal: descend greedily.** Among the children of the current block, take the one with the greatest
weight. Repeat. When a block has no children, that block is the head.

**Subgoal: notice what is being summed.** Not blocks, and not chain length — stake. A branch with one
block and most of the validators attesting to it beats a branch with ten blocks and few attesters.

### A worked descent

Take a small tree. `J` is the justified checkpoint's block, so the walk starts there. The number
beside each block is the total effective balance of validators whose *latest* attestation names that
exact block.

**Subgoal: turn per-block votes into subtree weights.** A block's weight is everything cast for it
plus everything cast for its descendants. So B2 carries 5 + 30 + 25 = 60, and B3 carries 0 + 10 + 28
= 38.

**Subgoal: descend one level at a time.** From B1 the children are B2 at 60 and B3 at 38. Take B2.
From B2 the children are B4 at 30 and B5 at 25. Take B4. B4 has no children, so B4 is the head.

**Subgoal: read what did not matter.** The branch through B7 is four blocks deep; the winning head is
two. Depth was never consulted. Had the 28 units of stake behind B7 moved their latest attestation to
B5, B5's subtree would be 53 against B4's 30, and the head would move — with no new blocks produced
at all. Weight comes from votes, and votes are revocable in the sense that a newer one replaces an
older one.

**Subgoal: notice why two nodes can differ.** Now suppose the attestations totalling 30 for B4 have
reached one node and not yet another. The second node computes B2 as 5 + 25 = 30, against B3 at 38.
It descends into B3, then B6, then B7, and reports B7 as the head — four blocks away from what the
first node reports.

Neither node is faulty. Neither broke a rule. They hold different subsets of the same message stream,
and the rule is defined over what has been observed. When the missing attestations arrive, the second
node recomputes, switches to B4, and everything it had built on B7 is discarded. That is the locality
discussed below, in arithmetic.

### Why "latest message"

Validators attest repeatedly — every epoch, forever. If the rule counted every attestation a
validator ever cast, a validator's old votes would keep pulling toward branches it has long since
abandoned, and the weight of a branch would reflect history rather than current opinion.

Counting only the most recent message from each validator makes the weight a snapshot of what the
validator set believes *now*. It also caps the influence of each validator at one vote regardless of
how prolific it has been, which matters when some participants are adversarial.

### Why the head is local, and why that is not a bug

Here is the consequence that surprises people, and it follows directly from "observed".

The weight a node computes depends on which attestations that node has received. Attestations
propagate through a peer-to-peer network with real latency. Two honest nodes, at the same instant,
can hold slightly different sets of messages and therefore compute slightly different heads.

This is not a malfunction being tolerated. It is what the design is for. A rule that required global
agreement before naming a head would be a rule that could stall, and stalling is the one thing this
component is not allowed to do. **A short reorganisation is the visible signature of a fork choice
doing its job**: two nodes disagreed for a moment, more attestations arrived, and the disagreement
resolved.<Cite src="eth2book-lmd-ghost" />

<Aside kind="note">
The fork choice also includes a bonus for the proposer of a block delivered on time — a temporary
addition to that block's weight while its slot is current, which decays afterwards. It exists because
the locality above turned out to be exploitable: an adversary able to time message delivery can
present different nodes with different pictures and keep the honest ones split. The boost gives a
timely block enough weight to break that kind of tie.<Cite src="consensus-specs" /> The exact constant
is in the fork-choice specification and has been changed before; look it up rather than quoting one
from memory.
</Aside>

## Casper FFG: the component that is allowed to wait

The finality gadget works on a much coarser grain. It ignores individual blocks and reasons only
about **checkpoints**: the block at the boundary of each epoch.<Cite src="eth2book-casper-ffg" />

### Votes are links, not endorsements

An FFG vote does not say "this checkpoint is good". It names a pair: a **source** checkpoint the
validator already considers justified, and a **target** checkpoint it is voting to justify. The vote
is a claim about a link between two points in the chain's history.

**Justification.** When votes for the same link, cast by validators holding at least two thirds of
the total active balance, are recorded, the target checkpoint becomes justified.<Cite
src="consensus-specs" />

**Finalisation.** A justified checkpoint becomes finalised when the checkpoint immediately after it
is justified by a link that starts from it. In the ordinary case that is one epoch later, which is
where the two-epoch floor from the last lesson comes from.<Cite src="casper-ffg-paper" />

The layering is worth stating plainly: justification is a supermajority saying "this is where we
are", and finalisation is a supermajority saying it again from the new position. The second statement
is what turns the first into something reversal cannot touch without producing evidence.

### The two rules that make safety accountable

FFG defines exactly two prohibited voting patterns, and the specification states them as one
predicate over any two attestations from the same validator.<Cite src="consensus-specs" />

- **Double vote.** Two different votes with the same target epoch. A validator gets one vote per
  epoch; casting two is a contradiction on its face.
- **Surround vote.** A vote whose source-to-target span strictly surrounds the span of an earlier
  vote from the same validator. This is the subtler one: it is how a validator would try to walk back
  a previous commitment without literally repeating an epoch.

Both are checkable from the two signed messages alone. No context, no accusation, no investigation —
the pair *is* the proof, and anyone who holds it can submit it.

The surround rule repays a concrete example, because it is the one that catches an attacker being
careful.

Suppose a validator votes for the link from epoch 4 to epoch 5, and epoch 5 duly gets justified. Some
time later, that validator wants to support a different history — one in which epoch 5 went a
different way. It cannot cast a second vote with target epoch 5; that is a double vote, and the
double-vote rule catches it immediately. So instead it votes for a link from epoch 3 to epoch 8: a
span that starts before its earlier source and ends after its earlier target.

That vote never repeats an epoch. It looks, in isolation, like an ordinary vote from a validator that
was simply behind. What gives it away is the relationship between the two spans: 3-to-8 strictly
surrounds 4-to-5. A validator claiming both is claiming that epoch 5 was the right target *and* that
the history containing epoch 5 can be skipped over. The two claims cannot both be honest, and no
narrative about network conditions rescues them.

The pair of rules is designed to be exhaustive over the ways a vote can contradict an earlier one, so
that the counting argument below has no gaps to exploit.

This yields the property that the whole arrangement exists for. If two conflicting checkpoints are
ever both finalised, then validators holding at least a third of the total stake must have broken one
of those two rules, and the evidence of it is publishable by anyone.<Cite src="casper-ffg-paper" />
That is **accountable safety**, and it is a much stronger claim than "safety holds": it converts a
safety violation from an unattributable disaster into an identified set of participants whose stake
can be destroyed.

### Why the gadget works at epoch grain

An obvious question at this point: if finality is what everyone wants, why not run the gadget every
slot and finalise every block?

The answer is message volume, and it is worth seeing because it explains the entire slot-and-epoch
structure that otherwise looks like arbitrary scheduling.

A supermajority decision requires hearing from more than two thirds of the validator set. That set is
very large, and every one of its members' votes is a signed message that has to cross a peer-to-peer
network and be verified. Asking all of them to vote on every block, in every slot, is not a tuning
problem; it is far beyond what the network can carry.

So the set is partitioned. Each validator is assigned to exactly one committee in each epoch, and
each committee attests during exactly one slot of that epoch.<Cite src="consensus-specs" /> Spread
over the epoch's slots, the whole validator set votes once, and the votes for the epoch's target
checkpoint accumulate across all of those slots rather than arriving at once.

That single design decision produces both grains:

- **The fork choice runs at slot grain**, because it only needs whatever attestations have arrived so
  far, and each slot brings one committee's worth.
- **The gadget runs at epoch grain**, because that is how long it takes for the whole set to have had
  a turn, and a supermajority of the whole set is exactly what it needs.<Cite
  src="ethereum-org-attestations" />

Read the two-epoch finality floor again with that in mind. It is not a safety margin someone chose
conservatively. It is the time required to collect two full rounds of votes from the entire validator
set — one to justify, one to confirm the justification from the new position — using a mechanism in
which each slot brings in only one of the epoch's 32 committees.<Cite src="consensus-specs" />

The trade-off it encodes is the one this whole module has been circling. A smaller validator set would
finalise faster and be cheaper to capture. A larger one is harder to capture and slower to poll. The
twelve-plus minutes is what that choice costs, and the fork choice exists to give users something to
look at while the bill is being paid.

## One message, two protocols

Here is the detail that makes the arrangement click, and it is a single data structure.

A validator's attestation carries, in one signed container: the slot, the committee index, a
**beacon block root**, and a **source** and **target** checkpoint pair.<Cite src="consensus-specs" />

The beacon block root is the LMD-GHOST vote — this validator's opinion about the head. The source and
target are the Casper FFG vote — this validator's opinion about what should be justified. One
message, one signature, one gossip transmission, two protocols served.<Cite
src="ethereum-org-attestations" />

That is not merely an optimisation. It ties the two protocols together: a validator cannot support
one branch with its fork-choice vote and a conflicting checkpoint with its finality vote without the
inconsistency being visible in a single signed object.

<Check type="explain" concept="casper-ffg">
Why can Ethereum not drop the fork choice and run Casper FFG alone? It has the supermajority
threshold, the slashing conditions and the accountable-safety property — everything the safety story
needs.

<Answer>
Because FFG votes are votes *about a chain*, and something has to produce one.

Work through what FFG needs as input. A validator casting a vote must name a target checkpoint, which
is the block at an epoch boundary — so blocks must already exist, and there must be an agreed answer
to "which block is at this boundary". If two candidate blocks sit at the boundary, FFG has no
machinery to choose between them; validators would split their votes across both, neither would reach
two thirds, and nothing would ever be justified. FFG resolves disagreement by threshold, not by
selection, and a threshold applied to a split vote produces silence.

There is a second reason, and it is the one that matters to users. Even with the gadget working
perfectly, finalisation happens at epoch boundaries and takes two of them. During the twelve-plus
minutes in between, someone has to answer "what is the current state" — for every RPC call, every
wallet balance, every block being built. FFG has nothing to say during that window. It is silent by
design.

So the fork choice is not a helper for the gadget. It supplies liveness, which is a property FFG does
not have and was never intended to have.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Ebb and flow: two ledgers from the same blocks

Now assemble the pieces and look at what a client can offer an application.

From the same tree of blocks, two ledgers are derived.

**The available chain** is what the fork choice outputs. It extends under partition, under
asynchrony, under fluctuating participation — there is always a head, because the rule decides from
whatever has been observed. It can reorganise.

**The finalised chain** is what the gadget outputs. It stops advancing when a supermajority is not
reachable. It does not reorganise without a provable, punishable violation.

The finalised chain is always a prefix of the available chain. Under normal conditions it trails by a
couple of epochs. Under a partition it falls further behind, and when the network heals it catches
up. That rising and falling gap is the **ebb and flow**, and it is the reason the previous lesson's
dilemma did not force Ethereum to pick a side: the protocol declines to choose and hands both ledgers
to the application.<Cite src="ebb-and-flow-paper" />

Which one you should read is not a matter of preference. It follows from what your action costs to
undo, which is exactly the policy question the last lesson set out.

## What is honestly known about whether this works

This is the point at which most explanations stop, having described a tidy machine. The tidy version
would leave you unprepared for the literature, so here is the state of it.

The paper that named the ebb-and-flow shape did so partly in order to ask whether Gasper achieves it.
Its answer was no: the authors exhibited an attack in the standard synchronous network model, framed
it as a general difficulty with finality-gadget designs of this kind, and then presented a *different*
construction — take snapshots of a dynamically available chain and feed them into an off-the-shelf
BFT protocol — which they proved secure with optimal resilience.<Cite src="ebb-and-flow-paper" />

Ethereum did not adopt that construction. The fork choice has been patched instead, which is where
the proposer boost mentioned earlier comes from. Later work argues that LMD-GHOST in its current form
remains brittle, that the patches are patches, and proposes a structurally similar replacement
designed to be a drop-in.<Cite src="goldfish-paper" />

<Aside kind="warn">
So calibrate the claim correctly. "Ethereum is an ebb-and-flow protocol" is an accurate description
of its *shape*: two ledgers, one available, one finalised, the second a prefix of the first. It is
not a citation of a proof that the deployed protocol is secure in that model. The fork-choice rule is
an area of live research and has changed in response to attacks more than once — which is why this
lesson is marked as evolving material, and why the module's concepts around it carry the same mark.
</Aside>

None of this should read as instability for its own sake. The finality half has been remarkably
stable: the voting rules, the two slashing conditions and the accountable-safety argument are
essentially what the original paper described.<Cite src="casper-ffg-paper" /> It is the fork choice —
the liveness half, the one that must answer immediately from incomplete information — that keeps
attracting attacks and revisions. That asymmetry is itself the module's thesis restated: the
component that is allowed to stall is the one that is easy to make safe.

<Check type="recall" concept="ebb-and-flow">
A monitoring dashboard shows: the head advancing normally every slot, the justified checkpoint
advancing every epoch, and the finalised checkpoint four epochs behind the head and holding steady at
that distance.

Is anything wrong? Which of the two protocols is running, and what would you expect the dashboard to
look like during a genuine partition?

<Answer>
Nothing is wrong. Both protocols are running normally.

The finalised checkpoint trailing the head by a couple of epochs is the ordinary state of affairs —
finalisation requires two epoch boundaries after the fact, so a steady gap of a few epochs is the
mechanism working, not falling behind. The signal to watch is not the size of the gap but whether it
is *stable*. A steady gap means the gadget is keeping pace. A growing gap means it is not.

Under a genuine partition, the shape changes distinctly: the head would keep advancing, because the
fork choice always produces an answer from whatever it has observed, while the justified and
finalised checkpoints would stop moving, because no side of the partition holds two thirds of the
stake. That divergence — an available chain that keeps going and a finalised chain that stops — is
the ebb-and-flow behaviour made visible, and it is precisely the moment when a product that acts on
the head is exposed and a product that acts on the finalised tag is not.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

You now have the vocabulary the protocol track uses without introduction.

**Attestations, slots and epochs** stop being scheduling trivia. An attestation is the message that
carries both votes; a slot is the fork choice's grain; an epoch is the gadget's grain. The whole
validator duty cycle is these two protocols asking for input on their own schedules.

**Proposer boost, reorg depth and the late-block reorg rules** are all patches on the locality of the
fork choice, and they make sense only once you know the head is a local computation over observed
messages.

**Finality versus confirmations** and **transaction confirmation policy** now have a mechanism
underneath them. When you write down that acting on a finalised block means an attacker must destroy
a third of the stake, you can say why: the two slashing conditions, and the counting argument that
says conflicting finalisation implies a third of the stake violated one of them.<Cite
src="eth2book-consensus" />

**Rollups** inherit all of this. An L2 that settles to Ethereum inherits Ethereum's finality
timeline, not a better one, and a soft confirmation from a sequencer sits below every rung on the
spectrum you have just learned. The scaling track leans on that fact constantly.

The module's reflection prompt asks you to write a confirmation policy you would defend to a risk
reviewer. You now have everything you need to make it a real argument rather than a number: the two
properties, the price of each rung, the mechanism that sets the price, and an honest account of what
is proved and what is merely current practice.
