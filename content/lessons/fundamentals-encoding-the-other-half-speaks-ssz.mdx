Everything in the previous lesson was true about the execution layer. A running Ethereum node is two
processes, and the other one does not use RLP at all.

The consensus layer — the beacon chain, its state, the objects validators sign — is encoded with SSZ,
Simple Serialize. It replaced RLP everywhere on that side, with one deliberate
exception.<Cite src="ethereum-org-ssz" />

The exception is worth naming precisely, because it is the one place both layers meet. Node discovery
runs on discv5, whose wire messages are RLP,<Cite src="devp2p-discv5-wire" /> and whose node records
are the RLP structure defined by EIP-778.<Cite src="eip-778" /> Discovery is shared infrastructure
that predates the beacon chain and serves both halves, so it kept the older format.

## What RLP could not do

RLP tells you how to turn a structure into bytes. It says nothing about how to hash it. On the
execution layer that decision is made separately and per structure — the state trie has its own
hashing scheme layered on top of RLP-encoded nodes.

The consensus layer has a requirement that arrangement serves badly. A beacon state is large, it
changes every slot, and clients need to prove *one field* of it — a validator's balance, a sync
committee, a block root — to someone who will never download the rest. Under a
serialize-then-hash-somehow design, every such proof needs bespoke code that knows where that field
landed.

SSZ's answer is to make hashing part of the type definition. For every SSZ type the specification
defines both how it serialises and how it Merkleizes, so the shape of the Merkle tree is a
consequence of the schema rather than a separate design.<Cite src="ethereum-org-ssz" /><Cite src="ssz-specs-merkleization" />
The stated design goals are exactly those two properties: deterministic, and efficient to
Merkleize.<Cite src="ethereum-org-ssz" /> That single decision explains most of what follows.

## The type system

Where RLP has two types, SSZ has a catalogue: booleans, unsigned integers at declared widths from 8
up to 256 bits, fixed-length `Vector` and variable-length `List` (with a declared maximum),
byte-oriented and bit-oriented versions of both, and `Container` for a named-field
struct.<Cite src="ssz-specs" />

Two consequences follow immediately.

**Integers are little-endian.**<Cite src="ssz-specs" /><Cite src="ethereum-org-ssz" /> The opposite
of every execution-layer value you have seen so far. The convention is total: it covers the payload
integers, and it covers the machinery too — the offsets that locate variable-length fields are
`uint32` values in little-endian byte order, four bytes
each.<Cite src="ssz-specs-base" /><Cite src="ssz-specs-container" /> This is the endianness split
from the first lesson, and it is where it bites.

**Nothing is self-describing.** The bytes carry no type tags and no field names. A decoder that does
not already hold the schema cannot parse the data at all — it cannot even find the field
boundaries.<Cite src="ethereum-org-ssz" />

## Layout: fixed part, then a heap

Fixed-size fields are written in place, in declaration order. A variable-size field cannot be written
in place, so its slot holds an **offset** and its real bytes go into a heap after the fixed
part.<Cite src="ethereum-org-ssz" /> The offset counts from the start of the serialized
object.<Cite src="ssz-specs-container" />

Take a container of a `uint64`, a `List` of `uint8`, and another `uint64`, holding 1, `[1,2,3,4]`
and 2:

```text
0100000000000000    a: uint64 = 1, little-endian, 8 bytes
14000000            offset for b: 0x14 = 20 = width of the fixed part
0200000000000000    c: uint64 = 2
01020304            heap: the four elements of b
```

**Step 1 — size the fixed part from the types.** Eight bytes for `a`, four for the offset standing in
for `b`, eight for `c`. Twenty bytes, known before any value is read.

**Step 2 — the first offset must equal that width.** `0x14` is 20. The heap begins exactly where the
fixed part ends, and a decoder that finds any other value there rejects the
object.<Cite src="ssz-specs-container" />

**Step 3 — a payload runs to the next offset.** `b` has no length field of its own; its extent is the
distance to the following variable field's offset, or to the end of the object. The length is
recovered from the layout, not stored.<Cite src="ssz-specs-container" />

Compare that with ABI encoding. The idea is identical — heads with pointers, tails with payloads —
and every constant is different. ABI offsets are 32 bytes, big-endian, and every field is padded to a
word; SSZ offsets are 4 bytes, little-endian, and nothing is
padded.<Cite src="ssz-specs-base" /> Two teams solving one problem, twice, with opposite defaults.

## Merkleization is not hashing the bytes

Here is where SSZ stops resembling a serialization format.

Every SSZ type has a **hash tree root**. It is computed like this: the value's data is packed into
32-byte chunks; the chunk count is rounded up to a power of two with all-zero chunks; and pairs are
hashed upward with SHA-256 until one root remains.<Cite src="ssz-specs-merkleization" /> A container
is Merkleized over the roots of its fields, not over its serialized bytes. A variable-length list
additionally **mixes in its length** — one final hash of the data root together with a 32-byte
little-endian word holding the element count — so two lists that differ only in length cannot share a
root.<Cite src="ssz-specs-merkleization" />

Note the hash function: SHA-256.<Cite src="ssz-specs-merkleization" /> The execution layer hashes
with keccak256.<Cite src="yellow-paper" /> Two encodings, two byte orders, two hash functions, one
protocol.

### The tree is sized by the type, not by the data

This is the detail that makes everything else work, and it is the one most write-ups skip.

A `List` declares a maximum. Its Merkle tree is padded to the depth that maximum needs, regardless
of how many elements it currently holds — a single eight-byte element under a 1024-element limit
spans 256 chunks and costs eight hashes rather than one.<Cite src="ssz-specs-collections" /> The
padding is not materialised: the specification substitutes pre-computed roots of all-zero subtrees,
so the work is proportional to the real data even though the tree's *shape* is proportional to the
declared capacity.<Cite src="ssz-specs-merkleization" />

Why pay for depth you are not using? Because a field's position in the tree must not move when the
data changes. If the tree grew with the element count, adding a validator would relocate every
existing leaf, and every proof anyone had already issued would stop verifying.

That fixed shape is what gives every field a computable address: its **generalized index**, numbering
the root 1 and counting left to right along each level, so that a node's index is
`2 ** depth + index-in-row`.<Cite src="ethereum-org-ssz" /> A proof for any field is then the sibling
hashes along its path, and one generic verifier handles every field of every type. No per-field proof
code, which is what makes light clients verifying beacon state practical at
all.<Cite src="ethereum-org-ssz" />

### Two types, the same bytes, different trees

The cleanest proof that serialization and Merkleization really are independent is a pair of types
that share one and differ in the other.

EIP-7916 introduces `ProgressiveList`, a variable-length sequence with **no declared capacity**. It
encodes exactly like a bounded `List` — fixed-size elements packed back to back, variable-size
elements behind an offset table — so the two produce **the same bytes, and only their Merkle trees
differ**.<Cite src="ssz-specs-collections" /> Instead of padding to a capacity, the progressive shape
grows the tree with the data, which costs fewer hashes for short lists and, more importantly, assigns
a given chunk index the same generalized index however many elements follow
it.<Cite src="eip-7916" /> Stability without having to guess a maximum up front. The EIP is in Review
rather than Final, so treat the type as real but not yet settled.<Cite src="eip-7916" />

EIP-7495's `ProgressiveContainer` does the same thing for structs: fields keep their tree positions
as the field set changes across versions, so a layout outlives any one version of the
struct.<Cite src="eip-7495" /><Cite src="ssz-specs-container" /> To make that safe it mixes the
active-field layout into the root as a 32-byte word, in the same way a list mixes in its
length.<Cite src="ssz-specs-merkleization" /> The reason is worth reading twice: a field that has
been removed and a field that holds zero both leave a zero leaf, so the leaf tree alone cannot
separate them, and a version that dropped a field would otherwise root exactly like one that zeroed
it.<Cite src="ssz-specs-merkleization" />

That is the same argument as the packed-encoding lesson, moved from bytes to trees. Two different
meanings must not produce one digest, and the fix is to mix in the thing that distinguishes them.

<Answer>
**One — the tree is not a hash of a byte string.** The root is built from the field roots: each field
is Merkleized on its own, the results are paired and hashed upward, and zero chunks pad the level to
a power of two — for a bounded list, all the way to the depth its declared maximum
needs.<Cite src="ssz-specs-merkleization" /><Cite src="ssz-specs-collections" /> Those zero chunks
are never present in the serialized bytes, so the two computations have different inputs even for the
same value. Note the size of that gap: the serialized form of a four-element list is a few dozen
bytes, while its tree may be hundreds of chunks wide because the *type* said so.

**Two — the list's length is mixed in separately.** A variable-length list finishes with an extra
hash of its data root against a 32-byte length word.<Cite src="ssz-specs-merkleization" /> In the
serialized form, that same length is implicit in the distance between offsets. The information is
present in both, but it enters the digest by completely different routes.

The deeper objection is what the shortcut throws away. A digest over the flat byte string proves only
that you hold the whole object. The hash tree root lets someone who holds nothing but the root verify
one field against it, at a generalized index computable from the schema
alone.<Cite src="ethereum-org-ssz" /> That is the entire reason SSZ defines Merkleization in the
first place, and it is exactly the capability the shortcut deletes.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What is moving

Two things make this an evolving topic rather than a settled one, and both matter if you are reading
older material.

**The specification has moved house and grown.** SSZ is now maintained as its own reference
implementation repository rather than as a document inside the consensus specs, and its type
catalogue has grown beyond the classic set — progressive lists,<Cite src="eip-7916" /> a progressive
container whose fields keep their tree positions as the field set changes,<Cite src="eip-7495" /> and
a union type constrained to share one tree shape.<Cite src="ssz-specs" /> If you learned SSZ from a
2022 write-up, the list of types is no longer complete.

**Execution-layer SSZ is proposed, not shipped.** The door was left open on purpose: EIP-2718 made
the transaction payload an opaque byte array precisely so that a future type could use a different
encoding, and it names SSZ first among the possibilities.<Cite src="eip-2718" /> Two drafts walk
through that door — EIP-6404 defines SSZ transactions, and EIP-6493 defines a signature scheme for
natively-SSZ transactions built on `hash_tree_root`.<Cite src="eip-6404" /><Cite src="eip-6493" />
Both were still in **Draft** status when this lesson was verified, which is the honest summary:
designed in detail, not adopted. Treat any claim that a particular execution object has already moved
as needing a fresh check against the execution specs — that is a live area, and secondary sources
describe proposals and shipped changes in the same tone.

The draft is worth one look regardless of whether it ships, because of what it does about
ambiguity. EIP-6493 mixes a `DomainType` into the signing root, stating the purpose directly:
to uniquely identify the underlying specification and avoid hash collisions across different
signature kinds, with vendor-defined networks required to use a different
`DomainType`.<Cite src="eip-6493" /> Third format, third syntax, same move — `0x19` in EIP-191, the
type byte in EIP-2718, a domain in the tree here.

## Where this pays off

The layer split is the shape of a node: two processes, two encodings, talking over a defined
interface. Once that is concrete, the two-process architecture stops being trivia and starts
explaining why certain data is awkward to get at from the other side.

SSZ Merkleization is the machinery behind light-client proofs against beacon state — and now you know
why those proofs keep working across upgrades, which is the capacity-sized tree and the stable
generalized index rather than anything about the hash function.<Cite src="ssz-specs-collections" /><Cite src="ethereum-org-ssz" />
The same chunk-and-Merkleize construction reappears in data availability, where a commitment has to
be checkable by someone sampling a fraction of the data.

Little-endian is the detail that will bite you first, and this is where you were warned. The next
lesson takes the property both formats have been quietly leaning on — that a value has exactly one
encoding — and shows what happens when it fails.
