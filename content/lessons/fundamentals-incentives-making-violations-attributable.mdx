A price only exists if there is a mechanism that charges someone. Saying "an attack costs a third of
the stake" is a fantasy unless the protocol can identify *whose* stake, from evidence, without a
trusted observer deciding who was at fault.

So before any of the arithmetic: **how does a distributed system turn a safety failure into a bill
addressed to a specific party?**

## Detection is easy. Attribution is the design problem.

Noticing that two conflicting checkpoints have both finalised is trivial — anyone holding both sees
a contradiction immediately. That tells you the system failed. It does not tell you who broke it,
and a system that cannot answer that question has no way to charge anyone.

Casper FFG's voting rules are built so that the answer is forced. The property is called
**accountable safety**: if two conflicting checkpoints are both finalised, then at least one third
of the total stake must have signed a pair of messages that contradict each other under the slashing
conditions.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos" /><Cite src="eth2book-casper-ffg" /> The original paper
introduces it as one of the system's proved properties, alongside plausible liveness, and the
combination of Casper FFG with the fork-choice rule Ethereum actually runs is analysed under the name
Gasper, which proves safety together with liveness results under different
assumptions.<Cite src="casper-ffg-paper" /><Cite src="gasper-paper" />

The counting argument is short enough to carry in your head.

**Subgoal 1 — recall what finalisation costs in votes.** Finalising a checkpoint requires a
supermajority: at least two thirds of the total stake voting for it.<Cite src="eth2book-casper-ffg" />

**Subgoal 2 — assume the failure.** Two conflicting checkpoints both finalise. Each of them
independently drew at least two thirds of the stake.

**Subgoal 3 — count.** Two thirds plus two thirds is four thirds of the stake's worth of votes. There
is only one stake. So at least one third of it appears on both sides.

**Subgoal 4 — read the consequence.** That third did not merely fail. It *signed* for both
outcomes — two positions that cannot both be honestly held. The messages are public, and each one
carries the signature of the validator that produced it.

That is the whole trick, and it is worth appreciating how unusual it is as an engineering move. The
supermajority threshold is not chosen only for robustness against faults. It is chosen so that a
safety violation is *arithmetically impossible* without a large, identifiable set of
self-incriminating signatures existing in the open. The protocol does not detect misbehaviour; it
arranges for misbehaviour to leave a receipt.

<Aside kind="note">
Notice the direction of the guarantee, because it is easy to read backwards. Accountable safety does
not say "if you misbehave you will be caught". It says "if the system's safety property is broken,
then evidence naming at least a third of the stake necessarily exists". A validator who signs one
contradictory pair while the chain never actually forks is equally catchable — the evidence is the
same object. What the threshold buys is the guarantee that a *successful* attack cannot be quiet.
</Aside>

## The two slashing conditions

The contradictions are defined precisely, and the definition is short enough to read in full. In the
consensus specification, `is_slashable_attestation_data` is the entire adjudication
procedure:<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" />

```python
def is_slashable_attestation_data(data_1: AttestationData, data_2: AttestationData) -> bool:
    """
    Check if ``data_1`` and ``data_2`` are slashable according to Casper FFG rules.
    """
    return (
        # Double vote
        (data_1 != data_2 and data_1.target.epoch == data_2.target.epoch)
        or
        # Surround vote
        (data_1.source.epoch < data_2.source.epoch and data_2.target.epoch < data_1.target.epoch)
    )
```

- **Double vote** — two different attestations with the same target epoch. You voted twice for what
  should have been one decision.
- **Surround vote** — one attestation whose source-to-target span strictly surrounds another's.
  Casting both is claiming a history that contains and contradicts a history you already claimed.

Read the signature of that function rather than its body. It takes two pieces of attestation data
and nothing else — no chain state, no timestamps, no view of the network, no notion of who is
currently honest. Every input is already inside the evidence. That is what makes the verdict
reproducible by anyone, and it is the property the first lesson of this module called *evidence*.

### Why there are two conditions rather than one

The double vote is intuitive: two votes for the same decision. The surround vote is the one people
skip, and skipping it hides why the pair is *sufficient* rather than merely reasonable.

Every attestation carries two checkpoints — a **source**, the last checkpoint the validator considers
justified, and a **target**, the checkpoint it is voting to justify next. The pair is a claim about
an interval of history, not a point.

**Subgoal — construct the cheat the double-vote rule alone would allow.** A validator attests with
source at epoch 20 and target at epoch 21, joining the honest chain. Later it attests with source at
epoch 10 and target at epoch 30. The target epochs differ, so this is not a double vote. But the
second claim spans right across the first: it asserts that nothing between 10 and 30 was settled,
while the validator has already sworn that 21 was justified from 20.

Without the surround rule, that pair would be free — and it is exactly the move needed to abandon one
finalised branch and justify a competing one, because it lets a validator retroactively deny the
interval it previously endorsed. The condition catches it with the simplest possible test: one
interval strictly containing the other.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" />

Two conditions, both decidable from two signed messages, and together they are enough to force the
counting argument at the top of this lesson to have somewhere to land.<Cite src="casper-ffg-paper" />

The block-level analogue has the same shape: signing two different beacon block headers for the same
slot is proposer equivocation, and the check verifies only that the slots match, the proposer indices
match, the headers differ, and both signatures are
valid.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" />

What is *not* on that list matters as much as what is. Being offline is not slashable. Being slow is
not slashable. Running an unpopular client is not slashable. Missing an entire epoch of duties is
not slashable. Slashing requires a signature on two incompatible claims, and absence is not a claim.

## Nobody is watching. Someone has to file.

The last piece is the one that surprises people who expect a protocol to behave like a monitoring
system.

The protocol does not scan for offences. Slashing happens when a slashing object — the two
contradictory signed messages — is included in a block by a proposer, at which point the state
transition verifies both signatures, confirms the contradiction, and applies the
penalty.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" /> The whole path is reactive and
permissionless: the evidence must be produced, propagated and included by participants who chose to
do it.

Three details of that path are worth having, because each is a small mechanism-design problem solved
the same way as everything else in this module.

**An accusation names a set, not a person.** An attester slashing carries two indexed attestations,
each of which lists the validators that signed it. Processing it takes the *intersection* of the two
lists and slashes every member of it that is currently slashable — so one object can indict an entire
committee at once.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" />

**A useless accusation is an invalid one.** If the intersection turns out to contain nobody who can
actually be slashed, the operation fails outright rather than succeeding with no
effect.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" /> Evidence that charges nobody cannot be used
to consume block space.

**Reporting pays, and the pay is exact.** When a validator is slashed, a whistleblower reward is
credited, equal to the offender's effective balance divided by
`WHISTLEBLOWER_REWARD_QUOTIENT_ELECTRA`, which the mainnet preset sets to
4096.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" />
The proposer who included the evidence takes a share of that reward set by
`PROPOSER_WEIGHT / WEIGHT_DENOMINATOR`, or 8/64 — one eighth — and the whistleblower keeps the other
seven eighths.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-altair-beacon-chain" />

<Aside kind="note">
Line the two constants up and something falls out that is rarely noticed. The immediate slashing
penalty is the offender's effective balance divided by `MIN_SLASHING_PENALTY_QUOTIENT_ELECTRA`, which
the same preset also sets to 4096.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" />
Under the current preset the immediate charge and the reporting reward are therefore the *same
quantity*: what the offender loses on the spot is transferred to the reporter and the proposer rather
than destroyed.

It was not always so. Under the Bellatrix preset the penalty quotient was 32 and the whistleblower
quotient was 512, so the immediate charge was sixteen times the reward and the remainder was
burned.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" /> The detection layer's budget and the punishment's
severity are two dials, and they have been turned independently.
</Aside>

There is even a block-space budget for accusations. The mainnet preset allows at most 16 proposer
slashings per block, and phase 0 allowed 2 attester slashings; the Electra preset reduces that to
1.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" /> Attribution is not free — it competes for room in a
block like everything else, which is one reason a single attester slashing is built to indict many
validators at once.

<Check type="explain" concept="slashing-conditions">
An operator runs a validator, has a bad week, and manages both of these:

1. Their node is offline for six days.
2. They restore from a backup while the original process is still running, so two processes sign
   attestations with the same key for a few epochs.

Only the second is slashable. Why is that the right line to draw — and why could it not be drawn
anywhere else?

<Answer>
Because only the second produces a signed contradiction.

The two running processes each attested for the same target epoch with different data. That is a
double vote, and it satisfies the first clause of the predicate above: the data differ, the target
epochs match.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" /> The two messages together are
complete, self-contained proof that one key made two incompatible claims. Any observer verifies it
from the messages alone.

The six days of downtime produce no message at all. And that is exactly the problem — **absence is
not attributable.** From the outside, a validator that is offline is indistinguishable from a
validator that is online behind a network partition, or one whose messages were dropped, or one
whose messages arrived late somewhere else. There is no function of the form
`is_slashable(evidence) -> bool` that could separate those cases, because they differ only in facts
that never entered anyone's view. Punishing it as though it were an offence would mean punishing
honest operators for the network's behaviour, and would make honest staking a lottery.

So the line is drawn where evidence is possible, not where harm is. That is a real cost, and worth
stating plainly: more than a third of the stake going quiet is genuinely damaging and completely
unpunishable by this instrument. Downtime is instead handled by a different mechanism, sized as a
lost opportunity rather than as a confiscation — and the escalating version of it, for when absence
stops being an accident, is in the lesson after next.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

Accountable safety is what makes "finalised" a claim with a number behind it rather than a
reassurance. Every cost model in the rest of this module traces back to the counting argument above:
one third of the stake, forced to sign, on the public record.

It also explains a piece of validator operations that otherwise looks like superstition. The reason
serious staking setups go to such lengths to guarantee that exactly one process ever holds a key is
that the failure mode is not "downtime" but "signed contradiction" — the one category the protocol
punishes. A redundant, highly available signer is a *worse* design than a single one that sometimes
stops, and that inversion comes directly from which failures leave evidence.

And when you eventually write a confirmation policy for your own product, the distinction you are
encoding is between a state someone would have to pay for and a state they would not.
