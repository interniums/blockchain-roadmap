Everything so far has been machinery. This lesson assembles it into the one number people actually
ask for — and then insists you notice which number you produced, because there are two candidates
and they are not close.

## The derivation

**Subgoal 1 — name the property.** A checkpoint that has been finalised stays finalised. This is the
strongest claim the consensus layer makes, and it is the one worth pricing, because it is the one
products lean on when they treat money as irreversibly received.

**Subgoal 2 — name what the attacker must do.** To reverse it, they have to cause a conflicting
checkpoint to finalise. Nothing weaker will do: controlling the fork choice with a majority of stake
reorganises unfinalised blocks and censors, but it does not undo a
finalisation.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

**Subgoal 3 — apply accountable safety.** Two conflicting finalisations require at least one third
of the total stake to have signed contradictory messages. That is the counting argument, and there
is no path around it.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

**Subgoal 4 — apply the correlation penalty.** One third of the stake, slashed inside one window, is
exactly the point at which the correlation charge takes each offender's entire effective
balance.<Cite src="mikeneuder-slashings" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" /> The
canonical documentation states the same thing from the attacker's side: an attacker who reaches
double finality necessarily destroys their whole stake, because their validators double-voted
simultaneously and incurred the maximum correlation
penalty.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

**Subgoal 5 — check which quantity the fraction is *of*.** "One third of the stake" is not one third
of a dashboard figure. The penalty function divides by `get_total_active_balance`, which the
specification defines as the combined **effective** balance of the validators active in the current
epoch.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" /> Two exclusions matter and both cut the same
way. Capital sitting in the deposit queue is not active yet — a validator counts only from its
activation epoch until its exit epoch<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" /> — and
*effective* balance is a rounded, capped shadow of the real one, moving in whole-ETH increments and
capped at the post-EIP-7251 maximum of 2048 ETH.<Cite src="eip-7251" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" />
The number the protocol computes against is therefore slightly smaller than the number a staking
dashboard reports, and it is the protocol's number that decides the bill.

**Result.** The cost of reversing a finalised checkpoint is approximately **one third of all staked
ETH, destroyed** — not spent, not risked, not locked up. Burned and unrecoverable.

### That figure is a floor, not the whole cost

The one-third burn is the only term the protocol computes for you, which is why it is the one that
gets quoted. Three more costs land on the attacker, none of them optional, and all of them push the
same direction.

**The stake cannot be sold ahead of the burn.** Slashing initiates the validator's exit immediately
and pushes its withdrawable epoch out by `EPOCHS_PER_SLASHINGS_VECTOR` — 8192 epochs in the mainnet
preset, a little over 36 days.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" />
Exits are separately rate-limited: the Electra spec meters them against
`get_activation_exit_churn_limit`, capped by the mainnet configuration at 256 ETH per
epoch.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-config-mainnet" />
A position of that size cannot leave quickly even before the correlation charge lands on it.

**The residual holdings lose value.** Budish's analysis names the condition under which an attack
becomes a stock-against-stock comparison rather than a flow-against-stock one: the attack must be a
*sabotage*, in the sense that carrying it out collapses the economic value of the
chain.<Cite src="budish-economic-limits" /> That is a design feature working as intended — the
attacker destroys the value of everything they still hold. Hold on to the exception, though, because
the next lesson is entirely about the attacker for whom collapse is the payoff rather than the cost.

**The revenue stops.** A destroyed validator earns nothing further. The stake was a productive
position and the attack converts it into zero, permanently.<Cite src="vitalik-why-pos" />

So the honest sentence is: *at least* one third of the total active effective balance, destroyed. You
can compute that term from the protocol. You cannot compute the other three, which is a reason to
state the floor and label it as one — not a reason to pretend the floor is the total.

## Denominate it in ETH, and compute it when you need it

The quantity is a fraction of a number that changes daily, priced in an asset whose value changes by
the minute. Two rules follow, and both are violated constantly.

**Never bake in a fiat figure.** A dollar number in a threat model is stale within weeks and wrong
by a large factor within a year. The canonical Ethereum documentation itself carries fiat figures
that have aged, and the way they are misused is worth naming precisely. The dollar figure on the
attack-and-defense page appears as the *defence* against a majority-stake attacker: it prices the
cost of holding a majority of the stake, and is offered alongside the observation that the social
layer is likely to devalue that stake by adopting an honest minority
fork.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> Lift it out of that sentence and you have made
two errors at once. It is a fiat number, so it has expired. And it prices the *acquisition of a
majority*, which is a different attack from the one this lesson derives — one that does not reverse
finalised checkpoints at all.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

**Do not hardcode the staked total either.** Reported totals for August 2026 do not agree between
sources: one widely cited dataset puts staked ETH at around 34% of
supply,<Cite src="theblock-staking-share" /> while other reporting from the same period gives an
absolute total several million ETH higher. This course's research register records the discrepancy
as unresolved, and a spread of that size moves the answer by more than a million ETH. Pull the
current figure at the moment you need it<Cite src="ethereum-org-staking" /> and state the date and
source beside your result.

The defensible form of the claim is therefore a *method*, not a value: one third of the staked
supply as measured on a stated date, destroyed. Anyone reviewing your model can then re-run it
rather than trusting your arithmetic from six months ago.

### The method, in five steps

Here is the method as something you could hand to a colleague. Every step exists to remove one way
the number silently goes wrong.

**Subgoal 1 — get the denominator from the chain, not from a headline.** The standard beacon node
HTTP API exposes validator balances per state, so a node you trust will tell you the active set and
its balances directly.<Cite src="beacon-apis" /> The purpose of this step is to make the figure
*reproducible*: a reviewer can re-run it against the same state and get the same answer, which a
screenshot of a dashboard never allows.

**Subgoal 2 — reason in balances, never in validator count.** Since EIP-7251 raised the maximum
effective balance to 2048 ETH, effective balances range from 32 up to 2048 and validator count no
longer multiplies by anything useful.<Cite src="eip-7251" /> The purpose of this step is to avoid an
error that is silent rather than loud: multiplying a validator count by 32 produces a plausible
number that is simply not the quantity the penalty formula uses.

**Subgoal 3 — take one third, and know why it is exactly one third.** The endpoint comes from a
preset multiplier meeting a cap, not from a constant named "one third" anywhere in the
code.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" />
The purpose of knowing that is to know what would have to change for your model to change — and to
know that the endpoint survives the one genuine uncertainty here. The shape of the penalty curve
*between* zero and one third is disputed in the literature, but the two candidate forms agree exactly
at the one-third point, so the anchor holds under both.<Cite src="mikeneuder-slashings" /> Do not
print the intermediate curve unless you have read `process_slashings` for the fork you are actually
running against.

**Subgoal 4 — stamp it.** Record the state you read, the timestamp, and the source. The purpose is
that the model expires *visibly*. An undated number is indistinguishable from a current one, which
is precisely how stale figures survive.

**Subgoal 5 — decide whether to convert at all.** If the audience needs fiat, convert at the moment
of presentation and label the output as derived, echoing the price and the timestamp used. The
purpose is to keep the volatile input visible in the artefact rather than baked invisibly into it.

The sentence that survives review then reads: *reversing a finalised checkpoint requires roughly one
third of the total active effective balance to be destroyed — that is N ETH, measured against state S
on date D from source U, and it is a floor rather than the attacker's full cost.* Every clause is
there because a specific way of being wrong exists without it.

## The other number: capital versus corruption

Now the part that separates a real model from a headline.

**Cost of capital** is what it would take to *acquire* one third of the stake. This is the figure
that gets quoted, because market capitalisation is the easiest number in the world to find. It is
also the wrong one, for three reasons that compound.

It assumes the position can be bought at the current price. Buying that much of a liquid asset moves
the price violently against the buyer long before the position is complete — the marginal cost rises
as you go, and the average cost is nowhere near the spot price you started from.

It assumes that buying the asset buys the votes. It does not. The counting argument is about signed
attestations, and attestations are signed by validator keys, so the fraction that matters is the
fraction of the active set whose signing keys the attacker controls.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" />
Buying a liquid staking token is buying a claim against somebody else's validator, not the ability to
make it equivocate. The route from capital to votes runs through deposits — which is exactly the
route the protocol meters.

And it ignores that the accumulation is slow and visible, in a way you can put a rate on.

### Put a rate on the queue

**Subgoal 1 — find the cap.** New stake becomes active through `process_pending_deposits`, which
draws each epoch on `get_activation_exit_churn_limit` — the minimum of a balance-proportional churn
and the constant `MAX_PER_EPOCH_ACTIVATION_EXIT_CHURN_LIMIT`, set to 256 ETH per epoch in the
mainnet configuration.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-config-mainnet" />

**Subgoal 2 — check that the cap is the binding term.** The proportional half of that expression is
the total active balance divided by `CHURN_LIMIT_QUOTIENT`, which the mainnet configuration sets to
65536.<Cite src="consensus-specs-config-mainnet" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" />
That exceeds 256 ETH once the total active balance passes roughly 16.8 million ETH — so for any
plausible current total, the flat cap is what binds and the rate does not scale with the size of the
network.

**Subgoal 3 — convert to a per-day figure.** An epoch is 32 slots and a slot is 12
seconds,<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-config-mainnet" />
so an epoch is 6.4 minutes and a day holds 225 of them. The ceiling on newly activated stake is
therefore about 57,600 ETH per day, regardless of how much capital is queued behind
it.<Cite src="consensus-specs-config-mainnet" />

**Subgoal 4 — turn it into a time.** Divide the third-of-stake figure you computed above by that
daily ceiling and you have a floor on how long the accumulation takes — measured in months, in
public, on a chain anyone can read.

The canonical documentation makes the same point with a worked example, and it is worth reading in
its actual context. The 200-day figure it quotes is the time to add enough validators to *double* the
staked ether, offered as the reason a forcibly exited attacker cannot immediately try
again.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> Run it against the rate above and 200 days
corresponds to doubling a staked total near 11.5 million ETH. Whether that describes today's network
is a question you answer with the measurement from the previous section — which is the whole point.
Inherit the method; re-derive the figure.

**Cost of corruption** is what the attacker *loses by attacking*: the stake destroyed. This is the
honest framing for a security claim, and the reason is simple. An attacker who acquires a large
position and never uses it has lost nothing — they hold an asset, and one that has appreciated if
their buying moved the price. The cost only becomes real at the moment they attack, and at that
moment it is the burn, not the purchase price.

<Misconception
  belief="The cost of attacking Ethereum is the market price of a third of the stake."
  reality="That is the cost of acquisition, and it is recoverable — the attacker keeps an unused position. The cost of corruption is the stake destroyed by the protocol, which is not recoverable."
  why="Headline comparisons reach for market capitalisation because it is the easiest figure to obtain, and acquisition sounds more like a cost than destruction does."
  src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense"
/>

<Aside kind="note">
The two framings are not rivals to be picked between; they answer different questions. Cost of
capital answers *how hard is it to get into position*, which is a question about queues, liquidity
and visibility — and it is the right question for a monitoring system, because accumulation is the
part you can see coming. Cost of corruption answers *what does pulling the trigger cost*, which is
the question a security claim needs, because it is the only one where the money actually leaves.
Quote corruption in the claim; track capital in the alerting.
</Aside>

A draft threat model for a bridge you are reviewing:

> "Reversing a finalised Ethereum block would require an attacker to buy 34% of all staked ETH.
> At current prices that is far more than the 900 million dollars our bridge holds, so a
> finality-reversal attack is not economically rational against us. We can treat finalised
> deposits as irreversible."

Two of those sentences are defensible and two are not. Which, and how would you rewrite them?

<Answer>
**Defensible:** the threshold, and the choice to compare against a bounded prize. Naming 34% and
naming the specific property being violated is exactly right, and comparing the attacker's cost to a
specific figure is the correct shape for the argument.

**Not defensible — "would require an attacker to buy."** This is cost of capital. The attacker does
not have to buy the stake at spot, cannot buy it at spot, and — crucially — keeps it if they never
attack. Rewrite to the corruption form: the attack requires roughly one third of the total active
effective balance to be *destroyed*, which is a cost incurred only on attacking and is not
recoverable afterwards. If you want the acquisition side in the document as well, put it where it
belongs: as a *detection* argument, priced in months at the activation ceiling of 256 ETH per
epoch<Cite src="consensus-specs-config-mainnet" />, not as the headline cost.

**Not defensible — "at current prices."** A fiat comparison bakes in two volatile quantities, the
staked total and the ETH price, on the day the document was written. Six months later both numbers
are different and the conclusion has silently changed. Rewrite as ETH quantities, with the staked
total dated and sourced, and re-derive on a schedule.

There is a third problem the exercise did not ask about, and it is the largest: the bridge's own
balance is not the attacker's maximum payoff. That is the subject of the final lesson.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

This model is the load-bearing paragraph of any threat model you write for a system holding
significant value on-chain, and the method matters more than the number: a reviewer can check a
dated, sourced, reproducible figure, and cannot check a remembered one.

It is also what makes a bridge's balance a design constraint rather than a metric — a bridge is the
standard way that the payoff side of this comparison grows without anyone deciding it should.

And it sets the ceiling on what a confirmation policy can buy. Waiting for finality buys you a
property with this price attached; waiting longer than that buys you nothing further from the
protocol, because there is no stronger claim to wait for. Everything past that point is a bet about
the *other* side of the comparison — which is where the last lesson of this module goes.
