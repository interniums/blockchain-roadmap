Every system you have shipped had an operator. Someone could revoke an API key, ban an account,
roll back a bad write, or call the customer. The rules in your system were real because a party with
privileged access enforced them.

There is no such party here. So the question this whole module answers is: **what makes a rule real
when nobody can enforce it?**

## Game theory, run backwards

Game theory takes a set of rules and predicts what self-interested players will do. Mechanism design
runs the arrow the other way: you start from the behaviour you want and construct rules that produce
it, on the assumption that every participant is rational and pursuing their own profit.

That inversion is the entire discipline, and in a permissionless system it is the only enforcement
available. You do not get to assume good faith and patch the exceptions. Any property you want has
to survive being the exact thing a profit-maximising participant is trying to break, because the
participant trying to break it is a legitimate user of the system with no account to suspend.

This is not a framing imported from outside. It is the register the protocol's own documentation
writes in: Ethereum's consensus mechanism is described as deriving its **crypto-economic security**
from a set of rewards and penalties applied to capital locked by stakers, an incentive structure
whose stated purpose is to encourage honest operation, punish the rest, and make attacking
expensive.<Cite src="ethereum-org-consensus-mechanisms" /> Note the shape of that sentence. It does
not claim attack is impossible. It claims attack is *priced*, which is a different kind of promise
and the subject of the second half of this module.

So when you read a protocol design, the first two questions are always the same:

- What is each actor optimising?
- What do the rules pay them for?

If the answers do not line up with the behaviour the protocol needs, the protocol does not have that
behaviour. It has a wish.

## Three things a rule needs when nobody is in charge

That misconception hides more structure than it looks. Pull it apart and a permissionless rule turns
out to need three separate mechanisms, each of which can fail on its own.

**Evidence.** The violation has to be decidable from data every participant already holds, so that
any two honest observers reach the same verdict without trusting each other. In practice this
usually means the offender must have *signed* something that contradicts something else they signed.
Ethereum's slashing rules are exactly this shape: the predicate that decides whether two attestations
are punishable takes only the two signed messages and returns a
boolean.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" /> No context, no history, no judgement.

**A bond.** When identities are free, exclusion is not a punishment — the offender makes a new one.
The only thing that can be taken from a party you cannot otherwise reach is something they placed at
risk in advance. That is what a stake is: a deliberately created hostage, locked so that there is
something to confiscate.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos" />

**A reporter.** Evidence that nobody submits charges nobody. The protocol does not patrol; a
participant has to notice the contradiction, package it, and get it into a block — so somebody has to
be paid for doing that, and in Ethereum somebody is.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" />

These fail independently, and each failure is a real design pattern rather than a hypothetical. A
rule can be perfectly detectable and carry no cost — plenty of validator behaviour is visible to
everyone and charged at nothing, which is the whole content of the cheap-reorg lesson later in this
module. A rule can carry a devastating cost and produce no evidence — which makes the cost
unreachable, and therefore zero, which is why being offline is not slashable no matter how much harm
it does. And a cost that is real and provable but that nobody is paid to collect is a cost the
attacker can ignore.

<Aside kind="note">
Notice which of the three is the hard one. Bonds are easy: lock capital. Rewards for reporters are
easy: pay a fraction of what you confiscate. Almost all of the engineering difficulty in this module
is in the first item — arranging the protocol so that the behaviour you want to punish *cannot be
performed without producing a signed, self-contained proof that it was performed*. Two lessons from
now that requirement will visibly bend the design of the voting rules themselves.
</Aside>

## Incentive compatibility

A mechanism is **incentive compatible** when following the protocol truthfully is at least as good
for a participant as any deviation available to them.

This is a strong thing to have. If a mechanism is incentive compatible with respect to some class of
deviations, you need no monitoring and no punishment for that class, because nobody wants to deviate
in the first place. The behaviour is not policed into existence; it falls out. Where you can get it,
it is strictly better than the three-part machinery above, because it removes the need for all three
parts at once.

It is also a much narrower claim than it sounds, and the narrowing lives entirely in the qualifiers.
No specification defines the term — it comes from the economics literature, and the treatment this
track follows is Roughgarden's analysis of Ethereum's fee
market.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> Three qualifiers do all the work:

- **Best against whom?** Optimal no matter what every other player does — a *dominant* strategy — or
  optimal only on the assumption that everyone else is following the same recommendation? These are
  genuinely different guarantees, and the difference decides whether a wallet has to model other
  users.
- **Over what time horizon?** This block, or the indefinite future? An analysis that only considers
  the current block is called **myopic**, and a producer willing to lose money now to shape the
  price later is simply outside it.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />
- **Deviating alone, or together?** Almost every incentive result is about *unilateral* deviation:
  one party changing behaviour while everyone else follows the rules. A producer and a user who
  agree privately to something the protocol does not offer are not covered by any of
  them.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

Almost every real disagreement about protocol design is a disagreement about which of those a
mechanism satisfies, not about whether it is "incentive compatible" in the abstract. Each of the
three gets a formal name and its own section in the third lesson of this module, and the reason
there are three names rather than one is that Ethereum's fee market needed all three answered
separately.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

That boundary is not a technicality. The last lesson of this module is about attackers whose payoff
comes from entirely outside the fee market or the consensus protocol, and every incentive result you
are about to learn is silent on them.

## Which deviations should actually worry you

Once you accept that participants will do whatever pays, the next problem is triage. Every mechanism
has deviations available; you cannot design against all of them, and most of them never happen.

Roughgarden's report gives a usable filter, developed while working out which forms of miner
collusion to take seriously. The coordinated strategy that deserves your attention is one that meets
all three of these at once:<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

1. it favours the participants rather than the network;
2. it is **undetectable**; and
3. it is **game-theoretically robust** — cartel members are individually incentivised to stay in.

The filter earns its keep on the strategies it rules *out*. Double-spend, censorship and
denial-of-service attacks all fail the second test: they are visible in the chain's own record,
which triggers every response the community has. Meanwhile a cartel of block producers restricting
supply to prop up prices fails the third: each member does better by quietly defecting and packing
their own block full, so the arrangement is only as strong as its ability to punish defectors — and a
strategy that has to be sustained by credible punishment, at short-term cost to every member, may
pose very little risk in a well-secured chain.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

EIP-1559 makes the same argument in its own security considerations, about the specific attack of
producers mining empty blocks to drive the base fee down. The EIP's answer is not that the attack is
impossible: it is that any defector profits more than a participant for as long as the attack
continues, that defection is anonymous and unprovable, and that the strategy therefore requires
control of more than half the hash power — at which point the attacker has strictly more profitable
things to do.<Cite src="eip-1559" />

<Aside kind="note">
Both arguments have the same shape, and it is a shape worth stealing. Neither says "this cannot
happen". Both say: here is the deviation, here is who has to sacrifice what to sustain it, and here
is why the first participant to break ranks profits. When you write a threat model, that third
clause is the one that turns a worry into a finding or dismisses it.
</Aside>

<Check type="explain" concept="mechanism-design">
Your API enforces a rate limit at the gateway: a client exceeding it gets a 429 and, if it keeps
going, its key gets revoked. Now port that rule into a permissionless setting, where anyone can join
anonymously and there is no gateway and no key to revoke.

What has to change about the rule for it to still be a rule?

<Answer>
All three ingredients have to be built, and the first is the one that reshapes the rule itself.

**It has to be self-evidently violated.** "Sent too many requests" is a judgement your gateway makes
from state only your gateway has. In a permissionless system the violation has to be visible in data
every participant already holds, so that any observer reaches the same verdict without trusting the
observer. In practice this usually means the offender must have *signed* something that contradicts
something else they signed — and note what that costs you: some perfectly real harms simply cannot be
expressed that way and have to be handled by a different instrument entirely.

**It has to be expensive for the offender specifically.** Revocation works because the key was
valuable and scarce. When identities are free, the only thing that can be taken away is something
the participant put at risk in advance. That is what a stake is.

**Someone has to be paid to report it.** Your gateway watched every request because it sat in the
path. Nothing sits in the path here, so the accusation is a piece of work that a participant chooses
to do, and choosing has to beat not choosing.

Notice what does *not* transfer: detection by a privileged observer, and punishment by exclusion.
Both assume an operator, and each one silently assumed it in a different place.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

Everything downstream is this lens applied to a specific actor.

The **fee market** is mechanism design aimed at the block producer, and the next three lessons are a
single worked example of it: an auction is chosen, the standard theory turns out to assume something
false here, and the resulting design pays a named price for a named
property.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

**MEV** is what happens when the payoff of a deviation is larger than anyone modelled — the second
misconception on this page, industrialised.

A **rollup sequencer** is an auctioneer with the same conflict of interest as a block producer, in a
system where it is also the only party who can order transactions, and usually with no stake to
confiscate and no reporter to pay. Read that as three separate missing pieces rather than one vague
centralisation concern.

And when you get to writing a threat model, the useful form of every line will be the pair from the
top of this lesson — what is this actor optimising, and what do the rules pay them for — filtered
through the three-part test above, so that the findings you raise are the ones that are quiet,
profitable and stable, rather than the ones that are merely imaginable.
