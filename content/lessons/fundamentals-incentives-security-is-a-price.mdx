You already know what a hash function guarantees. Finding a collision is not forbidden; it is priced
at an amount of work that no quantity of hardware turns into a plan. The guarantee is a statement
about computation, and it holds regardless of how much anyone wants to break it or how much they are
willing to spend.

Consensus offers nothing of that kind. Outside of the cryptography it uses, **a blockchain's safety
is not a mathematical impossibility. It is a price.** An attack is possible, it costs something, and
the system is secure exactly to the extent that the cost exceeds what the attack pays.

This is not a sceptic's framing. It is how the protocol's own documentation describes itself: a
proof-of-stake system is secure *crypto-economically*, because an attacker attempting to take
control must destroy a large amount of ETH.<Cite src="ethereum-org-consensus-mechanisms" /> The word
doing the work in that sentence is "destroy", and everything in the rest of this module is an
attempt to put a number next to it.

Two things follow, and both of them change how you write a risk assessment.

**The security level moves.** It is denominated in an asset whose value changes by the minute. The
same chain, the same validator set, the same code, is a different security proposition on two
different days.

**The comparison is against a number the chain does not control.** Security is cost versus payoff,
and the payoff is a property of what has been built on top — the largest prize an attacker could
take. Deploy something that concentrates enough value in one place and you have lowered the chain's
effective security for everyone, without touching the chain.

## The economics has a shape, and it is uncomfortable

The second point is worth taking further than intuition, because it has been worked out formally and
the result is more demanding than "attacking should cost more than it earns".

The classical analysis is Budish's, written about anonymous, decentralised proof-of-work chains. It
starts from two conditions that have to hold at the same time in
equilibrium:<Cite src="budish-economic-limits" />

1. **Zero profit among block producers.** Producing blocks is an open competition for a prize, so
   participants enter until the cost of competing eats the reward. Nobody earns a durable margin.
2. **Attacking must not pay.** The cost of a majority attack must exceed its benefit — which is just
   incentive compatibility applied to the largest available deviation.

Put those together and a third statement falls out, and it is the one that stings: the **recurring**
payments made to block producers must be large relative to the **one-off** benefit of attacking the
chain.<Cite src="budish-economic-limits" /> The security budget is a flow, measured per block, per
day, per year. The prize is a stock, sitting there in one lump. Requiring a flow to dominate a stock
is, in the paper's own summary, very expensive.

**Subgoal — see why the mismatch is the whole problem.** An attacker rents capacity for the duration
of the attack, so their cost scales with *time*. The value they can seize does not: it is whatever
is sitting on the chain at the moment they strike. Double the value settled on the chain and the
prize doubles, while the security budget does not move at all unless fees or issuance also double.

The paper names exactly two conditions under which the constraint softens to something more
comfortable — a stock cost against a stock prize:<Cite src="budish-economic-limits" />

- the technology used to secure the chain is **scarce and non-repurposable**, so an attacker's outlay
  cannot be recovered by selling or redeploying it afterwards; and
- an attack is a **sabotage**, in the sense that carrying it out collapses the economic value of the
  chain — so the attacker destroys the thing they were attacking to capture.

Hold on to both, because they are the two hinges everything else in this module turns on. The first
is what a slashed stake is. The second is what the last lesson of this module is about, and it cuts
in the opposite direction from how it first reads: an attacker who is *short* the asset is paid by
the collapse rather than punished by it.

<Aside kind="warn">
The paper's conclusion is worth quoting the shape of, because it is the least comfortable sentence
in this field: the model implies a chain would be majority-attacked if it became sufficiently
economically important, which suggests intrinsic economic limits to how important it can become in
the first place.<Cite src="budish-economic-limits" />

That is a claim about proof-of-work Bitcoin under a specific model, not a prediction about Ethereum,
and the design choices in the rest of this module are largely responses to it. Do not carry it as a
verdict. Carry it as the reason the design has the shape it has.
</Aside>

## What a cost-of-attack model actually is

A usable model does three things, in order.

**Name one property.** Not "attacking Ethereum" — a specific guarantee, violated in a specific way.
Preventing a transaction from being included is one property. Reorganising the most recent
unfinalised blocks is another. Reversing a checkpoint that has already been finalised is a third.

**Describe concretely what the attacker must do.** How much stake, what messages they have to sign,
what network conditions they need, how long they have to sustain it.

**Price that, and state every assumption behind every input.** Which figure for total stake, at what
date, from which source, and what the model assumes about the attacker's ability to acquire it.

A model that survives review reads roughly like this, and every clause is load-bearing: *to violate
[property], an attacker must [action] using at least [threshold] of [resource], which the protocol
charges at [mechanism], costing [quantity, in units, measured on this date, from this source] — against
a maximum payoff of [prize], bounded by [reason it is bounded].* If any bracket is empty you have an
opinion rather than a model, and the two that are usually empty are the last two.

Skip step one and the exercise is worthless, because those three properties differ by orders of
magnitude in cost.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> The next two lessons work through
exactly which fraction of stake buys which of them, and why the numbers are so far apart.

The protocol's own documentation names the third property in a way that admits all of this. The
strongest form of reorganisation is called *finality reversion*, and it is described as possible only
if more than a third of the total staked ether is destroyed by the attacker — a guarantee the
documentation calls **economic finality**.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> Read the
adjective as a warning label rather than as marketing. "Final" here does not mean *cannot be
undone*; it means *undoing it has a bill attached, and here is the size of the bill*.

<Aside kind="note">
This is why "is it final?" is the wrong question to ask about a transaction and "what would undoing
it cost, and is that more than it is worth to someone?" is the right one. The first question has a
yes-or-no answer that hides the model. The second forces you to name a property, a threshold and a
number — which is the same three steps as above, run from the defender's side.
</Aside>

## Rental versus burn

There is one more reason a single number cannot travel: under proof of work and proof of stake, the
cost is not even the same *kind* of quantity — and the difference is precisely Budish's
repurposability condition.

**Under proof of work, an attack is a rental.** The attacker needs more hash power than the honest
producers for the duration of the attack. Hash power can be rented, and the machines that produce it
are capital that survives the attack — they can be pointed at something else afterwards, or
sold.<Cite src="vitalik-why-pos" /> The expenditure is an operating cost for a window of time, which
is the flow-against-stock comparison in its most unfavourable form.

**Under proof of stake, an attack is a burn.** A safety violation on Ethereum is provable and
attributable, and the offending stake is destroyed by the protocol itself — the attacker's stake and
nobody else's.<Cite src="vitalik-why-pos" /><Cite src="ethereum-org-pos" /> There is no residual
asset. The capital is gone.

Read that against the two softening conditions above and the design intent is legible. Staked ETH is
scarce by construction and, once slashed, non-repurposable in the strongest possible sense: it does
not exist any more. The move from proof of work to proof of stake is, among other things, a
deliberate move from comparing a flow against a stock to comparing a stock against a stock.

The recovery story differs in the same direction. When a proof-of-work chain is attacked, the
historical response has largely been to wait for the attacker to lose interest, because the chain
has no way to charge them.<Cite src="vitalik-why-pos" /> A proof-of-stake chain has a mechanism that
takes the money, which is the subject of the rest of this module.

<Aside kind="warn">
Both cost models are routinely quoted in fiat, including on the canonical documentation itself: the
attack-and-defense page prices a majority stake in dollars, in a sentence written when the staked
total and the exchange rate were both different from today's.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />
A fiat number in a threat model is wrong within weeks and wrong by a large factor within a year, and
it will be quoted back at you long after it stopped being true.

Quote the quantity — hash rate for a window, or ETH destroyed — and convert at the moment of use if
you have to convert at all. The lesson two ahead of this one turns that rule into a method.
</Aside>

From a security review you have been asked to sign off on:

> "Chain A has an annual security budget of about two billion dollars; chain B's is about eight
> billion. Chain B is therefore roughly four times harder to attack, so we can safely settle
> four times the value on it."

Three things are wrong. Name them.

<Answer>
**No property is named.** "Harder to attack" is not a claim until you say which guarantee is being
violated. On the same chain, censoring a transaction and reversing a finalised checkpoint differ by
orders of magnitude, so a single ratio cannot describe both.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

**The two budgets may not be the same kind of quantity — and "annual" is the tell.** An annual
issuance figure is a *flow*. If chain A's attack is a rental of capacity for an hour, the relevant
comparison is one hour of that flow against the prize; if chain B's attack destroys stake, the
relevant comparison is a *stock* against the prize.<Cite src="budish-economic-limits" /> Dividing one
by the other compares a rate with a quantity, and neither the ratio nor the difference means anything.

**The conclusion runs the argument backwards.** Attack cost bounds nothing on its own; security is
cost versus *payoff*. Settling four times the value on chain B raises the payoff of attacking chain
B by the same factor, which is the term the sentence just moved without accounting for it. This is
the flow-versus-stock problem in its purest form: the reviewer proposed to quadruple the stock while
citing the flow as reassurance.<Cite src="budish-economic-limits" /> That final step is the subject
of the last lesson in this module.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

This is the frame every later risk decision sits in.

Whether a **bridge** is a honeypot is a question about payoff, not about the bridge's code quality —
a perfectly audited contract holding a large single balance is exactly the stock-shaped prize the
economics warns about.

Whether a **rollup that keeps its data off-chain** is acceptable is a question about which property
you are relying on and what violating *that* property would cost, which is step one of the model
above applied to a system whose guarantees differ from the L1's.

And how many confirmations your product waits for before it treats money as received is, in the end,
a price you have chosen to accept. The next three lessons give you the machinery to say what price
that is: which fraction of stake buys which violation, what the protocol charges for it, and how to
compute the number without baking in a figure that expires.
