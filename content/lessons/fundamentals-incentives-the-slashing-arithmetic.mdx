Ask what being slashed costs and the honest answer is a question back: **slashed alongside whom?**

That is not evasion. The penalty is deliberately built in two pieces, one of which is a function of
what everybody else did in the same window. Once you see why, the whole design reads differently:
it is not a punishment schedule, it is a price on correlation.

## Charge one: immediate, and small

When a valid slashing is processed, the validator is marked slashed, its exit is initiated, and it
is charged straight away an amount equal to its effective balance divided by a protocol
constant.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" /> Its withdrawable epoch is pushed out by
`EPOCHS_PER_SLASHINGS_VECTOR`, which the mainnet preset sets to 8192 epochs — at 32 slots per epoch
and 12-second slots, a little over 36 days before the remaining balance can
leave.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" /><Cite src="consensus-specs" />

The constant is the interesting part, because it has moved by orders of magnitude across four forks.
The mainnet presets record the whole history: the quotient was 128 in phase 0, 64 under Altair and 32
under Bellatrix, and `MIN_SLASHING_PENALTY_QUOTIENT_ELECTRA` is
4096.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" />

Put numbers on it. A validator with a 32 ETH effective balance used to lose about 1 ETH immediately
under the Bellatrix quotient; under the Electra quotient the same validator loses about 0.008 ETH. A
validator at the post-EIP-7251 maximum effective balance of 2048 ETH loses about 0.5
ETH.<Cite src="eip-7251" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" />

<Aside kind="warn">
Almost every article about slashing written before 2025 quotes the older quotient, and the resulting
"you lose roughly 1 ETH plus your rewards" figure is now off by more than two orders of magnitude
for the immediate charge. Check which fork's preset a source is describing before you reuse its
arithmetic — the constants are versioned by fork name in the preset files precisely because they
change.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" />
</Aside>

That second one matters more than it looks for everything in this lesson. Every formula below is
written in terms of *balances*, not validator counts — the slashing vector sums balances, the
correlation penalty divides by total active balance, the thresholds are fractions of stake. A
threat model that counts validators is measuring the wrong quantity.

## Charge two: much later, and conditional

The second charge lands roughly 18 days after the first. The spec applies it when the current epoch
plus half of `EPOCHS_PER_SLASHINGS_VECTOR` equals the validator's withdrawable epoch, which works
out to 4096 epochs after the
slashing.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" />

What it charges depends on how much stake was slashed in the surrounding window. The beacon state
keeps a rolling vector of slashed balances covering `EPOCHS_PER_SLASHINGS_VECTOR` epochs; the sum of
that vector is the input.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" />

The design target is unambiguous and it is the only number the security argument needs: **when the
balance slashed in the window reaches one third of the total active stake, the penalty consumes the
offender's entire effective balance.**<Cite src="mikeneuder-slashings" /><Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

### Where the one third actually comes from

That endpoint is usually quoted as if it were a natural constant. It is not — it is the product of
one preset value, and seeing that changes how you read it.

Here is the epoch-processing function as the Electra specification defines
it:<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" />

```python
def process_slashings(state: BeaconState) -> None:
    epoch = get_current_epoch(state)
    total_balance = get_total_active_balance(state)
    adjusted_total_slashing_balance = min(
        sum(state.slashings) * PROPORTIONAL_SLASHING_MULTIPLIER_BELLATRIX, total_balance
    )
    increment = EFFECTIVE_BALANCE_INCREMENT
    penalty_per_effective_balance_increment = adjusted_total_slashing_balance // (
        total_balance // increment
    )
    for index, validator in enumerate(state.validators):
        if (
            validator.slashed
            and epoch + EPOCHS_PER_SLASHINGS_VECTOR // 2 == validator.withdrawable_epoch
        ):
            effective_balance_increments = validator.effective_balance // increment
            penalty = penalty_per_effective_balance_increment * effective_balance_increments
            decrease_balance(state, ValidatorIndex(index), penalty)
```

Walk it with the multiplier in hand. `PROPORTIONAL_SLASHING_MULTIPLIER_BELLATRIX` is 3 in the mainnet
preset.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" />

**Subgoal 1 — find the saturation point.** The first line multiplies the total slashed balance by 3
and then caps it at the total active balance. That cap binds as soon as the slashed balance reaches
one third of the total stake.

**Subgoal 2 — see what the cap does to the per-unit penalty.** Once capped, the adjusted figure
*equals* the total active balance, so the penalty per unit of effective balance becomes one unit of
effective balance.

**Subgoal 3 — apply it to an offender.** Each slashed validator is charged that per-unit rate times
its own number of units. At saturation, that is its entire effective balance.

So one third is not a threshold written into the code anywhere. It is the point at which a multiplier
of 3 meets a cap — and the multiplier has changed twice. The presets record it at 1 in phase 0, 2
under Altair and 3 under Bellatrix.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" /> Under the original
value of 1, total confiscation would have required *all* of the stake to be slashed in the same
window. The alignment between "the fraction that accountable safety forces to sign" and "the fraction
at which you lose everything" is engineered, and it was engineered in two steps.

<Aside kind="stop">
The shape of the curve *between* zero and that endpoint is contested, and this course's research
register lists it as unresolved: one analysis presents a linear form as current and a quadratic form
as proposed, and a 2026 secondary source reports the quadratic version as
shipped.<Cite src="mikeneuder-slashings" />

Read today, the function above is linear in the slashed fraction, and no later fork in the repository
redefines it.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" /> That is what the specification says;
it is not a claim that the disagreement in the literature has been settled.

The good news is that the endpoint is robust either way. The linear candidate multiplies the slashed
fraction by 3, and the quadratic candidate multiplies its square by 9 — and at a slashed fraction of
one third both evaluate to exactly 1, the whole balance.<Cite src="mikeneuder-slashings" /> So the
security argument in the next lesson holds under both. If you need the curve rather than the
endpoint, read `process_slashings` for the fork you are actually running against, and do not inherit
a formula from a blog post.
</Aside>

Whichever form is live, notice what the multiplier does: it means the penalty saturates well before
every validator has been slashed. Being one of a third that misbehaved is enough to lose everything;
you do not need to be one of everybody.

## What the two charges encode

Read together, the two charges are a policy, and it is worth stating plainly.

An isolated slashing is an operational accident — a misconfigured client, a restore that left the
old process running, two signers on one key. It is priced as an accident: recoverable, embarrassing,
not ruinous.

A large fraction of the stake slashed simultaneously is not an accident. It is either an attack or a
correlated failure with the same consequences as one, and it is priced accordingly.

The mechanism is not measuring how wrong you were. It is measuring **how many people were wrong in
the same way at the same time.**

<Misconception
  belief="Slashing exists to punish careless validators."
  reality="It exists to make coordinated attack unaffordable. The careless-operator case is deliberately made cheap so that honest participation is not a lottery."
  why="A penalty naturally reads as a judgement on the individual who incurred it, rather than as a price on the correlation between individuals."
  src="mikeneuder-slashings"
/>

Two consequences follow that are easy to miss.

**The window is a design parameter too.** "Simultaneously" means inside the same 8192-epoch rolling
vector — about 36 days.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" /> An attacker who could spread
their slashable actions across a longer period would pay the isolated price each time. They cannot,
because a finality reversal requires the contradictory votes to be *concurrent*; but a correlated
*bug*, discovered and fixed slowly across an operator fleet, can absolutely straddle the window in
ways that change the bill.

**This is the sharpest argument for client and operator diversity**, and it converts a taste
preference into a risk calculation. A bug in a client running one tenth of the stake produces a
correlated event one tenth the size of a bug in a client running a third of it, and the penalty curve
is steep in exactly that variable. Under the linear form, a tenth of the stake slashed together costs
each offender roughly 30% of its balance; a third costs everything.

## The other instrument: the inactivity leak

Slashing needs evidence. The previous lesson showed that more than a third of the stake can stall
finalisation by doing nothing at all, which produces no evidence and is therefore not slashable.
That gap is closed by a different mechanism, with a completely different shape.

When the chain fails to finalise for more than `MIN_EPOCHS_TO_INACTIVITY_PENALTY` epochs — four, in
the mainnet preset — the leak begins.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" /> Every validator
carries an **inactivity score**, and each epoch the score of one that missed the timely target vote
rises by `INACTIVITY_SCORE_BIAS`, which the mainnet configuration sets to
4.<Cite src="consensus-specs-altair-beacon-chain" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-config-mainnet" /> A
validator that did vote has its score reduced by 1.

The penalty charged each epoch is the validator's effective balance multiplied by its inactivity
score, divided by the bias times an inactivity penalty
quotient.<Cite src="consensus-specs-altair-beacon-chain" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-bellatrix-beacon-chain" />
Two features of that formula do all the work.

**The loss accelerates.** The per-epoch charge is proportional to the score, and the score climbs
steadily while a validator stays absent — so the *cumulative* loss over a stall grows roughly with the
square of its duration rather than linearly. A short outage is nearly free; a long one is not, and it
gets worse at an increasing rate.

**Getting back is slower than falling behind.** During a leak, missing an epoch adds 4 to the score
while attesting removes only 1.<Cite src="consensus-specs-altair-beacon-chain" /> A validator has to
be present for four epochs to undo one absence. The faster recovery — up to 16 per epoch — applies
only once the chain is finalising again.<Cite src="consensus-specs-config-mainnet" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-altair-beacon-chain" />

The quotient that scales the whole thing has also been tightened twice: 2²⁶ in phase 0, then
50331648 under Altair, then 2²⁴ under
Bellatrix.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-bellatrix-beacon-chain" />
The leak is four times sharper today than at genesis.

The effect is to bleed the absent stake until the validators that *are* participating once again
exceed the two thirds needed to finalise.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> And there is
a floor to fall through: a validator whose balance drops to `EJECTION_BALANCE` — 16 ETH in the mainnet
configuration — is ejected from the active set
entirely.<Cite src="consensus-specs-config-mainnet" />

The strategic shape of that is worth appreciating. The attacker does not have to be defeated,
detected, or identified. They have to be **outlasted**, and the cost of continuing to be absent
grows while they wait.

<Misconception
  belief="If a third of the validators go offline, the chain is stuck forever."
  reality="The inactivity leak drains the balances of the non-participating validators until the active set is a supermajority again. The stall is self-correcting, at the absentees' expense."
  why="The two-thirds requirement looks like a hard deadlock condition, because in most consensus protocols it is one."
  src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense"
/>

<Aside kind="warn">
The leak does not distinguish an attacker from a victim. A validator that is absent because its
operator is attacking and a validator that is absent because its data centre lost power are the same
object to this mechanism, and both are drained. That is the necessary price of not being able to
attribute absence — and it is why an operator's disaster-recovery plan is a consensus-layer concern
and not only an availability one.
</Aside>

<Check type="predict" concept="slashing-correlation-penalty">
Two operators commit the identical offence — a double vote, from two signing processes on one key.

Operator A is alone: nothing else of note is slashed in the window.
Operator B commits it as part of a coordinated group holding about a third of the total stake.

Rank the two costs, and say which charge does the ranking.

<Answer>
**The initial charge is identical for both**, up to their effective balances: it is a fixed fraction
of the validator's own balance and takes no account of anyone
else.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" />

**The correlation charge does all the work.** For A, the summed slashed balance in the window is a
rounding error against the total active stake. Trace it through the function: the sum times 3 is
nowhere near the cap, so the per-unit penalty is a tiny fraction of one unit of effective balance,
and A's total loss is dominated by the initial penalty and by the rewards forgone while waiting out
the ejection period.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" />

For B, the slashed balance in the window is around one third of the total active stake, the cap
binds, the per-unit penalty becomes a full unit, and the charge equals B's entire effective
balance.<Cite src="mikeneuder-slashings" /><Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> B loses
everything.

Same offence, same signatures, same protocol. The difference in cost is not a difference in what
they did — it is a difference in how many people did it with them.

One more thing worth noticing: B's bill did not depend on B being one of the *attackers*. Any
validator slashed inside that window pays the correlated rate, including an unlucky operator whose
misconfigured node happened to double-vote on the same day. The mechanism prices the correlation, and
it cannot tell why the correlation exists.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

The one-third-of-stake endpoint is the input to the finality-reversal cost model in the next lesson,
and you now know exactly which preset constant produces it — which means you know what would have to
change for the number to change.

The two-charge structure is why a staking operator's architecture — how many independent signing
processes, on which clients, behind which failover — is a security decision with a number attached
rather than an ops preference. Redundancy that protects availability by duplicating a signer converts
the cheap failure into the expensive one.

And the inactivity leak is what turns "the chain stopped finalising" from an unbounded outage into a
bounded, self-correcting one — which is the assumption every downstream system makes when it decides
how long to wait before declaring an incident rather than a delay.
