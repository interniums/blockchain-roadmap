Auction theory managed for decades with essentially one incentive property. The formal analysis of
Ethereum's fee market needs three.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

That is not academic inflation, and the question worth answering before the definitions is: **what
is present in this setting that the standard theory has no vocabulary for?**

The answer is one actor. In a normal auction, the auctioneer runs the auction. Here, the auctioneer
is also a bidder, also a participant who can be bribed, and also the party who writes the record of
what happened. Each of those three roles needs its own property, because a mechanism can be safe
against one and wide open to another.

## The property aimed at the user

Start with the user side, because it is the one with a name people misuse.

A **bidding strategy** is a rule that turns the value of your transaction into a bid — a function of
a number you know, and of nothing you do not.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> The question is whether
some strategy is *obviously* the right one to follow. There are two strengths of answer, and the gap
between them is the whole subject.

**The strong form: a dominant strategy.** Your recommended bid maximises your utility no matter
what any other participant does — even if they behave arbitrarily, even if they lie. You need to know
nothing about anyone. A second-price auction with a neutral auctioneer has this, which is what made
the previous lesson's design so tempting.

**The weaker form: a symmetric equilibrium.** Your recommended bid maximises your utility *provided*
every other transaction's bid was set by the same recommendation.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> You
still do not have to know anyone's values — an enormous improvement on the first-price auction, where
you did — but you do have to keep the faith that others followed the rule.

Roughgarden's report calls the user-side condition **UIC**, incentive compatibility for users, and
defines it as the existence of such a symmetric equilibrium — that is, the existence of an obvious
optimal bid.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> This is deliberately the weaker of the two, and the
weakness is load-bearing rather than sloppy.

Under EIP-1559 the obvious bid has a closed form: set your fee cap to what the transaction is
actually worth to you, and set your tip to the producer's marginal cost of including
it.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> Follow that and you pay the base fee plus a nominal tip, and there
is nothing to estimate — the mechanism behaves as a **posted-price** mechanism, with the price
announced in the parent block rather than discovered by bidding.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

<Aside kind="warn">
It is worth being exact here, because the headline gets flattened constantly. That obvious bid is
*not* a dominant strategy under EIP-1559. The report says so explicitly, and names the failure: if
other transactions overstate their fee caps, the base fee can be too low relative to the *stated*
demand even though it is fine relative to the real demand, and the argument for your bid breaks
down.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

The variant with a fixed, protocol-set tip *does* achieve the dominant-strategy version — there, the
obvious bid is optimal no matter what anyone else bids.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> The strength of
the user-side guarantee is therefore a design choice with a name, not a property of having a base fee.
</Aside>

## The property aimed at the block producer

**MMIC** — myopic miner incentive compatibility — asks a different question: can the party assembling
the block gain by deviating for *that block*? Formally, the mechanism is MMIC if a producer
maximises its own utility by creating no fake transactions and following the intended allocation
rule.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

The deviations available are the ones the producer's position affords: inserting transactions that
exist only to move the price, excluding real transactions, or manipulating what the winner pays.
Second-price fails MMIC for exactly the reason the previous lesson priced.

There is a stronger property hiding behind it that is worth naming, because it is where the burn
first shows up. A mechanism is **costly** if fake transactions do not merely fail to help the
producer but actively charge them.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> Under a plain first-price auction,
a producer paying a fee to itself nets to zero, so fakes are free — MMIC holds, but there is no
deterrent, only an absence of reward. Under EIP-1559, every included transaction burns the base fee,
including the producer's own fakes, so a fake transaction costs the producer the base fee plus its
marginal cost of gas for every unit of gas it occupies.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

That difference is not incidental. If the base fee were paid to the producer instead of burned, the
mechanism would still be MMIC, but fakes would cost only the marginal cost of gas — which is to say,
almost nothing.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> "Nobody gains by cheating" and "cheating is expensive"
are different guarantees, and only the second survives an attacker who is willing to spend a little
to disguise something.

### What "myopic" excludes, precisely

The word is a deliberate limit on the claim. MMIC considers only the current block and says nothing
about strategies played across several — a producer willing to lose money now to shape the base fee
later is outside the model.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

That is not a hypothetical gap, and the EIP addresses the concrete version of it directly. Producers
could mine empty blocks until the base fee falls very low, then resume filling blocks and pocket the
difference. EIP-1559's security considerations concede the attack is possible and argue it is not a
stable equilibrium: any defector earns more than a participant for as long as the attack continues,
defection is anonymous and unprovable, and sustaining it therefore requires more than half the hash
power — at which point the attacker has more profitable options
available.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> The EIP also names the dial for tightening this, which is the
elasticity multiplier: raise it and an attacker needs a larger share before the strategy is even
theoretically profitable against defectors.<Cite src="eip-1559" />

Note the structure of the defence. It is not a proof inside the mechanism. It is the three-part
filter from the first lesson applied by hand — the strategy is detectable in aggregate, and it is
game-theoretically fragile because each member must sacrifice for the group. Multi-block
manipulation is a live research area, MMIC is not a defence against it, and does not claim to be.

## The property aimed at the coalition

Both properties so far are about *unilateral* deviation: one party changing their own behaviour
while everyone else follows the rules. That leaves a hole, and it is the one the burn exists to
close.

An **off-chain agreement** is a precise object rather than a vague worry: a set of users and a
producer agree on what each user will bid on-chain, together with a private per-unit-of-gas transfer
from each user to the producer.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> The mechanism never sees the deal. It
only sees the on-chain transactions the parties chose to submit.

**OCA-proofness** is the property that no such coalition beats honest
participation.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> It is a strictly different question from MMIC, and a
mechanism can satisfy one and fail the other.

The reason it reduces to something checkable is worth carrying, because it is the whole argument of
the next lesson in one line. Inside the coalition, on-chain payments and off-chain transfers both
move value from users to the producer, so from the coalition's point of view they cancel. The one
term that leaves the coalition is the fee **burn**.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> So a coalition is
trying to maximise total transaction value minus the burn — and a mechanism is OCA-proof exactly when
its own allocation rule already maximises that same quantity, leaving nothing for a side deal to
improve.

That is why the destination of the base fee is a mechanism question rather than an accounting one.

## The report card

With the three properties in hand, the result is precise. EIP-1559 is always MMIC and always
OCA-proof, and is incentive compatible for users **except when the base fee is excessively
low**.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /><Cite src="roughgarden-tfm-arxiv" />

The exception is not a footnote, and quoting the headline without it is the most common way this
result gets mangled. It also has a real definition rather than a vibe: a base fee is **excessively
low** when the total gas demanded at the price "base fee plus marginal cost" exceeds the maximum
block size.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> Not "fees are high" — demand at the announced price does
not fit.

Here is the mechanism of the exception. While the base fee is roughly right, a user pays it and adds
a nominal tip; nothing to model. When demand jumps faster than the base fee can adjust, blocks fill
to their maximum, the announced price no longer clears the queue, and inclusion is decided by the
tip. At that moment the tip is a first-price auction again, with all the bid-shading and
distribution-guessing the first lesson of this pair walked through.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

**How long does the lapse last?** Long enough to matter. In the report's worked example — demand
that more than doubles in one period — the base fee climbs from 33.33 through 37.5, 42.18, 47.46 and
53.39 to 60.06, and the base fee is excessively low for five consecutive periods before blocks stop
running at their maximum.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> The guarantee lapses precisely during
congestion, for as long as congestion lasts, which is exactly when anyone cares about the fee market.

<Aside kind="note">
The same analysis proposes an alternative with the mirror-image trade-off. In the **tipless
mechanism**, users specify only a fee cap and the tip is a protocol constant. It is MMIC; it is
incentive compatible for users *always*, and in the stronger dominant-strategy sense, because during
congestion users have no vocabulary left to outbid each other with; and it is OCA-proof except when
the base fee is excessively low, at which point producer and users can do better by agreeing
off-chain on which transactions to include.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

Line the two up and the shape of the result is clear. It is not "1559 is optimal". It is: **under
congestion something has to give, and the design chooses what.** EIP-1559 spends the user-side
guarantee and keeps coalition-resistance. The tipless mechanism does the reverse. A user-facing
parameter is exactly the thing that lets users express more — which is both how they escape a queue
and how a coalition finds an edge.
</Aside>

<Check type="explain" concept="oca-proofness">
A builder offers users a private endpoint: submit your transaction there instead of the public
mempool, pay the builder directly off-chain, and they will include it.

Which of the three properties does this arrangement attack, and why is it not covered by the other
two?

<Answer>
**OCA-proofness.** It is a coalition between the producer and a user, settled outside the mechanism,
and it matches the formal object exactly: an agreed on-chain bid plus a private transfer from user
to producer.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

The user-side property does not cover it, because that property is a statement about one user's
bidding strategy against other users' bidding strategies. Nothing here is a bid.

MMIC does not cover it either, and this is the part worth being precise about: MMIC asks whether the
producer can profit by deviating *unilaterally* within a block. In this arrangement the producer
deviates *with* the user, and both come out ahead relative to honest participation. A
unilateral-deviation property is silent by construction on a deal that requires two consenting
parties.

Then ask the question the coalition analysis makes available: what does the arrangement actually
move? If the private endpoint only relabels who pays whom, it moves value around inside the
coalition and changes nothing the mechanism cares about. It becomes an attack on the mechanism at
the point where it lets the coalition avoid the burn — which is the term that leaves the
coalition — or lets it choose an allocation the protocol's own rule would not have
chosen.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

That distinction is why the destination of the base fee is the whole of the next lesson.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

These three properties are the vocabulary for most of what follows, and each one has grown into an
industry.

**Proposer-builder separation** is an attempt to answer MMIC structurally, by splitting the
producer's roles so that the party choosing the contents is not the party proposing the block. Read
it as a response to the credibility problem from the previous lesson rather than as a scaling
feature.

**Private order flow and order-flow auctions** are the OCA-proofness question showing up as a
market. Once you can name what a coalition is trying to maximise, "is this order-flow arrangement
harmful?" becomes a question with a structure: what leaves the coalition, and does the protocol's own
allocation rule already maximise what is left?

And the **user-side exception during congestion** is why fee estimation is still code you have to
write rather than a field you fill in — and why the code that matters is the code that runs when the
base fee has not caught up. A wallet that is correct only in the calm case is correct only when
nothing was at stake.
