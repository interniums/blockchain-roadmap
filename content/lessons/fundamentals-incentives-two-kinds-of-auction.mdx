Demand for space in a block is far larger than the supply of it, so something has to choose which
pending transactions get in.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> That is an allocation problem with a
well-studied answer: run an auction.

Which immediately raises the question this lesson answers: **which auction, and what does the choice
cost the people bidding in it?**

## First price: you pay what you bid

In a first-price auction the winner pays their own bid. This is the obvious design, and it is what
Ethereum used before EIP-1559: each transaction carried a bid — its gas limit times its gas price —
and the entire amount was transferred to the producer of the block that included
it.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

It has one structural consequence, and everything else follows from it: **bidding your true value
wins you nothing.** If a transaction is worth 100 to you and you bid 100, you capture no surplus
whether you win or lose. So you shade the bid downward — and how far down you shade depends entirely
on what you think everyone else is bidding.

### Why that is worse than it sounds

The usual complaint about first-price auctions is that guessing is annoying. The actual result is
sharper than that, and it is worth walking, because it is the thing EIP-1559 was built to fix.

**Subgoal 1 — grant the bidder every advantage except one.** Suppose there is room for one
transaction. Yours is worth 10 to you. And suppose you *know*, as a certainty, the strategy every
other bidder is using: each of them bids three quarters of their own value.

**Subgoal 2 — try to compute your optimal bid anyway.** You cannot. If the highest competing value
happens to be 10, the highest competing bid is 7.5 and your best bid is 7.51. If the highest
competing value happens to be 8, the highest competing bid is 6 and your best bid is
6.01.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

**Subgoal 3 — name what was missing.** Not the other bidders' strategy — you were handed that. Their
*values*. Knowing how everyone bids is not enough; the optimal bid depends on numbers that live
inside other people's heads.

That is the precise sense in which a first-price auction has no obvious optimal bid, and it is why
fee estimation was ever a product problem at all. Every wallet shipped its own estimator, they
disagreed with each other, and none of them could be right, because the quantity being estimated was
not a property of the chain.

EIP-1559's own motivation section lists this alongside three other defects of the status quo, and it
is worth seeing the whole list because only one of the four is about price. Fee levels were volatile
out of all proportion to the actual social cost of one more transaction. Users faced needless delays,
because a hard per-block gas limit meets naturally lumpy demand with no slack. First-price auctions
are known to be inefficient and to require complex fee estimation that still overpays. And a chain
with no issuance and only fees to pay producers is unstable in the long
run.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> Three of the four are mechanism complaints. None of them is "fees are
too high".

## Second price: you pay the runner-up's bid

The textbook fix is the second-price, or Vickrey, auction: the highest bidder wins, but pays the
*second*-highest bid.

The effect is worth deriving rather than memorising. Your bid now does exactly one thing — it
decides whether you win. It has no influence on what you pay, because what you pay is somebody
else's number. So raising your bid above your true value can only make you win at a price you did
not want, and lowering it below your true value can only make you lose at a price you would have
been happy with. Bidding your true value is optimal, and it is optimal *regardless of what anyone
else does*.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> You no longer have to model the other bidders at all — the
exact thing the previous section proved you cannot do.

That property is the one the next lesson names formally. It is genuinely excellent, and Ethereum
did not adopt it.

## Why it fails here: the auctioneer is a bidder

Every second-price result carries an assumption so standard that it is rarely written down: the
auction is implemented by a **trusted third party** who runs it and does not participate in
it.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

A block producer is not that. The producer decides which bids are included in the block, can add
bids of their own at no cost, and writes the record of what the auction contained. Give that party
a second-price rule and the numbers come apart immediately.

**Subgoal 1 — set up a concrete auction.** A block with room for three transactions, all the same
size. The three highest bids in the mempool are 10, 8 and 3. Under a second-price rule the top three
are included and each pays the lowest of the three winning bids.

**Subgoal 2 — price the honest outcome.** Three transactions at 3 each. The producer collects
3 × 3 = 9.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

**Subgoal 3 — let the producer bid.** They insert one fake transaction of their own bidding 8. Now
the three winners are the real 10, the real 8, and the fake 8; the price everyone pays is 8.

**Subgoal 4 — price the dishonest outcome.** Two *real* transactions at 8 each. The producer collects
2 × 8 = 16.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

**Subgoal 5 — read the two costs of deviating.** The producer gave up one real transaction's revenue
and paid a fee to itself on the fake one. Both are free: the excluded transaction was earning 3, and
a payment from a producer to itself nets to zero. Revenue rose from 9 to 16 — a 78% raise for
inserting one number that nobody can disprove.

The literature on the EIP-1559 fee market records the same conclusion from the other direction:
second-price and VCG-style mechanisms reduce bidder complexity and are efficient in ordinary
settings, but are easily gamed by producers who can artificially inflate demand for their own
blocks, and are additionally vulnerable to collusion.<Cite src="eip-1559-dynamics-arxiv" />

<Aside kind="warn">
There is a second, quieter problem, and it generalises past auctions. A textbook Vickrey auction
charges the winner the highest *losing* bid. On a blockchain that number does not exist: losing bids
are off-chain and therefore unimplementable, so the closest on-chain approximation is the lowest
*winning* bid — the version priced above.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

Whenever a mechanism's rule references a quantity that is not written into the chain, the
implementation quietly substitutes something the chain does know, and the guarantee attached to the
original quantity does not automatically follow the substitute.
</Aside>

<Answer>
They insert a bid of their own at, say, 99, and you pay 99 instead of 10.

Three details make this cheaper for the producer than it first looks.

**The fake bid costs nothing even if it "wins."** If their inserted bid somehow ends up highest,
they are paying the fee to themselves. There is no downside branch to price in — which is exactly
why the worked example above could raise revenue from 9 to 16 with no offsetting
loss.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

**They do not need the bid to be credible.** Nobody can distinguish a genuine competing bid from a
manufactured one, because the producer is the party who reports what was in the auction. Bids that
lost leave no trace.

**They do not need to be greedy, either.** Inserting 99 is conspicuous; inserting 60 captures most
of the surplus and looks like ordinary demand. A deviation that is profitable *and* indistinguishable
from honest behaviour is the exact profile the previous lesson flagged as the one worth worrying
about.

So the truthful-bidding property that made second price attractive is not merely weakened here — it
is inverted. You bid your true value and the auctioneer, who is also a bidder, takes all of it.
</Answer>
</Check>

## The result that reframes the whole module

It would be reasonable to read this lesson as: first price is bad, second price would be better but
is unsafe here, so Ethereum picked something clever and new.

That is not what happened, and the sharper story is worth carrying.

Call a mechanism **credible** if the party tasked with running it has no plausibly deniable
utility-improving deviation.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> The worked example above is precisely a
proof that a Vickrey auction is not credible in that sense: the profitable deviation exists, and it
is indistinguishable from honest operation. There is a theorem behind this, and it is unusually
blunt — among static, single-shot mechanisms, **first-price auctions with a restricted bid space are
the only credible ones**.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

Now look at where that leaves the design space. Ethereum did not escape the first-price auction.
EIP-1559, and the alternative designs analysed alongside it, are all first-price auctions with
*different restricted bid spaces*.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> What changed was not the family of
mechanism but which numbers a user is permitted to choose, and the entire improvement in user
experience comes from taking one of those numbers away from them and having the protocol announce it
instead.

That is the thread to pull through the next two lessons: the base fee is not a new kind of auction,
it is a restriction on what you are allowed to bid.

## Where this pays off

The producer's conflict of interest does not disappear when you stop paying them the fee; it moves.
Order-flow auctions, proposer-builder separation, and the outright sale of the right to order
transactions are all attempts to put a neutral party back into a position that structurally has
none — and each should be read against the credibility test above rather than against a textbook
auction result.

A **rollup sequencer** inherits the same conflict in a smaller system where it is also the only
party who can order anything, which is why "the sequencer runs a fair auction" is a claim about
governance rather than about mechanism design.

And the specific failure you just priced — a party inserting orders into a market it also settles —
is the shape of most of what gets called manipulation further up the stack, including in oracles and
in AMM routing. The lever is always the same: the auctioneer writes the record.

The next lesson gives this failure a formal name, alongside two others, and explains why one
incentive property was not enough.
