Most people arrive with one number: 51%. It came from proof of work, where a majority of hash power
is the single threshold that matters, and it does not survive the journey.

Under proof of stake there is no such number, because **different fractions of the stake buy
different properties, at prices that differ by orders of magnitude.** This lesson is the map. Read
it as a list of purchases, not a ladder of severity — the expensive ones are not simply "more" of
the cheap ones, and one of the cheapest is the one most likely to affect something you ship.

<Aside kind="note">
Two mechanisms are doing the work behind every row below, and it helps to keep them separate.
Ethereum's consensus is a finality gadget layered on a fork-choice rule — Casper FFG deciding what is
irreversible, LMD GHOST deciding which unfinalised branch is currently the head. The combination has
been analysed formally, with safety, plausible liveness and probabilistic liveness proved under
different assumptions.<Cite src="gasper-paper" /> The thresholds differ across the rows because
attacks on the fork choice and attacks on finality are attacks on two different machines.
</Aside>

## Well under a third: reorganising the recent, unfinalised head

Start at the bottom, because this is the part that gets skipped.

An attacker can withhold their own blocks and attestations and release them at a chosen moment,
forcing a reorganisation of the most recent blocks and taking advantage of it by double-spending or
front-running.<Cite src="low-cost-attacks-arxiv" /> A related strategy uses the same withholding to
delay finality, increasing both the average time to finalisation and its
variance.<Cite src="low-cost-attacks-arxiv" /> Both are analysed for a validator holding well under
one third of the stake.<Cite src="low-cost-attacks-arxiv" />

The documented requirement kept getting cheaper. A one-block ex-ante reorg was originally described
for an attacker with 30% of the stake, was later shown viable with 2% of the total stake, and then
for a single validator using balancing techniques.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

The paper behind the last step is worth knowing about by name, because its abstract is the clearest
statement of the situation. It takes two previously published attacks, relaxes their requirements
considerably — less stake, weaker assumptions about network timing — and then combines the techniques
into a third attack that lets an adversary with a *vanishingly small* fraction of stake, and **no
control over message propagation at all**, cause even long-range reorganisations of the consensus
chain.<Cite src="three-attacks-pos-ethereum" /> The authors are explicit that this is not only an
attacker's tool: honest-but-rational or ideologically motivated validators could use it to increase
their profits or stall the protocol, which is a threat to incentive alignment rather than merely to
uptime.<Cite src="three-attacks-pos-ethereum" />

Here is the part that matters for a cost model: **none of this triggers a slashing condition.**
Withholding a block and publishing it later is not a signed contradiction. Voting late is not a
signed contradiction. The attacker signs one thing, once, at an inconvenient time. There is no
evidence of an offence, because no offence in the protocol's sense occurred.

### What the protocol did about it

This class of attack has been answered, partially, and a cost model that ignores the defences is as
wrong as one that ignores the attacks.

Both the balancing and bouncing attacks depend on very fine control over message timing across the
network, which is itself a strong requirement.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> Against
them, the fork choice gives additional weight to messages that arrive promptly compared with slow
ones — proposer weight boosting.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> Separately, the
fork-choice rule was updated to discard equivocating validators from consideration altogether and to
discount their future influence, which closes the balancing attack while keeping resilience against
avalanche attacks — a different class again, requiring the attacker to control several *consecutive*
block proposers.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

The honest summary is therefore neither "the head is safe" nor "the head is free to rewrite". It is:
this is a live area where specific attacks have been published, specific mitigations shipped, and the
frontier has moved several times. Which is precisely why a product should not be relying on the
unfinalised head for anything it cannot undo.

The product consequence is direct. "My transaction is in the latest block" and "my transaction is
under a finalised checkpoint" are not two points on a confidence gradient. They are claims defended
by completely different mechanisms, one of which is free to attack.

## At or above one third: stalling finality

One third is the first structural threshold, and it buys a liveness attack rather than a safety one.

Finalisation needs a two-thirds supermajority. An attacker holding more than one third does not need
to control anyone else's behaviour, to time messages, or to sign anything incriminating. The
documentation puts the reason plainly: a third is a benchmark because beyond it an attacker can
prevent finalisation *without finely controlling the actions of the other validators* — they can
simply all disappear together.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> If a third or more of
the stake is failing to attest, a two-thirds supermajority cannot exist.

Nothing about that is slashable. It is absence, and absence is not attributable.

It is answered by a separate mechanism, the inactivity leak, which is the subject of the next
lesson. The short version: the stake of the non-attesting validators is bled away until the
remaining participants are a supermajority again, so the chain finalises. The attacker is not
punished for an offence — they are drained for being absent, and persistent inactivity across a third
of the total stake is very expensive *even though those validators are never
slashed*.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

## Around a third, with control of message timing: double finality

Just above the same threshold sits a genuinely different and far more expensive attack. Work the
arithmetic, because the "34%" figure looks arbitrary until you do.

**Subgoal 1 — equivocate as proposer.** On an asynchronous network, an attacker holding about 34% of
the stake proposes two conflicting blocks, then double-votes with all of their
validators.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

**Subgoal 2 — split the honest set.** The remaining 66% is nudged so that roughly half sees each
branch first.

**Subgoal 3 — count the votes on one branch.** The attacker's 34%, plus half of the honest 66%, is
about 67% — a supermajority. The same arithmetic holds on the other branch, because the attacker
voted on both. Two conflicting checkpoints
finalise.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

**Subgoal 4 — read the requirement you just used.** Each competing block had to reach about half of
the honest validators, so the attack is viable only with meaningful control over the timing of
message propagation.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> That is a strong assumption in
practice, and it is the reason this row is not simply "34% and you win".

**Subgoal 5 — read the bill.** Every one of the attacker's validators double-voted simultaneously.
That is a slashable offence incurred with the maximum correlation penalty, so the attacker
necessarily destroys their entire stake to achieve
it.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> This is the attack the finality-reversal cost model
prices, two lessons from now.

## Exactly half: two forks that both finalise

There is a case between the famous ones that almost never gets taught, and it is instructive because
its outcome is the worst on this page relative to its cost.

At 50% of the stake, an adversarial group can split the chain into two equally sized forks and vote
contrarily to the honest set, sustaining both and preventing either from finalising. The inactivity
leak, doing its job on each fork independently, then eventually causes *both* chains to finalise — at
which point the only remaining option is social
recovery.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

The documentation is also clear about why this is not the attack to fear: holding precisely 50%
through the ordinary flux in validator numbers and network latency is very unlikely, the cost is
huge, the success probability low — and a small additional investment past 50% unlocks a great deal
more power.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> It is a reminder that thresholds are not a
staircase of increasing damage; the mechanism has corners.

## Above half: the fork choice is yours

Majority stake buys control of the fork-choice rule. The attacker can attest with the majority vote,
so their preferred branch is the one honest clients also see as heaviest — no fooling required. That
gives them censorship by exclusion, short-range reorganisations, and the ability to extract maximum
value by reordering blocks in their
favour.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

What it does **not** buy is the reversal of a finalised checkpoint. That requires two conflicting
finalisations, and by accountable safety that requires signing the evidence that destroys the
attacking stake. Majority control of the fork choice does not exempt anyone from the counting
argument.

Note also what the documentation names as the defence at this level, because it is not a protocol
mechanism: the enormous cost of a majority stake *and* the fact that it is put at risk, because the
social layer is likely to adopt an honest minority fork and devalue the attacker's
stake.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> From here on, the protocol is no longer the only
thing defending the chain — the final lesson of this module takes that seriously.

## Two thirds or more: finalising whatever you like

At a supermajority the attacker no longer needs to coerce, time, or out-vote anyone. They vote for
their preferred fork and finalise it, because they *are* the supermajority. They control the contents
of finalised blocks, with the power to spend, rewind and spend
again.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

The documentation frames the step from a majority to a supermajority in the sharpest possible terms:
buying the difference is buying the ability to do ex-post reorganisations and finality
reversions — **to change the past as well as control the
future**.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> That single sentence is the best summary of
this whole lesson. Every row below it is about controlling the future. Only the last one is about
the past, and it is the only one the chain charges the full price for.

Read the last column downward and the striking thing is how often it says nothing. Exactly one row
on this page is priced by slashing. Every other defence on the list is either a fork-choice heuristic,
a slow drain, the market value of a large position, or people deciding which chain to follow.

That is not a flaw being confessed. It is what "security is a price" means in practice: the protocol
charges hard for the one violation it can prove, and everything else is defended by cost, by
difficulty, or by us.

An attacker controls a small single-digit percentage of the stake. What can they do to you, and what

<Answer>
They can reorganise the head block out of existence and have the deposit disappear, and it costs
them **nothing in slashing**.

The mechanics do not require a supermajority or any signed contradiction: withhold a block and the
attestations for it, release them at the moment that makes the fork containing your deposit lighter,
and the honest fork choice does the rest.<Cite src="low-cost-attacks-arxiv" /> One-block ex-ante
reorgs are documented well below the famous thresholds, including at 2% of the total stake and for a
single validator using balancing techniques<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />, and a
later line of work relaxes the requirement further still.<Cite src="three-attacks-pos-ethereum" />

The reasoning error in the sentence you were given is the one this whole module exists to fix: that
enormous quantity prices a *different property* — the irreversibility of finalised checkpoints — and
your product was never relying on that property. It was relying on the head block, which is defended
by fork-choice heuristics such as boosting prompt messages and discounting
equivocators<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />, none of which destroy anybody's capital.

Now notice which direction the error runs, because it is the useful lesson. Nobody misquoted the
security budget. The number was right; it was attached to the wrong claim. The fix is not more
confirmations chosen by feel. It is deciding, per action and per amount, which property you actually
need, and then waiting for the state that has it.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

The distinction between the unfinalised head and a finalised checkpoint reappears everywhere: in how
deep a reorganisation your **indexer** must survive, in how an **L2** decides when an L1 batch is
irreversible, and in the **forced-inclusion** escape hatches that exist because censorship is one of
the cheap purchases on this list rather than an exotic one.

It also sets up the two lessons that follow. The next one explains the two instruments the protocol
uses against the rows above — the slashing charge for signed contradictions, the inactivity leak for
absence — and why they are shaped so differently. The one after that takes the 34% row and turns it
into a number.

When you write a confirmation policy, this lesson is the menu you are choosing from.
