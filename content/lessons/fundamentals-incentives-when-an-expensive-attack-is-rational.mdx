Every number in this module has been a cost. A cost is only a defence if the payoff is bounded, and
the entire security argument rests on one sentence: *attacking costs more than it earns.*

So the last question of the module is the one that sentence smuggles past you: **how do you know
what it earns?**

## Four ways the bound breaks

The cost side is measurable — you have just derived it. The payoff side is not a property of the
chain at all. Four mechanisms unbound it most reliably.

**A short position.** The attacker does not need to steal anything. An attack that destroys
confidence in the chain moves the price of the asset, and a position taken beforehand pays out
precisely when the attack lands. The canonical documentation makes this explicit for the cheapest
attack on the list: a finality-delay attacker probably has no direct profit motive, *unless they
hold a strategic short position*.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> The stake being
destroyed is a cost that can be sized in advance and hedged against, and the hedge is sized by the
attacker, not by the protocol.

This inverts the condition that made the economics work. The argument for destroying stake as a
deterrent depends on the attack being a *sabotage*: carrying it out collapses the value of what the
attacker was capturing, so their outlay cannot be recovered.<Cite src="budish-economic-limits" /> A
short position turns that collapse from the attacker's punishment into their revenue line.

The timing compounds it. The hedge settles when the market moves, at the moment of the attack. The
protocol's charge does not: the correlation penalty lands thousands of epochs later, the withdrawable
epoch is pushed out by `EPOCHS_PER_SLASHINGS_VECTOR` — 8192 epochs in the mainnet preset, a little
over 36 days<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" /> —
and exits are metered at 256 ETH per epoch on top of
that.<Cite src="consensus-specs-config-mainnet" /> The bill is levied slowly, into a market the
attack already moved. The hedge does not wait.

**Reordering, at majority stake.** The documentation prices this as a capability, not an aspiration:
an attacker with a majority of the stake can censor transactions, perform short-range
reorganisations, and extract maximum MEV by reordering blocks in their
favour.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> It differs in kind because it is *recurring*. A
finality reversal is a one-shot trade against a one-shot burn; reordering is a revenue stream lasting
as long as the position does, and it never touches a slashing condition.

**A bridge holding a large balance.** A bridge accepts a message about one chain's state and releases
value on another. Reverse the message and the value has been released against a state that no longer
exists. The payoff is bounded not by the transaction but by the bridge's balance — accumulated by
users, not authorised by the chain.

**An exit window.** An L2 whose withdrawals depend on L1 finality has a period in which a reversal on
L1 invalidates the basis of an exit. The prize in that window is not one withdrawal; it is whatever
the bridge holds.

Underneath all four is the same structural fact: **the largest prize on a chain grows without the
chain's consent.** Security was a comparison, and someone else keeps changing the right-hand side.

## The payoff that is not profit

There is a category the comparison misses entirely, and the protocol's design literature is built
around it rather than around profit: the attacker who is not trying to earn anything, only to cause
damage. Ethereum's documentation says as much about the cheapest attacks — a finality-delay attack is
likely aimed *simply to disrupt* rather than to profit
directly.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> Against that attacker, "attacking costs more
than it earns" is not false. It is irrelevant.

The framework that takes this seriously is **griefing factor analysis**: the money lost by the
victims divided by the money lost by the attacker, with a protocol's factor being the highest it
permits. Zero means there is no way to make others lose money; infinity means you can do it at no cost
to yourself, or at a profit. The stated design principle is not "make attacks unprofitable" but the
stronger goal of bounding **the ratio of harm suffered to cost incurred**, in every direction an
attack can run: minority against the protocol, minority against the majority, majority against the
protocol, majority against the minority.<Cite src="vitalik-triangle-of-harm" />

That bound cannot be pushed arbitrarily low. Wherever **speaker/listener fault equivalence** holds —
where the chain cannot distinguish "A never sent the message" from "B refused to hear it" — the only
way to make both attacks expensive is to penalise both sides, and then either side can grief the
other. If one side griefs at factor *x*, the other griefs back at 1/*x*, and those cannot both be
below 1. So the protocol picks its asymmetry: a larger factor tolerated for majority attackers in
exchange for a smaller one for minorities, because minority attackers are more
likely.<Cite src="vitalik-triangle-of-harm" />

This is why the inactivity leak drains the absent without asking why, and why slashing prices
correlation rather than intent. Those are not rough edges. They are the shape a mechanism takes when
it bounds a *ratio* rather than forbidding an action — and the reason Casper penalises *equivocation*
specifically, so those who caused a safety failure pay and everyone else is left
alone.<Cite src="vitalik-triangle-of-harm" /> Contrast proof of work, where difficulty adjustment
holds long-run miner revenue roughly fixed: an attack that costs others revenue *transfers* it to the
attacker rather than destroying it, so harm and gain point the same way and the griefing factor is
unbounded.<Cite src="vitalik-triangle-of-harm" />

## The last defence is not in the protocol

Suppose an attacker does it anyway — enough stake, willing to burn it, payoff or grievance large
enough. What stops them keeping the winnings?

Nothing in the protocol. Recovery from a successful attack is difficult and often impossible *within*
the rules, which is why an extra-protocol strategy has to exist even though it should almost never be
used.<Cite src="vitalik-responding-to-51" /> The remaining defence is that the community coordinates
out of band on a fork that does not respect the attacker's chain.

### The recovery is not one thing

"Social consensus decides" is where most treatments stop, and it hides a taxonomy: the burden on the
community differs enormously by case.

**One chain finalises first — a minor burden.** The fork-choice rule clients implement forbids
reverting a finalised block, so every node that was online stays where it is. Only nodes offline
during the attack return to two candidates and have to ask which is legitimate: a few participants
asking what the consensus already is.<Cite src="vitalik-responding-to-51" />

**Two chains finalise simultaneously — a major burden.** The natural heuristic is to follow the chain
the fork choice prefers counting only unslashed validators, but it has a boundary case with no
principled answer: one chain wins the fork choice while the other finalised slightly earlier. The
community must then pick, partly arbitrarily.<Cite src="vitalik-responding-to-51" /> An attacker
capable of muddying which chain came first is buying exactly that ambiguity.

**The attack is censorship — there is a technical move.** The censored minority can run a *minority
soft fork*: refuse to build on the chain that will not include their attestations. Part of it can be
automated, since a client whose attestations are excluded can de-prioritise that chain in its own
fork choice until it stops looking dominant. The sharp part is what stops the attacker following. If
the minority waits until the attacker's chain has justified a new checkpoint before starting their
own, the attacker cannot join the minority chain without casting a surround vote and being slashed
for it.<Cite src="vitalik-responding-to-51" /> The social layer is not purely a show of hands — its
instruments are built from the slashing conditions you met two lessons ago.

Alongside these sit the protocol-level moves the documentation names: forcibly exit the attacker's
validators, pushing them back through an activation queue slow enough to buy the honest set a long
response window; and, if the community chooses, revoke past rewards or burn as much as the whole of
the attacker's staked capital.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

None of this is a patch over a gap. The design intent predates the need: the stated approach for
large-scale attacks is to force a chain split in which each side is penalised on one of the two
chains, then trust the market to pick the chain where attackers are not
favoured.<Cite src="vitalik-triangle-of-harm" /> The social layer is the mechanism's final step, and
the protocol's job is to make sure that when the choice reaches people, the evidence is already
recorded and the attacker already charged.

### Why it is a social fact

Every move above is a governance act rather than a protocol one, and it works only insofar as clients,
exchanges, applications and users agree on which chain is legitimate. Legitimacy is best defined as a
pattern of *higher-order acceptance*: people play their part because they expect everyone else to do
the same.<Cite src="vitalik-legitimacy" /> That tells you what the attacker has to break. Not anyone's
belief about which chain is correct — everyone's belief about *what everyone else believes*.

It has all the failure modes of a social process. Some users, possibly large ones, will have profited
on the dishonest chain and will oppose the fork to protect those gains; once ill-gotten value has been
mixed, deposited into other protocols or wrapped in derivatives, "unwind it fairly" stops having a
clean definition; and honest validators can end up penalised for attesting to the honest chain while
the dishonest one is canonical.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

It is also the thing proof of work does not have: there, a majority attack has historically been
answered by waiting for the attacker to lose interest, because the protocol cannot charge
them.<Cite src="vitalik-why-pos" /> Worse, the attack is profitable, so its proceeds can fund the
next one, and once the hardware is bought, repeating it is
cheap.<Cite src="vitalik-responding-to-51" />

### The attacker's counter-move: attack the coordination

If the last defence is out-of-band coordination, then degrading the ability to coordinate is itself
an attack — the cheapest one in this module. The documentation calls it a **layer-0 attack**: people
running no client software at all can target the social layer through misinformation that erodes
trust in the roadmap, the developers or the applications, and in many cases very little capital or
technical knowledge is required. Its real function is as a **multiplier** — if censorship or a
finality reversion has been achieved by a majority stakeholder, undermining the social layer first
makes the out-of-band response harder to mount.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

Read against the cost model, that is a serious result. Every figure in this module assumed the social
backstop holds, so a cheap attack that weakens it raises the expected value of an expensive attack
that needs it to fail. The two are complements, not alternatives, and a threat model pricing only the
expensive half is pricing a combination it never modelled. The named defences are correspondingly
unlike everything else here — a high signal-to-noise ratio for public information, a governance
process that distinguishes good actors from bad, a community open rather than
exclusive.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

<Aside kind="stop">
Recovery is a **race**, and speed is a security parameter. A response that lands quickly leaves the
attacker holding illiquid staked ether on a chain the ecosystem has agreed to ignore; one that does
not gives their chain time to become the one everybody is already building on. Hence the calls to
*rehearse* the response rather than improvise it.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> An
untested plan is a hope — and the same machinery covers a finalised but incorrect chain arising from
a bug in a majority client, with no attacker at
all.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> Client diversity is a consensus concern, not an
operations preference.
</Aside>

The obvious objection is: why not automate it? Part of it already is — a client can de-prioritise a
chain that refuses its attestations without anyone voting on
anything.<Cite src="vitalik-responding-to-51" /> What cannot be automated is the *judgement*.
Deciding which chain is blacklist-worthy means writing down in advance who decides what counts as an
attack, and handing that authority to whoever wrote the automation: the power the social layer exists
to check is exactly the power the automation would have. The documentation notes, drily, that a
layer-0 emergency response has already happened twice in Ethereum's
history.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> And the deterrent does not require the
response to be exercised — merely the *possibility* of an unfavourable determination raises the
expected cost of attacking, which is why the strategy must exist and be credible even though it
should almost never run.<Cite src="vitalik-responding-to-51" />

<Check type="explain" concept="attack-cost-is-not-attack-profit">
You are reviewing two designs that hold the same total value on the same chain.

**Design A**: a lending market where value is spread across thousands of independent positions, each
of which requires its own transaction to touch.

**Design B**: a bridge holding the same total in one contract, released by a single message proving
an L1 state.

The chain's cost of attack is identical for both. Why are they not equally safe?

<Answer>
Because the cost of attack is one side of a comparison and these two designs put very different
numbers on the other side.

Against design A, a successful reversal yields whatever the attacker can extract from the positions
they were party to. The prize is bounded by their own exposure and roughly linear in the effort they
put in — thousands of positions do not fall to one reversed message.

Against design B, a single reversed message releases the whole balance: the *entire* contract,
obtained with one action, growing every time a user deposits. The design converted a distributed
prize into a single-shot one, which is the specific shape that unbinds attacker profit.

The useful conclusion is not "bridges are bad". It is that **the payoff side of the security
comparison is a design variable you control**, and concentrating value behind a single message is
the most common way engineers hand an attacker a bound they did not intend to grant. When you write
the sentence "an attacker who wanted to reverse this would have to do X, which costs Y, and would
gain at most Z" — Z is the term your architecture decides.

Now run the comparison for an attacker who is not trying to profit. Against A, disruption costs a
great deal and inconveniences many people moderately. Against B, the same expenditure removes one
contract's entire balance and every application depending on it. The harm-per-unit-cost ratio — the
griefing factor — is worse for B too.<Cite src="vitalik-triangle-of-harm" /> Concentration loses on
both objective functions, which is stronger than the profit argument alone supports.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

This closes the module and opens the threat-modelling work that follows. Whether a bridge is a
honeypot, whether a rollup that withholds its data is acceptable, and how long your product waits
before treating value as received are the same comparison three times, with the same two sides — only
one of them set by the chain.

One concrete carry-away for the last of those: the recovery analysis makes a specific product
recommendation. A client that waits a short interval after finalisation before displaying a
transaction as final makes a double-finality attack substantially less useful for a double-spend,
because the ambiguity resolves inside the window rather than after the money has
moved.<Cite src="vitalik-responding-to-51" /> That is a defence you implement rather than wait for,
priced in a delay you choose.

The general form is the point. Every attack in this module is priced against a prize, and you build
the prize. Bound it deliberately — with limits, with delays, with distribution — or an attacker will
discover the bound you left implicit.
