Ethereum destroys most of what users pay in fees. Not redistributes, not escrows — destroys. The
money leaves circulation and no party receives it.

Almost every explanation of this you will encounter frames it as monetary policy: a supply sink, a
deflation story, a token-economics decision. That framing is wrong in a way that matters, because it
makes the burn look like a dial someone could turn. **The base fee is burned because there is
nowhere else it can go without breaking the mechanism.**

## The mechanism, briefly

Under EIP-1559 each block carries a **base fee per gas** set by the protocol rather than bid by
users.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> It is derived from the previous block: the block has a gas target
equal to its gas limit divided by an elasticity multiplier of 2, and the base fee moves up when the
parent block was above that target and down when it was below.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> The size of
that move is bounded — the adjustment is divided by a maximum-change denominator of 8, so the base
fee changes by at most one eighth, 12.5%, from one block to the next.<Cite src="eip-1559" />

A transaction then specifies two numbers: a **max priority fee per gas** — the tip it is willing to
give the producer — and a **max fee per gas**, the total it is willing to pay. The producer keeps
only the priority fee. The base fee is burned.<Cite src="eip-1559" />

Two consequences of the design are worth naming immediately.

**The clearing price is announced, not guessed.** This is the point of the whole exercise. Under a
first-price auction, the number you needed was a property of other people's private valuations.
Under this mechanism it is a field in the parent block header, and the recommended bid is simply
"pay it".<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

**Block size became elastic.** Because the target is half the limit, blocks can run at double the
target to absorb a burst, and the base fee then rises to push them back down.<Cite src="eip-1559" />
Congestion is handled by price over a few blocks rather than by an instantaneous bidding war.

That reality is the first of the ten conclusions in the formal analysis of the proposal, stated
before anything else: no transaction fee mechanism is likely to substantially decrease average
transaction fees, because the scarcity is real.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> What the mechanism was
expected to reduce was the *variance* of fees and the delays caused by a hard per-block
limit.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

## Why the producer cannot have it

Now the real question. The base fee is a real quantity of value collected from every transaction.
Why not pay it to the block producer, who is after all doing the work?

Run the coalition argument from the previous lesson.

**Subgoal 1 — assume the producer receives the base fee.** Call this design 1559-R, identical to
EIP-1559 except that base-fee revenue is refunded to the producer of the
block.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> Since producers can no longer be relied on to exclude
transactions bidding below the base fee, assume the protocol rejects those outright, so the rule is
enforced structurally.

**Subgoal 2 — find an arrangement both sides prefer.** Every user bids exactly the base fee
on-chain, and privately tells the producer what they would have bid in an old-style first-price
auction. The producer includes a transaction only if the user then transfers the difference between
that private bid and the base fee, off-chain.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

**Subgoal 3 — check what the mechanism sees.** A block full of transactions all paying exactly the
base fee. Perfectly ordinary. The side payments are invisible to it and to every honest participant.

**Subgoal 4 — read what the mechanism has become.** Not "slightly weakened". The outcomes reachable
under 1559-R-plus-side-deals correspond one-to-one with the outcomes of a plain first-price auction.
Transferring a block's base-fee revenue to that block's producer is **economically equivalent to
having no base fee at all**.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

That is a stronger and stranger result than "the producer might cheat". The base fee does not become
manipulable; it becomes *inert*. Every user-facing benefit — the announced price, the obvious bid,
the absence of estimation — is a benefit of value leaving the producer-and-user coalition, and if it
does not leave, the benefit was never purchased.

The structural fix is therefore to send the base fee somewhere **neither party to that deal can
reach**. If the producer does not receive it, there is no difference for them to split with the user,
and the side deal has nothing to offer.

### Two ways of half-doing it, both of which fail

Two compromises suggest themselves immediately, and the analysis kills both. They are worth knowing
because they are the two proposals that keep coming back.

**Burn only part of it.** Burning some fraction of the base fee and refunding the rest is not a
middle ground: it is economically equivalent to a *fully* burned base fee that is smaller by that
same fraction. Burn half, and the mechanism simply settles at roughly double the base fee, with half
of it burned and the rest split between producer and users through a side
deal.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> The report notes a live example — one chain shipped a variant
burning 70% of the base fee and routing 30% to contracts used in the previous
epoch.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> The partial refund does not buy a partial mechanism; it
rescales the number.

**Keep the first-price auction and burn the fees instead.** If the burn is what does the work, why
not skip the base fee and burn the winning bids? Because a coalition maximises what it keeps, and
what it keeps is everything not burned — so producer and users move *all* payments off-chain, bid
zero, and the burn collects nothing at all.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> The mechanism that was
supposed to destroy the most value destroys none.

Put those together and the report's sharpest conclusion follows: the seemingly orthogonal goals of
easy fee estimation and fee burning are **inextricably linked** through the threat of off-chain
agreements.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> A non-trivial burn requires a base fee, and a base fee is
only meaningful if it is withheld from the producer. You cannot have either one alone.

<Aside kind="note">
EIP-1559's own rationale gives a second, independent reason the base fee must be paid in ETH and
destroyed: it ensures only ETH can ever be used to pay for Ethereum transactions, cementing the
asset's role and reducing risks associated with miner-extractable value — and separately, that
keeping the base fee away from the producer removes their incentive to manipulate the fee in order
to extract more from users.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> Three different arguments, same conclusion.
</Aside>

## "Burned" is the implementation; "withheld" is the requirement

It is worth being precise about what the argument actually demands, because the difference is a real
design space rather than a quibble.

The requirement is that base-fee revenue not reach the producer *of that block*. Destruction is the
simplest way to guarantee it, and it needs no assumption about anyone's future behaviour. It is not
the only way.

Consider paying the revenue **forward**: the base fee collected in a block is split among the
producers of the next several blocks, over a window fixed in the protocol. A producer's payout is
then determined entirely by history and is unaffected by anything they do now — so it acts as a fixed
bonus, and every guarantee from the previous lesson survives unchanged. The mechanism is still
MMIC, fake transactions are still costly, the user-side guarantee still holds outside a demand
spike, and it is still OCA-proof.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

What changes is not incentive compatibility but who benefits — ETH holders under a burn, block
producers under pay-forward — and how variability lands. Under the burn, demand variability shows up
as variable issuance while the reward paid for producing a block stays roughly constant. Under
pay-forward, issuance is predictable and the reward for producing a block varies with
demand.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

And there is a failure mode worth carrying, because it appears elsewhere in this curriculum under
another name. If the pay-forward window is short, consecutive blocks carry very different rewards: a
block that follows an unusually lucrative one is worth attacking. A producer can try to build on the
*earlier* block, orphaning the block that would have collected the windfall, and take the reward
instead — which argues for a long window, so that consecutive blocks are worth nearly the
same.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> A design that made block rewards lumpy would have created a
reorg incentive out of nothing.

<Aside kind="warn">
The base-fee **update rule** is a separate choice from the destination of the revenue, and it is
much less settled than the 12.5% figure suggests. The report describes the rule as somewhat
arbitrary and expects it to be adjusted over time.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> A dynamical analysis
of the fee market makes the trade-off concrete: small enough step sizes in the update rule are proved
to converge to equilibrium, while larger step sizes admit instability and formally chaotic
behaviour.<Cite src="eip-1559-dynamics-arxiv" />

So "the base fee adjusts by at most 12.5% per block"<Cite src="eip-1559" /> is a parameter with a
stability argument behind it, not a law of nature. Treat any reasoning that depends on the *speed* of
adjustment — including how long a demand spike keeps the user-side guarantee suspended — as resting
on that parameter.
</Aside>

<Check type="explain" concept="base-fee-burn">
A new chain proposes: instead of burning the base fee, route it to an on-chain public-goods treasury
governed by token holders. The chain's designers argue this is strictly better — same removal from
the producer, plus the money funds something.

Does this preserve OCA-proofness?

<Answer>
It depends entirely on one thing: whether a producer can arrange to be paid out of that treasury.

The requirement is not "burn it". The requirement is that the destination be unreachable by the
producer-and-user coalition — which is exactly why paying the revenue forward to the producers of
*later* blocks keeps every guarantee intact, even though nothing is destroyed: no producer's payout
depends on anything they do in the block they are building.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" />

A treasury that no producer can ever benefit from satisfies the requirement. A treasury that funds
grants, subsidies, rebates or infrastructure a producer might receive does not — because a producer's
expected recovery from the treasury becomes a term in their calculation, and the coalition reappears
in a slower, more diffuse form.

And it does not have to be all-or-nothing to fail. If producers can expect to recover a fraction of
treasury spending, the arrangement behaves like a partial burn, which is equivalent to a smaller
fully-withheld base fee rather than to a full one.<Cite src="roughgarden-tfm" /> The mechanism
silently gets weaker in proportion to how much of the treasury flows back, and nobody has to do
anything visibly wrong for that to happen.

Burning is chosen because it is the one destination where none of this can be argued about. It needs
no assumption about governance staying honest, and it stays true no matter who controls the chain
later.

There is a second-order failure worth naming too: a treasury creates a party with an interest in the
base fee being *high*, which is a new actor with a new incentive in a mechanism that was carefully
designed around the incentives of the actors it already had.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

The two fee fields on every modern transaction — max fee and max priority fee — are this mechanism
surfaced in an API, and the gap between them is where transactions get stuck: a max fee that was
adequate when it was signed stops being adequate after a few blocks of 12.5%
increases.<Cite src="eip-1559" />

The same adjustment machinery, with its own target and its own burned fee, prices **blob space** for
rollups — so everything on this page about targets, elasticity and how fast a price can move applies
there, with different constants.

And when you read an L2's revenue figures, the distinction between what users paid and what the
operator kept is this lesson repeated one layer up. The question to ask of any such chain is the one
this lesson answers for Ethereum: is the sequencer's fee revenue withheld from the party that orders
the transactions, or is "the fee goes to the protocol" a sentence about an account somebody controls?
