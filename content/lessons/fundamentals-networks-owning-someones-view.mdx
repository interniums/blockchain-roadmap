Here is a question with an uncomfortable answer. What would it take to make a correctly running,
fully synced, unmodified node believe something false — **without breaking a single consensus rule?**

Not a bug. Not a fork. Not a majority of stake. The answer is: control every peer it talks to.

A node's beliefs are a pure function of the messages it receives. It validates every one of them
perfectly. If an adversary supplies all of the messages, the node validates a curated reality with
complete rigour and arrives at exactly the conclusion the adversary intended. That is an **eclipse
attack**, and it is the direct consequence of the topology you have spent three lessons building.

## What an eclipse actually is

The discv5 rationale document defines it plainly: an eclipse attack is usually based on generating
sybil nodes with the goal of polluting the victim's routing table, and once the table is overtaken
the victim "has no way to find any other nodes but those controlled by the
adversary".<Cite src="discv5-rationale" />

Note where the attack lands. Not on the consensus rules — on **discovery**. The candidate set a node
draws its peers from is a local data structure, populated by messages from strangers. Poison the
candidate set and you have not defeated validation; you have chosen its inputs.

What the victim then experiences is not obviously wrong. Blocks arrive, signatures verify, the chain
extends. It is simply a different chain, or the same chain with some things withheld: a stale head,
transactions that never propagate, a deposit that appears to confirm.

<Aside kind="note">
The rationale document also names a cruder route to the same outcome. Even if creating sybils were
impossible, a node can be eclipsed by directing enough traffic at it that it loses contact with the
network and is forced to re-bootstrap its routing table — "a situation in which it is most
vulnerable".<Cite src="discv5-rationale" /> A cold start is the moment of maximum exposure, which is
why several of the defences later in this lesson are about young nodes specifically.
</Aside>

## The cost, measured

Abstract threat models are easy to nod at and hard to take seriously. This one was measured, on the
real client, and the number is small.

The 2019 *False Friends* paper studied go-ethereum's peer selection. At the time, a geth node
maintained 25 connections by default: 17 inbound, and 8 outbound computed as one third of the maximum
peer count.<Cite src="eclipse-eth-paper" /> The outbound slots are the ones that matter, because
inbound connections are simply accepted from whoever dials in, while outbound connections are drawn
from the node's own discovery table.<Cite src="eclipse-eth-paper" />

The attack did not flood the discovery table with sybils. It inserted a limited number of adversarial
nodes with an activity pattern that biased geth's selection toward them.<Cite src="eclipse-eth-paper" />
The resource requirement the authors report:

> An adversary only needs two hosts in distinct /24 subnets to launch the eclipse.

Two hosts. Not a botnet — two IP addresses in different /24 blocks, plus around 18 generated node
identities.<Cite src="eclipse-eth-paper" /> In two of the authors' experiments the victim was reduced
to a single remaining honest connection after 4.875 days and 9.5 days
respectively.<Cite src="eclipse-eth-paper" />

The vulnerability was responsibly disclosed and countermeasures shipped in geth
v1.9.0.<Cite src="eclipse-eth-paper" /> Three of them are worth knowing because they show the shape
of the defences available:

1. **Double the connections.** Total peers went from 25 to 50, doubling the outbound slots an
   attacker must capture.<Cite src="eclipse-eth-paper" /> go-ethereum's `--maxpeers` still defaults
   to 50 today.<Cite src="geth-cli-options" />
2. **Select from the whole table.** The random-node selection function was changed to draw uniformly
   from all nodes in the table rather than from bucket heads, so an attacker must dominate the entire
   table rather than a privileged subset.<Cite src="eclipse-eth-paper" />
3. **Slow repeated inbound connections.** Since v1.9.0, an inbound attempt from an IP that has
   already connected has to wait 30 seconds.<Cite src="eclipse-eth-paper" />

The authors were explicit that these raise the bar rather than close the hole, and argued for
replacing the structured Kademlia-derived table with a large unstructured candidate set drawn from
uniformly at random.<Cite src="eclipse-eth-paper" /> They also noted the table's small absolute size:
17 buckets of at most 16 entries is 272 possible nodes, and real tables are far
emptier.<Cite src="eclipse-eth-paper" /> A structure that small does not need many adversarial entries
to be meaningfully polluted.

## Why identities are free, and what that forces

Every defence above is a workaround for one underlying fact, which the discv5 rationale states
directly: the sybil attack rests on the observation that **creating node identities is essentially
free**.<Cite src="discv5-rationale" />

A node identity is a keypair. Generating one costs a few microseconds of entropy. There is no
registry, no fee, no waiting period — by design, because any of those would need an authority, and an
authority is the thing this system exists to avoid.

The consequence is a rule with no exceptions: **any protocol logic of the form "if most of my peers
say X" is meaningless.** A thousand agreeing peers can be one adversary with a script. Counting
identities measures nothing unless something scarce sits behind each one.

That this remains a live problem, and not merely a historical one, was shown again in 2023. The
Tikuna monitoring work developed an eclipse exploit that filled a victim's peer-table buckets **from
a single attacking server with one public IP address**, defeating the per-IP restriction by
generating many crafted node records — secp256k1 keypairs producing fake peer IDs aimed at specific
buckets — and testing it against a modified consensus client on
mainnet.<Cite src="tikuna-monitoring" /> Per-IP limits raise the price of identities; they do not
create scarcity.

## The two answers, and why the difference matters

Ethereum's response to free identities is layered, and the layers are not the same kind of thing. If
you take one idea from this lesson, take this one.

### At the consensus layer: economic scarcity

Consensus weight is not denominated in nodes. It is denominated in staked ether. The whole analysis
of attacking Ethereum is framed as shares of *total stake* — a third of it, half of it, two thirds of
it — with each threshold unlocking different capabilities, and an attacker's objective described as
controlling as much staked ether as possible.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

Spinning up ten thousand consensus clients buys an attacker exactly nothing in fork choice, because
fork choice never counts them. This is what real sybil resistance looks like: influence is tied to a
scarce, externally costly resource, and the cost is not a heuristic to be tuned but a price to be
paid.

### At the networking layer: heuristics

Below consensus, there is no stake and nothing to price. The defences are therefore heuristics — good
ones, chosen carefully, and unable in principle to offer a guarantee.

The discv5 rationale recommends IP-based limits on routing-table content: for example, at most two
nodes per bucket and ten nodes per table from any single /24 subnet. The stated justification is that
IPv4 addresses are a scarce resource, and the authors argue subnet-based limits stay effective as
IPv6 adoption progresses.<Cite src="discv5-rationale" /> It further recommends not admitting new
bucket members on incoming contact unless the table is already well stocked from outbound queries,
running liveness checks on a schedule independent of incoming requests — checking on contact
"increases the risk of emptying the table through DoS" — and favouring long-lived
nodes.<Cite src="discv5-rationale" /> That last point is why the k-bucket eviction rule from lesson 1
protects the incumbent: age is the closest thing to a costly signal available
here.<Cite src="discv5-theory" />

The document is also candid about the option everyone proposes first. Making identity creation
computationally expensive is "well-researched" and rejected: resource-constrained devices such as
phones may not be able to join, and continual advances in hashing hardware mean the thresholds need
constant adjustment and "can never beat determined attackers".<Cite src="discv5-rationale" /> The
escape hatch left open is mixed identity schemes, so an optional constraint could be introduced
later.<Cite src="discv5-rationale" />

And the summary sentence, which you should keep: for any structured overlay network, sybil and eclipse
attacks must both be considered, and **there is no single optimal solution to fully protect against
them**.<Cite src="discv5-rationale" />

<Aside kind="warn">
These countermeasures are recommendations, and the discussion is still open. The devp2p issue asking
for more discv5 eclipse countermeasures was filed in 2019 and remains
open.<Cite src="devp2p-issue-109" /> A follow-up proposing rate limits on unsolicited messages, and a
trial period during which a new node responds only to peers it contacted first — so that it can
explore the network before an attacker can eclipse it — was filed in 2020, also remains open, and
opens with the line that clients should make their own decision about whether to implement
any of it.<Cite src="devp2p-issue-161" /> This is the policy-not-protocol point from lesson 1, at the
place where it is most expensive.
</Aside>

## What the consensus layer's gossip adds

The libp2p side carries its own defences, and they are more structural than discovery's, which makes
them a useful contrast.

**Outbound mesh quotas.** Gossipsub v1.1 defines `D_out`, a floor on how many of a node's mesh peers
must be connections that *it* dialled. When oversubscribed, a node preserves at least `D_out` outbound
peers; when it receives a graft request while at or above `D_high`, it accepts only outbound
connections. The specification states the goal in one sentence: a coordinated inbound attack "can
never fully take over the mesh of a target peer".<Cite src="libp2p-gossipsub-v1-1" /> That is a
structural bound, not a heuristic, and it is the same insight the eclipse paper reached from the other
direction — outbound slots are the ones worth defending, because the victim chose them.

**Peer scoring.** Each node locally computes a score for every peer from a weighted mix of
observations: time in mesh, first message deliveries, mesh delivery rate and failures, invalid
messages, an application-specific term, an IP colocation factor, and a behavioural
penalty.<Cite src="libp2p-gossipsub-v1-1" /> Two details are worth singling out. The IP colocation
term penalises many peers sharing an address, explicitly "to make it difficult to carry out sybil
attacks by using a small number of IPs".<Cite src="libp2p-gossipsub-v1-1" /> And scores are retained
for a period after a peer disconnects, so a misbehaving peer cannot reset its reputation by
reconnecting.<Cite src="libp2p-gossipsub-v1-1" />

**Adaptive gossip.** Under attack, a node increases how widely it emits `IHAVE` metadata. With the
recommended gossip factor and three rounds, each peer receives gossip about a fresh message with
probability about 0.578 — a floor prescribed, in the specification's words, to counter sybil attacks
and ensure an honest node's message propagates with high probability.<Cite src="libp2p-gossipsub-v1-1" />

The measured effect is substantial. In the gossipsub authors' testbed of 1000 honest nodes against
4000 sybils — a 20:1 connection ratio, deliberately worst-case — the attackers ended up occupying
between two and four connections in a node's mesh on average, and the protocol showed no message loss
and no latency penalty against its attack-free baseline.<Cite src="gossipsub-paper" /> Plain gossipsub
without scoring, in the same test, was "completely devastated".<Cite src="gossipsub-paper" />

But recall the caveat from lesson 2, because it is the whole point of this section: the consensus
specification does not fix the scoring parameters.<Cite src="consensus-specs-p2p" /> The mechanism is
specified; the weights that decide who gets dropped are each client's judgement.

<Check type="explain" concept="eclipse-attack">
An exchange credits deposits after seeing the transaction included and buried under enough blocks. It
runs its own node — deliberately, so as not to trust a provider — and reads confirmations from that
node alone.

Describe the attack this permits, say why no cryptography is broken, and give two changes that
actually reduce the exposure.

<Answer>
**The attack.** Eclipse the exchange's node. The attacker fills the node's discovery table and peer
slots with nodes it controls, then feeds it a private chain containing the deposit transaction. The
node validates every block correctly — signatures, state roots, everything — because they are
genuinely valid blocks on a chain nobody else is following. The confirmation count rises. The exchange
credits the account and the attacker withdraws. The private chain is then abandoned and the deposit
never existed on the chain everyone else follows.

**Why nothing is broken.** The node's software is correct and its verification is sound. Verification
answers "is what I received valid", never "is what I received everything". Absence of a competing
chain is indistinguishable from absence of peers who would have told you about one — and the attacker
supplied the peers.

**Two changes that reduce exposure.**

*Add independent observers.* Compare the head against sources that do not share a peer set — a second
node in a different network location, an external provider, a public explorer. The attacker now has
to eclipse several distinct vantage points at once, which is a different order of problem. Agreement
between sources with a common peer set proves nothing, so the independence is the whole value.

*Pin trusted peers.* The eclipse paper's own recommendation for high-value nodes is to stop relying on
purely trustless peer selection and statically include known, trusted peers, so that at least one
honest connection cannot be displaced.<Cite src="eclipse-eth-paper" /> This is a deliberate, bounded
reintroduction of trust — and stating it that way, rather than pretending it is free, is what the
threat-modelling material later in the curriculum will ask you to do.

*What does not help:* waiting for more confirmations. On a private chain the attacker produces,
confirmations are cheap. Depth is only evidence when it is depth on the chain the rest of the network
is building.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**Threat modelling.** You now have a concrete, measured example of an attack that breaks nothing and
compromises everything. The pattern generalises: ask not "is this component correct" but "who chooses
its inputs". That question is the spine of the security track.

**Provider architecture.** The rule for using RPC providers follows directly. Redundancy only buys you
something when the redundant sources have independent peer sets; two endpoints behind one operator's
infrastructure are one observation wearing two hats.

**Running nodes.** When you operate a node later, the settings that matter for this — peer counts,
static and trusted peers, discovery configuration — will look like ordinary tuning knobs. They are
not. They are the parameters of your own trust assumption, and this lesson is why.

Which is the question the module's reflection prompt asks you to answer in your own words: when your
product says "pending", what exactly did you observe, which node observed it, and what would have to
be true for a different node to disagree?
