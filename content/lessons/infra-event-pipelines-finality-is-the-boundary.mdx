The constant was called `CONFIRMATIONS_REQUIRED` and it was set to 12. It had been set to 12 in the
first commit, in a file that also contained a comment reading `// same as Coinbase`. It gated the job
that credited deposits, and it had never once been wrong.

It had also never been *right*, in the sense that nobody on the team could say what guarantee twelve
confirmations bought. Asked directly, the answers were "it's what exchanges use", "twelve blocks is
about two and a half minutes", and "deeper is safer". The first is true and irrelevant, the second is
arithmetic, and the third is a belief about a chain that stopped existing in September 2022.

This lesson replaces the constant with a boundary the chain will tell you about directly, deals with
the wall-clock cost of doing that honestly, and then does the part that catches people out: what the
same question means on an L2, where neither number is right.

## Why counting confirmations was ever reasonable

Under proof of work there was no explicit statement of finality anywhere in the protocol. What existed
was a probability that decayed with depth, because rewriting *n* blocks meant redoing *n* blocks of
work faster than the rest of the network extended the chain. Depth was a proxy for cost-to-revert, and
a confirmation count was a way of buying a probability with latency.

That heuristic has three properties worth naming, because all three are gone:

- It was **continuous** — there was no point at which the answer changed from "probably" to "yes".
- It was **externally calibrated** — you picked the number by deciding how much value you were
  protecting.
- It was **the only tool available** — the chain published no opinion about which blocks were settled.

Post-Merge Ethereum breaks all three. The chain publishes an opinion, the opinion is discrete, and it
is not a function of how much money you are moving.

## The boundary the chain hands you

You know the mechanism from [how long finality takes](/c/epoch-and-finality-timing) and
[finality as a spectrum](/c/finality-is-a-spectrum). What matters here is that the mechanism surfaces
as a value you can read on every request.

The execution-layer specification defines the block tags normatively, and the definitions are the
whole design:<Cite src="execution-apis-block-tags" />

- **`finalized`** — the most recent crypto-economically secure block, which cannot be re-orged outside
  of manual intervention driven by community coordination.
- **`safe`** — the most recent block that is safe from re-orgs under honest majority and certain
  synchronicity assumptions.
- **`latest`** — the most recent block in the canonical chain observed by the client; this block may
  be re-orged out of the canonical chain even under healthy/normal conditions.

Three tiers, defined by the assumptions each one rests on rather than by a count. `safe` assumes an
honest majority and some synchrony. `finalized` assumes an attacker unwilling to lose an enormous
amount of stake — ethereum.org states it as needing a majority slashing of stakers, with an attacker
reverting a finalized block committing to losing at least one-third of the total supply of staked
ETH.<Cite src="eth-pos-finality" /> `latest` assumes nothing, and promises nothing.

Reading it is one parameter. The block parameter accepts `earliest`, `latest`, `safe`, `finalized` and
`pending` on every state-querying method<Cite src="ethereum-org-json-rpc" />, and viem's `getBlock`
takes exactly that set as `blockTag`, defaulting to `latest`:<Cite src="viem-get-block" />

```ts
// viem 2.x
const finalized = await client.getBlock({ blockTag: 'finalized' });
const safe      = await client.getBlock({ blockTag: 'safe' });
// finalized.number is the boundary; everything above it is provisional
```

### One consequence people find uncomfortable

A confirmation count is tunable and a checkpoint is not. You cannot buy a stronger guarantee by
waiting longer past `finalized`, and you cannot buy a weaker-but-faster one by waiting a bit past
`safe` — those are the only two rungs the protocol publishes. The knob you *do* have is which rung each
consumer stands on, and that is the design decision the next lesson is built around.

## What it costs in wall-clock time, stated honestly

Here the sources disagree, and this material does not pick a winner.

**The mechanism's figure.** Checkpoints sit at epoch boundaries and Casper FFG justifies then
finalizes, which places finality roughly two epochs behind the head.<Cite src="eth-pos-finality" />
Working that through the slot and epoch constants you already have from
[epoch and finality timing](/c/epoch-and-finality-timing) gives about 12.8 minutes as the mechanism's
lower bound.

**An observed figure.** A third-party benchmark reported a median substantially higher — near 16
minutes over a 24-hour sampling window. That measurement was not reproduced for this material and the
two numbers were never reconciled.

**How to hold both.** Treat roughly 13 minutes as a floor rather than an estimate, and measure the
actual lag yourself against the endpoint you use, because `finalized` is what your provider's node
reports and providers are not identically fresh. If a product decision depends on the difference
between 13 and 16 minutes, the decision is too tightly coupled to a number nobody has pinned down.

Bilinear's practitioner write-up frames the same trade as three options rather than a number: handle
reorgs properly and stay at the head; index two or three blocks behind for roughly a thirty-second
delay and a much lower chance of hitting one; or index only finalized blocks for a delay of around
thirteen minutes and a very high guarantee that nothing you wrote will be
rewritten.<Cite src="bilinear-indexing-events" /> Those are three positions, not a spectrum, and each
suits different consumers of the same table.

<Aside kind="note">
`finalized` and `safe` are not universally available. The specification requires that, before the merge
transition is finalized, any call querying for a `finalized` or `safe` block **must** be responded to
with a `-39001: Unknown block` error.<Cite src="execution-apis-block-tags" /> On a chain that has not
merged, or a local devnet that never will, your finality gate has to degrade to something explicit
rather than silently reading a null.
</Aside>

## On an L2, neither number is the right one

This is the part that produces real losses, because the L2 user experience is deliberately built to
hide it.

An L2 sequencer confirms your transaction in seconds. That confirmation is an operator's promise, not
a consensus outcome, and OP Mainnet's own documentation lays out the ladder with timings and the
JSON-RPC call for each rung:<Cite src="op-transaction-statuses" />

Two details in that table deserve to be read twice.

**The "unsafe" rung has a real failure mode with a documented duration.** A transaction can still be
excluded from the final chain if the sequencer fails to publish the block to Ethereum within the
Sequencing Window, which the documentation puts at approximately 12
hours.<Cite src="op-transaction-statuses" /> Applications are told directly to consider this
possibility when displaying information about transactions in that state.<Cite src="op-transaction-statuses" />

**The upper bounds are twelve hours, not thirty minutes.** "Typically 15–30 minutes, up to 12 hours" is
one status, not two. A finality gate sized to the typical case is a gate that opens late during exactly
the incidents where it matters.

The OP Stack's interop documentation names the same three levels as block safety levels — unsafe (the
block has been produced and gossiped but its source data is not yet on L1, and the sequencer that
produced it could still equivocate), safe (once a block is safe, no participant including the sequencer
can roll it back without an L1 reorg), and finalized (the L1 data the block derived from is finalized
and no longer subject to L1 reorgs).<Cite src="op-interop-safety-levels" /> The cross-chain wrinkle is
worth knowing even if you never touch interop: a block is only treated as safe once every initiating
message it references has itself reached at least the same safety
level.<Cite src="op-interop-safety-levels" /> Safety composes downward, which means a cross-chain
product's boundary is the weakest chain in its dependency graph.

The workable news is that the mechanism is the same on both layers: `eth_getBlockByNumber` with `safe`
or `finalized` works on OP Mainnet and returns that chain's version of the
boundary.<Cite src="op-transaction-statuses" /> You do not need per-chain special-casing in the gate
itself. You need per-chain honesty about how far behind the gate sits.

## What this changes in a pipeline

Nothing about ingestion. You still index the tip, because a product that only ever sees finalized data
feels thirteen minutes broken. What changes is that the pipeline now carries a **second cursor**:

- `ingest_cursor` — how far you have consumed. Advances at the tip.
- `finalized_cursor` — the finalized block number, refreshed by polling
  `getBlock({ blockTag: 'finalized' })`.<Cite src="viem-get-block" />

Every consumer then declares which cursor it reads behind. That is a one-line property per consumer and
it is the entire architecture of the next lesson.

Both frameworks already maintain the equivalent internally. Ponder periodically drops finalized data
from its reorg transaction log<Cite src="ponder-indexing-overview" />, and Envio retains entity state
for unfinalized blocks specifically so it can roll them back.<Cite src="envio-reorgs" /> The boundary
exists in your system whether or not you named it; naming it is what lets your product features use it.

### Worked example: the finality gate, in four subgoals

**Subgoal: poll the boundary on its own schedule, not on the ingester's, so the gate keeps working when
ingestion stalls.** A separate small job reads `getBlock({ blockTag: 'finalized' })`<Cite src="viem-get-block" />
and writes the number and its observation time to one row. Polling every slot is wasteful — the value
changes at epoch boundaries<Cite src="eth-pos-finality" /> — and polling every ten minutes means your
gate can be a full epoch stale. Every 30 to 60 seconds is the uninteresting correct answer.

**Subgoal: make the value monotonic in your own store, because your provider may not be.** A
load-balanced endpoint can route two consecutive requests to two nodes at different heights, so the
`finalized` number you read can appear to move backwards. It never does on the chain; it can in your
process. Store `GREATEST(existing, observed)` and the problem disappears. Note that this is safe
precisely because finalized cannot be re-orged outside of manual intervention driven by community
coordination<Cite src="execution-apis-block-tags" /> — the same clamp applied to `latest` would be a
bug, because `latest` legitimately moves backwards during a reorg.<Cite src="quicknode-reorg" />

**Subgoal: record the observation time, so staleness is detectable rather than assumed.** A gate that
holds a finalized number from forty minutes ago behaves exactly like a healthy gate: it releases
things, just the wrong things — anything below a boundary the chain has long since passed is released
correctly, and everything above stays stuck forever with no error. The timestamp is what turns that
into an alert.

**Subgoal: define the fallback before you need it, and make it "stop", not "use the tip".** When the
finalized value is missing or stale, the tempting default is to fall back to `latest` so the product
keeps moving. That converts a monitoring problem into a payout problem. The correct fallback for an
irreversible action is to stop and page. For a read model, falling back to the tip is fine — provided
it is labelled, which is the next lesson's subject.

Four subgoals, roughly forty lines of code, one row of state. This is a smaller change than the
constant it replaces, and it is the only one in the module that turns an argument about a number into
a question with an answer.

<Check type="explain" concept="finality-vs-confirmations">
Your team runs a product on OP Mainnet that pays out referral rewards in fiat when a referred user's
first swap lands. The current gate is `CONFIRMATIONS_REQUIRED = 12` against the L2's block number,
which on a chain whose sequencer confirms a transaction typically within 2 to 4
seconds<Cite src="op-transaction-statuses" /> is well under a minute.

Someone proposes raising it to 200 confirmations — several minutes rather than seconds — as a
compromise between safety and user experience. Say what that buys, what it does not, and what you would do instead.

<Answer>
**What 200 L2 confirmations buys: almost nothing that 12 did not.** Both numbers measure depth below
the *sequencer's* head. The sequencer's confirmation is a promise from an operator, and the documented
risk at that rung is not that the block gets reorged out by depth — it is that the block is never
published to L1 within the Sequencing Window of approximately 12
hours.<Cite src="op-transaction-statuses" /> Waiting 200 blocks does not make publication more likely.
It waits inside the same failure mode, longer.

**What it costs: minutes of the thing the product was optimising for.** The referral payout was fast
because the L2 is fast. Trading seconds for minutes buys a guarantee that is qualitatively identical,
so the trade is pure loss.

**The confirmation-count framing is importing a proof-of-work assumption.** Depth-as-safety worked
because depth cost work. On an L2 the depth below the sequencer head costs the sequencer nothing, and
on L1 the relevant guarantee is a discrete checkpoint rather than a
probability.<Cite src="execution-apis-block-tags" />

**What to do instead: read the rung, and pick one per consumer.**

*For the fiat payout — gate on `finalized`.* Poll `getBlock({ blockTag: 'finalized' })` on the
L2<Cite src="viem-get-block" /><Cite src="op-transaction-statuses" /> and release only when the swap's
block number is at or below it. OP Mainnet documents this as typically 15 to 30 minutes and up to 12
hours<Cite src="op-transaction-statuses" />, and the "up to 12 hours" is the number to design the
communication around, not the typical case. A fiat transfer is irreversible; it belongs on the only
rung that is also irreversible.

*For the referral status shown in the interface — read the tip and label it.* "Swap detected, reward
pending settlement" is accurate at 2 seconds and costs nothing. The label is not decoration: an
optimistic value shown as though it were settled is how a correct architecture still produces a support
ticket.

*For the internal ledger entry — `safe` is a defensible middle.* Once a block is safe, no participant
including the sequencer can roll it back without an L1 reorg<Cite src="op-interop-safety-levels" />,
which is a strong enough claim for an accounting row that a later L1 reorg would correct.

**The structural answer.** The mistake is not the value of the constant. It is that one constant gates
consumers with completely different reversibility. A pending badge, a ledger row and a fiat payout have
three different tolerances, and the chain publishes three rungs. Map them.

**And the operational tail.** Whatever you choose, alert on the gap. If `latest` minus `finalized`
starts growing beyond its usual range, finality is not arriving — on L1 that is the condition the
inactivity leak exists to resolve<Cite src="eth-pos-finality" />, and on an L2 it means the sequencer
has stopped publishing. In both cases your payouts should stall rather than fall back to the tip, and
a stalled payout queue is a page.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

You now have the boundary, and the next lesson spends itself entirely on what to do with it.

- **The asymmetry.** Reads can be optimistic, because a revised number is recoverable. Irreversible
  actions cannot, because a revised action is not. Applying one latency to both is wrong in whichever
  direction you apply it.
- **The scope problem.** The framework rollbacks you met in the last lesson cover the indexer's own
  tables<Cite src="envio-reorgs" /> and nothing you copied downstream. Finality is the boundary that
  makes downstream copies safe.
- **The monitoring signal.** `latest` minus `finalized` is one of the numbers worth graphing, alongside
  cursor lag, and both of them are the last lesson in this module.

The thing to do this week is a grep: find every constant in your codebase whose name contains
`CONFIRMATION`, and for each one write down which consumer depends on it and whether that consumer's
action can be undone. That list is the input to the next lesson, and in most codebases it is shorter
and more alarming than people expect.
