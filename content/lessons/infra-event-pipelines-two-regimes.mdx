The backfill finished at 04:12 on a Sunday. Eleven hours, 4.1 million blocks, nineteen million rows,
and a checksum that matched the reference export exactly. The same binary then switched into its
second job — following the head — and for nineteen days it did that job correctly too.

On day twenty the accounting export was short by 412 rows, all from a four-minute window three weeks
earlier. Nothing had errored. No alert had fired. The code that wrote those rows was the same code
that had written the other nineteen million.

That is the shape of this whole module, and it is worth naming before anything else. **A pipeline's
two regimes share source files and share almost none of their assumptions.** The historical backfill
is a bounded computation over an input that will never change again. The live tail is an unbounded
computation over an input whose last few blocks are still being decided. Every property you verified
during the eleven hours was a property of the first thing. The bug lived in the second.

This lesson separates them properly, then does the engineering work for the half you can actually
finish: chunking a historical range against caps that are not where your 2024 constants think they
are.

## The two regimes, side by side

Ponder's documentation states the split plainly: internally the indexing engine has two distinct
modes, backfill and live indexing, which use different approaches for fetching data from the RPC,
while indexing-function logic works the same way in both.<Cite src="ponder-indexing-overview" /> That
second half is the useful design goal and the dangerous one. Your handler *should* be
regime-agnostic. Everything around your handler is not.

The asymmetry in the last two rows is the reason this module exists. A backfill that goes wrong tells
you: requests fail, the job dies, the dashboard shows a red bar, someone looks. A live tail that goes
wrong hands you rows that are the right shape, the right type, and describing something that did not
happen.

### What is genuinely deterministic about backfill

Take a historical range that sits well below the finalized checkpoint. Ask for it a hundred times and
you get the same logs in the same order, because that history is settled — the execution-layer spec
defines `finalized` as the most recent crypto-economically secure block, which cannot be re-orged
outside of manual intervention driven by community
coordination.<Cite src="execution-apis-block-tags" /> That single property is what makes backfill an
ordinary distributed batch job. Ranges are independent, so workers are independent. A worker that dies
mid-range can be restarted on the same range. Two workers that accidentally get the same range produce
the same rows.

It also means the whole regime is a throughput problem with a cost attached, and both of those have
knobs. That is the rest of this lesson.

### What is genuinely non-deterministic about the tail

Ask for the last three blocks a hundred times and you can get different answers, because the tip is
not settled. The spec is direct about it: `latest` is the most recent block in the canonical chain
observed by the client, and this block may be re-orged out of the canonical chain even under
healthy/normal conditions.<Cite src="execution-apis-block-tags" /> "Even under healthy/normal
conditions" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. It is not describing an incident.

Three consequences, all of which get their own lesson later:

- The rows you already wrote can stop being true. That is [what a reorg does](/c/reorg-definition).
- Your transport can hand you the same event twice, or skip past a gap after a reconnect. Geth's
  pub-sub interface exposes exactly this shape — you subscribe and receive notifications as blocks
  arrive<Cite src="geth-pubsub" /> — and a subscription that drops and re-establishes has no
  obligation to tell you what happened while it was gone.
- Because the tail never ends, every process restart, deploy and crash happens *inside* it.

### The tail's transport is a choice, and both options are lossy

Backfill has exactly one transport: you ask for a range and it answers. The tail has two, and neither
is complete on its own.

**Polling.** You ask every *n* seconds whether there is a new block. The cost is a worst-case delay of
*n*, and the benefit is that a poll is stateless — there is nothing to reconnect, so there is nothing
to reconnect *past*. Ponder's per-chain `pollingInterval` documents a default of 1,000
milliseconds.<Cite src="ponder-config-reference" />

**Subscription.** You hold a socket open and the node pushes notifications as blocks
arrive.<Cite src="geth-pubsub" /> You learn about a block immediately, and you inherit the connection's
failure modes: the socket can drop, and you can be offline for a stretch without the client raising
anything you would call an error.

Bilinear's recommendation after building this in production is to run both — subscriptions for
latency, polling as the backstop — and, regardless of transport, to constantly verify that the next
block you see is the previous one plus one.<Cite src="bilinear-indexing-events" /> That check is worth
noticing because it is the same shape as the contiguity invariant in the last lesson of this module:
the transport will not tell you it skipped, so you assert that it did not.

## The engineering half you can finish today: chunking

A historical range does not arrive in one request. You break it into chunks, and the chunk size is the
only tuning parameter you get. It is worth being precise about why the cap exists, because there are
two of them and they behave differently.

**Subgoal: name the two caps, so you can tell which one you are hitting from an error message.** The
spec's `eth_getLogs` filter takes `fromBlock` and `toBlock` and imposes no limit of its own — the
method's normative schema is a filter object, not a quota.<Cite src="execution-apis-getlogs-method" />
The limits are provider policy, and there are two independent ones.

- **A block-span cap.** Alchemy publishes this as a table by plan tier: on Ethereum, Base, Optimism,
  Arbitrum, Polygon and several others, a free-tier key is capped at 10 blocks per query while paid
  tiers are unlimited; on "all other chains" the paid ceiling is 10,000, with a footnote that queries
  wider than 10k on those chains are possible but less likely to
  succeed.<Cite src="alchemy-eth-getlogs" />
- **A result-size cap.** The same page states that all responses are capped at
  150MB.<Cite src="alchemy-eth-getlogs" />

**Subgoal: see why one number cannot satisfy both.** These two caps bind under opposite conditions. A
quiet range — a contract that emitted nothing for a month — hits the span cap: you are burning
requests to walk past empty blocks. A busy range — a token during a launch — hits the size cap: a
single 500-block window returns more than the response can carry, and the request fails or truncates.
Any fixed chunk size is therefore wrong at one end of your history. Bilinear Labs' practitioner
write-up lands on the same place from the other direction, recommending something around 1,000 blocks
as a working default, observing that more than 10,000 is definitely too much, and noting that the
right size depends entirely on whether the contract you are indexing has tons of events or almost
none.<Cite src="bilinear-indexing-events" />

**Subgoal: pick the adaptive strategy rather than the constant.** The move that actually works is to
target a *result count* rather than a block span: if a chunk returned far fewer logs than your target,
widen the next one; if it returned too many or failed, narrow it. Bilinear describes this as a binary
search over the range, aiming to fetch a given number of events per
request.<Cite src="bilinear-indexing-events" /> Ponder ships a version of the same idea as
configuration: `ethGetLogsBlockRange` sets the maximum block range for `eth_getLogs` requests, and
its documented default is undefined, in which case Ponder will attempt to determine the block range
automatically based on error messages.<Cite src="ponder-config-reference" /> Read that last clause
carefully. The framework is parsing provider error strings, because the caps are not discoverable any
other way.

<Aside kind="note">
There is no standard method for asking a provider what its `eth_getLogs` limits are. The spec does not
define one<Cite src="execution-apis-getlogs-method" />, so every indexer discovers the caps by
failing. That is why chunk-size code that works against one provider fails against another, and why
"switching providers is a URL change" is only true if you never issue a wide log query.
</Aside>

### The constants you inherited are now wrong

Chunk sizes copied from pre-2026 material assume a block that carries fewer logs than a 2026 block
does. EIP-7935 raises the default block gas limit to 60M, up from the 36M it describes as the mainnet
value at the time of writing.<Cite src="eip-7935" /> More gas per block means more transactions per
block means more logs per block, so a chunk size tuned when a block was half the size now returns
roughly twice the payload for the same span.

The practical effect is not a clean failure. It is a chunk size that worked for two years starting to
hit the 150MB response ceiling<Cite src="alchemy-eth-getlogs" /> during busy ranges only — so the
backfill fails at 60% on Tuesday, succeeds on Wednesday when you retry the same range while the target
contract is quiet, and nobody writes it down. **Re-measure chunk size against a busy historical
range, not an average one.**

### Chunk size is a bill, not only a setting

Halving the chunk size doubles the request count for the same history. You know from
[the expensive methods](/c/getlogs-is-the-cost-driver) that log queries are the ones that dominate an
RPC bill, so this is a direct trade: smaller chunks buy reliability with money, larger chunks buy
money with failures. There is no free position on that curve — there is only the position you chose
deliberately and the one you inherited.

Worth being honest about one thing here: the per-request cost of `eth_getLogs` is not something to
quote confidently. The provider's own documentation table and third-party comparisons of the same
provider disagree about whether the charge is flat per request or scales with the number of logs
returned, and this material did not resolve that.<Cite src="alchemy-eth-getlogs" /><Cite src="alchemy-compute-units" />
Measure your own bill against your own request count; do not budget from a blog post's multiplier.

### The property that makes parallelism safe

Chunks can only run in parallel if each one is independently retryable, and a chunk is only
independently retryable if re-running it is harmless. That is idempotency, and it is the subject of
the next lesson — but note where the requirement *comes from*. It is not a nice-to-have bolted on for
robustness. It is the precondition for the parallelism that makes backfill finish this week instead of
next month.<Cite src="bilinear-indexing-events" />

Two more things Bilinear's write-up flags that are easy to skip and expensive to skip:

- **Backpressure.** Extraction is usually faster than transform-and-load, so an unbounded in-memory
  queue between them grows until the process dies. Cap the queue and pause fetching when it
  fills.<Cite src="bilinear-indexing-events" />
- **Batch inserts.** Inserting 1,000 events one at a time is dramatically slower than one batch
  insert of 1,000, with the caveat that if event *n* depends on reading event *n−1*, you need the
  batch to let you read your own writes or you must commit sooner.<Cite src="bilinear-indexing-events" />

## Where the regimes meet, and why the seam leaks

The handoff is the moment backfill's last chunk lands and the tail takes over. It is a seam, and seams
leak in one specific way: the tail starts from a block number that backfill has not finished writing,
or backfill's final chunk overlaps blocks the tail already consumed. Both are invisible until someone
counts.

The frameworks handle the seam by never really having one. Ponder's engine keeps a trigger-based
transaction log of every change and, on a restart with identical configuration, rolls back all
unfinalized changes and resumes from the finalized block — the same machinery it uses for reorg
reconciliation.<Cite src="ponder-indexing-overview" /> Envio's HyperIndex takes the parallel approach
of retaining unfinalized entity state so it can roll back, with reorg handling enabled by default via
`rollback_on_reorg`.<Cite src="envio-reorgs" /> In both cases the boundary that matters is not
"backfill ended" but "this block is finalized", which is a much better boundary because the chain
defines it rather than your scheduler.

If you are writing the pipeline yourself, copy that: **make the handoff a block number derived from
finality, not from whichever job happened to finish.**

There is a second seam that is easier to miss: the one you create yourself every time you restart the
process. A pipeline that has been tailing for three weeks and is then redeployed has to backfill the
minutes it was down, using the backfill code path, at a point in history that is *not* below finality.
So the "backfill only reads settled history" simplification is true for the initial run and false for
every catch-up after it. Ponder's configuration makes the distinction visible in an unrelated place —
a contract with an `endBlock` set is not indexed in realtime at all, because a bounded range is a
different object from an open one.<Cite src="ponder-config-reference" /> Your catch-up path is bounded
in the same sense but sits in the unsettled zone, which means it needs the tail's guarantees and the
backfill's chunking at the same time. That combination is the single most common place a
hand-written pipeline loses rows.

That benchmark point is not a digression — it is the same regime split seen from outside. Published
indexer speed figures measure backfill, because backfill is the part with an end you can put a
stopwatch on.<Cite src="sentio-obib" /> Nothing in a sync-time benchmark tells you how the tool
behaves on day twenty of a live tail, which is where the 412 rows went.

You are indexing one contract's `Transfer` events. Your ingester uses a fixed chunk of 2,000 blocks
and a worker pool of eight. It has one code path: fetch a chunk, write the rows, advance.

You point it at the historical range and it completes cleanly. You then point the same code at the
head by having it fetch `[head - 2000, head]` every twelve seconds.

Name three distinct failures that this second configuration will produce and the first configuration
will not, and say which of them would show up in your logs.

<Answer>
**Failure 1 — duplicate rows from overlapping windows.** Twelve seconds is roughly one slot, so
consecutive fetches of `[head - 2000, head]` overlap by about 1,999 blocks. Every event is fetched
about 2,000 times. In backfill the ranges were disjoint, so the same code never had to survive
re-delivery. Whether this corrupts anything depends entirely on whether your write is idempotent on
the event's natural key — which is the next lesson, and the reason it is the next lesson.
**Visibility:** none. The inserts succeed. Aggregates are quietly too large.

**Failure 2 — rows describing blocks that stopped existing.** The spec is explicit that `latest` may
be re-orged out even under healthy conditions.<Cite src="execution-apis-block-tags" /> Your backfill
range sat below finality, so this could not happen there. At the head it happens routinely, and the
row you wrote for a discarded block does not delete itself. **Visibility:** none, unless you compare
against the canonical chain. QuickNode's Streams documentation describes the detection signal you
would have to implement yourself — a reorg is identified when a new block's parent hash does not match
the hash of the previous block that was streamed.<Cite src="quicknode-streams-reorgs" />

**Failure 3 — a permanent gap after any pause longer than the window.** If the process is down for
five minutes, the head moves about 25 blocks; your fixed window still covers it, so you get lucky. If
it is down for ninety minutes, or if the chain produces blocks faster than your fetch interval on a
different network, the next window starts above where you stopped and the blocks in between are never
requested by anything. **Visibility:** none. This is the failure that produces exactly the 412-row
shortfall in the opening scene, and it is why the cursor lesson insists the cursor be a persisted
number rather than an offset from the head.

**The one that would show up in your logs:** none of them, which is the point. Backfill fails loudly
because it fails against a provider that returns errors. The tail fails silently because everything it
does returns `200 OK`. If you want a signal you have to assert one — contiguity and lag — and that is
the last lesson in this module.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

You now have the frame the rest of the module hangs on. Each remaining lesson is a specific defect
that lives on one side of the split:

- The **natural key** and the **idempotent write** are what make a chunk independently retryable, so
  they are backfill's precondition and the tail's only defence against re-delivery.
- The **cursor in the same transaction** is what removes the gap between "rows written" and "progress
  recorded", which is a live-tail failure that a backfill can never show you.
- **Reorg handling** and **finality** are about the tail exclusively, because backfill by definition
  reads history that is already settled.
- **Re-backfill** is about the moment those two regimes collide: a schema change means running the
  bounded job again while the unbounded one keeps running.
- **Gap detection** is the instrument you need because, as the answer above says three times, none of
  this shows up in your logs.

The immediate practical takeaway is smaller and worth doing this week: go and find the chunk size in
your pipeline, find out when it was last chosen, and re-run one busy historical range with it. If that
number predates the 60M default gas limit<Cite src="eip-7935" />, it is a number chosen for a
different chain than the one you are indexing now.
