Open the published emergency pause runbook expecting a document about smart contracts, and the first
surprise is how little of it is about smart contracts.

Its "Immediate Actions (First 30 Minutes)" block is three steps. Step one, **alert team**: send an
alert to the emergency Signal group, page signers via the configured paging system, notify the
security contact — with a literal alert template to fill in. Step two, **assess situation**: confirm
the threat is real rather than a false alarm, identify affected contracts or assets, determine which
pause function to call, estimate urgency. Step three, **prepare transaction**: the proposer, who may
be any signer or a delegated proposer, opens the wallet UI, uses the transaction builder, selects the
target contract, selects `pause()` or the appropriate emergency function, and creates the
transaction.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-emergency-pause-runbook" />

Then a signing process, described as streamlined: emergency signing follows abbreviated verification,
with a six-item minimum checklist — correct multisig address, correct network, target contract address
verified, function is `pause()` or the expected emergency function, no unexpected additional calls,
hash matches the hardware wallet. Sign immediately after verification. Post `Signed - [X/Y] - [your
name]`. **Stay available until executed.** Execute as soon as the threshold is reached, verify the
pause took effect by checking contract state, and post `EXECUTED - pause
confirmed`.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-emergency-pause-runbook" />

Count what is technical in that. One line: `pause()`. Everything else is paging, people, verification
discipline and a shared channel. **That is what a pause capability is made of**, and it is why a team
can ship a correct `Pausable` implementation and still not be able to pause.

It is also explicit about when it applies: an active exploit, suspicious activity on protocol
contracts, a key compromise, or a vulnerability disclosure requiring immediate
action.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-emergency-pause-runbook" /> Three of those four are not "our
contract has a bug", and a team that rehearsed only the bug case is improvising in most of them.

## The five things that actually fail

Each of these is in the runbook because it has gone wrong.

**Nobody is reached.** The runbook names an emergency Signal group *and* a configured paging system
*and* a security contact — three channels, because any one of them has a failure mode. Immunefi's
readiness checklist is more pointed about the human side: reachability at 3am on a Sunday for every
critical contact, with do-not-disturb settings needing configured exceptions, and phone numbers,
backup channels and escalation paths verified live rather than
assumed.<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" /> A phone on silent is an unavailable signer, and SEAL's
on-call expectation — keep the alerting device accessible, respond to pages within 15 minutes — holds
only if somebody defined what that device is.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-ir-roles" />

**Nobody knows what to sign.** Under time pressure, a signer presented with an unfamiliar transaction
either signs without checking — which is how a compromised proposer wins — or stalls. The abbreviated
checklist exists to give a third option: a fixed, short list that can be completed in under a minute
and still catches the substitution attacks. Note its last item, *hash matches hardware wallet*: this
is the one that survives a compromised UI, and it is last because it is the one people skip.

**The threshold is not reached in time.** The runbook has a whole escalation branch for this: contact
backup signers, escalate to decision makers, consider alternative mitigations, and **document why the
threshold was not met**.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-emergency-pause-runbook" /> That last item is the
one to notice. It presumes failure is a live outcome and turns it into information for the
post-mortem, rather than a thing to be embarrassed about later.

**Signers disappear after signing.** "Stay available until executed" is a rule because the natural
behaviour after signing is to close the laptop. If four of five sign and the fifth is asleep, the
transaction sits. If the executor's transaction gets stuck at a stale gas price, someone has to notice.

**The wallet UI is down.** This is the item almost nobody has. The runbook's "If Primary UI is Down"
section names backup infrastructure by product — Eternal Safe for EVM, configured with a backup RPC,
loading the Safe address and building a custom transaction from pause calldata; the equivalent backup
path on Solana — and cross-references a whole page on backup signing and
infrastructure.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-emergency-pause-runbook" /> Your emergency plan depends on a
hosted web application belonging to somebody else. That is a single point of failure sitting inside a
control you built specifically to remove single points of failure.

## Prepared calldata, and why it is not premature

The single highest-leverage preparation is that the pause transaction should not be composed for the
first time at 03:50. Compose it now, in daylight: the exact target address, the exact function
selector, the exact arguments. Simulate it against a fork at head. Save the resulting calldata in the
runbook, next to the contract address and the expected behaviour — the runbook's own contract table
has columns for exactly that: contract, address, pause function, expected
behaviour.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-emergency-pause-runbook" />

This is the same technique you used for [rehearsing an upgrade at head](/c/upgrade-rehearsal), pointed
at a different transaction: fork at a recent block, impersonate the guardian, execute the real
calldata, then assert the intended effect and the unintended ones. It answers questions that are
expensive to answer live. Does `pause()` revert if it is already paused? Which functions actually stop
— did somebody add an entry point last quarter without the modifier? Does an in-flight liquidation in
your integrator's contract now revert? What is the gas cost at a stressed base fee?

The objection is that a saved calldata blob goes stale when you redeploy. Correct — which is why the
runbook has a `Last Updated` field in its quick reference, and why regenerating it belongs in your
deploy checklist rather than in a quarterly review.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-emergency-pause-runbook" />
Stale calldata that reverts is worse than none, because it consumes the minutes you were saving.

### The map the calldata sits inside

Prepared calldata for the contracts you remember is a partial capability. The other artifact on
Immunefi's readiness list is a full infrastructure map: every deployed contract, every admin key,
every signer on every multisig, and every external dependency — with the observation that mapping this
under fire is how a team misses the actual attack
surface.<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" /> That map is also what makes the fourth triage question
answerable in two minutes instead of forty, because "which of our deployments has this code" is a
lookup rather than an investigation. Generate it rather than writing it: a script walking your deploy
artifacts across every chain and reporting code hashes stays true through a redeploy in a way a
hand-maintained page will not.

## Runbooks decay; drills do not

A runbook is a hypothesis about what your team can do. Nothing in the document has been tested, and
documents record intentions.

The published preparedness lists say this in their own way. Immunefi's baseline readiness checklist
ends with **at least one tabletop exercise or war game**, on the grounds that a simulated incident
reveals exactly where the playbook breaks down and gives the team a chance to fix it before it
matters — and the same checklist is blunt that most teams skip most of the preparation, which is why
most teams struggle when the call comes.<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" /> SEAL's on-call expectations
put participating in tabletop exercises in the *off-shift* responsibilities, alongside staying current
on documentation and reviewing new post-mortems.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-ir-roles" /> Rehearsal is
scheduled work, not an event.

The ecosystem now runs organised versions of this: SEAL Wargames are described as live security drills
that simulate realistic exploit scenarios such as oracle manipulation and leaked private keys, so
protocol teams can pressure-test their detection, coordination and response before a real attack
hits.<Cite src="seal-our-work" /> The scenario list is worth noticing — a leaked private key is on it,
which is the case your contract-bug rehearsal does not cover.

### Two formats, two different things tested

SEAL's first-responder training lists the prerequisites for both: review the incident-response policy
and the log and post-mortem templates, read two or three past post-mortems, understand the basic
architecture, know how to reach the SMEs and decision makers, and **test access to the alerting
system**.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-ir-roles" /> Only the last is a test rather than a reading
assignment, and it is the one that fails.

**Tabletop** costs an hour and a room. Someone narrates the incident, and everyone else says what they
would do and who they would tell. It tests decision-making, role separation, escalation and the
comms cadence, and it finds the gaps that are about *knowledge*: nobody knows who has authority to
approve a bounty offer, nobody knows the SEAL 911 contact path, two people believe they are the
incident leader.

**Live-fire** costs a day and a forked mainnet. Someone runs a scripted drain against a deployed test
protocol while the rest of the team must detect it and get a pause transaction included. It tests the
mechanics, and it finds the gaps that are about *artifacts*: the calldata reverts, a signer's hardware
wallet is flat, the fork environment takes twenty minutes to stand up, nobody has the credentials for
the announcement account. Verify access to the tools first — alerting system, communication
platform, video conferencing, monitoring dashboards, on-call schedule, contacts
list.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-ir-roles" />

## Worked example: a live-fire drill you can actually run

The goal is a number: **wall-clock time from the first malicious transaction to the pause transaction
being included**, plus the fraction of value that left in that window. Everything else is commentary.

**Subgoal: build a world where the attack is real but the consequences are not.** Fork mainnet at a
pinned recent block and deploy into it. Pinning matters for the usual reason — an unpinned fork
re-fetches state on every run, so the timings you measure are your RPC provider's, not your team's.

**Subgoal: make the attacker independent, so the defenders cannot anticipate the trigger.** One person
writes a drain script and runs it at a time the others do not know. If the defenders know the minute,
you are measuring their reaction to a countdown rather than to an alert, and every number will
flatter you.

**Subgoal: start the clock at the transaction, not at the alert — because detection latency is part of
what you are measuring.** T-zero is the block containing the first malicious transaction. The gap
between that and the page is your [detect-to-respond pipeline](/c/detect-to-respond-pipeline)'s
contribution, and it is often the largest single segment on a first drill.

**Subgoal: run the real signing path, with real people, on real devices.** This is the step teams cut
and it invalidates the drill. Signers must use the hardware wallets they would actually use, through
the wallet UI they would actually use, from wherever they actually are. A drill where the pause is
sent from a single hot key measures nothing you care about.

**Subgoal: record segment boundaries as they happen, using a scribe.** First malicious transaction,
alert fired, first human acknowledgement, decision taken, signers paged, threshold reached,
transaction included. Six intervals. You cannot improve what you did not split, and the point of the
drill is to find *which* interval dominates, not to produce one aggregate number.

**Subgoal: run the failure branches on purpose, at least once.** Take one signer offline without
warning and see whether the escalation branch actually works. Block the primary wallet UI and make the
team use the documented backup path. Both are in the runbook precisely because they
happen,<Cite src="seal-frameworks-emergency-pause-runbook" /> and neither has ever been exercised by
anyone who has not deliberately exercised it.

**Subgoal: run a scenario you did not choose.** Your own scenarios are the ones you are already good
at, which is why the organised drills simulate cases such as oracle manipulation and leaked private
keys against teams that did not design them.<Cite src="seal-our-work" />

**Subgoal: convert the result into owned, dated items, not a feeling.** The output of the drill is a
short post-mortem in the same shape as a real one — which is the subject of the last lesson in this
module — with an owner and a deadline against each finding. "Signing took 31 minutes" is an
observation. "Pre-build and simulate pause calldata for all four contracts, owner Priya, by the 12th"
is a change.

**Subgoal: re-run it after the fixes and state which segment moved.** This is the part that makes the
exercise a measurement rather than a ritual. If the second run is faster, you should be able to say
which interval shrank and why. If you cannot, you improved morale.

Your first drill produces this: alert at T+0:40, first acknowledgement T+4:10, decision T+9:00,
threshold reached T+38:00, included T+38:30. Three of your five guardian signers are in the same
office in the same city. Someone proposes the fix: "lower the threshold from 3-of-5 to 2-of-5". Argue
both sides, then say what you would do instead and why.

<Answer>
**The case for it is real and should not be dismissed.** The dominant segment is *authorise* — 29
minutes of the 38. Trail of Bits' own guidance is that the pause guardian's threshold should be
relatively low precisely to allow a quick response, and that this role should not sit behind a
timelock.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" /> Lowering a guardian threshold is a legitimate,
recommended move, not a corner being cut.

**The case against it is what the drill did not measure.** The threshold is also the thing standing
between one compromised signer and a freeze of every user's funds. You are trading a security property
for latency, and the drill produced no evidence that the threshold is *why* it was slow. It could
equally have been paging, device availability, or someone composing calldata from scratch.

**What the drill actually points at, if you split the 29 minutes.** That is one number covering at
least four different failures, and lowering the threshold only helps one of them. Split it before
changing anything: time to first signature tells you about paging and reachability; the gap between
first and second signature tells you about device availability and verification confidence; time spent
composing the transaction tells you whether prepared calldata would have helped; and any time spent
arguing about whether the transaction is correct tells you the verification checklist is not being
used.

**What I would do instead, in order of cost.** Pre-build and simulate the calldata, so the proposer
composes nothing. Publish the abbreviated verification checklist to every signer so verification is a
minute rather than a debate.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-emergency-pause-runbook" /> Fix paging: the
alert must reach a device that overrides do-not-disturb, which Immunefi's checklist names
explicitly.<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" /> Then re-run, and see what 29 minutes has become.

**And fix the thing the drill exposed that nobody asked about: three of five signers in one city.**
That is a correlated failure — one power cut, one office fire, one regional network outage, one
regional holiday, and your 3-of-5 is mathematically a 2-of-2 elsewhere. Geographic and timezone spread
is a property of the signer set, not of the threshold, and it costs nothing to fix at the next signer
rotation. Note that the drill only revealed it because real people signed from real locations, which
is the reason to run the drill the expensive way.

**If, after all that, authorise is still the dominant segment, then lower the threshold — with the
bounds from the previous lesson doing the work.** A 2-of-5 guardian is far more acceptable when the
pause auto-expires, cannot be re-armed during a cooldown, and cannot be lifted by the same role that
imposed it. The bounds are what let you buy speed without buying a hostage-taking risk.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**Next**, the contacts. Some of what you need at 03:47 is not inside your team at all: a hotline
staffed by people who have seen this mechanism before, a pre-adopted agreement that tells a whitehat
what they are allowed to do, block explorers, exchanges, and the downstream protocols your fourth
triage question just identified. The published contacts template treats all of it as a document with
an owner and a quarterly review, which is a strong hint about how it
decays.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-contacts" />

**Then the two documents you will be judged by** — what you say while the exposure is still open, and
what you publish afterwards. Both have published templates, and both are places where a team that did
everything right operationally can still lose the thing it was protecting.
