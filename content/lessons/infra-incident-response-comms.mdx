At T+14 the containment lane is waiting on a fifth signature and somebody in the war room says: "we
should probably post something."

Everything about that sentence is a decision, and none of it is a public-relations decision. Post too
early with mechanism detail and you have written the attacker's documentation for every fork of your
code. Post too late, or not at all, and you have chosen a slow erosion of trust that does not protect
you — because the people who matter most in that window are reading the chain, not your feed. Post
from the wrong account, or from three accounts saying different things, and you have created a second
incident on top of the first.

This lesson is the shape of the message and the machinery around it. Both are published; almost none
of this needs inventing.

## The two failure modes, and why silence is not the safe one

**Publishing the mechanism early arms the copycats.** Once the mechanism is stated in plain language,
anyone holding a fork of your code or a similar design has a recipe, and your own sibling deployments
are on the list. That is a real cost, and it is the same instinct the published guidance encodes when it says to avoid
speculation about root cause until confirmed.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-comms-templates" /> "We will
explain everything shortly" is a defensible position for the first hours.

**Silence is not the conservative alternative.** It reads as conservative because operational-security
instincts say reveal nothing, and those instincts are right about *mechanism* and wrong about
*acknowledgement*. Two reasons. First, the attacker already knows; the audience your silence keeps in
the dark is your users, who are still depositing. Second, the guidance's own framing is that communication matters precisely because outsiders and
observers need to understand what is happening,<Cite src="seal-frameworks-incident-comms" /> and
silence has its own dynamics: Immunefi's
guide observes that going quiet while the investigation runs erodes confidence hour by hour, that poor
communication can produce bank-run behaviour faster than the original exploit, and that protocols with
strong communications discipline have survived nine-figure exploits while smaller incidents have
destroyed teams that went dark at the wrong moment.<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" />

The resolution is that **acknowledgement and explanation are separable**, and the published guidance
separates them. You can say that something is wrong, that you are responding, what users should do,
and when you will speak again — without saying how it works.

## The machinery: four things decided in advance

SEAL's guidance on incident communication is short and every line is a preparation
item.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-incident-comms" />

**Secure channels first.** The list opens with defining and establishing secure communication channels
for the response team, using encrypted messaging.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-incident-comms" /> The
channel you coordinate on is itself part of the attack surface.

**Designated speakers.** Appoint primary *and backup* spokespersons for internal and external
communication.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-incident-comms" /> The backup is not ceremonial: the primary may be the person deepest in the technical
work, or asleep, or the one whose account is the compromised one.

**A fixed update schedule.** Updates go out on a cadence, and the guidance is explicit that it can
often be that the update is that there is currently no new information
available.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-incident-comms" /> That sentence is the whole discipline. An
update saying nothing new has happened is not a wasted post; it is the signal that the process is
still running, and it removes the incentive for anyone to fill the silence for you.

**Pre-approved templates.** Develop notification, update and press-release templates in advance to
ensure consistency and speed.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-incident-comms" /> Writing under pressure is
where the sentence that gets quoted for two years comes from.

**Facts, not speculation.** Providing information before confirming it is accurate is called out
directly as often very negative and a cause of
uncertainty.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-incident-comms" /> The corollary is that "we do not know yet"
is an acceptable, publishable statement, and a far better one than a guess you retract at T+90.

To that, add the on-chain refinement the general guidance does not cover: **designate one channel as
authoritative**, and say so in the message. If the authoritative channel is named, the absence of a
message anywhere else means nothing — which is what defends your users against the impersonation
accounts that appear within minutes of any visible incident.

## The shape of a message

The templates page turns all of this into something you can assemble under pressure. Its
**before-you-post checklist** is five items: get approval from the incident leader or a decision
maker, verify the facts are accurate, avoid speculation about root cause until confirmed, include what
users should do or not do, and state when you will provide the next
update.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-comms-templates" />

Its **what-to-include** table is more useful still, because it marks which elements are conditional
and which are not. What happened, at a high level: always. When you will update next: always. User
funds are safe: only if true. What users should do: if action is needed. What users should *not* do:
if relevant. What still works: if the outage is partial. A link to a status page or
thread: if available.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-comms-templates" />

Two entries there are doing more work than they look like. **"User funds are safe — if true"** is a
guard against the most damaging sentence a team can publish, because it is the one everybody wants to
write and the one that becomes a lie retroactively at T+40. And **"what still works"** is the sentence
that converts a partial containment into a usable service instead of a perceived total failure — which
is the communications half of choosing rung 2 over rung 1 on the containment ladder.

### Building blocks rather than paragraphs

The templates are deliberately modular — short, reusable fragments to be combined rather than a set of
whole announcements. Acknowledgement. Funds safe. Funds at risk, flagged *be careful*. Action required.
Do not interact. Service paused. Partial outage. Timeline unknown. Next update. Resolution.
Post-mortem coming.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-comms-templates" />

The modularity is the point. Under pressure you are assembling from a fixed vocabulary rather than
composing, which is faster and, more importantly, produces messages that are consistent with the ones
before and after them.

<Aside kind="note">
There is a template for the case this module keeps returning to: a compromised social account. Its
instruction is to post from an *alternate channel*, state that the account is compromised, tell people
not to click links or interact with it, name the verified channels legitimate announcements will come
from, and tell anyone who interacted to revoke token approvals
immediately.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-comms-templates" /> Read that as a preparedness requirement
rather than a template: it only works if a second channel exists, is known to your users in advance,
and is controlled by different credentials.
</Aside>

## Channel behaviour, briefly

The published channel notes are small operational details that matter because they are decisions
nobody wants to make at T+14: on Twitter or X, keep the initial post short, use a thread for updates
and pin the important ones; in Discord, use `@everyone` or `@here` sparingly and only for P1, create a
dedicated thread for ongoing updates, and lock the thread afterwards to preserve the record; on
Telegram, pin critical messages and consider disabling chat during an active incident to reduce
noise.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-comms-templates" />

The policy adds one line about the internal half that is easy to miss: internal updates go in the
incident channel at a frequency that depends on severity, and external messages are drafted by the
communication manager and approved before posting.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-ir-policy" />

And the tone guidance, which is short enough to memorise: be direct and factual, avoid jargon users
will not understand, do not speculate on root cause until confirmed, do not blame — individuals, third
parties or users — acknowledge the impact, and avoid excessive apologies, because one is
enough.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-comms-templates" />

"Do not blame third parties" is the one that will be hardest to obey and the one worth obeying most.
It is also the same norm that governs the post-mortem, for the same reason: the audience can tell the
difference between an explanation and a deflection, and reaching for one destroys the credibility of
the other.

### Communication that is not an announcement

Two items in the war-room checklist's communications lane are worth separating out, because they are
not broadcasts and they are easy to lose behind the public post. **Notify block explorers so attacker
addresses get labelled**, and **continuously monitor social media for users providing additional
information that aids whitehat efforts**.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" /> The second one
inverts the usual direction: during a visible incident the outside world is watching the same chain
you are, sometimes with better tooling, and a stranger's thread has more than once contained the
transaction nobody inside had found. Assign someone to read, not only to post.

## Worked example: the T+14 message

Containment is in flight, the mechanism is understood internally, exposure is still open. Write the
first public message. Subgoals name what each step is protecting.

**Subgoal: fix the approval path before writing a word, so the message cannot ship without an owner.**
The communication manager drafts and gets approval before posting — that is the policy's own
division.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-ir-policy" /> Two people, thirty seconds, and it is the control
that prevents the misjudged post.

**Subgoal: choose only from the blocks whose preconditions currently hold.** Acknowledgement: yes.
Service paused: yes, if the pause has actually executed and you have verified contract state. Funds
safe: **no** — you cannot verify it, so the honest fragment is the "be careful" one. Do not interact:
yes. Next update: yes.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-comms-templates" />

**Subgoal: state the user action concretely, because a vague warning produces panic without
protection.** "Be careful" is not an action. "Do not deposit; do not interact with the vault contract
at `0x…`; withdrawals are unaffected" is one, and it also implicitly answers *what still works*.

**Subgoal: commit to a next update with a time on it, to remove the incentive for anyone else to fill
the gap.** A timeframe converts an anxious audience into a waiting one, and it is the only promise in
the message you fully control.

The assembled result:

```text
We are aware of a security incident affecting [protocol]. We are actively responding.

We have paused [contract/feature]. Withdrawals are unaffected.

Do not deposit or interact with [contract address] until we confirm otherwise.

We are still assessing the situation and will not speculate on the cause.
Our next update will be within 60 minutes, or sooner if anything changes.

This account is the only authoritative source for updates on this incident.
```

Ninety words, no mechanism, no promise you cannot keep, one action, one clock, one channel. It maps
element-for-element onto the published active-exploit template, which likewise keeps the initial
announcement brief, adds the pause statement only if applicable, and defers detail to a follow-up once
stabilised.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-comms-templates" />

A team publishes this at T+22. Name four problems, in order of how much they cost.

> **URGENT:** We have detected a critical vulnerability in our staking contract where the reward
> calculation can be re-entered before `lastClaimed` is written, allowing repeated claims. An
> unknown attacker has exploited this. **All user funds are safe.** We are working with our auditors
> and will share more soon. We apologise sincerely — our team is devastated and we will do
> everything to make this right.

<Answer>
**1. It publishes the mechanism while the exposure is open — the most expensive error here.** The
first sentence is a complete, actionable description of the bug: the function, the missing write
ordering, the effect. Every fork of this code and every protocol with the same pattern now has a
recipe, and so does anyone looking at the team's own other deployments. The published guidance is to
avoid speculation about root cause until confirmed; the sharper on-chain rule is to withhold the
mechanism until the exposure is closed, everywhere, including for the people you
forked from.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-comms-templates" />

**2. "All user funds are safe" is unverifiable at T+22 and it is the sentence that will be quoted.**
The template marks this fragment as conditional — include it *if true* — and at twenty-two minutes into
an incident whose scope is not yet established, it cannot be known to be
true.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-comms-templates" /> If it turns out to be false, every subsequent
statement is discounted, including the accurate ones. "We are still assessing the situation" is the
fragment that exists for this case.

**3. There is no user action and no next update, which are the two the checklist marks as required.**
What should a staker do right now — nothing, withdraw, revoke an approval? The message does not say.
And "will share more soon" is not a commitment; the checklist requires stating *when* the next update
comes, and it is the item that stops other people from narrating the incident for
you.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-comms-templates" />

**4. The tone spends credibility it will need later.** "Devastated", "do everything to make this
right", an elaborate apology — the guidance is to be direct and factual and to avoid excessive
apologies, one being enough.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-comms-templates" /> Emotional language at T+22
reads as a substitute for information, and it sets up an expectation of restitution the team may not
be able to meet. Save the accounting for the post-mortem, where it belongs and where it will be read
carefully.

**The rewrite is shorter than the original**, contains no mechanism, states one action, commits to a
clock, and leaves every later statement compatible with it. That last property — that nothing here has
to be walked back — is the thing worth optimising for, because the document that follows this incident
will be read next to this message.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**Next**, and last in this module, the document that closes the loop. The post-mortem is where the
mechanism you correctly withheld today finally gets published, where the timeline your scribe kept
becomes checkable, and where three different audiences read the same page for incompatible reasons.
The published template is a good checklist to hold yourself to, and it opens with the instruction that
governs the whole exercise: focus on learning, not blame — followed immediately by the demand that
every action item carries an owner and a
deadline.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-postmortem-template" />
