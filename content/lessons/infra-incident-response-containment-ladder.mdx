The leader asks the question every incident eventually asks: "do we pause?"

It is the wrong question, and it is wrong in a way that costs money in both directions. Asked as a
binary, it forces a choice between freezing every honest user of a $40M protocol and doing nothing at
all — and whichever you pick, half the room will be able to argue afterwards that you were wrong. The
useful version is **which lever, at what cost, protecting what**, and answering it requires having
thought about the middle of the ladder on an ordinary Tuesday.

This lesson is the ladder. Its rungs are ordered by how much they cost honest users, not by how much
they inconvenience the attacker, because the cost side is the part teams have not priced.

## The rungs

### Rung 1 — Full contract pause

Every state-changing entry point stops. "Pause contracts if possible" is the second item in the
published protocol-actions lane, immediately after taking immediate corrective
action.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" /> This is the maximum protection of remaining value and
the maximum harm to everyone else: a lender cannot withdraw, a borrower cannot repay before liquidation, a
market maker cannot rebalance, and an integrating protocol's own liquidations start failing. It is the
right rung when the mechanism is not yet understood, the exposure is broad, and the remaining value is
large relative to the harm.

It is also the rung that assumes something. Immunefi's guide notes that a protocol with no admin keys
and no multisig faces a fundamentally different containment problem, and that knowing in advance
whether you have pause authority shapes every decision that follows.<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" />
If rung 1 does not exist for you, that is not a gap to discover at 03:50.

### Rung 2 — Disable the specific entry point

Stop `borrow` and leave `repay` and `withdraw` live. Stop deposits and leave exits open. This is a
smaller blast radius for a smaller amount of certainty, and it is available to anyone who built pause
as per-function rather than global. Halborn's treatment of the mechanism makes exactly this point:
by blocking calls to specific functions you can work on the issue without completely freezing users
out, and you can deliberately arrange it so users may still withdraw deposited funds while other
functions remain inaccessible.<Cite src="halborn-pause" />

Whether you *have* this rung is a design decision made months earlier. OpenZeppelin's `Pausable` gives
you `whenNotPaused` and `whenPaused` as modifiers you apply per function, and it is explicit that a
contract inheriting the module is not pausable until the modifiers are actually placed on
functions.<Cite src="oz-pausable" /> Where you placed them *is* your rung-2 vocabulary. A codebase
where every external function carries `whenNotPaused` has one rung. A codebase where the modifier
appears on entries and not on exits has two.

### Rung 3 — Cap or rate-limit the flow

Leave the entry point open but bound what can leave per block, per hour, or per address. The exposure
does not go to zero, it goes to a rate — and a rate is something a human can outrun. This is the rung
that converts "the remaining $34M is reachable" into "the remaining $34M is reachable at $500K an
hour, and we have sixty-eight hours", which is a completely different decision problem.

Its cost is a partial degradation rather than a stop, which honest users tolerate far better. Its
prerequisite is that the limit exists in code before the incident; you cannot add one to a deployed
contract by deciding to.

### Rung 4 — Change a parameter until the attack stops being profitable

Raise a fee, tighten a collateral factor, lower a cap, widen a slippage bound, delist a single asset.
The published lane calls the adjacent version of this "execute pre-made defensive
scripts",<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" /> which is the same idea with the work done in advance.
Many exploits are profitable by a margin, and margins can be moved. This rung is invisible to the vast
majority of users, which makes it the cheapest real containment available — and it is usually
reachable through an existing operations role rather than an emergency one.

It is also the rung with the highest chance of a self-inflicted wound, because parameters interact.
Tightening a collateral factor to stop an attacker can push honest positions into liquidation. This is
a rung you should have simulated before you needed it, which is the same argument as
[rehearsing an upgrade at head](/c/upgrade-rehearsal), applied to config.

### Rung 5 — Blacklist

Two very different things share the word. **Front-end blacklisting** — the checklist names both IP and
address blacklisting in the web-actions lane — costs an attacker nothing, because they are not using
your front end.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" /> Its actual purpose is protecting *users*: it
stops honest people from interacting with a contract that is unsafe right now. **On-chain
blacklisting** exists only where the asset supports it, which in practice means certain centrally
issued stablecoins, and reaching it means asking their issuer. That is a phone call, not a
transaction, and its speed is the issuer's, not yours.

Related and worth knowing: the checklist lists notifying block explorers for attacker address
labelling as war-room work.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" /> That is not containment; it is
making the attacker's next move visible to everyone else.

### Rung 6 — Rescue what you can reach

If the compromise is a key rather than a contract bug, there is a rung that does not appear in most
curricula: move the funds before the attacker does. SEAL's playbook links the open-source white-hat
front-running script for exactly this case — a Bash script needing only Linux native tools and
Foundry's `cast`, which bundles a gas top-up and a token transfer atomically through Flashbots so the
attacker never sees them in the public mempool, with a second flow that uses EIP-7702 delegation so
the victim wallet needs no ETH at all.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" /><Cite src="pcaversaccio-whitehat-frontrunning" />

Its caveats are the interesting part, and they generalise beyond this one tool. The main loop must be
customised for each rescue — it is not a one-click tool. Flashbots bundles are Ethereum mainnet only,
so other chains need a different private-mempool arrangement. And `DEBUG=true` still sends real
transactions: it adds verbose output, it is not a dry
run.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" /> A rescue path you have never run is a rung you do not
have.

### Rung 7 — Do nothing, on purpose

The bottom rung is a real choice and it is under-taught because it looks like failure. It is correct
when the remaining exposure to that mechanism is small, when the attack is not repeatable, or when the
harm from pausing exceeds the value at risk. A protocol that pauses $40M of honest positions to
protect a $200K residual has made a decision, and it is a bad one.

The requirement is that "do nothing" is *chosen and recorded*, with the reasoning in the incident log,
rather than arrived at by nobody being able to act. Those two look identical from outside and are
opposite from inside.

The final column is the one that turns this from a diagram into a plan. Rungs 2 and 3 are unavailable
unless somebody wrote them into the contract, rung 5's on-chain half is unavailable unless somebody has
an issuer contact saved, and rung 6 is unavailable unless somebody has run the script once against a
fork. Every unavailable rung pushes the decision back toward the binary this lesson opened with.

## Choosing a rung: what the choice is actually made of

Three inputs, and only one of them is technical.

**Exposure.** How much value is still reachable through the mechanism, and how fast. This is triage
question three from the previous lesson, and it is why that question is placed before the containment
decision rather than after it.

**Harm.** What a rung costs honest users per hour. Not a feeling — an estimate. A vault where users
withdraw once a month and a perpetuals venue where positions liquidate continuously have
order-of-magnitude different pause costs, and the number should be roughly known before the day.

**Reachability.** How long the rung takes to execute, including signatures. A rung that takes forty
minutes to authorise and a rung that takes four are not comparable options, and the ladder should be
annotated with its own latency. In many real timelines the *fastest* rung is a front-end change,
because it needs no signatures at all — which is why the web-actions lane runs in parallel rather than
waiting for a contract decision.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" />

<Aside kind="warn">
Note what is not on the input list: certainty. Immunefi's framing is that speed matters more than
certainty at the containment step, because a false positive costs inconvenience and reputational
friction while a slow response costs millions.<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" /> That asymmetry is real,
and it is also exactly the argument an attacker would like you to accept if they can trigger your
detector cheaply. Which is why the rung matters: accepting a false positive on rung 4 is nearly free,
and accepting one on rung 1 is not.
</Aside>

## Negotiating with the attacker

Sometimes the funds are gone and the attacker still holds them. Public on-chain negotiation — an offer
communicated in a transaction's calldata, a safe return path, a bounty for cooperation — is a real
recovery route, and it is worth being honest about what is and is not known here.

**What is documented.** Immunefi's guide describes the mechanics: open communication channels, with
on-chain messages as a workable medium; offer a safe return path; structure incentives so cooperation
beats running. It states plainly that negotiation works more often than people assume and fails more
often than it succeeds.<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" /> That leverage matters, and identifying
jurisdiction, exchange touchpoints or operational fingerprints changes the
calculus.<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" /> And that escalation must be proportional, with a ladder
running public warnings, then forensics firms, then exchange freezes, then legal action, then criminal
filings — noting that reckless escalation burns bridges you may need
later.<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" /> Its summary of the incentive structure is the sentence to
carry: attackers rarely return funds out of conscience, they return them when the incentives line
up.<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" />

**What is not documented, and must not be invented.** Typical bounty percentages and success rates
circulate constantly and were not traceable to a reliable source.<Cite src="chainpath-conflict-register" />
Do not anchor on a number you read, and be suspicious of anyone in the room who quotes one
confidently.

**What this means for preparation.** Every part of a negotiation is a decision with consequences
beyond your protocol: what you offer becomes a precedent others will be measured against, what you
promise may bind you, and what you say may matter legally. None of that should be settled by whoever
is awake at 04:00. Three things belong in the runbook: who has authority to make an offer, what the
ceiling is, and who reviews the wording before it is broadcast.

### The version you can decide in advance: Safe Harbor

There is a category adjacent to attacker negotiation where the ambiguity *can* be removed beforehand.
When a whitehat moves funds out of a vulnerable contract to protect them, their transactions look
identical on chain to an attack, and their legal position is unclear by default — which is exactly the
hesitation that costs the rescue.

SEAL's Safe Harbor is an on-chain agreement a protocol adopts in advance to pre-authorise whitehat
intervention during an active exploit. Its published terms: rescued funds must be returned within 72
hours, bounties are set at 10% of recovered assets capped at $1M, and participating whitehats are
backed by the Security Research Legal Defense Fund, which covers eligible researchers' legal
fees.<Cite src="seal-safe-harbor-project" /> As of this writing the project page reports 33 protocol
adopters, over $60B in assets protected, and deployment across more than ten
chains.<Cite src="seal-safe-harbor-project" />

Present it to your own team the way it should be presented: **a framework a protocol can adopt, not
established legal protection**. How it stands in any particular jurisdiction is not something to
assert, and the value it definitely delivers is different from a legal guarantee — during an incident,
everyone involved already knows the terms instead of negotiating them while funds drain.

Two incidents, same protocol, same week. For each, name the rung you would take first and the rung you
would take second, and say what the first one costs.

**Incident A.** A newly listed collateral asset is priced from a spot pool with $900K of liquidity.
An attacker has moved the price and extracted $4M. The asset has a $5M supply cap, of which $4.6M is
now used. Total protocol assets: $50M across eleven other collateral types.

**Incident B.** Your monitoring shows a withdrawal from the protocol treasury multisig that no signer
recognises. $2M left. The multisig holds $18M more. Nobody can yet say which signer is compromised.

<Answer>
**Incident A — first rung 4, then rung 2, and rung 1 is wrong.**

The exposure is bounded by the cap: at most about $400K of additional supply can enter through the
mispriced asset. Rung 4 — delist the asset, or set its collateral factor to zero, or drop its cap to
the current utilisation — closes the mechanism completely while touching nothing else. Its cost is
borne by holders of one asset, not by users of eleven others, and it is typically reachable through an
existing operations path rather than an emergency one.

Rung 2 second: disable `borrow` against that specific collateral if the parameter change cannot be
made atomically, so there is no window between the two.

**Rung 1 is the mistake here, and it is a tempting one.** Pausing protects $50M in a world where about
$400K is exposed, at the cost of freezing eleven markets, blocking repayments, and stalling every
integrator's liquidations. The exposure question — triage question three — is what tells you this, and
it is why that question is asked before the containment decision rather than after.

**Incident B — first rung 6, then rung 1, and the order is not intuitive.**

This is a key compromise, and the reflex list from the first lesson inverts: every privileged path you
would normally use is a path the attacker may also hold. You do not know which signer is compromised,
so you cannot trust the multisig to execute anything, including a pause.

Rung 6 comes first because the exposure is not rate-limited by anything — the attacker can take the
remaining $18M in one transaction whenever they choose. Moving the assets to a destination the
attacker does not control is the only action that reduces exposure, and it is a race, which is exactly
what the front-running tooling exists for: a bundle that the attacker never sees in the public
mempool.<Cite src="pcaversaccio-whitehat-frontrunning" />

Rung 1 second, and note what it does and does not do: pausing protocol contracts protects the protocol
from a compromised treasury signer's privileged calls. It does nothing about assets already sitting in
the compromised multisig.

**The cost of the first move in B is the one worth naming out loud.** A rescue transaction from a
possibly-compromised key set, executed at speed, is itself a large privileged movement of funds that
will look indistinguishable from the attack in every explorer and every screenshot for the next
several hours. That is a communications problem you create deliberately, and the previous lesson's
answer applies: the scribe records the decision and the rationale as it is made, because that record
is what separates "the team rescued the funds" from "the team moved $18M during an
incident".<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" />
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**Next**, the rung everybody builds. Pause deserves a whole lesson not because it is the most powerful
but because it is the most misunderstood: the same key that stops the attacker can freeze honest users
indefinitely, `_pause()` ships with no access control at all, and Trail of Bits' stated position is
that pause's potency declines as attackers automate and move into private
mempools.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" /> You will design it as a bounded power with four
structural limits rather than as a safety feature with a promise attached.

**After that**, the reason a rung on paper is not a rung in practice — and the drills that are the only
way to tell the difference. The ecosystem now runs organised exercises for exactly this, simulating
scenarios such as oracle manipulation and leaked private keys so teams can pressure-test detection,
coordination and response before a real attack arrives.<Cite src="seal-our-work" />
