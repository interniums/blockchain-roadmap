03:47 on a Sunday. Your phone lights up with the alert you built two modules ago: the vault's total
assets fell by more than the threshold you derived from your own baseline, inside one block. You open
the explorer on your phone. One transaction, one address you have never seen, and the number in your
dashboard is smaller than it was ninety seconds ago.

Now list what you would do if this were a web service.

Restore from the last good snapshot. Revoke the attacker's session. Push a firewall rule blocking the
source. Reverse the charge. Rotate the credential and force a re-login. Take the service offline while
you look.

Five of those six do not exist here, and the sixth is not what you think it is.

That is not a rhetorical opening. It is the whole architecture of the discipline. Every reflex you
carry from operating software assumes at least one lever that acts *backwards* — that reaches into
what already happened and changes it. On chain there is exactly one direction available, and it is
forward. This lesson is about what follows from that, and about the second constraint that makes it
bite: the clock the incident runs on is not the clock your team runs on.

## What "no undo" actually means, in order of hardness

The claim gets stated loosely and then argued with, so it is worth taking apart into three separate
statements of decreasing strength, because two of them are absolute and the third is where people go
looking for hope.

**No party can reverse an included transaction on your behalf.** There is no operator, no support
queue, no chargeback. This is not a policy that could be different; it is the absence of an entity
holding the authority. The nearest thing the ecosystem has to a central emergency function is SEAL
911, and what it provides is a hotline to vetted responders who can help you act, not a switch that
un-sends anything.<Cite src="seal-911" />

**Once the block containing it is finalized, it cannot be reverted at all.** Under proof of stake,
finality means reverting the block would require an attacker to destroy at least one-third of the
total staked ether.<Cite src="eth-pos-finality" /> This is the strong form: not "very expensive to
undo" in the sense that a determined party might, but a cost denominated in the destruction of stake,
priced deliberately so that nobody buys it.

**Before finality, reversal is possible in principle and useless to you in practice.** Reorgs do
happen. But a reorg is not something you can aim: you cannot request one, cannot target a
transaction, and cannot rely on one arriving. Waiting for the chain to change its mind is not a plan.

<Aside kind="note">
How long the window before finality actually lasts is a place where sources disagree, and it is worth
naming rather than smoothing. The mechanism gives roughly 12.8 minutes — two epochs of 32 twelve-second
slots — while one benchmark reported a median closer to 15.9 minutes over a 24-hour window. Neither
figure was adopted as canonical.<Cite src="chainpath-conflict-register" /> For incident response the
disagreement barely matters, because both numbers are far longer than an exploit takes and far shorter
than a multisig does.
</Aside>

## The one web-ops lever that does exist, and how it is different

Taking the service offline has an on-chain analogue: pausing the contract. This is the sixth item on
the list, and it is the one people latch onto, which is exactly why the next three lessons are spent
on it. Two differences matter right now.

**It is not the same operation as taking a server offline.** Once deployed, a contract's execution
cannot be stopped; what you can do is call logic you wrote in advance that temporarily disables
specific actions.<Cite src="halborn-pause" /> There is no host to power down, which is why the
capability has to have been built.

**It stops the next transaction, not the last one.** Pausing after a drain protects whatever is still
in the contract. It does nothing about what left. If the exploit emptied the vault in one transaction,
a perfect pause thirty seconds later has protected zero dollars.

**It is not available to everyone.** A protocol with no admin keys and no multisig cannot pause, and
Immunefi's field guide is blunt that this changes the entire response strategy rather than removing
one option from a menu — knowing in advance whether you have pause authority is a design input to the
whole playbook.<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" /> An immutable protocol's incident response is
communications, user-side action, and negotiation, from minute one.

### The case where the sixth lever is the attacker's too

There is a variant of this incident that most curricula skip and that the loss data says you should
plan for. Trail of Bits, citing Chainalysis's 2024 report, notes that 43.8% of all funds stolen via
hacks stemmed from compromised keys — more than any other verified attack type by a factor of
five.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" /> SEAL's severity table lists private key compromise
alongside active exploit as a P1 example.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-ir-policy" />

Re-run the reflex list under that assumption. The pause guardian is a key. The upgrade path is a key.
The multisig you would use to move funds somewhere safe is a set of keys, and you do not yet know
which of them is the problem. Every privileged path you would normally reach for is simultaneously the
path the attacker is using, and using it may be the thing that finishes the job. A response plan that
only covers "a bug in our contract" has no first move in the case that is empirically the most common
one — which is why the reflection prompt at the end of this module makes you write that plan.

## What is left when everything backwards is gone

Strip out every reversing lever and what remains is a short, specific list. The published war-room
checklist is organised around precisely these, which is a good sign you have the right list rather
than a philosophical one:

- **Take immediate corrective and preventative actions to prevent further loss of funds** — pause if
  you can, execute pre-made defensive scripts, then validate and execute a chosen
  solution.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" />
- **Reduce the surface a user can still be hurt through** — disable deposits or withdrawals in the
  web UI, enable front-end blacklisting, and stand up a front end for whatever users now need to do,
  such as revoking approvals or migrating funds.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" />
- **Warn the parties exposed to the same mechanism** — the checklist puts gathering security contacts
  for potentially affected downstream protocols, naming bridges and lending protocols specifically,
  inside the war room's parallel work rather than in the follow-up
  section.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" />
- **Record everything, because the record is the only thing that survives** — transactions involved,
  affected addresses, funds movement, attacker information, and a timeline in
  UTC.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" />

Notice that not one of those recovers anything. Every item is either protecting value that has not
left, shrinking the surface, or preserving the ability to reason later. Internalising that is the
reframe: during an incident the question is never *how do we get it back*, it is **how much is still
exposed, to what, and for how long**.

<Check type="predict" concept="irreversibility-changes-everything">

<Answer>
**The pause buys exactly one of these: number 2.** It protects value that has not moved. It has no
effect on 1, and no effect on 3 or 4 unless somebody separately does the work.

**Ranked by money, the order is 2, 3, 4, 1 — and the interesting part is that 1 is last.** The $6M is
the number that will be in the headline, and it is the one item on the list where your actions during
the incident have the least leverage. Return happens later, by negotiation, exchange cooperation or
law enforcement, on a timescale of weeks, with a probability nobody can quote you honestly.

**Numbers 3 and 4 are the ones teams under-serve, and both are pure incident work.** Your own sibling
deployment is not protected by pausing this one, and it is running the same mispricing right now.
Downstream protocols reading your feed are exposed to your mechanism through a dependency they may not
have modelled. The war-room checklist treats notifying potentially affected downstream protocols as
work performed *during* the response, in parallel, alongside the containment
itself.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" />

**The thing worth carrying out of this:** at 03:49 the largest recoverable number on the board is
$34M plus whatever is in the fork plus whatever the downstream protocols hold. Attention spent on the
$6M is attention taken from all of it.
</Answer>

## The second constraint: the clock is not yours

Irreversibility alone would be survivable if you had time. You do not, and the shape of the shortfall
is worth being precise about because it determines what preparation is worth doing.

**The attacker's unit of time is a slot.** A modern exploit is a program. It executes atomically
inside a single transaction — read the price, take the flash loan, execute the mispriced swap, repay,
exit — and either the whole thing lands in one twelve-second slot or it reverts and costs the attacker
gas. There is no partial state for you to interrupt. Trail of Bits adds the observation that attacks
are often submitted through private mempools specifically to prevent proactive
identification,<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" /> which removes even the seconds of mempool
warning that a public submission would have given you.

**Your unit of time is a page.** Look at what the published guidance actually commits humans to.
SEAL's on-call expectations ask a responder to *respond to pages within 15 minutes* during a
shift.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-ir-roles" /> The emergency pause runbook's example service level for
getting a multisig signed is measured in hours, with its alert template ending in a two-hour response
service level.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-emergency-pause-runbook" /> Those are not slow numbers. They
are what a competent, staffed, rehearsed team commits to in writing, and the runbook frames its whole
opening block as work for the first thirty minutes rather than the first
seconds.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-emergency-pause-runbook" />

Put the two side by side and the asymmetry stops being a slogan. Twelve seconds against fifteen
minutes to *acknowledge*, and hours to *assemble signatures*. You are not going to close that gap
during an incident. It has to be closed before one.

<Aside kind="warn">
Be careful with the numbers that circulate about this. Claims of the form "the first thirty to sixty
minutes determine the loss", or "contain at minute three on seventy percent confidence", are
memorable, widely repeated, and were not traceable to a source that measured
anything.<Cite src="chainpath-conflict-register" /> The structural claim is sound without them: an
automated attacker completes in seconds and a human path takes minutes. You do not need a fabricated
statistic to justify preparation.
</Aside>

worth writing out because each segment has a different fix, and only some of them can be shortened at

Two things fall out of that table. First, the only segment you cannot touch is the attacker's. Second,
the segment that most often dominates is *authorise* — the wall-clock time between "we have decided to
pause" and "the pause transaction is on chain". That is a human-coordination problem wearing a
cryptography costume, and it is why the emergency pause runbook is almost entirely about paging,
predefined signer paths, an abbreviated verification checklist and instructing signers to stay
available until the transaction executes, rather than about
Solidity.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-emergency-pause-runbook" />

## What monitoring bought you, and what it did not

You built the detection pipeline in the previous module, so it is worth being exact about what it
contributed here. Detection converts an unbounded segment into a bounded one. Without it, the first
segment of the table is "until a user notices and tweets", which has no upper bound and has been
measured in days for real incidents — SEAL's own framing of why detection matters opens with not
wanting to be the project that has funds stolen and does not notice for multiple
days.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-incident-response" />

SEAL's own detection list names five origins for an incident — monitoring alerts, community reports,
a team member noticing, bug bounty reports, audits and partner
notifications<Cite src="seal-frameworks-ir-policy" /> — and only the first is something you built.

What detection did not buy you is any of the segments after it. An [alert is not a
decision](/c/detect-to-respond-pipeline), and [monitoring is not
auditing](/c/monitoring-is-not-auditing): the monitor tells you a threshold was crossed, not what
mechanism crossed it, not whether the rest is exposed, and not which lever to pull. Everything from
*understand* onward is the subject of this module.

## What this unlocks

**Next**, the structure that makes the compressible segments actually compress: the war room. Its
central claim is counterintuitive to engineers — analysis, containment and communications are meant to
run *concurrently* from minute one, and the checklist is literally organised into parallel lanes by
role rather than into steps.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" /> You will also get the four triage
questions, in the order that matters, and the reason the fourth one is the one teams defer.

**Then**, the containment ladder — because "pause" is one rung on it, and a team that has only
considered the top rung will make an all-or-nothing decision at the worst possible moment.

**Then three lessons on the unglamorous half**: designing pause as a bounded power rather than a
permanent one, the operational readiness that decides whether the capability is real at 03:47 on a
Sunday, and the contacts and agreements you needed to have in place before tonight.

**And it ends with the two documents**: what you say while it is happening, and what you publish
afterwards. Both are load-bearing. The published guidance treats the review that follows an incident
as the thing that produces action items with owners and deadlines, not as a
formality<Cite src="seal-frameworks-ir-policy" /> — and the ecosystem now reads its absence as a
signal.
