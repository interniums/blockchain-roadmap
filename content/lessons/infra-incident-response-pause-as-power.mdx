Read the entire access-control surface that OpenZeppelin's `Pausable` gives you:

```solidity
function _pause() internal virtual whenNotPaused {
    _paused = true;
    emit Paused(_msgSender());
}
```

There isn't any. `_pause()` and `_unpause()` are `internal`,<Cite src="oz-pausable-source" /> the
module ships two modifiers, one boolean, two events — `Paused(account)` and `Unpaused(account)` — and
two custom errors, `EnforcedPause` and `ExpectedPause`,<Cite src="oz-pausable" /> and the documentation is explicit that inheriting the
module does not make anything pausable until you place the modifiers on
functions yourself.<Cite src="oz-pausable" /><Cite src="oz-pausable-source" /> Who may call `_pause()`
is a question the library deliberately declines to answer.

That is the correct design and it is also the whole lesson. The library gives you a mechanism. You
supply the power — and a power that halts an attacker is, in the same line of code, a power to freeze
everyone else's money for as long as its holder chooses. No code distinguishes the two uses. Nothing
in `Paused(address account)` says whether the account was defending or extorting.

This lesson is about designing that power so it is bounded by structure rather than by promise, and
about an honest read of how much it is worth in the first place.

## The two sides, stated together

The benign side is in the documentation: a common emergency response mechanism that can pause
functionality while a remediation is pending.<Cite src="oz-pausable" /> The other side is stated just
as plainly in the academic treatment: indefinite pause may be abused by the contract administrator, or
it can be triggered by an adversary who stole the administrator's private
key.<Cite src="arxiv-administrated-erc20" /> Same function, two readers.

The scale of this is not hypothetical. The paper that proposes the mitigation built a nine-feature
classifier for administrated tokens and ran it over 84,062 unique Ethereum smart contracts, finding
that nearly 58% of them were administrated ERC-20 tokens, accounting for almost 90% of all ERC-20
tokens deployed on Ethereum.<Cite src="arxiv-administrated-erc20" /> Freezing and halting method names
were one of the nine features precisely because they correlate so strongly with the administrated
pattern.<Cite src="arxiv-administrated-erc20" />

You already know the [pause switch is a trust assumption](/c/pause-is-centralisation) and that
[access-control failure](/c/access-control-failure) costs far more realised money than reentrancy —
the bug class curricula lead with — in counts scoped to smart-contract vulnerability categories and
in wider counts that also take in stolen keys and phishing. Which category sits at the very top
depends on which of those two populations is being counted, and no lesson here asserts
one.<Cite src="chainpath-conflict-register" />
Put those two facts next to each other and the shape of the risk is obvious: the pause guardian is a
privileged role, compromise of privileged roles accounts for the largest share of realised loss under
either accounting, and this particular role's compromise does not steal anything — it holds
everything hostage, which is a different and rarely modelled failure.

## Four structural bounds

Each of these converts a promise into a property. The test for whether a bound is real is simple: can
a malicious or compromised guardian defeat it by choosing to?

### Bound 1 — Make pausing easy

Low threshold, short signer path, no timelock — and note that the runbook's own quick-reference field
for a required threshold says to use the threshold and signer path *predefined for this multisig*,
not one chosen on the night.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-emergency-pause-runbook" /> Trail of Bits' role decomposition is explicit: the
pause guardian role should not sit behind any kind of timelock, and its multisig threshold should be
relatively low to allow a quick response in an
emergency.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" /> Speed is the entire point of the rung; a 5-of-7
pause guardian with a 12-hour timelock is a decorative control.

This bound is the one people are tempted to walk back after they think about bounds 2 to 4. Do not.
The other three exist so that this one can be as loose as it needs to be.

### Bound 2 — Make unpausing hard, slow and public

Pausing and unpausing should not share an access path. If they do, the guardian can pause and resume
at will, which is a market-manipulation primitive rather than an emergency control — the ability to
choose exactly when a protocol is usable, repeatedly, without anyone's approval.

Trail of Bits' four-role decomposition already separates authorities this way — a core system role
behind a large threshold and a long timelock, an operations role in the middle, the pause guardian
fast, and a cancel guardian for killing unauthorised timelocked
transactions.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" /> Route unpause through governance, or through
the high-threshold role, and through the normal verification process. SEAL's emergency pause runbook says this operationally, in its resolution
section: fix the underlying issue, test the fix thoroughly, plan the unpause procedure, and **execute
unpause with full verification, not the emergency process**.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-emergency-pause-runbook" />
The emergency path is a one-way door by design.

### Bound 3 — Force the pause to expire

This is the bound that does the most work and the one most often missing. The academic proposal calls
it safe pause: freeze all transactions in the contract **with a forced un-freeze after a certain
deadline**.<Cite src="arxiv-administrated-erc20" />

Notice what it changes about the failure mode. Without expiry, the default state of an abandoned or
hostile pause is "frozen forever" — inaction favours the freezer. With expiry, inaction returns the
protocol to service, and a guardian who wants the freeze to persist must keep acting, publicly, which
is observable. The bound does not require anyone to be trustworthy. It requires them to be present.

### Bound 4 — Cool down before re-pausing

Expiry alone is defeated by repetition: pause, expire, pause again, forever. The same proposal closes
it — once the contract is un-frozen, it cannot be frozen again for some
time.<Cite src="arxiv-administrated-erc20" />

The cooldown is the bound that makes the expiry mean something, and choosing its length is a real
tradeoff rather than a formality. Too short and the freeze is effectively permanent. Too long and a
second, genuine incident during the cooldown finds you with no rung 1 at all — which is an argument
for having built rungs 2 through 4 from the previous lesson, and for putting an explicit governance
override on the cooldown.

The same paper pairs safe pause with two other mechanisms worth knowing about, because they generalise
the idea beyond pause: **deferred maintenance**, which announces a privileged action and enacts it only
after a delay so users can exit if they disagree, and a **board of trustees**, which spreads a power
that would otherwise sit with a single administrator.<Cite src="arxiv-administrated-erc20" /> Deferred

level — timelocks on high-risk actions, plus a separate **cancel guardian** role whose only job is to
cancel an unauthorised transaction sitting in a timelock.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" />

## What it costs to have the switch

Two costs, both small, both worth knowing so nobody argues about them at design time.

**On every guarded call, one storage read.** The `whenNotPaused` modifier reads the boolean before
running the function. On this repository's measured configuration — forge 1.7.1, solc 0.8.36, optimizer
off — a warm `SLOAD` costs 100 gas and a cold one 2100.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /> For the
first guarded call in a transaction you are paying the cold price; for every one after it in the same
transaction, the warm one. That is the honest number, and it is not the reason anyone should skip the
control.

**On pause itself, one state write.** Flipping the flag from zero to non-zero in a cold slot measured
at 20000 plus the 2100 cold-access charge on the same configuration.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" />
You will pay it once, at the worst possible moment, so budget the guardian wallet accordingly — a
guardian multisig that cannot afford its own gas at a 300 gwei base fee is a rung you do not have.

## Worked example: a bounded-pause vault

Building the four bounds. Each subgoal names what the step is *for*, because the whole point is that
these are properties, not features.

**Subgoal: separate the two authorities so unpause cannot borrow pause's speed.** Two roles, not one.
The guardian is low-threshold and fast; governance is slow and public. This is the structural version
of bound 2 — it is enforced by the modifiers, not by a policy document.

```solidity
bytes32 public constant GUARDIAN_ROLE = keccak256("GUARDIAN_ROLE");
bytes32 public constant GOVERNANCE_ROLE = keccak256("GOVERNANCE_ROLE");

uint256 public constant MAX_PAUSE = 7 days;
uint256 public constant COOLDOWN  = 1 days;

uint256 public pausedUntil;      // 0 when never paused
uint256 public repauseAllowedAt; // set when a pause window ends
```

**Subgoal: make the pause self-limiting at the moment it is taken, so expiry is not a separate action
anyone has to remember.** The pause stores a deadline rather than a boolean. Nobody has to un-pause for
the pause to end; the deadline does it.

```solidity
function pause() external onlyRole(GUARDIAN_ROLE) {
    require(block.timestamp >= repauseAllowedAt, "cooldown");
    require(!_isPaused(), "already paused");
    pausedUntil = block.timestamp + MAX_PAUSE;
    emit Paused(msg.sender);
}

function _isPaused() internal view returns (bool) {
    return block.timestamp < pausedUntil;
}
```

**Subgoal: make the guard read the deadline, not a flag, so expiry cannot be forgotten anywhere.**
Every guarded function goes through one predicate. If the expiry lived in a separate keeper
transaction, a missed keeper run would silently restore "frozen forever" as the default.

```solidity
modifier whenLive() {
    require(!_isPaused(), "paused");
    _;
}

function withdraw(uint256 shares) external whenLive { /* ... */ }
```

**Subgoal: give governance a way to end a pause early, and make it start the cooldown so the fast path
cannot be re-armed instantly.** Early resumption is a *governance* power, deliberately, and taking it
sets `repauseAllowedAt` so a guardian cannot immediately undo the decision.

```solidity
function resume() external onlyRole(GOVERNANCE_ROLE) {
    require(_isPaused(), "not paused");
    pausedUntil = block.timestamp;
    repauseAllowedAt = block.timestamp + COOLDOWN;
    emit Unpaused(msg.sender);
}
```

**Subgoal: close the hole this design just opened, which is the one reviewers miss.** When a pause
expires *on its own*, nothing has executed, so nothing set `repauseAllowedAt`. A guardian could
re-pause in the very next block and the expiry bound would be defeated by repetition. Derive the
cooldown from the pause window instead of relying on a call happening:

```solidity
function _repauseAllowedAt() internal view returns (uint256) {
    uint256 fromExpiry = pausedUntil == 0 ? 0 : pausedUntil + COOLDOWN;
    return fromExpiry > repauseAllowedAt ? fromExpiry : repauseAllowedAt;
}
```

and check `_repauseAllowedAt()` in `pause()` rather than the raw variable. The general lesson is worth
more than the snippet: **a bound that depends on someone sending a transaction is not a bound.** Any
property whose enforcement requires an action can be defeated by the action not happening, and the
party with an interest in it not happening is often the one holding the key.

**Subgoal: prove the four properties rather than asserting them.** Four tests, and they are exactly
the acceptance criteria for this module's practice: a guardian below governance threshold can pause; a
guardian cannot resume; the pause stops being effective after `MAX_PAUSE` with no transaction sent;
and a guardian cannot re-pause during the cooldown, whether the previous pause ended by expiry or by
`resume()`. That last test is the one that would have caught the hole above, which is the argument for
writing it.

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="pause-design-patterns">
This is the same vault, as submitted for review. Three of the four bounds are present. Which one is
broken, how would a guardian exploit it, and which test would have caught it?

```solidity
bytes32 public constant GUARDIAN_ROLE = keccak256("GUARDIAN_ROLE");
bytes32 public constant GOVERNANCE_ROLE = keccak256("GOVERNANCE_ROLE");

bool    public paused;
uint256 public pausedAt;
uint256 public constant MAX_PAUSE = 7 days;
uint256 public constant COOLDOWN  = 1 days;
uint256 public lastUnpause;

function pause() external onlyRole(GUARDIAN_ROLE) {
    require(block.timestamp >= lastUnpause + COOLDOWN, "cooldown");
    paused = true;
    pausedAt = block.timestamp;
}

function unpause() external onlyRole(GOVERNANCE_ROLE) {
    paused = false;
    lastUnpause = block.timestamp;
}

function expirePause() external {
    require(paused && block.timestamp > pausedAt + MAX_PAUSE, "not expired");
    paused = false;
    lastUnpause = block.timestamp;
}

modifier whenLive() {
    require(!paused, "paused");
    _;
}
```

<Answer>
**Bound 3 is broken: the expiry is not forced, it is offered.**

`expirePause()` is permissionless, which looks like the fix and is not. The pause does not end when
seven days elapse; it ends when somebody sends a transaction. The state variable the guard reads is
`paused`, and `paused` stays `true` until a call flips it. Every honest user of this contract is
relying on a stranger to pay gas on their behalf, forever, on schedule.

**How a guardian exploits it.** They do not need to do anything clever. Three routes, in increasing
order of hostility:

1. **Do nothing and let apathy work.** The seventh day arrives during a quiet weekend, nobody notices,
   and the contract stays frozen. There is no alert for "an expiry that nobody claimed".
2. **Censor the call economically.** During congestion, `expirePause()` costs real money and pays
   nothing. A guardian can outlast the goodwill of whoever is paying for it.
3. **Race it.** On the block where expiry becomes claimable, the guardian is competing with a caller
   who has no incentive and no bot. Even if `expirePause()` lands, the cooldown starts from *that
   moment*, so the guardian only needs to re-pause a day later — and the freeze has cost them one
   transaction per eight days.

**The contrast with the corrected version.** When the guard is `block.timestamp < pausedUntil`, expiry
happens because time passed. There is no transaction to send, nobody to pay, nothing to race, and
nothing to censor. That is what "structural" means here — the property holds without anyone acting,
which is exactly the case a bound has to survive.

**The test that would have caught it.** Not "call `expirePause()` after eight days and assert the
vault is live" — that test passes. The test is: **warp past `MAX_PAUSE`, send nothing at all, and
assert that a withdrawal succeeds.**

```solidity
function test_pauseExpiresWithoutAnyTransaction() public {
    vm.prank(guardian);
    vault.pause();
    vm.warp(block.timestamp + 7 days + 1);
    vm.prank(user);
    vault.withdraw(1e18);   // must not revert
}
```

Writing the assertion that way is the discipline. A test that sends the maintenance call before
checking the property is testing that the maintenance call works, not that the bound holds.

**One more thing worth noticing.** `pause()` here has no `require(!paused)`. A guardian can call it
repeatedly, resetting `pausedAt` each time and pushing the expiry out indefinitely, one cheap
transaction at a time — a second, independent route to a permanent freeze. Bounds interact, and
reviewing them one at a time is how a design passes review while none of its properties hold.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What pause does not stop

Worth listing, because the mental model "paused means safe" is wrong in three specific ways and each
one has produced a surprise during a real response.

**It does not stop reads, and other people's contracts are reading you.** `whenNotPaused` on your
mutating functions leaves every `view` function live, which is correct and is also why a paused
protocol can still be feeding a price, a share ratio or a total-supply figure into somebody else's
solvency check. If your paused state makes one of those values stale or wrong, you have exported the
incident.

**It does not stop your token from trading.** Pausing a vault does not pause the secondary market in
its share token, and the market will price in whatever it believes about the pause within minutes. The
communications lesson later in this module exists partly because that repricing is driven by what you
say, not by what your contracts do.

**It does not stop your integrators from breaking.** A paused entry point is a revert, and reverts
propagate. Every protocol that composes with you now has a failing call path in the middle of its own
transaction — most notably liquidation, which is time-critical and unforgiving. This is a real cost
of rung 1 and it belongs in the harm estimate you make in advance, not in a post-incident discovery.

## Automating your side of the race

If the argument is that a human cannot beat an automated attacker, the obvious response is to remove
the human: a monitor that detects the condition and a relayer that submits a pre-authorised pause
transaction, with no page and no signature in the loop. This shortens the *acknowledge*, *decide* and
*authorise* segments to zero and leaves only detection and inclusion.

It is also the sharpest possible version of this lesson's problem. An automated pause is a key held by
a detector. Every false positive is now a freeze of everyone's funds with no human having agreed to
it, and the detector's thresholds — which you were warned in the monitoring module to derive from your
own baseline rather than copy — become the access-control policy for a privileged role. The four
bounds matter *more* here, not less: a forced expiry is what makes a false positive survivable.

<Aside kind="warn">
This is also a live example of why tooling recommendations have to be checked rather than inherited.
OpenZeppelin Defender was the default answer to "how do I wire a monitor to a relayer" for years. Its
own documentation now states that new sign-ups were disabled on 30 June 2025 and that the platform ran
until a final shutdown on 1 July 2026, with the effort moving to the open-source versions of the same
tools — OpenZeppelin Monitor and OpenZeppelin Relayer — and migration guides published for
both.<Cite src="oz-defender-docs" /> Any runbook written before mid-2026 that names the hosted product
as its automation path is now naming something that has been switched off. Check the vendor's own
current page before you put a dependency in a document you will read at 03:47.
</Aside>

## The uncomfortable question: how much is any of this worth?

Having built the control carefully, be honest about its trajectory.

Trail of Bits' stated position, in the same article that recommends the pause guardian role, is that
attackers have become more advanced and attacks are often conducted in private mempools to prevent
proactive identification — and that **the potency of pause as a mechanism to reduce the impact of an
attack will likely go down over time** as attackers become more
advanced.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" /> The same article is candid that the pause guardian, while necessary for emergencies, is not a golden
bullet.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" /> It is a considered position from a firm that sees
a great many of these, and it should be attributed rather than repeated as established fact: no
quantitative support for the trend was found, and it is an argued claim, not a measured
one.<Cite src="chainpath-conflict-register" />

The mechanism behind the argument is easy to check against the first lesson's table, though. Pause
works when there is a gap between the attacker's first transaction and their last. An attack that
completes atomically in one slot leaves no gap at all. An attack submitted through a private mempool
removes the observation window that would have let you start earlier. Both trends point the same way.

**The practical implication holds regardless of whether the trend is measurable.** A protocol whose
entire incident plan is "we can pause" has one tool, aimed at an adversary who may not leave time to
use it, and it is the tool that costs honest users the most when it fires. The response is not to
abandon it — Trail of Bits' own maturity model keeps the pause guardian at level 3 — but to treat it as
one bounded rung, and to invest alongside it in designs that reduce exposure without requiring a human
to act at all: caps, rate limits, per-market isolation, and at the far end the immutability that
categorically removes the role.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" />

## What this unlocks

**Next**, the part that decides whether any of this exists on the night. A pause function is not a
pause capability: the published emergency runbook is almost entirely about paging, signer paths,
prepared calldata, an abbreviated verification checklist and backup signing infrastructure for when
the wallet UI itself is down.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-emergency-pause-runbook" /> Teams discover
which of those they lack during their first real incident, and the missing item is usually
unglamorous.

**Then**, the drills that are the only honest test of the difference between a runbook and a
capability — and after that, the contacts and agreements that have to exist before the night, and the
two documents the outside world will judge you by.
