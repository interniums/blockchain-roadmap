The contracts are unpaused. The exposure is closed. Somebody has to write the document, and the first
argument in the room is always about who it is for.

That argument has a correct answer and it is not "one of them". A post-mortem is read by three groups
whose needs are genuinely incompatible, and the failure mode of most published post-mortems is that
they are written for a fourth group — the team's own discomfort — and therefore serve none of the three.

**Users want one thing.** Am I affected, and what do I do. They will read the first two paragraphs and
the impact section and nothing else, and if the answer is not in those, the answer they take away is
whatever the loudest reply says it is.

**The ecosystem wants the bug class and the mechanism**, at enough detail that other teams can check
whether they have the same problem. This is the part that feels like exposure and is actually the
obligation: SEAL's lessons-learned guidance lists sharing the lessons with the ecosystem to promote
awareness and improve overall security practices as a best practice in its own right, not as optional
courtesy.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-lessons-learned" /> You have been the beneficiary of other teams
doing this for as long as you have been in the field.

**The team, and anyone evaluating the team, wants to know which control failed.** Not which person.
Which missing thing let this reach production and let it run as long as it did.

A document written to minimise embarrassment answers none of those, and — the diagnostic that
matters — it produces no action items, which is the actual output the review exists to
generate.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-ir-policy" />

## Two documents, two clocks

A common source of confusion, worth resolving before the template: the published guidance gives
different timings, because it is describing different artifacts.

**The public write-up is fast.** Immunefi's guide says a full post-mortem within a day or two of
resolution, and lists it among the traits of teams that come out of an incident with their trust
intact — published honestly, within 48 hours.<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" />

**The internal review is slower and more thorough.** SEAL's process has the incident leader schedule
the post-mortem within a week of resolution, the scribe prepare the draft before the meeting, all
responders contribute, and the team review the timeline and root causes
together.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-postmortem-template" /><Cite src="seal-frameworks-ir-policy" />

These are not in conflict; they are sequenced. The fast document answers the users and the ecosystem
from what the scribe already recorded. The slower one answers the team, and it is the one that
produces owned, dated changes. Teams that publish only the fast one never change anything; teams that
do only the slow one leave the public narrative to somebody else for a week.

## The template, and why each field is there

SEAL's post-mortem template is a good checklist to hold yourself to. Walking it is faster than arguing
about structure, and every field maps to one of the three
audiences.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-postmortem-template" />

**Metadata** — incident date, severity, authors, status, and a link to the incident
log.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-postmortem-template" /> The link is the scribe's work becoming
checkable.

**Summary** — two to four paragraphs; what happened, when, how long, how it was resolved, written so
that someone unfamiliar understands the incident after reading it. This is the users' section, and the
template's phrasing sets the bar at *unfamiliar*, not *technical*.

**Impact**, split three ways so it cannot be blurred.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-postmortem-template" /> Users affected, duration, services unavailable.
Then financial: **funds at risk** and **actual losses**, as two separate numbers — which is the field
that stops a team from quoting whichever is more flattering. Then reputation: public visibility, media
coverage.

**Timeline**, with six load-bearing moments named as separate rows: incident began, detected, response
started, root cause identified, mitigation applied,
resolved.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-postmortem-template" /> That decomposition is the entire value.
It makes *how long did detection take* and *how long did response take* answerable as arithmetic
rather than as a matter of framing, and it is why the review questions later in this lesson can be
answered at all.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-lessons-learned" />

**Root cause**, split into primary cause and contributing factors, with a **5 Whys** table. The split
matters because "human error" is almost never a root cause and almost always a primary cause with four
contributing factors underneath it.

**What went well / What went wrong / Where we got lucky.** That third heading is unusual and it is the
best field in the template. It asks for the fortunate circumstances that helped and should not be
relied on next time — which is exactly the category of thing that otherwise disappears into "the
response went well".

**Action items**, each with an owner, a deadline and a status. The template states the rule twice: in
the how-to-use section, that every post-mortem produces action items with owners and deadlines, and
again above the table itself.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-postmortem-template" />

**Lessons for runbooks** — an explicit three-way choice: new runbook needed, existing runbook to
update, or no runbook changes needed. This is the field that closes the loop back to the first half of
this module.

**Detection** — how it was detected, time to detection, and *could we detect
faster*.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-postmortem-template" /> A whole section for the thing everyone
would otherwise mention in passing.

### The one thing to add for an on-chain incident

The generic template records times in UTC. Add **block numbers alongside the timestamps**. A reader
with an RPC endpoint can then verify your timeline against the chain instead of taking your word for
it, and the two numbers together make claims like "the attack ran for four blocks before the pause
landed" precise rather than approximate. The war-room checklist already asks the scribe to record
attack transactions and the team's own transactions in the event
timeline,<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" /> so the material exists — it just has to survive into
the published document.

## What a real timeline looks like when it is honest

The published example post-mortem is not an on-chain incident — it is a rate-limiter misconfiguration
that took the API down — and it is more useful for that, because nobody reading it is defending
anything. Look at what it is willing to write down.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-example-postmortem" />

The timeline: config change deployed at 14:15, incident began at 14:32, detected at 14:32 by an alert,
response started at 14:38, incident leader assigned at 14:42, root cause identified at 15:05, rollback
complete at 15:25, resolved at 16:45. Impact stated as roughly 3,000 users affected over 2 hours 13
minutes, funds at risk none, actual losses none.

The contributing factors: no validation for obviously wrong values, the change was not tested in
staging, no gradual rollout, and the alert threshold took 17 minutes to trigger. The 5 Whys terminates
at "never had an incident before (survivorship bias)". The detection section says time to detection 17
minutes and, in answer to *could we detect faster*, yes — lower the alert threshold to 1%. And where we
got lucky: it happened during business hours, the rollback was simple, and there was no financial
impact because this was the API layer rather than the smart
contracts.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-example-postmortem" />

Three things to steal from that. **The detection delay is stated as a number and then criticised in
the same document.** **A contributing factor names the team's own reasoning as flawed** — survivorship
bias, in writing. And **the luck is separated from the competence**, which is what makes the "what went
well" section believable.

## The public version: four fields the generic template does not have

The template is a general incident-management artifact, and an on-chain post-mortem carries four
things it does not ask for. Each exists because your reader can check it, which is a property no
web-service post-mortem has ever had.

**The transactions, by hash.** The attack transaction or transactions, and your own defensive
transactions, listed and labelled. The scribe's table already distinguishes the initial exploit, the
attacker moving funds, and the team's defensive move,<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" /> and
publishing that distinction is what stops a reader from mistaking your rescue for part of the attack.
A post-mortem that describes an exploit without naming its transaction is asking to be taken on faith
for the one claim that could have been verified in ten seconds.

**The addresses, with status.** Contracts affected, contracts paused, contracts unaffected, and the
attacker addresses. The status column from the war room maps directly onto what a user needs: *is the
thing I have money in on this list, and which column is it in*.

**Funds movement and its current state.** Where the value went, classified by destination, and its
recovery status.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" /> This is the field that lets you be precise
about a number that is otherwise argued about for months — funds moved to an exchange and frozen are
not the same as funds in a mixer, and readers will conflate the two if you let them.

**What happens next, with dates.** Whether users are being made whole, from what source, on what
schedule, and what the protocol's state is now: paused, partially live, or migrated. The generic
template's action items serve the team; this field serves the user, and it is the only part of the
document most of them will act on.

The general principle behind all four: **anchor every claim to something a stranger can verify.** A
block number, a transaction hash, an address, a date. The parts of a post-mortem that cannot be
checked are exactly the parts a sceptical reader discounts, and on chain there is far less of that
category than in any other kind of incident report — which means every unanchored claim you make is a
choice.

## The review questions

SEAL's lessons-learned page supplies the question set the internal review runs on, and it is worth
reading as a list of things a competent outsider will ask you
anyway:<Cite src="seal-frameworks-lessons-learned" />

- Was the incident detected as quickly as it should have been?
- Did the severity level reflect actual impact?
- Were the right people involved early enough?
- Did the team have an appropriate runbook, or was too much invented during the response?
- Was the external communication cadence appropriate?
- Did logs, dashboards and evidence collection support investigation effectively?
- What should change in monitoring, alerting, staffing or access controls?

Notice that only one of those is about code. The rest are about detection, staffing, documentation,
communication and evidence — the five things this module has been building. And the page is explicit
about what the review should produce beyond a write-up: updates to playbooks and runbooks, alert
thresholds and monitoring coverage, access control or break-glass procedures, communication templates,
and training and tabletop exercises — ending with the line that the review should end with tangible
changes, not just a document.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-lessons-learned" />

## Blameless about people, ruthless about controls

The norm is precise in an asymmetric way, and the template states both halves in adjacent breaths:
*focus on learning, not blame*, and *every post-mortem produces action items with owners and
deadlines*.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-postmortem-template" />

Those two rules together are the whole discipline, and each is doing something the other cannot.

**Naming a person explains nothing.** The next person in that seat faces the same system, the same
tooling, the same review process and the same 03:47. An explanation that terminates in "someone made a
mistake" has stopped one step short of every actionable finding.

**Naming the missing control explains everything.** The invariant nobody wrote. The alert routed to a
channel nobody reads. The review step that had no owner. The drill never run. The calldata never
pre-built. Each of those converts directly into the owned, dated item the template demands, and each
of them survives the departure of everyone currently in the room.

**The failure mode is using blamelessness as cover for vagueness.** "Process improvements are underway"
is neither blameless nor specific — it is empty, and readers can tell. Immunefi's version of this is
worth quoting as a diagnosis: the worst post-mortems strip the timeline of context, offload blame onto
"sophisticated attackers" as if the phrase erased months of ignored warnings, and read more like a
legal filing than an honest accounting — a pattern that destroys communities faster than the original
exploit.<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" />

The same source points at the opposite pattern with equal confidence: teams that recovered well
published honestly, credited contributors, laid out the precise timeline, and walked through the
vulnerability in full technical depth — and it adds the distinction that matters most, that negotiating
with an attacker can be reasonable, while misleading the community about what happened is not
survivable, because the real story always leaks and the trust damage then becomes
permanent.<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" />

## What this unlocks, and what the track was for

**The immediate thing.** You now have the whole loop: detect, assemble, triage, contain, communicate,
review, and change something. Each stage has a published artifact you can adopt rather than invent, and
the module's two practices are the two halves of proving it works — building the bounded-pause vault
so the four properties are tested rather than promised, and running the red-team drill so the numbers
in your first post-mortem are your own.

**The thing worth noticing about the reading list.** Two of this module's exercises are reading
exercises, and they are not filler. Score two real post-mortems from the last year against the
template's fields and you will find that most published documents omit the detection delay, the
response delay, or both — and once you have seen that, you cannot write a document that omits them
without knowing you did. SEAL's first-responder training makes the same move, listing *read two or
three past post-mortems* as a prerequisite for going on
call.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-ir-roles" />

**And the thing this track was building toward.** Every earlier module produced a capability that only
becomes real here. Threat modelling produced the invariants your monitors watch. Fork testing produced
the technique your drill runs on. Simulation produced the triage loop and the pre-built calldata.
Auditing produced the habit of naming a class rather than an instance, which is what a long-term
action item is. Incident response is where those stop being skills and become an operational posture —
and the posture is measured by exactly one thing, which is what you have written down before you
needed it.

**The last item on that list is the one that costs nothing.** Save the hotline's contact path, in a
document you can reach from a phone, before you close this
