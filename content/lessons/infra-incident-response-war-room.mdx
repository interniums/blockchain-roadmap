Here is the failure mode this lesson exists to prevent, and it is not dramatic.

One engineer is in a call. They are decoding the attack transaction. Someone from the community team
joins and asks whether they should post something; the engineer says "give me a minute". A second
engineer joins and asks what is happening; the first re-explains from the top. A founder joins and
asks whether to pause; the engineer says they are not sure yet. The community team asks again. The
engineer finds the mispriced oracle read at minute nineteen, which is genuinely fast work, and looks
up to discover nobody has drafted a message, nobody has checked the deployment on the other chain,
nobody has told the two protocols that read this price, and the multisig signers have not been paged
because everyone assumed someone else did it.

Nothing in that story is incompetence. It is one thread executing a workload that is not serial.

## Roles are jobs, not headcount — and the published sources disagree about the headcount

Two well-sourced playbooks describe a war room's role structure, and reading them together is more
useful than reading either alone, because they disagree in a specific and instructive way.

**Immunefi's field guide says three roles, and is emphatic about the ceiling.** An Ops Lead who
conducts: keeps decisions timeboxed, runs the workflow, assigns players, divides timezone coverage.
A Security Analyst who finds the truth: identifies the attack vector and its scope, builds mitigations,
monitors for secondary attacks, validates readiness before any unpause. A Comms Lead who owns trust:
clear, calm, factual updates on a predictable cadence, and who acts as a firewall between the
technical team and the community so the analysts stay heads-down. Their stated reason for the ceiling
is that every additional participant slows decision-making and raises leak
risk.<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" />

**SEAL's incident-response template says the roles are these and the headcount is yours.** Its policy
page opens the role section with the line that one person can hold multiple roles, and that roles can
be reassigned during an incident as needed. It then names six: Detector, Incident Leader, Scribe,
Communication Manager, Subject Matter Experts, Decision Makers.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-ir-policy" />
The staffing page names three core roles that every incident needs *even if one person wears multiple
hats* — Incident Leader, Scribe, Responders — and adds Communication Manager and SMEs for larger
incidents.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-ir-roles" /> And SEAL's own war-room playbook lists six named
seats to fill: Operations, Scribe, Strategy Lead, Protocol Lead, Web/Infrastructure Lead, External
Communicator.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" />

So: three, three-plus-two, or six, depending on which page you open. Resolving that by picking a number
is the wrong move. The resolution is that **these are decompositions of the work, not staffing
mandates**, and the sources agree completely about the work.

**There is exactly one headcount rule that is actually published, and it is worth memorising.** SEAL's
staffing guidance for small teams says the leader also serves as scribe for minor incidents, and then
draws the line: a **separate scribe and incident leader for P1 and P2**
incidents.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-ir-roles" /> P1 is the severity band that includes an active
exploit and a private key compromise.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-ir-policy" /> That is the one place
the guidance says a role must be a different person, and it is worth understanding why rather than
just obeying it.

### Why the scribe cannot also be fixing things

SEAL's policy is unusually direct about this role: the scribe documents everything in the incident log
and maintains timestamps in UTC, capturing decisions and rationale — and then, in its own sentence,
*this is a focused role, do not also assign the scribe to fix
things*.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-ir-policy" />

The reason is structural rather than about diligence. Documentation during an incident is not
note-taking; it is the shared state that every other role reads from. The war-room playbook makes the
scribe's duty explicit — consolidating the intel that accurately scopes impact, informs new war-room
members and third parties efficiently, and feeds external
communication.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" /> Every person who joins at minute forty reads the
scribe's document instead of interrupting an analyst. Every message the comms owner sends is drawn
from it. Every claim in the post-mortem is checked against it. A scribe who is also debugging produces
the document in bursts, and the gaps are exactly the minutes when the most decisions were being made.

There is a second reason, which nobody enjoys: the log is the record of whether you acted in good
faith. Immunefi's guidance frames war-room documentation as a signal in its own right — it demonstrates
professionalism, supports the eventual post-mortem, and proves the team operated in good faith under
pressure.<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" />

## Parallel, not serial — and the checklist says so structurally

The engineering instinct is to diagnose, then decide, then act, then announce. In most systems that
ordering is correct and disciplined. Here it spends the response window on step one.

The strongest evidence for this is not an argument, it is a document layout. SEAL's war-room playbook
has a section titled "Perform Immediately" — notify SEAL 911, create a war room with audio, duplicate
the collaboration document, share the link — followed by a section titled **"Perform in Parallel by
Role"**, under which four lanes run at once:<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" />

- **Analysis** — scope the impact, gather transactions involved, gather affected addresses, record
  funds movement, gather attacker information, investigate and update the issue description, propose
  workable solutions.
- **Protocol actions** — take immediate corrective and preventative actions to prevent further loss,
  pause contracts if possible, execute pre-made defensive scripts, prioritise proposed solutions,
  validate and execute the solution, prepare monitoring alerts for situations requiring future action.
- **Web actions** — disable deposits and withdrawals in the UI as needed, enable front-end IP or
  address blacklisting, create a front end for any user action now required such as revoking approvals
  or migrating funds.
- **Communications** — identify the platforms that must be reached, prepare internal and external
  messages, gather security contacts for potentially affected downstream protocols such as bridges and
  lending protocols, notify block explorers for attacker address labelling, monitor social media for
  information that aids whitehat efforts, and maintain the event timeline.

Read the Protocol actions lane again. "Take immediate corrective and preventative actions to prevent
further loss of funds" sits in a lane that runs *concurrently* with "investigate the issue". The
document is not asking you to pause before you understand; it is refusing to make understanding a
precondition for protecting what has not left yet.

The consequence is uncomfortable and correct: **decisions get made on partial information and then
revised.** That is not sloppiness, it is the only available policy when the alternative is a perfect
decision made after the exposure is gone. The discipline is to keep revising — which is why the Ops
Lead role is described in terms of timeboxing and enforcing frequent sync cycles rather than in terms
of technical judgement.<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" />

## The four triage questions, in order

Inside the Analysis lane there is a loop, and the order of its questions is not arbitrary — each one
gates a decision the lead is waiting on. Running them out of order produces a beautiful write-up and a
late containment decision.

**Question one: which transaction is the attack?** This is the first item on the published analysis
checklist for a reason.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" /> It is the concrete artifact everything
downstream works from: the mechanism is read out of it, the exposure is scoped from it, the timeline
anchors on it, and the comms owner needs its hash. Until you have it, every other question is being
answered about a hypothesis.

**Question two: what mechanism did it use?** Enough to reason about, not enough to publish. You need
the answer to be shaped like "the collateral price is read from a spot pool that a flash loan can
move", not like a full write-up with a proof of concept. [Simulating the attacker's transaction with a
decoded trace](/c/simulation-for-incident-triage) is the fastest route to that shape, and it is the
same tool you will use to check your own response before you send it.

**Question three: is the value that has not left still exposed to that same mechanism?** This is the
scoping question, and it is the one the containment decision actually depends on. "The attacker took
$6M" does not tell the lead what to do. "The remaining $34M is reachable by the same path, and the
attacker's transaction is repeatable" does. So does its opposite: "the mispriced asset had a $6M cap
and it is now exhausted", which is a real and frequently correct answer that argues for *not* pausing.

**Question four: does the same mechanism exist in our other deployments, our other chains, or code
someone forked?** This is the one that gets deferred, and deferring it is how a contained incident
restarts somewhere else forty minutes later. Note where the checklist puts the outward-facing half of
it: gathering security contacts for potentially affected downstream protocols is listed as war-room
work in the Communications lane, not as
aftercare.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" /> The inward-facing half — your own sibling
deployments — follows the same logic and nobody else will do it for you.

<Aside kind="note">
Question four is also the strongest argument for calling for outside help early. SEAL 911's stated
role includes coordinating response across the industry and connecting you with security researchers
and other affected protocols,<Cite src="seal-frameworks-contacts" /> and a responder who sat in the
last three incidents with this mechanism knows which other protocols share it. You will meet the
contact path itself in a later lesson; the point here is that the call belongs inside the parallel
lanes, not after them.
</Aside>

### The shape the answers get written into

The playbook does not leave "record what you find" as an instruction; it gives the scribe four tables,
and their column headers are worth stealing directly because each column is a question somebody will
ask you later.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" />

- **Transactions involved** — link plus notes, so that "the exploit transaction", "the attacker moving
  funds" and "our defensive move" are distinguishable a week later.
- **Affected addresses** — address, link, and a **status** field, with values like at risk, impacted,
  paused, saved, needs review, uncertain. The status column is the one that converts a list into a
  work queue.
- **Funds movement** — origin, transaction, amount and asset, destination classified as contract,
  exchange, bridge or mixer, and a recovery status. Destination class is what determines who you
  contact next: an exchange address is a freeze request, a bridge address is a cross-chain problem, a
  mixer address usually is not recoverable by you.
- **Attacker information** — address, how it was funded, notes. This is the material a forensics firm
  or law enforcement will ask for, and it is far cheaper to capture live than to reconstruct.

The events timeline sits alongside them, recorded in UTC, with first notice, war-room creation,
external communications, attack transactions and the team's own transactions as the events worth
capturing.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" /> That timeline is the raw material of the
post-mortem, and the last lesson in this module is about what it becomes.

## Worked example: the first twelve minutes, run in parallel

A price-feed-driven lending market. The monitor fires on a total-assets drop inside one block. Three
people are awake: you, a second engineer, and the founder. Watch how the work is assigned rather than
sequenced.

**Subgoal: establish a single place where state lives, before doing any analysis.** The playbook's
"Perform Immediately" block is four items and none of them are technical: notify the hotline, create a
war room with audio, duplicate the collaboration document, share the
link.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" /> Doing this first costs ninety seconds and is what makes
the next ten minutes parallelisable at all — without a shared document, every new arrival costs an
analyst a re-explanation.

**Subgoal: name the leader out loud, so that decisions have an owner.** The founder takes it. The
naming matters more than the choice: an unnamed leader means every decision becomes a discussion, and
the role that gets described in the sources is a coordinator who assigns and decides, not the person
who knows the code best.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-ir-policy" />

**Subgoal: separate the scribe, because this is a P1.** The second engineer is a better analyst than
the founder, so the founder cannot be the scribe *and* the leader here — this is exactly the P1 case
where the guidance asks for a separate scribe.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-ir-roles" /> With three
people the honest answer is that you need a fourth, and the fastest way to get one is to page a
teammate whose only job for the next hour is to write down what everyone says. That page should go out
in the same minute as the war room is created.

**Subgoal: start the analysis loop on question one, and only question one.** You pull the block, find
the outsized transaction, and put its hash in the shared document. Two minutes. Resist the pull toward
explaining it before it is written down, because the hash is what unblocks two other lanes: the comms
owner cannot say anything specific without it, and nobody can check the sibling deployment without
knowing what to check for.

**Subgoal: run the containment lane concurrently, on the information that exists.** The leader does
not wait for the mechanism. With a confirmed outsized outflow and no benign explanation, the pause
signers get paged now, in parallel with your analysis, because paging is the slow segment and it can
be started on partial information. If the analysis comes back benign in four minutes, the cost is an
apologetic message to five signers. That trade is not close.

**Subgoal: answer question three before recommending a rung.** Simulation at the current block: does
the same call still succeed against remaining collateral? If yes, the exposure is live and the pause
is worth its cost. If the mispriced asset's cap is exhausted, you have just discovered that the
correct containment action might be to delist one asset rather than to freeze $34M of unrelated
positions — which is the subject of the next lesson.

**Subgoal: start question four before the incident feels finished.** While signatures are being
collected, somebody — not the analyst, not the scribe — enumerates every chain you have deployed to
and every protocol that reads this feed. This is dead time for the containment lane and it is the
highest-value use of a spare person in the room.

**Subgoal: keep the room small and closed.** The playbook's appendix advises limiting occupancy, being
careful not to invite too many people early, warning members not to publicise anything without the
protocol's consent, and not speaking to press or
publications.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" /> This reads as etiquette and is not: everything in
that document is the mechanism an attacker would most like to read, and the exposure it describes is
still open.

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="parallel-not-serial">
An internal post-mortem contains this timeline. Every entry is true and the team is proud of it. Name
the three structural mistakes, and say what the timeline would look like if the response had been run
in parallel.

```text
03:47  Monitor alert: vault assets -18% in one block
03:52  On-call engineer acknowledges, opens explorer
04:06  Attack transaction identified
04:31  Root cause identified: spot-price oracle, flash-loan movable
04:34  Decision to pause taken
04:38  Signers paged
05:22  Pause transaction executed (3/5 collected)
05:40  First public statement
06:15  Discovered the Arbitrum deployment had been drained at 04:58
```

<Answer>
**Mistake one: the page to the signers waited on the root cause.** Look at the gap between 04:34 and
05:22 — forty-eight minutes to collect three signatures. That is the *authorise* segment, and it is
the longest single block on the timeline. It did not start until 04:38 because the decision waited for
04:31. Paging is the one step that costs almost nothing if you turn out to be wrong and costs
everything if it starts late; it should have gone out at 03:53, on the strength of the alert alone,
concurrently with the analysis. The published checklist puts "take immediate corrective and
preventative actions" in a lane that runs alongside investigation rather than after
it.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" />

**Mistake two: communications is at the end of a chain instead of in its own lane.** 05:40 is nearly
two hours after the alert, and it is late for a reason that has nothing to do with communications
skill: it was blocked behind pause, which was blocked behind root cause. In a parallel structure the
comms owner is drafting from the moment the war room opens, working from prepared building blocks, and
the only thing gating the first message is approval — not the mechanism, which should not be in the
first message anyway.

**Mistake three, and the expensive one: question four was never asked until it answered itself.** The
Arbitrum deployment was drained at 04:58 — twenty-seven minutes *after* the root cause was known on
mainnet, and twenty-four minutes before the mainnet pause even executed. There was a window in which
somebody with the mechanism in hand could have paused it, and nobody was assigned to look. Checking
your own sibling deployments and notifying downstream protocols is war-room work performed in
parallel, and the checklist places it there deliberately.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" />

**The parallel version, same facts:**

```text
03:47  Monitor alert
03:49  War room opened, shared doc created, leader named, scribe paged
03:51  Signers paged (containment lane, on the alert alone)
03:52  Analysis lane starts; comms lane starts drafting from templates
03:58  Attack transaction identified and posted to the shared doc
04:02  Chain/deployment enumeration started by the fourth person
04:05  First public statement: acknowledgement, no mechanism
04:14  Pause executed on mainnet (signers were already assembled)
04:16  Arbitrum pause executed
04:31  Root cause identified
```

Root cause still lands at 04:31. Nothing about the analysis got faster. Everything else moved because
it was never waiting on it.

**The generalisation worth keeping:** in a serial timeline, the longest segment is whatever is at the
end. In a parallel one, the longest segment is the true bottleneck — and on almost every real timeline
that bottleneck is collecting signatures, which is why two later lessons in this module are about
nothing else.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**Next**, the decision the leader is actually making in the containment lane. "Pause" is the rung
everyone knows; the ladder has rungs above and below it, each with a different cost to honest users,
and a team that has only rehearsed the top rung will take an all-or-nothing decision under time
pressure. The published checklist already names several of the other
rungs.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" />

**Then**, why the top rung is a two-sided power — the same key that stops the attacker freezes every
honest user, and Trail of Bits' position is that its potency declines as attackers
automate.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" />

**And the comms lane you just ran concurrently** gets its own lesson, because "what you say and when"
is a security decision with its own tradeoffs, and the guidance on it is specific: designated
spokespersons, pre-approved templates, a fixed update schedule, and updates that say there is no new
information.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-incident-comms" />
