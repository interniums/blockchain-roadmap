There is a category of preparation that costs nothing, takes twenty minutes, and is missing from most
teams: the list of people outside your company who can help, saved somewhere you can reach it from a
phone, with each entry tested.

It is missing for an ordinary reason. Every other item in this module produces an artifact you can
point at — a contract, a runbook, a drill report. A contacts list produces nothing until the night it
is the only thing that matters, and then it produces the difference between spending your first
fifteen minutes on containment and spending them working out who to ask.

## SEAL 911

The ecosystem has a standing emergency function. SEAL 911 is described by the Security Alliance as a
free, around-the-clock emergency hotline connecting anyone facing an active or imminent security
incident with a vetted team of security researchers, covering everything from exploit triage to fund
recovery.<Cite src="seal-911" />

Its published operating figures give a sense of the volume it handles: over 3,300 tickets handled,
over 200 war rooms coordinated, more than $180M in assets rescued, and 31 volunteer
responders.<Cite src="seal-911" /> A responder writing about the experience from the inside describes
the range as vulnerability disclosures, war rooms stood up both to prevent exploitation of live
vulnerabilities and to help protocols under active attack, and a large volume of individual-victim
cases from scams, phishing and drainer malware — plus significant on-chain forensics work, tracing
stolen funds and producing something a victim can take to law
enforcement.<Cite src="dedaub-seal-911" />

**Three things about it are load-bearing for your runbook.**

**It is a contact path, not a service you are entitled to.** The project's own repository states that
it operates as a volunteer organisation, does not provide paid services, and handles tickets based on
available volunteer bandwidth — noting that many tickets need extensive on-chain tracing over long
periods that the volume does not
permit.<Cite src="seal-911-repo" /> Firms offering that work commercially are listed separately. Plan
for triage help and coordination, not for an outsourced response.

**The contact mechanism must be read from the source, not from an article.** As of this writing the
repository states the group is reached through a Telegram bot.<Cite src="seal-911-repo" /> That is the
kind of detail that changes, and SEAL's own contacts template acknowledges it — the SEAL 911 row in
that template has the *how to contact* cell left as an instruction to add the current method from
their site rather than hard-coding one.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-contacts" /> Copy the mechanism from
the repository on the day you write your runbook, and re-check it when you review the document. A
stale instruction is worse than no instruction, because it burns minutes before it fails.

**There is an impersonation protocol, and you should know it before you need it.** Membership is
invite-only, with at least one existing member typically vouching for a new one and commercial
organisations limited to two members.<Cite src="seal-911-repo" /> That trust model creates an obvious
attack: contact a team in crisis, claim to be a responder, and ask for something. The published
counter-measure is specific — if a member reaches out to you directly, share an unguessable keyword
sentence with them, then open a ticket through the official bot and ask the member to produce that
keyword; provided the bot itself is uncompromised, this verifies
them.<Cite src="seal-911-repo" />

### What to have ready when you open the ticket

The repository asks one thing of the person contacting them: give as much information as possible, so
that responders are not duplicating work.<Cite src="seal-911-repo" /> That is easy to comply with and
easy to fail at 03:50, and the fix is that you already built the artifact in the war-room lesson. The
scribe's four tables — transactions involved, affected addresses with a status column, funds movement
with destination class and recovery status, attacker information with funding
source<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" /> — are precisely the intake a responder needs. Sharing a
link to that document is a better opening message than any summary you could write, and it means the
answer to "what have you tried" is already visible.

Note the ordering this implies. The playbook's "Perform Immediately" block puts *notify the hotline*
and *create the collaboration document* in the same four-item list, before the parallel lanes
start.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" /> Calling early and calling with structure are not in
tension; the structure takes ninety seconds and it is the thing that makes the call worth making.

### The surrounding institution, briefly

The hotline is the part you use during an incident, and it sits inside a larger set of things worth
knowing exist before one. The Security Alliance also publishes SEAL Intel, a threat-intelligence
function distributing alerts on emerging threats such as phishing campaigns, wallet drainers and
state-sponsored attackers; SEAL Frameworks, the vendor-neutral open-source library this module has
been quoting throughout; SEAL Wargames, the live drills from the previous lesson; a modular
certification programme covering incident response and multisig operations; and Safe Harbor, which is
the subject of the next section.<Cite src="seal-our-work" />

Two of those change what you do on an ordinary Tuesday rather than during an incident. The Frameworks
library is where your runbook should be derived from rather than invented, and the threat intelligence
is how you find out that the drainer campaign hitting three of your peers is coming for your users
next.

## The rest of the list

SEAL's contacts template is a good inventory of who else exists, and its structure is worth copying
wholesale because each section answers a question that comes up at a predictable point in a
response.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-contacts" />

- **Decision makers** — role, name, phone, Telegram or Signal, email. These are the people who can
  authorise the expensive decisions, and the guidance is that they should be reachable 24/7 for
  critical incidents.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-ir-roles" />
- **Subject matter experts**, with a primary and a backup for smart contracts, infrastructure,
  frontend and security. A single name in a cell is a single point of failure wearing a person's face.
- **Security partners** — auditors, an incident-response retainer if you have one, your bug bounty
  platform. The auditor who reviewed the contract knows its assumptions; that is a shortcut to triage
  question two.
- **Infrastructure vendors** — hosting, CDN, DNS, RPC provider, monitoring — with both a support
  contact and an escalation path. Not every incident is a contract exploit, and a DNS compromise is
  answered by a registrar, not by a multisig.
- **Legal and communications**, named in advance.
- **Key partners** — protocols, exchanges and integrators to notify during major incidents, with a
  *when to notify* column. This is where the answer to triage question four gets written down before
  you need it.

The template also gives a **P1 escalation order**: SEAL 911 if there is an active exploit, then
internal decision makers, then security partners, then legal if there is fund loss or regulatory
exposure.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-contacts" /> And it treats the document as something that rots —
review quarterly, verify contact details after team changes, test escalation paths periodically, and
assign an owner to maintain it.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-contacts" />

Two entries from the war-room checklist belong here too, because they are outward-facing and easy to
forget: **block explorers**, for getting attacker addresses labelled, and **security contacts for
potentially affected downstream protocols** such as bridges and lending
protocols.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" /> Both are far easier to arrange in advance than to
find at 04:00.

## Deciding the legal question before the rescue

There is one preparedness item that is not a phone number, and it exists because of a genuine
asymmetry: when a whitehat moves funds out of a vulnerable contract to protect them, their
transactions look identical on chain to the attack. Same privileged call, same large outflow, same
unfamiliar destination. Whether they are allowed to do that is unclear by default, and the hesitation
that ambiguity produces is measured in the minutes during which someone else takes the funds.

SEAL's Safe Harbor is the framework built for this. It is an on-chain agreement a protocol adopts in
advance to pre-authorise whitehat intervention during an active exploit, with published terms:
rescued funds must be returned within 72 hours, bounties are set at 10% of recovered assets capped at
$1M, and participating researchers are backed by the Security Research Legal Defense Fund, which
covers eligible legal fees.<Cite src="seal-safe-harbor-project" /> The project page reports 33 protocol
adopters, over $60B in assets protected, and deployment across more than ten
chains.<Cite src="seal-safe-harbor-project" />

**What adoption buys is definite: shared terms that already exist.** Who is authorised to act, under
what conditions, where rescued funds must be sent, and what the rescuer receives — all settled while
nobody is under pressure. During the incident, the conversation is about execution rather than about
whether execution is permitted.

**What adoption does not buy is a legal guarantee, and this is where to be careful.** Present it as a
framework a protocol can adopt, not as established legal protection. How it stands in any particular
jurisdiction was not something this curriculum could establish, and asserting otherwise would be
overclaiming on a question where being wrong is expensive for a real
person.<Cite src="chainpath-conflict-register" /> The Legal Defense Fund's existence is itself an
admission that the underlying risk has not disappeared.

## Why this is a containment topic and not aftercare

The copycat dynamic is the reason all of this is timed rather than merely useful. Once an exploit
transaction is public, it is a working, readable recipe: [unsophisticated actors replay it with the
address swapped](/c/copycat-window), against your forks, your sibling deployments and anyone running
the same code. The window between publication and replay is short and it is not under your control.

Two of the outward-facing items above are what you can actually do about it. Notifying downstream
protocols and integrators is faster than each of them discovering it independently. And SEAL 911's
stated role includes coordinating response across the industry and connecting you with other affected
protocols<Cite src="seal-frameworks-contacts" /> — which is leverage no individual team has, because a
responder who has seen this mechanism in two other incidents knows who else has it.

2. **Your auditor.** Tested means you have a named human and a channel that is not a ticketing form,

3. **Your RPC and infrastructure providers.** Tested means you have raised one real escalation and
   know how long it took, because the incident is not the moment to discover your support tier.
4. **Downstream protocols and integrators.** Tested means someone at each has replied to a message
   from you. A contact who has never responded is a hypothesis.
5. **Block explorers.** Tested means you know the submission path for address labelling before you
   need to use it.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-war-room" />
6. **Legal counsel with crypto experience.** Tested means they have been briefed on your architecture
   once, in calm conditions, so the first conversation is not also the education.

**The entry you cannot test is the whitehat.** You cannot rehearse a stranger deciding to rescue your
funds. What you can do is remove the reason they would hesitate: adopt Safe Harbor in advance so the
terms — authorisation, the 72-hour return window, the 10% bounty capped at $1M, the Legal Defense Fund
backing — are already defined rather than negotiated during the
drain.<Cite src="seal-safe-harbor-project" /> That is the substitute for testing: you cannot verify
the person, so you pre-answer the question that would slow them down.

**One more property to check on the whole document.** Every entry must be reachable from a phone, from
outside your own infrastructure, by more than one person. A contacts list living in a wiki behind the
SSO you have just disabled during a compromise response is a list you do not have — which is the same
class of failure as the emergency plan that depends on a wallet UI being up. And it needs an owner and
a quarterly review, because the guidance is explicit that stale contacts during an incident waste
critical time.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-contacts" />

## What this unlocks

**Next**, the thing you say while all of this is happening. Communication during an incident is not the
public-relations task that follows the engineering; it is one of the parallel lanes, with its own
tradeoffs and its own failure modes in both directions. Published guidance is unusually specific about
the shape — designated primary and backup spokespersons, pre-approved templates, a fixed update
schedule that explicitly includes updates saying there is no new information, and confirmed facts
rather than speculation — and there are published message templates for the exact situations you will
be in.<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" />

**Then the document you publish afterwards**, which three different audiences read for three
incompatible reasons, and whose absence the ecosystem now reads as a statement in itself.
