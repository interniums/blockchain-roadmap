Two published tables. Both describe the same set of indexing platforms. Both were produced by running
real workloads on real infrastructure. Both are cited by people making real decisions.

One of them reports The Graph at 3,780 times slower than the fastest platform on an event-indexing
workload.<Cite src="envio-hyperindex-benchmarks" /> The other reports the same comparison at roughly
27 times.<Cite src="sentio-obib" />

That is a factor of 140 between two honest-looking measurements of the same thing. This lesson is
about how that happens, what the more carefully specified of the two actually found, and what you
should do with any indexer performance number you are handed.

## Every vendor wins their own benchmark

Start with the observation that motivates the whole topic, made by one of the vendors about the field
including itself. The open benchmark's introduction lists the prior art: Envio's 2023 benchmark
reporting 9.67-minute sync times for Uniswap V3 events, 2x faster than Subsquid, 80x faster than
Ponder and 100x faster than The Graph; Subsquid's own numbers claiming 200x faster than The Graph;
Ponder claiming 10x faster than Graph Node; and Ormi Labs publishing rankings with Ormi at number
one.<Cite src="sentio-obib" />

Four vendors, four benchmarks, four winners.

The post then names the three structural reasons this keeps happening, and they are worth memorising
because they apply far beyond indexing: **one test case**, usually event processing, with nobody
testing blocks, transactions or traces; **no completeness checks**, so nobody asks whether all the data
actually arrived; and **mixed infrastructure**, with cloud against local, different RPC providers and
different hardware, making cross-study comparison impossible.<Cite src="sentio-obib" />

None of that requires anyone to lie. Pick the workload you are good at, do not verify record counts,
and run on the setup that suits you. Every number you publish is true.

It is also worth noticing how these numbers propagate. Envio's benchmarks page distinguishes
"independent" benchmarks conducted by a competitor from its own internal benchmarking blog post, and
links both.<Cite src="envio-hyperindex-benchmarks" /><Cite src="envio-benchmark-blog" /> That is more
transparent than most vendor pages manage. But by the time a figure reaches a comparison article, a
conference slide or a colleague's message, the distinction between "we measured this" and "someone
else measured this" has usually been lost — along with the date, the version and the workload. Ponder's
own documentation, for contrast, leads with what the framework *is* rather than with a
multiplier.<Cite src="ponder-docs" /> Neither choice is dishonest; they are different bets about what a
reader is shopping for.

**The publisher of the second one states a reason for part of the gap.** The OBIB post says that when
Envio forked their earlier benchmark repository to add their own results, they mixed HyperSync times —
a raw data extraction engine — with full indexer times, comparing a data dump tool against platforms
that parse, transform, store and serve data through APIs, and that this comparison made it into
marketing materials.<Cite src="sentio-obib" />

That is one party's account. It is a specific, checkable claim rather than an insinuation, and both
repositories are public,<Cite src="obib-repo" /><Cite src="envio-hyperindex-benchmarks" /> so it is
resolvable by anyone willing to read configurations. This lesson does not resolve it. What it asks you
to take away is the *mechanism*: HyperSync is documented by Envio itself as a raw retrieval layer
distinct from the full HyperIndex framework,<Cite src="envio-hypersync-overview" /> so "which of the
two produced this number" is exactly the kind of question a headline multiplier cannot answer and a
published configuration can.

## What the well-specified benchmark actually found

Set the dispute aside and read the more carefully specified results for what they show. OBIB ran six
cases on Ethereum mainnet, each isolating a different capability, each with a fixed block range and an
expected record count.<Cite src="sentio-obib" />

**Case 1 — events only.** Blocks 0 to 22,200,000, 294,278 expected records. Envio 6.94, Sentio 11.02,
Sentio Subgraph 14.90, Ponder 34.80, Subsquid 40.94, Subgraph 188.79 minutes. All platforms returned
correct data.<Cite src="sentio-obib" />

**Case 2 — events plus contract reads.** Blocks 22,400,000 to 22,500,000, 7,634 expected. Sentio 7.78,
Envio 8.54, Sentio Subgraph 29.23, Subsquid 46.85, Ponder 64.86, Subgraph 66.41 minutes. All correct.
The post argues this is the more useful test, because production indexers are almost always fetching
balances or reading contract state while processing events.<Cite src="sentio-obib" />

**The ranking has already changed.** Envio leads Case 1 by a clear margin; Sentio edges ahead in Case
2. Two workloads, two leaders, and the workloads differ only by whether handlers make contract reads.

**Case 3 — blocks.** Blocks 0 to 100,000, 100,001 expected. Subsquid 0.17 minutes, Sentio 2.51, Sentio
Subgraph 2.67, Ponder 9.63, Subgraph 50.58 — and Envio HyperIndex marked not supported.<Cite src="sentio-obib" />

**Case 4 — transactions.** A 10,000-block range, 1,696,641 expected. Subsquid 1.25 minutes at 100%
completeness, Sentio 22.12 at 100%, Ponder timed out at 2.5% after two hours, and three platforms —
Envio, Subgraph and Sentio Subgraph — could not run it at all.<Cite src="sentio-obib" />

**Case 5 — traces.** A 90,000-block range, 50,191 expected. Sentio Subgraph 2.17 minutes at 91%,
Sentio 2.54 at 100%, Subsquid 7.42 at 100%, Subgraph 17.81 at 58%, Ponder 74.71 at 88%, Envio not
supported.<Cite src="sentio-obib" />

**Case 6 — factory templates.** A 10,000-block range, 35,039 expected. Envio 1.92 minutes matching
exactly, Sentio Subgraph 4.26 reporting 75,951, Subsquid 5.34 reporting 33,972, Ponder 6.44 reporting
182,767, Sentio 14.36 reporting 75,951, Subgraph 16.83 matching exactly.<Cite src="sentio-obib" />

### Three findings that outrank every timing

**The N/A rows.** Three platforms cannot index transactions; one cannot index blocks or traces
natively. A platform that cannot execute a workload is not slow at it — it is absent, and no
optimisation closes that gap. This is a categorical difference wearing the same table cell as a
quantitative one.

**The completeness column.** On traces, the same six platforms returned 100%, 100%, 91%, 88%, 58% and
"not supported". A 58% result is not a slower answer to the same question; it is a different, wrong
answer delivered on time. The next lesson is entirely about that number.

**The record-count divergence on templates.** Expected 35,039. Two platforms matched, one came within
3%, and two reported 75,951 while another reported 182,767 — same contracts, same block range. The
authors are explicit that this is not a bug in any platform: there is no industry standard for what
"count the template events" means, with some counting only events from dynamically created contracts,
others including factory events, and others counting differently when one transaction fires events
across several related contracts.<Cite src="sentio-obib" /> Until that is standardised, those numbers
are not directly comparable at all.

Sit with the last one, because it has a consequence beyond benchmarking. If two indexers disagree by a
factor of five on how many events a factory workload produced, then **migrating between them will
produce a diff you cannot explain by looking at either system's logs**. That is the subject of this
module's final lesson, and this table is where you should have first suspected it.

## Reading a methodology section like an adult

OBIB publishes its setup, which is what makes the critique below possible at all. From the post:<Cite src="sentio-obib" />

- All local platforms used Sentio's RPC service — every platform got the same RPC, which the authors
  call the biggest methodological improvement over previous benchmarks.
- Local tests ran on a MacBook Air M3 with 24 GB of RAM. Cloud platforms ran on their own
  infrastructure, which means they pay network latency the local ones do not. The authors kept it that
  way on the grounds that it reflects how people actually use the tools.
- 72-hour timeout per test.
- Sentio ran five iterations per case with anomaly exclusion. Other platforms ran single iterations.
- Data collected January 2026; published May 2026; code and data in a public
  repository.<Cite src="obib-repo" />

Now the honest reading. **Sentio publishes this benchmark, competes in it, and wins two of the six
cases.** The methodology is open and the source is not neutral, and both of those facts are true at
once. The correct response is not to discard it — an open methodology from an interested party is
strictly more checkable than a closed benchmark from anybody — but to read the configurations before
quoting the numbers, and to notice the places where the setup could favour the publisher without
anyone intending it.

At least three such places are visible in the list above.

**Cloud versus local is not a controlled variable.** Three of the six entries run on their own
infrastructure and three run on one laptop. Whether that helps or hurts a given platform depends on the
workload's shape, and it is not knowable from the results.

**Five iterations with anomaly exclusion for one platform, single runs for the others.** Averaging away
outliers is good practice. Applying it to one entrant and not the rest means the publisher's numbers
are systematically less exposed to a bad run than everyone else's — which is a difference in variance,
not in intent.

**Non-comparable record counts, which the authors flag themselves.** Case 6's timings are ranked
against outputs that mean different things. The post says so plainly.<Cite src="sentio-obib" />

Being able to write that list is the actual skill. It does not require distrusting anyone. It requires
reading the section most people skip.

<Aside kind="warn">
And the fastest way to make any of these numbers wrong: time. The benchmarked versions are pinned in
the post — Sentio SDK 3.1.0, Envio HyperIndex v2.32.3, Ponder v0.16.1, Subsquid v1.27.3, graph-cli
v0.97.0<Cite src="sentio-obib" /> — and Envio has since shipped a V3 with its own migration
guide.<Cite src="envio-docs" /> A benchmark is a dated measurement of dated software on a dated chain.
Nothing about it is a standing property of a product, and quoting one without its date and versions is
how a number outlives the thing it described.
</Aside>

## Worked example: turning a benchmark into a decision input

The subgoals name what each step is for.

**Subgoal — locate your workload among the cases, and refuse to average.** If your fold is events with
contract reads, Case 2 is your row and the others are irrelevant. Averaging across six cases produces a
number describing a product nobody is building.

**Subgoal — eliminate on N/A before comparing minutes.** If your product needs transactions or traces,
strike the platforms marked not supported. That single step usually shrinks the field further than any
timing does.

**Subgoal — convert the surviving times into your units, which are hours of replay.** A twenty-minute
difference on a 100,000-block case scales to your range and your event density. It is a ratio to apply,
not a duration to adopt — your contracts are not LBTC and your range is not theirs.

**Subgoal — multiply by replay frequency, not by one.** From the first module: backfill duration is the
deploy latency of every change to your fold. Six schema changes a quarter turns a two-hour gap into a
twelve-hour annual difference, which may or may not clear the bar for changing anything.

**Subgoal — measure the one thing no benchmark covers.** Run a short live-tail trial on your own
chains and record lag as a distribution. This is the regime your product actually lives in, and there
is no published number for it because it cannot be made to terminate.

**Subgoal — write down the date and the versions next to whatever you concluded.** So that the next
person to open your decision document can tell whether it is evidence or archaeology.

<Answer>
**Question one: which workload, and does my product have that workload?** The 142x figure is attached
to a Uniswap V2 factory case from an April 2025 run.<Cite src="envio-hyperindex-benchmarks" /> The
later, more fully specified factory-template case reports 1.92 minutes against 16.83 minutes for the
subgraph — roughly nine times, not one hundred and forty-two.<Cite src="sentio-obib" /> Even taking
both at face value, a multiplier from one workload is not a property of the platform. And on the
workloads where the two published sets overlap, the ranking is stable but the *magnitudes* are not,
which tells you the multiplier is measuring the setup at least as much as the software.

**Question two: how much of the job is inside the measurement?** The publisher of the later benchmark
states that the earlier fork mixed raw extraction times with full indexer times,<Cite src="sentio-obib" />
and Envio's own documentation describes HyperSync as a raw data layer distinct from the HyperIndex
framework.<Cite src="envio-hypersync-overview" /> Whether a given figure covers "fetch the bytes" or
"fetch, decode, transform, store and serve" changes it by an order of magnitude on its own — and the
answer is in the configuration, not the headline. Note that this question is worth asking of *any*
vendor's number, not only this one.

**Question three: can it index what I need at all?** This is the question that ends the conversation
for most products, and no speed claim can address it. The same platform is marked not supported on
blocks, on transactions and on traces in the later benchmark.<Cite src="sentio-obib" /> If your product
needs internal value transfers, a 142x multiplier on events is a fact about a workload you do not have,
attached to a platform you cannot use.

**What the questions have in common.** None of them requires you to doubt the vendor's integrity, and
none of them requires re-running anything. They require converting a scalar back into the conditions
that produced it — workload, scope, date, version, hardware, baseline — and noticing which of those
your situation shares.

**And the practical upshot.** Both benchmarks agree on one durable thing: a data source built for bulk
historical reads beats JSON-RPC for backfill by a large margin. That mechanism is the finding worth
carrying. Every specific multiplier attached to it is a measurement you would have to repeat.
