Every indexer comparison you will find compares the same six things: languages, chains, query API,
hosting, pricing, speed. Two questions that decide more outcomes than any of them are on none of the
grids.

**Who runs the process at three in the morning?**

**Who decides where the project goes next?**

Both are answerable from public information. Neither has a number attached, which is most of why they
get left off. This lesson is those two axes.

## Axis one: the operating model

Framework choice gets the discourse. Operating model changes your team's week.

### What self-hosting actually contains

An indexer is a stateful service. That sentence is doing all the work, so unpack it into the tasks it
implies.

**A database with a backup and restore story.** Not "we have snapshots" — a tested restore, because an
indexer's database is the only copy of a fold that took hours to produce.

**A resync procedure.** Every schema change replays history, as the first module established. That
means a second database, a cutover, and a plan for what production reads during the interval.

**Disk that grows.** Derived tables plus, in the reorg-safe designs, retained history or a transaction
log for the unfinalized range. The log is trimmed as blocks finalize, but it is a working set you must
size for.

**Alerting that is not a liveness check.** The process being up tells you nothing; Ponder ships a
status endpoint and metrics separately from a health endpoint for exactly this
reason.<Cite src="ponder-observability" /> You need lag, contiguity and error rate as distinct signals,
each with a threshold somebody chose.

**A person who is on call for it.** This is the item that is usually discovered rather than decided.

None of that is exotic infrastructure work. It is *ordinary* infrastructure work, and the question is
simply whether your team already does it. If you run Postgres in production today, an indexer is a
marginal addition — another service, another dashboard, the same runbooks. If you do not, it is a new
competence acquired under deadline, which is the most expensive way to acquire one.

### What managed actually contains

The mirror image. You own a bill, a vendor relationship, and a class of failure you cannot fix.

The bill is the easy part to reason about. The dependency is not, and it has two specific shapes worth
naming.

**You cannot debug it.** When indexing stalls on a managed platform, your entire diagnostic toolkit is
a status page and a support channel. That is genuinely fine most of the time and genuinely awful during
an incident, and the difference between those two states is not something you control.

**It can be withdrawn.** This is not speculative. Alchemy Subgraphs was sunset on 8 December 2025, with
all subgraph and query endpoints discontinued and users directed to
Goldsky.<Cite src="alchemy-subgraphs-sunset" /> The Graph's own hosted service — the default for four
years — was announced for sunset and, per the banner on that announcement, ceased serving queries as of
12 June 2024.<Cite src="thegraph-sunsetting-hosted-service" /> Two of the most prominent managed
indexing services of the last five years no longer exist.

The useful heuristic is not about the indexer at all: **does your team already operate stateful
infrastructure?** If yes, self-hosting is incremental and you should weight the vendor risk heavily. If
no, managed is correct and the right move is to argue for it explicitly, with the withdrawal risk named
in the same document, rather than discovering the operational load in your first outage.

## Axis two: who steers the project

Now the axis that is genuinely unusual about this field in 2026. Three of the significant options
changed hands or changed status inside eighteen months.

Four events, one market, twenty months. That is not a normal dependency landscape, and reading it
correctly requires resisting two opposite instincts: treating every change as an obituary, and treating
none of them as signal.

It is worth noticing what caused the churn, because the cause tells you whether to expect more. The
first event on that list removed a free default that a large population of projects depended on. That
converted thousands of teams from "we have an indexer" into "we must choose and pay for one", which
created a market where there had not really been one — and markets that appear suddenly consolidate
suddenly. The other three events are downstream of the first. Read that way, the churn is not random
bad luck; it is the predictable shape of a category forming, and the reasonable expectation is that it
is not finished.

### Ponder and the Monad Foundation: read the announcement, not the pattern

The first-party post is dated 17 February 2026 and written by Kevin Koste. It states that the Monad
Foundation has acquired the team behind Ponder, describes Ponder as an open-source framework for
blockchain data indexing that has grown to power hundreds of applications across the EVM ecosystem, and
names the team as three people — Kevin, Kyle and Jay.<Cite src="monad-ponder-team" />

Two sentences in it are the ones that matter for a selection decision. The team will contribute
infrastructure, tooling and open standards addressing EVM data-layer limitations "at the protocol layer
rather than working around them". And: "The team will continue to develop Ponder as the leading
open-source indexing solution for EVM developers."<Cite src="monad-ponder-team" />

The open benchmark published three months later reaches the same reading independently, recommending
Ponder for self-hosting with minimal setup while noting the February 2026 move and that the project's
future direction may tilt toward Monad-specific work.<Cite src="sentio-obib" />

So the honest risk is **not abandonment**. It is two other things, and both are worth stating plainly:

**Roadmap alignment.** Maintainers employed by one L1 foundation will, over time, prioritise that L1's
needs. That is not a criticism; it is how incentives work, and the announcement says as much when it
describes protocol-layer work for Monad. The practical question is whether the features you will need
in two years are the features that serve that goal.

**Bus factor.** Three people was already thin. It is thin now with different employment attached to it.
The mitigation is not to avoid the project; it is to know that the mitigation exists — the framework is
open source, and a fold written in TypeScript against Postgres is a thing you could maintain a fork of
in a way that a fold written against a hosted API is not.

### SQD and Rezolve AI: acquired is not shut down

SQD, formerly Subsquid, was acquired by Rezolve AI in October 2025, with the acquirer framing the
purchase around building a data and payments backbone for the AI
economy.<Cite src="rezolve-subsquid-acquisition" /> Rebranding and token migration were still in
progress through 2026.

Two things are true at once, and holding both is the skill this lesson is trying to teach.

**The technology is alive and shipping.** Its documentation today presents a Portal HTTP API for raw
blockchain data with arbitrary ranges, streaming and finality handling; two TypeScript SDKs — Squid SDK
and Pipes SDK — for decoding, transforming and persisting to any database; managed hosting as SQD
Cloud; and a decentralised data lake behind the Portal.<Cite src="sqd-docs-home" /> The product site
describes that network as having 2,000 or more worker nodes and lists the Portal API as generally
available.<Cite src="sqd-site" /> This is not the surface of a discontinued project.

**The direction is now set outside crypto infrastructure.** An AI-commerce company's strategy has no
particular reason to prioritise blockchain indexing over the long run, and that is a continuity risk of
a different kind from Ponder's — not "will it be maintained next quarter", but "whose problems will it
be optimised for in three years".

Of the main options this is the one carrying the highest strategic uncertainty. That is a statement
about direction, not a prediction of shutdown, and the distinction matters because the two call for
different responses: uncertainty about direction is managed by keeping your fold portable; a prediction
of shutdown would be managed by not adopting at all.

### The options whose ownership did not change

For completeness, because the axis is not only about the ones that moved. Envio develops HyperIndex in
the open and operates its own hosted offerings alongside it.<Cite src="envio-docs" /> Goldsky is an
independent company whose entire product is running other people's subgraphs, which aligns its
incentives with the format's continuity in a way worth noting.<Cite src="goldsky-subgraphs-docs" /> The
Graph's decentralised network has a different governance shape entirely, and the final lesson of this
module is about what that buys and costs.

## Why an indexer is a worse dependency than a library

One structural point before the exercise, because it explains why this axis deserves its own lesson at
all.

A library you can vendor. Copy the source, pin the version, and you have bought yourself years. The
project can be abandoned and your build still works.

An indexer you cannot. Its output is a database whose contents were produced by *its* semantics — its
ordering rules, its reorg window, its notion of what counts as a template event, its type coercions.
Fork the code and you still have to run it, operate it, and keep it correct against a chain that keeps
changing underneath. And migrating away is not a dependency swap; it is a rewrite plus a re-backfill
plus a dual-run, which the last lesson of this module prices in detail.

That asymmetry is the reason stewardship belongs in the decision rather than in a footnote. You are not
choosing a library. You are choosing whose engineering judgement your derived state will encode for as
long as the product lives.

## Worked example: writing the dependency note

Half a page, written before you commit, and revisited annually. The subgoals name what each part is
for.

**Subgoal — record the operating model as a commitment, with a name attached.** "Self-hosted on our
Kubernetes cluster; Postgres in our existing RDS; on-call is the platform rotation." If you cannot fill
in the on-call line, you have not chosen self-hosting, you have deferred it.

**Subgoal — record the steward and the date you checked.** "Maintained by a three-person team employed
by the Monad Foundation since February 2026; continuing as open source per the first-party
announcement; checked 26 August 2026."<Cite src="monad-ponder-team" /> The date is the load-bearing
part. A stewardship fact without one is indistinguishable from a stale one, and this market moves.

**Subgoal — state the specific risk, not a generic one.** Not "vendor risk". "Roadmap may prioritise
Monad-specific protocol work; we index Base and Arbitrum, so features we need may be deprioritised
rather than removed." A risk you can recognise when it arrives is worth ten that you cannot.

**Subgoal — name the trigger that would make you act.** "Two consecutive quarters with no release
touching non-Monad chains" is a trigger. "If it seems abandoned" is not. Triggers are what turn a risk
note into a control rather than a document.

**Subgoal — price the exit, roughly, in the same paragraph.** "Migration to a subgraph host is a
rewrite of the fold plus a full re-backfill, estimated three engineer-weeks plus eleven hours of
replay." An unpriced exit is not an exit; it is an intention. The final lesson of this module makes
that number defensible.

**Subgoal — write the same note for the boring option, so the comparison is honest.** Managed platforms
get a note too, and theirs contains the December 2025 sunset as prior
art.<Cite src="alchemy-subgraphs-sunset" /> A dependency note that only exists for the scary choice is
a rationalisation, not an assessment.
