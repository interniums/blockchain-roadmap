Two screenshots of one indexer's dashboard, four hours apart.

The first shows 41,000 events per second, forty parallel workers, CPU pinned, a progress bar at 62%.
The second shows three events per second, one worker, CPU at idle, no progress bar at all, and a
single number that matters: *seconds behind head*.

Nothing was reconfigured between them. The indexer crossed from one regime into the other, and the two
have almost nothing in common — different bottlenecks, failure modes, correctness properties and
metrics. Ponder's documentation names them as two distinct
internal modes that fetch data in different ways, while noting that your handler logic is identical
across both.<Cite src="ponder-indexing-overview" /> The handler being identical is exactly what makes
the difference easy to miss.

Everything below is an unpacking of that table.

### Backfill is a throughput problem with a known answer

The blocks are already there. Nothing about them will change. You know the first block, you know the
last block, and the only question is how fast you can get through them.

Because the range is fixed and the fold is deterministic, backfill parallelises: hand block ranges to
independent workers, let each one fetch and decode, and reconcile at the end. Restartability comes from
the same property: a framework that crashes mid-backfill can roll back its unfinalized changes and
resume from the finalized block, because replaying the range is guaranteed to reproduce the same
rows.<Cite src="ponder-indexing-overview" /> The constraints are the
ones you already priced in the RPC module — [the range and response-size caps on log
queries](/c/getlogs-is-the-cost-driver), which providers publish per plan and per chain along with a
hard ceiling on response size,<Cite src="alchemy-eth-getlogs" /> and
[the two independent rate-limit axes](/c/rate-limit-two-axes). Parallel backfill workers press on the
concurrency axis, not the volume axis, which is why adding workers turns a slow backfill into a
429-storm rather than a fast one.

This is also the regime that the entire alternative-data-source argument is about. Envio's HyperSync
exists because bulk historical retrieval over a request-response protocol designed for point queries
is the wrong shape; its documentation describes scans that take hours or days over RPC completing in
seconds on the workloads it benchmarks.<Cite src="envio-hypersync-overview" /> Whatever you make of
the multipliers — the next module takes them apart — the *category* of the claim is a backfill claim.
It says nothing about the second screenshot.

How much of backfill is retrieval rather than transformation? The open benchmark ran three workloads
against Envio's raw extraction engine as well as against full indexers: 3.19 seconds for 100,001
blocks, 128.64 seconds for roughly 1.7 million transactions, 33.84 seconds for 50,191
traces.<Cite src="sentio-obib" /> Those are not indexer times — raw data still has to be decoded,
transformed, stored and served<Cite src="sentio-obib" /> — but the gap to the full-indexer times on
the same workloads shows how the total splits.

### Live tail is a latency problem with a rewritable input

Now the range is unbounded and the blocks arrive one at a time, roughly on the chain's cadence. You
cannot go faster than the chain produces blocks, so throughput has stopped being interesting. Three
other things start being interesting instead.

**Ordering.** A fold is order-dependent, and you cannot process block N+1 before block N and reconcile
later, because the intermediate states are what your product reads. The parallelism that made backfill
fast is unavailable by construction.

**Rewritability.** The blocks you are consuming are at the tip, and the tip is exactly the part of the
chain that gets replaced. Every row written in this regime is provisional. The next lesson is about
what that costs.

**Silence.** A backfill that fails usually fails loudly — a range errors, a worker crashes, the
progress bar stops. That benchmark ran with a 72-hour timeout per test and still recorded one platform
reaching 2.5% of a transactions workload after two hours before being stopped<Cite src="sentio-obib" /> —
a failure that is unmistakable precisely because the job was supposed to end. A live tail fails by continuing to look healthy. The process is up. The
`/health` endpoint returns 200. Ponder ships a separate status endpoint and metrics precisely because
process liveness is not indexing liveness,<Cite src="ponder-observability" /> and its dev logs mark the
transition into backfill explicitly rather than leaving you to infer
it.<Cite src="ponder-docs" />

### The handoff is a moving target

There is a third thing worth naming, which is neither regime but the seam between them.

Backfill is defined against an endpoint — "catch up to block N". But by the time you reach N the chain
is at N plus several thousand, because it kept producing while you worked. Catching up is therefore a
sequence of shrinking passes that converges. An indexer processing ten thousand blocks per minute
closes the gap quickly; one at eight hundred closes it slowly; one processing fewer blocks per minute
than the chain produces **never closes it at all**, while its progress bar approaches a target that is
running away from it.

That case is not hypothetical for expensive folds on high-throughput chains, and it makes "how long is
the backfill" the wrong question. The right one is "what is my sustained blocks-per-minute, and is it
comfortably above the chain's block production rate" — which decides whether the two regimes are
connected by a bridge or by a gap that never closes.

## Why every benchmark measures the first one

This is not vendor dishonesty. It is a property of what can be measured.

Look at how the one open indexer benchmark with published configurations defines its work. Every case
is a fixed historical block range with an expected record count attached: events over blocks 0 to
22,200,000 expecting 294,278 records; events with contract reads over blocks 22,400,000 to 22,500,000
expecting 7,634; blocks 0 to 100,000 expecting 100,001; transactions over a 10,000-block range
expecting 1,696,641; traces over a 90,000-block range expecting 50,191; factory templates over a
10,000-block range expecting 35,039.<Cite src="sentio-obib" />

Each of those has a start, an end, and a right answer. You can run it, time it, and verify
completeness. Live tail has none of those things. It has no end, so "how long did it take" is not
defined; the correct answer changes while you measure it; and any number you produce is a property of
the chain's block time and your network path as much as of the platform.

So the honest reading of any indexer performance claim is: **this is a statement about catching up.**
It may correlate with keeping up. It is not a measurement of it.

### What to measure for the regime nobody benchmarks

If you want a number for live tail, you have to define it yourself. Three that are worth wiring in:

**Lag, as a distribution.** Not "seconds behind head" as a single gauge, but its p50, p95 and p99 over
a window. A tail that is usually current and periodically twelve minutes behind is a different product
from one steadily forty seconds behind, and a mean hides the difference
completely.<Cite src="ponder-observability" />

**Contiguity.** Assert that the set of ingested block numbers has no holes in the ingested range. This
is a query you schedule, not a dashboard you look at — a healthy pipeline returns zero rows, and an
injected gap returns the gap. A
subscription that dropped and reconnected past a gap looks identical to a healthy one from every other
angle — a practitioner write-up on exactly this pipeline shape makes the point that "the process is
running" is the default health check and the wrong one.<Cite src="bilinear-indexing-events" /> The same
write-up covers the related tradeoff of deliberately indexing some distance behind the
head.<Cite src="bilinear-indexing-events" />

**Handler duration at the tail.** In backfill, a handler that takes 40ms is a throughput cost you
amortise. In live tail, a handler that takes longer than the block time is a lag generator that
compounds — it can never catch up, only fall further behind, and it will look fine for hours before
the lag becomes visible.

## Worked example: deciding whether backfill speed is on your critical path

The previous lesson established the real reason backfill duration matters: a change to the fold's
logic requires replaying the fold, so backfill time is the deploy latency of every data-model change.
Here is how to turn that into a number before you choose anything.

**Subgoal — establish the size of the replay, not the size of the chain.** Count blocks from your
contract's deployment block to head, not from genesis — the tutorial that set `startBlock: 0` made the
chain's full history relevant for no reason. For scale, the benchmark's events case spans blocks 0 to
22,200,000 for 294,278 records; yours is almost certainly a narrower range at a higher event
density.<Cite src="sentio-obib" />

**Subgoal — establish the frequency of replay, a property of your team and not your chain.** Count the
last quarter's tickets that would have changed a handler, a derived field or an entity's shape. Active
development produces several a month; maintenance produces one or two a year. That number multiplies
everything else.

**Subgoal — convert to release latency and check it against how you ship.** Multiply estimated backfill
duration by replay frequency. Four hours, six times a quarter, is an annoyance you absorb. Four *days*,
six times a quarter, is not a performance issue — it is a constraint that will reshape your
architecture, because engineers start avoiding the change rather than paying for it.

**Subgoal — name what the workaround costs, because the workaround is what actually happens.** When
replay is too expensive, teams compute the field in application code, run a second pipeline, or patch
rows by hand — each of which splits one fold into two sources of truth that will disagree. The cost of
slow backfill is rarely a slow release; it is the divergence you get from avoiding one.

**Subgoal — only now, look at platform numbers.** With replay frequency in hand, six minutes versus
thirty-five on a benchmark is usually noise; twenty minutes versus two hours might not be. And a
platform that *cannot run your workload at all* is not slow at it — a distinction the next module
makes with data.<Cite src="sentio-obib" />

<Check type="predict" concept="backfill-vs-live">
A team's indexer backfilled 5 million blocks in ninety minutes and has been tailing the head for three
weeks with no alerts. They add a handler that, for each `Swap` event, fetches the pool's current
reserves with a contract read and writes a derived price.

They re-run the backfill: it now takes eleven hours, which they accept as a one-off. It completes, the
indexer switches to live tail, and the dashboard is green.

Predict what happens next, and name the metric that would have shown it.

<Answer>
**The dashboard being green is the prediction to distrust.** Two things are likely happening, and
neither of them raises an error.

**The lag grows, slowly, forever.** The handler now does a network round trip per `Swap`. In backfill
that round trip is amortised across parallel workers and shows up as an eleven-hour total — annoying,
finite, done. In live tail there is one ordered consumer, and if the per-event cost times the events
per block exceeds the block time, the indexer can no longer keep pace. It does not fail; it falls
behind at a constant rate. Twenty seconds of lag on day one is four hours of lag on day thirty, and
nothing in the process's health signals changes. The metric is **lag as a distribution over time** —
and specifically its *trend*, because a single gauge read on a good day tells you nothing about a
number that is monotonically increasing.

**The derived price may also be wrong.** "Fetches the pool's current reserves" is the determinism
violation from the first lesson of this module. If the read is not pinned to the event's block, during
backfill it resolves against the head at the moment of indexing, so the eleven-hour run wrote prices
sampled at wall-clock-now rather than at each swap. In live tail the two happen to nearly coincide,
which is why the bug will look like "historical data is weird and recent data is fine" — the most
misleading symptom shape there is.

**What they should have done differently.** Declared the call rather than making it inside the
handler, so the runtime can prefetch and batch it per block instead of issuing it per event — the
mechanism from the previous lesson. That addresses the throughput cost. It does not address the
pinning bug, which is a separate decision the handler has to make explicitly.

**And the general lesson.** A change that multiplies backfill time by seven did not simply make the
indexer "slower". It changed which regime the indexer is capable of surviving. Backfill absorbed the
cost by parallelising; live tail cannot, because it has nothing to parallelise over.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this goes

You now have the two regimes separated, which is the prerequisite for reading any performance claim in
this field without being misled by it.

The next lesson stays in the second regime and takes its defining property seriously. Live tail
consumes blocks that can be replaced — and the failure that causes is the one thing in indexing that
corrupts data without raising anything at all.
