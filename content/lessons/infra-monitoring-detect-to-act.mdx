Put a stopwatch on each hop and the shape of the problem stops being arguable.

Detection, on a well-built state monitor, is a multicall and a comparison: hundreds of milliseconds
after the block is available. Alert delivery is a webhook: another second or two. Then a phone
vibrates on a bedside table, and the next number is measured in minutes — how many depends on how
deeply someone was asleep, how legible the alert was, and whether the person who received it has the
authority to act or has to wake somebody who does. Then the response transaction is built, checked,
signed by however many people your threshold requires, and broadcast.

Every optimisation instinct points at the first hop, and the first hop is already three orders of
magnitude faster than the one that matters. **The pipeline is only as fast as its slowest human
step**, which is why pre-authorised response paths and pre-built, pre-simulated calldata exist. They
remove work from the slow hop instead of shaving the fast one.

## First, the reference implementation you have read about is gone

Almost every tutorial describing this pipeline describes the same product: a hosted monitor firing a
hosted serverless function that calls a hosted relayer holding a key. That was OpenZeppelin Defender,
and it was genuinely the reference implementation of the pattern.

Defender's own documentation now carries the notice: new sign-ups were disabled on 30 June 2025, and
the platform remained fully operational until a final shutdown on 1 July 2026 while OpenZeppelin
focused on open-source versions of tools like Relayers and Monitor, with migration guides published
for moving from Defender Monitor to OpenZeppelin Monitor and from Defender Relayer to OpenZeppelin
Relayer.<Cite src="oz-defender-docs" />

The successors exist and are ordinary software you run.

**OpenZeppelin Monitor** is a blockchain monitoring service that watches for specific on-chain
activities and triggers notifications based on configurable conditions, with multi-chain support,
configurable schedules using cron expressions, flexible trigger expressions, checkpoint persistence,
and notification channels including Slack, Discord, email, Telegram, webhooks and custom Python,
JavaScript or Bash scripts.<Cite src="oz-monitor-docs" /> It supports EVM-compatible networks along
with Stellar and Solana, and is built in Rust.<Cite src="oz-monitor-docs" /><Cite src="oz-monitor-repo" />

**OpenZeppelin Relayer** provides infrastructure to relay transactions to EVM and non-EVM networks,
with transaction signing through configurable key management, nonce management to keep transaction
order correct, transaction status monitoring, configurable network policies, metrics and
observability through Prometheus and Grafana, and a plugin mechanism for custom logic in
TypeScript.<Cite src="oz-relayer-docs" />

**What changed and what did not.** The shape did not change: something detects, something notifies,
something with a key acts. What changed is that the hosting, the uptime and the key custody are now
yours, which is a real operational cost and also removes a vendor from the critical path of your
incident response. Note the second-order effect too — a self-hosted detector is cheap to trial against
your own history, which is exactly the evaluation lesson three said you should be doing before
believing anyone's precision number.

<Aside kind="warn">
The relayer holds a key that can execute privileged transactions on your protocol. That puts it in
lesson four's authority inventory, not just your infrastructure inventory, and it means the
key-management configuration is a security decision rather than a deployment
detail.<Cite src="oz-relayer-docs" /> A response mechanism you have not threat-modelled is a new
attack surface wearing a safety label.
</Aside>

## The mempool is an optimisation, not a backstop

The most seductive idea in this whole area is that you can see the attack coming. Watch the public
mempool, detect the exploit transaction before inclusion, and get a pause in first.

It works, sometimes, and it is worth having. It is not something to build coverage on.

Private transaction routing is an ordinary, documented, freely available product. Flashbots Protect
describes itself plainly: transactions are sent to a private Flashbots mempool where they will be
hidden from frontrunning and sandwich bots.<Cite src="flashbots-protect-docs" /> That is the feature,
advertised as the feature, available to anyone who changes an RPC URL. An attacker who would rather
not be seen before inclusion does not have to be.

HOUSTON's authors handle this precisely, and their treatment is the honest one. Their system can
analyse both pending transactions in the mempool and confirmed ones in a block; prevention requires
observing the transaction in the mempool; and a transaction routed through a private relay is first
observed by the public only when it is announced as confirmed in a block, so no such system can detect
it before inclusion without cooperation from the relay.<Cite src="houston-ndss" />

Then the part worth carrying into your design. They give the Revest attack as the case that shows
post-inclusion detection still buys you something: it was carried out with four different private
transactions, sent in two different blocks, over 17 minutes. A detector watching confirmed state would
have caught the first one and alerted the administrators, leaving time to react to the
rest.<Cite src="houston-ndss" />

**Design consequence.** Post-inclusion state monitoring is the reliable layer and mempool watching is
a bonus on top of it. A monitoring design whose coverage depends on seeing transactions before they
land has a blind spot shaped precisely like the attacker who was careful — and multi-transaction
exploits, which are common, hand the post-inclusion detector a window anyway.

<Aside kind="stop">
You will find confident percentages for how much Ethereum order flow is private. The sources this
curriculum consulted disagree with each other and measure different quantities — share of
transactions, share of gas, share of value — so **no single figure is published here**. The share is
unknown; the availability is certain and documented, and the availability is the part your design has
to survive.<Cite src="flashbots-protect-docs" />
</Aside>

## Make the alert carry what triage would have gathered

The gap between an alert and an action is triage, and triage time is mostly spent assembling context
the detector already had in memory when it fired.

An alert that says a predicate is false starts a twenty-minute investigation conducted by somebody
who is half asleep. An alert that arrives with the evidence attached is a two-minute decision. The
components are all cheap because the detector computed or can compute every one of them:

- **The decoded call trace**, in function names rather than selectors.
- **Balance deltas per address** across the transaction, so the direction of value is visible.
- **How the initiating address was funded**, which is often the single most decision-relevant fact —
  a fresh address funded from a mixer minutes earlier reads very differently from a known integrator.
- **How much value is still exposed** to the same mechanism, which is what determines urgency.
- **The predicate's terms**, before and after, as in lesson two.
- **A link to the pre-built response**, so acting does not require finding anything.

Concretely, the difference between the two pages looks like this. Same event, same detector, two
payloads:

```
P1  vault-solvency  FAILED
```

```
P1  vault-solvency  FAILED   block 23,914,882   (0.4s after block)
  predicate   totalAssets >= totalSupply * pricePerShare / 1e18
  totalAssets    412,904.11e18   (was 511,203.77e18   -19.2%)
  totalSupply    508,110.02e18   (unchanged)
  shortfall       98,299.66e18   ~ $310M at current marks

  trigger tx   0x7c2a…  Vault.redeem(shares=…) via 0x9d41…
  trace        StrategyAdapter.divest -> Pool.swap -> Vault.sync
  deltas       0x9d41…  +98,299.66 vault-asset
               vault    -98,299.66 vault-asset
  funding      0x9d41… first funded 00:41 today from 0xTornado…
  exposure     3 other markets share StrategyAdapter: $84M

  RUNBOOK      pause markets [A, B, C] with stored tx  ->  #incident
  ACTION       https://ops.internal/respond/vault-solvency
```

Every line in the second block is something the detector either already had or could compute in under
a second. What it buys is that the reader does not have to open anything to decide.

HOUSTON's authors make an argument for this that goes beyond convenience. Their system shows directly
which calls are anomalous or which invariants have been violated, which they say makes it much easier
for analysts to determine root cause **and to discard potential false
positives**.<Cite src="houston-ndss" /> Put that next to their poisoning scenario, where an attacker's
plan depends on a security team inadvertently dismissing an initial
warning,<Cite src="houston-ndss" /> and enrichment stops being presentation polish: it is what makes
the *dismissal* safe as well as the escalation.

## Automating the response is a trade, and both sides are real

Wiring the detector straight to a `pause()` removes the slowest hop entirely. It is genuinely
powerful, and it does two things that never appear on the architecture diagram.

**It creates a new privileged key.** The automation's signer can now halt your protocol, and it
answers to a program rather than a person. That key needs the custody, rotation and monitoring you
apply to every other privileged key — and it is a key whose authorising logic is a detector you wrote,
which means a bug in the detector is now a bug in your access control.

**It converts every false positive into a liveness incident.** A pause is a real outage with real user
harm: positions that cannot be closed, liquidations that cannot execute, integrations that break. So
the detector's precision has stopped being a quality metric and become a **liveness property of the
protocol**. Everything in lesson three now has teeth it did not have before.

The Security Alliance's guidance permits automation and bounds it in the same sentence: alerts should
map to a concrete documented response, which for smart contract protocols typically means a pause
mechanism or circuit breaker and, in higher-maturity setups, possibly a carefully designed automated
system that can trigger when a critical alert fires — but only where the trigger conditions and
failure modes have been explicitly reviewed.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring" />

Three mitigations follow from the two costs, and they compose.

**Require multiple independent signals.** Fire the automation on a conjunction — the invariant break
*and* an unexpected counterparty concentration — rather than on any single detector. False-positive
rates multiply while the true-positive rate barely moves, which is the same arithmetic that made
multi-layered thresholds work in lesson three.

**Restrict automation to cheaply reversible actions.** Pausing a single market is recoverable in
minutes. Anything that moves funds, revokes a role or triggers an upgrade is not, and belongs on the
human-gated rungs of the response ladder.

**Design the pause as a two-sided power.** Trail of Bits' current position is that pause efficacy
declines over time as attackers automate, and that key-centric controls are the thing to mature past
rather than to perfect.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" /> The pattern that follows is
asymmetric: a low threshold to pause, so it is usable at 3am; governance-only to unpause, so an
attacker cannot undo your containment; a forced auto-expiry, so a pause cannot become a permanent
freeze by neglect; and a re-pause cooldown.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" /><Cite src="oz-pausable" />

### The rung below automation: a human who can actually sign

Before automating anything, notice that most teams have an unmeasured failure earlier in the chain.
The response ladder's cheapest rung is not "a bot pauses" — it is "the person on call has the
authority and the means to pause without waking four other people."

Three questions with uncomfortable answers, all of which are liveness questions about your response
path rather than security questions about your keys:

**Can the on-call sign, or only escalate?** A rota whose members can detect but not act has added a
mandatory hop between the alert and the action, and that hop is measured in minutes at 3am.

**When was the signing path last exercised?** A hardware wallet whose firmware needs an update, a
multisig UI that requires an account nobody has logged into since January, a signer whose phone
number changed. None of these announce themselves, and all of them are discovered during the
incident.

**Does the pause path still work against current state?** This is the recurring simulation from the
next section, and it is the single most neglected alert in the entire module.

Trail of Bits' framing is useful here precisely because it is uncomfortable: key-centric controls are
the thing to mature past rather than to perfect, which means the goal is not a better-guarded
guardian key but a design where fewer outcomes depend on one.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" />
Automation is one way to depend less on a specific human being awake; an asymmetric pause with an
auto-expiry is another; both are structural rather than operational fixes.

## Wiring one detector to one transaction

**Subgoal — decide the action before the detector, because the action determines what you must
detect.** "Pause the vault" and "pause borrowing in market X" need different predicates: the second
needs to identify the market, which means the alert must carry it, which means the detector must
evaluate per-market. Designing the detector first and the response afterwards is how you end up with
an alert that proves something is wrong and cannot say where.

**Subgoal — write the transaction now, and store it decoded.** The response is a specific call to a
specific address with specific calldata. Build it, decode it, review it, and put it somewhere the
on-call can reach in one click at 3am. The framework's own emergency pause runbook exists because this
material is procedural and has to be written down before the night it is
used.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-emergency-pause-runbook" />

**Subgoal — simulate it against forked state, on a schedule, not once.** A pause transaction that
worked when you wrote it can stop working after an upgrade changes a role, a parameter or a modifier.
Simulate the stored response against current forked state on a recurring job and alert when it stops
succeeding.<Cite src="eth-simulatev1-spec" /><Cite src="tenderly-simulations" /> This is a liveness
alert on your response path, and it is the one nobody builds — the runbook that has quietly stopped
working is indistinguishable from the runbook that works, right up until it is used.

**Subgoal — measure the whole pipeline with a drill, and record real numbers.** Fire a synthetic
alert into the real channel at a realistic hour, without warning, and record the timestamps: alert
sent, alert acknowledged, decision made, transaction broadcast, transaction included. That series of
numbers is your actual response time, and it will not match anyone's estimate. Rehearsal is the point
of a war-room exercise rather than a nice-to-have, and the roles being assigned in advance is most of
what makes the numbers small.<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" />

**Subgoal — decide the escalation path outside your own team.** SEAL 911 is a free emergency channel
staffed for exactly this situation, and it is a phone number you want to already have rather than one
you look up mid-incident.<Cite src="seal-911" /> The same is true of the notification channel
guidance: the tier that carries "the protocol is being drained" is the tier that needs escalation
policies and delivery receipts.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-tools" />

**Subgoal — decide what happens when the detector and the responder disagree about reality.** The
monitor that fired and the relayer that acts read the chain through different endpoints, at different
moments. A pause built on a state the responder can no longer see will revert, and a revert at that
moment is indistinguishable from an outage. Build the response so it is safe to submit twice and
harmless if the condition has already resolved — pausing an already-paused contract should be a no-op
or an explicit, quiet failure, never something that requires a decision.<Cite src="oz-pausable" />

**Subgoal — write the runbook line into the alert itself.** Every alert names who receives it and what
they do when it fires — that is the framework's first principle, and the cheapest place to satisfy it
is inside the alert payload.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring" /><Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-guidelines" />
One sentence: *pause market X using the stored transaction, then post in the incident channel.*

**Nineteen minutes of the twenty-nine is one person deciding.** Every instinct to optimise the
detector is aimed at a hop worth half a second, and the whole detection stack could get ten times
slower without materially changing the outcome.

**Change 1 — enrich the alert, which attacks the largest number directly.** Nineteen minutes is
someone opening a block explorer and re-deriving what the detector already knew. The decoded trace,
per-address deltas, the funding path of the initiating address and the value still exposed to the same
mechanism turn most of that interval into reading. HOUSTON's argument that explainability is what lets
an analyst establish root cause applies to your own alerts
verbatim.<Cite src="houston-ndss" />

**Change 2 — pre-build and pre-simulate the response, which attacks the second largest.** Some of the
nineteen minutes is not analysis at all; it is constructing and sanity-checking a transaction. If the
pause calldata already exists, already decoded, already simulated against this week's forked state,
that work is done and the decision is a yes or a no.<Cite src="tenderly-simulations" />

**Change 3 — fix the seven-minute acknowledgement, which is a channel problem rather than a people
problem.** Seven minutes to wake up is a paging tool with an escalation policy working roughly as
intended; seven minutes to *see* it is a chat notification. Confirm which one you have, and if the
page tier is on a channel with no delivery guarantees, that is the change to make before any of
the others.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-tools" />

**What the drill has not measured, and it is a long list.**

*Whether the response transaction actually works.* This was synthetic; nobody executed anything. A
pause that reverts because a role changed in March is a runbook that has silently stopped working,
which is why the recurring simulation job exists.<Cite src="eth-simulatev1-spec" />

*Whether the signers are reachable at 03:40.* A 3-of-5 multisig hit 2m25s here because the drill was
scheduled inside somebody's head. The real question is how many signers answer an unannounced call at
that hour, and the answer is a number you should know.

*Whether the exploit would have waited.* Twenty-nine minutes is a long time. It was enough for the
Revest case, which ran over 17 minutes across two blocks<Cite src="houston-ndss" />, and
it is not enough for a single-transaction exploit — where the honest conclusion is that no human
pipeline was ever going to be fast enough and the defence had to be on-chain. That is the argument for
runtime invariant guards, which blocked 18 of 27 exploits in the benchmark study, sitting alongside
monitoring rather than being replaced by it.<Cite src="trace2inv-fse24" />

*Whether anyone communicated.* No user-facing message appears in this timeline. During a real
incident, silence from the team is its own failure mode, and it is the incident-response module's
subject rather than this one's.

**The framing to take away.** Detection latency is the number everyone reports and it is almost never
the number that determines the outcome. Measure the whole chain, publish the slowest hop, and spend
your engineering there.

## What this unlocks

**You have a complete detection-to-response loop**, and — more usefully — you have a way to measure
it. The drill is repeatable, the numbers are comparable across quarters, and the slowest hop is the
one to argue about at the next planning meeting.

**The incident-response module picks up where this stops.** Everything past "the pause transaction was
included" is a different discipline: the [containment ladder](/c/containment-ladder),
[war-room roles](/c/war-room-roles), [communications during an incident](/c/comms-during-incident),
negotiation, and the [post-mortem](/c/postmortem-purpose) that produces the new invariant which
becomes, by lesson two's argument, a new test and a new detector on the same
day.<Cite src="immunefi-war-room" />

**Two things to do this week, both small.** Add the recurring simulation of your stored response
transaction, because it is the liveness alert on your response path and almost nobody has it. And run
one unannounced drill, because the numbers you write down will be the first honest measurement of
your response time you have ever had.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-emergency-pause-runbook" />

**And keep the ordering from this module.** Privileged actions first, because nothing else covers
them. Invariant breaks second, because they are definitionally right and they lead. Liveness alerts
third, because they are cheap and structurally easy to omit. Thresholds last and on a channel that is
read rather than pushed. That ordering is not about what is most dangerous; it is about where each
hour of monitoring effort buys the most detection you would not otherwise
have.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring" />
