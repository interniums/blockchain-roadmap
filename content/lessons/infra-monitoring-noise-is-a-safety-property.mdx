Here is what a good anomaly detector looks like when somebody actually runs it and counts.

HOUSTON, the NDSS 2026 real-time detector, was deployed live against 20 real protocols and left
running for 20 days in April 2024. Over that window it observed 42.6 million transactions, of which
95,600 were directed at the monitored protocols. It raised **75 warnings in total** — 0.08% of the
relevant transactions.<Cite src="houston-ndss" />

Break that down, because the distribution matters more than the total. For 9 of the 20 protocols it
never raised an alert at all. For 10 more it raised fewer than 10 alerts across the entire 20 days.
The single exception was one protocol that produced 24 warnings over 901 transactions — 2.7% — and
the cause was mundane: a newly introduced pool address tripped several invariants at various program
points.<Cite src="houston-ndss" /> A repeat experiment on the same 20 protocols over three weeks in
late 2025 produced 71 alerts across 108,500 relevant transactions, 0.07%, and manual analysis
confirmed that none of them were attacks.<Cite src="houston-ndss" />

So: a peer-reviewed detector, at the state of the art, in production conditions. Nineteen protocols
generating something between zero and a handful of alerts over three weeks, and one generating a bit
more than one a day because somebody deployed a new contract.

That is the standard. Not "we tuned it down until it stopped bothering us" — that is what the good
case *is*.

## Percentages are the wrong unit, and the paper knows it

HOUSTON's headline accuracy on its historical benchmark is a detection true-positive rate of 94.8%
— 109 of 115 DeFi protocol incidents — with a false-positive rate of
0.16%.<Cite src="houston-ndss" /> A sixth of one percent sounds like a solved problem.

Now do the arithmetic that turns it into a rota. 0.16% of transactions means: on a protocol seeing
100 relevant transactions a day, roughly one false alert every six days. On a protocol seeing 10,000
relevant transactions a day, **sixteen false alerts a day**. Same detector, same rate, two completely
different jobs — one a mild annoyance, the other a resignation letter.

The paper reports both units for exactly this reason, and the second one is the one that describes
the human: mature protocols typically see fewer than one non-exploit alert per
day.<Cite src="houston-ndss" /> It also reports the shape of the distribution across protocols rather
than only the mean — a false-positive rate below 10% for 92.2% of protocols, and at or below 1% for
55.7% of them.<Cite src="houston-ndss" /> A mean hides the protocol that is generating all of your
pages.

**The operational rule that follows:** never accept a precision figure without converting it. Take
the claimed rate, multiply by your own relevant-transaction volume, and read the answer in alerts per
week per person on the rota. That number is the one you are actually buying, and it is the one no
vendor page states because it depends on you.

## There is one more thing that gets consumed, and it is not time

The obvious cost of a noisy channel is the minutes spent triaging. The real cost is that a channel
which fires on noise teaches its readers to dismiss it, and that training does not switch off on the
night it matters. The true positive arrives into a channel people have learned to skim.

The Security Alliance's framework states the principle without hedging: a monitoring setup without
defined responses is noise, consuming attention without producing safety, and for every alert you
configure you must answer who receives this and what do they do when it
fires.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring" /> Elsewhere, more bluntly: an alert with no one
responsible is indistinguishable from no alert at all.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-guidelines" />

Read those as engineering constraints rather than as management advice. They say that precision is a
**safety property of the alerting system**, in the same sense that a solvency invariant is a safety
property of the vault. A paging alert that keeps firing with no incident behind it is not a
configuration preference someone should get around to; it is a defect, and the bug it causes is
latent until the worst possible moment.

### Dismissal is a write operation

There is a sharper version of this that most treatments miss entirely, and it comes from HOUSTON's
own limitations section.

The authors describe a concrete poisoning strategy against an adaptive detector. Consider a contract
where two functions can change the same flag, but only one of them enforces the admin check. An
attacker who wants to call the unguarded one knows a direct invocation would trip an alert. So they
wait — for a *legitimate* admin action through the guarded function that also violates an invariant
and generates a warning. If the team dismisses that warning as an intentional admin action, the
detector integrates the event into its notion of normal behaviour, and the attacker's later call no
longer fires.<Cite src="houston-ndss" />

The authors' own conclusion is measured: even where poisoning is feasible it raises the difficulty,
because the adversary must align their actions with rare administrative events *and* rely on the
security team inadvertently dismissing the initial warning.<Cite src="houston-ndss" />

That second clause is the part to carry. **For any detector that learns, clicking "acknowledge" is
not a null action — it is training data.** Which means a channel that has trained its readers to
dismiss quickly has also trained the model, and the two failures compound. It also means enrichment
is not cosmetic: the same paper argues that showing directly which calls were anomalous or which
invariants were violated is what lets analysts determine root cause and discard false positives on
grounds rather than on habit.<Cite src="houston-ndss" />

## Thresholds: there are no defaults, and the reason is worse than you think

Everything above is about how to spend a false-positive rate. This section is about the alerts that
*have* one by construction — thresholds and statistical detectors on the economic layer. They are
genuinely worth having, because they are the only layer that can notice something you never modelled;
an invariant only constrains the states you thought to constrain, and the invariant-effectiveness
benchmark had four exploits that no combination of guards caught.<Cite src="trace2inv-fse24" />

So you will want a TVL-drop alert, a mint-volume alert, a price-divergence alert. And you will go
looking for numbers to put in them. Here is what you will find.

**Published threshold sets disagree by orders of magnitude, and at least one of them is not measuring
what you think.** The most-cited quantitative set — a 5 million USD absolute daily change, a 200%
relative change, and a median-absolute-deviation score above 12 — comes from a 2026 network-based
study of DeFi TVL dynamics. Read the surrounding paragraph and they turn out to be **data-cleaning
cut-offs**. Their purpose is to decide which daily observations in a DefiLlama-derived panel are
artifacts of oracle disruptions, API glitches, contract upgrades or temporary reporting errors, so
that those observations can be repaired by interpolation before a systemic-risk model runs over
them.<Cite src="arxiv-tvl-fragility" /> Under that rule, 3.31% of protocol-day observations were
flagged as technical anomalies to be repaired, and only 0.34% were classified as large persistent
changes worth keeping as real liquidity shifts.<Cite src="arxiv-tvl-fragility" />

Nothing about that procedure is a claim that a 200% daily TVL move indicates an exploit. It is a
claim that a 200% daily TVL move in a public aggregator's data is more likely to be a bad data point
than a real one. Those are close to opposite statements, and the number survives the journey between
them unchanged, which is how it ends up in blog posts as a recommended alert threshold.

**The framework guidance, meanwhile, gives you a process and deliberately no numbers.** Establish
baseline metrics for normal activity from historical data. Define thresholds considering both
absolute amounts and relative percentages. Use adaptive thresholds that adjust as activity patterns
change, and review them periodically. Use multi-layered thresholds — primary thresholds for critical
alerts and secondary thresholds for less urgent notifications — and define thresholds based on a
combination of metrics to reduce false positives and improve
accuracy.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-thresholds" />

That last item is the one with real teeth. A single-metric threshold on a volatile quantity is a
coin flip with extra steps; a conjunction of two weakly-correlated conditions is a different animal
entirely, because the false-positive rates multiply while the true-positive rate barely moves.

<Aside kind="stop">
**This course is not going to give you a false-positive budget.** You will see rules of the form
"no paging alert should fire more than N times per month" quoted with great confidence, and there is
no source in this curriculum's corpus that supports any particular N. The defensible statements are
the ones above: HOUSTON's measured live rates on 20 named protocols, the observation that mature
protocols in that study saw fewer than one non-exploit alert per day, and the framework's structural
rule that a paging alert without a defined response is a
defect.<Cite src="houston-ndss" /><Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring" /> Derive your own number
from your own measured baseline, write it down, and treat breaching it as a bug in the alert rather
than a fact about the world.
</Aside>

### Deriving a threshold, with the step people skip

**Subgoal — measure the baseline before you have an opinion.** Replay 30 to 90 days of your own
history through the candidate predicate and count what it would have fired on. Not a sample: the
whole window, because the interesting days are the tails.

**Subgoal — separate the two populations you just produced.** Every firing is either a legitimate
event you now understand or a data artifact. That split is the actual output of the exercise, and the
artifact rate is the floor on your precision — the TVL study's 3.31%-versus-0.34% ratio is a warning
about how large that floor can be on aggregate data you do not
control.<Cite src="arxiv-tvl-fragility" />

**Subgoal — convert to a workload before choosing.** Two candidate thresholds are not "tight" and
"loose"; they are "eleven pages a week" and "one page a month". Put those numbers next to each other
and the argument stops being about taste.

**Subgoal — add a second condition instead of loosening the first.** When the workload is too high,
the reflex is to raise the threshold, which trades away recall. The better move is usually the
framework's multi-layered form: require the TVL drop *and* an unusual counterparty concentration, or
the mint spike *and* a price divergence.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-thresholds" /> You keep
the sensitivity and pay for it in detector complexity rather than in coverage.

**Subgoal — schedule the review, because the baseline moves.** A threshold derived in March against a
50 million dollar TVL is wrong in September against 400 million, and nothing will tell you. The
guidance asks for periodic review and adaptive thresholds for precisely this
reason.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-thresholds" />

## Tiering, and the part about delivery nobody designs

Two tiers is enough for most protocols and the boundary is not severity of consequence — it is
expected false-positive rate.

**Page tier.** Predicates whose expected false-positive rate is near zero: invariant breaks and
privileged actions. These wake somebody. They go to a tool with escalation policies, on-call
rotations and delivery receipts — the framework names dedicated paging tools as the preferred
channel for exactly this reason.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-tools" />

**Review tier.** Thresholds and statistical detectors, which are wrong regularly by design. These go
to a channel that is *pulled* rather than *pushed*: read on a cadence, triaged in daylight, with a
standing owner. Chat destinations are fine here — the framework calls them useful for visibility but
easy to miss, with no delivery guarantees, and that is an accurate description of a review
channel.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-tools" /> It also says not to rely on email as the sole
channel for critical alerts, because of latency and spam
filtering.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-tools" />

**Give every review-tier alert an expiry.** An alert nobody has acted on in six months is either
firing on nothing — delete it — or firing on something nobody understands, in which case it belongs
in the page tier or in a ticket. A review channel with a permanent backlog is a page tier that has
already failed. (The delivery-testing and tampering habits from the framework apply to both tiers and
are covered properly in lesson five.)<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-guidelines" />

<Answer>
**A — roughly one false page every 45 days, and it is not really a false positive.** Two firings in
90 days is a workload of about one page every six or seven weeks, and both had an identifiable,
non-random cause. Page tier, unambiguously. The change is not to the threshold, because there is no
threshold: add the finality re-evaluation from the previous lesson so a head-only break that resolves
at the finalised checkpoint closes itself instead of paging. That takes an already-good alert to
approximately zero false pages without touching its sensitivity.

**B — 41 firings in 90 days is about one every 2.2 days, and it is disqualified from the page tier by
arithmetic alone.** Three pages a week, indefinitely, on a two-person rota, is how a channel becomes
unread. Review tier. The improvement is *not* to raise the threshold until the count drops, because
that trades recall for quiet and you will not know what you gave up. Add a second condition —
concentration of the outflow, or divergence from a reference price — so the conjunction fires when
both are unusual.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-thresholds" /> Also: some of those 41 are
probably not real drops at all. If the detector reads an aggregate you do not control, the artifact
rate in that data is part of your floor.<Cite src="arxiv-tvl-fragility" />

**C — 0.15% of 3,200 is 4.8 alerts per day, about 34 a week.** Notice that the vendor's number sounded
better than B's while being roughly ten times worse in workload. Neither tier accepts this as
configured. First ask what the denominator is: 0.15% of transactions, of blocks, of monitored
addresses, or of some internal scoring event? The unit moves the answer by orders of magnitude and is
frequently unstated. Then ask for the per-protocol distribution rather than the aggregate — HOUSTON's
own rate was at or below 1% for 55.7% of protocols and below 10% for 92.2%, so the aggregate hides a
tail and you might be it.<Cite src="houston-ndss" /> Then trial it against your own history.

**The single best system-level change: separate the channels before you tune anything.** With A, B
and C all landing in one place, the 34-a-week detector destroys the credibility of the
once-every-six-weeks one, and the invariant break — your highest-value signal in the entire stack —
arrives into a stream people have learned to skim. Split the destinations first, with the page tier
on a tool that escalates and the review tier on a channel that is read rather than
pushed.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-tools" /> Tuning B and C is worth doing afterwards; it
is worth much less before.

**The trap in the framing.** It is tempting to rank these by how much value they protect, and every
one of them protects the same protocol. Tier by expected false-positive rate, not by consequence. A
detector's consequence tells you how much you care; its precision tells you whether anyone will still
be listening.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring" />

## What this unlocks

**The tiering rule you just applied gives the next lesson its whole argument.** Privileged actions —
role grants, ownership transfers, upgrades, timelock queueing — are events with a near-zero natural
false-positive rate and a very low natural frequency. That combination means they can be alerted on
*unconditionally*, with no threshold at all, at almost no cost in attention. An alert that fires four
times a year is an alert people still read.

**It also tells you where the invariant work pays a second time.** The reason a state-level predicate
is worth more than an equally clever threshold is not that it is more sophisticated; it is that its
false-positive rate is a property of the protocol's accounting rather than of the market's
mood.<Cite src="tenderly-invariant-monitoring" /><Cite src="acm-invariant-effectiveness" />

**And when you evaluate tooling,** the questions from this lesson are the ones to lead with: what is
the denominator of your published rate, what is the per-protocol distribution, and what does a
self-hosted trial against our own history produce. A self-hosted option makes that trial cheap,
which is one of the underrated consequences of the open-source shift in this
space.<Cite src="oz-monitor-docs" />
