The strongest published attack detector in the literature has a scope section, and the scope section
contains a hole shaped exactly like your admin key.

HOUSTON states that it considers out of scope threats whose root cause is actions performed by
entities such as protocol administrators with a high level of privilege over the protocol, and it
gives the reason in one sentence: an anomaly detection system has limited efficacy in addressing such
issues, because past actions that were considered normal — the paper's own example is a protocol
administrator updating an administration key — can now turn out to be
malicious.<Cite src="houston-ndss" />

That is not a limitation of one system. It is a statement about what learned detection *is*. A model
of normal behaviour cannot flag the action whose defining property is that it looked normal last
quarter. Every anomaly detector you might buy inherits this, and the better ones say so.

Which produces the ordering this lesson argues for. The privileged-action category is the one with no
automated detector, so it is the one you write by hand — and because those events are rare and
unambiguous, writing them by hand is almost free.

## The arithmetic that makes "no threshold at all" correct

Lesson three's rule was: tier by expected false-positive rate, not by consequence. Apply it here and
the answer falls out immediately.

`Upgraded(address)` on your own proxy has a natural false-positive rate of zero. It fires when the
implementation changed, and the implementation either changed or it did not. There is no baseline to
compare against, no volatility to absorb, no threshold to tune. The same is true of
`OwnershipTransferred`, `RoleGranted`, `RoleRevoked`, `Paused`, and a timelock queueing an operation.

Now the frequency side. A protocol with a mature governance process performs a handful of these a
year. Perhaps a dozen if it is busy. An alert that fires twelve times a year and is right twelve times
costs your on-call roughly nothing, and it is a channel people will still be reading in eighteen
months.

**Alert on every one of them, unconditionally, including when it was you.** Especially when it was
you — because the version of this alert that is filtered to "unexpected" callers requires you to have
correctly enumerated the expected ones, and the whole scenario you are defending against is an
expected caller being someone else today. An admin action you performed produces a page you
acknowledge in four seconds. An admin action you did not perform produces the only warning you are
going to get.

The Security Alliance's monitoring checklist puts the same items in its named categories: changes in
contract ownership or admin roles, contract upgrades and proxy implementation changes, access-control
modifications such as role grants and revocations, oracle and governance monitoring including
unexpected governance proposals or accelerated vote
execution.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-guidelines" />

## The catalogue, and where each event actually comes from

The point of naming the standard is that it lets you watch contracts you did not write. A protocol
you depend on did not agree to emit your custom event; it very likely does emit the standardised one.

### Proxy and implementation

ERC-1967 fixes three storage slots and names the event that should accompany each
change:<Cite src="eip-1967" />

| What changed | Slot | Event |
|---|---|---|
| Logic contract | `0x360894a13ba1a3210667c828492db98dca3e2076cc3735a920a3ca505d382bbc` | `Upgraded(address indexed implementation)` |
| Proxy admin | `0xb53127684a568b3173ae13b9f8a6016e243e63b6e8ee1178d6a717850b5d6103` | `AdminChanged(address previousAdmin, address newAdmin)` |
| Beacon | `0xa3f0ad74e5423aebfd80d3ef4346578335a9a72aeaee59ff6cb3582b35133d50` | `BeaconUpgraded(address indexed beacon)` |

Read the specification's verb carefully: changes to each slot **SHOULD** be notified by the
event.<Cite src="eip-1967" /> Not MUST. A proxy that writes the implementation slot without emitting
is unusual and standards-compliant, and an event-only watcher on such a contract is silently blind.

So watch both. Subscribe to the events for latency, and additionally sample the three slots with
`eth_getStorageAt` on a slower cadence — every few minutes is plenty — comparing against the last
known value. The event tells you within a block; the slot poll tells you eventually and cannot be
skipped. On your own proxies the event alone is fine because you know what your code emits. On a
[UUPS proxy](/c/uups-proxy) you do not control, the slot is the ground truth and the event is a
courtesy.

<Aside kind="note">
The slot poll has a second use that is worth the cost on its own. It catches the case where your
event pipeline is healthy but wrong — a bad ABI, a filter that lost an address during a config
change, a subscription that silently dropped. Two independent paths to the same fact is the cheapest
correctness check in monitoring, and here the second path costs one storage read per contract per
poll.
</Aside>

### Roles and ownership

OpenZeppelin's own documentation makes the design consequence explicit: the base `AccessControl`
contract does not support on-chain enumeration of role members, and to track which accounts hold a
role you should rely on the `RoleGranted` and `RoleRevoked` events, processed off
chain.<Cite src="oz-docs-access-control" /> For `AccessManager`-based systems the same applies with
`TargetFunctionRoleUpdated` added, because permissions live in one contract rather than being spread
across many.<Cite src="oz-docs-access-control" />

That is not a monitoring recommendation; it is a statement that **the event stream is the canonical
read path for who can do what.** Your alerting layer and your access-control inventory are the same
system, whether or not you built them that way.

Add `RoleAdminChanged`, which is the one people forget. `DEFAULT_ADMIN_ROLE` acts as the default
admin role for all roles and is its own admin, which OpenZeppelin flags as carrying significant
risk.<Cite src="oz-docs-access-control" /> A change to *who administers a role* is a change to who can
grant it tomorrow, and it produces no immediate visible effect at all — which is precisely the shape
of a patient attacker.

For `Ownable`, watch `OwnershipTransferred`, and note that `renounceOwnership` produces one too. The
docs describe renouncing as a common pattern after an initial centralised stage, and also warn that
removing the owner means administrative tasks become permanently
inaccessible.<Cite src="oz-docs-access-control" /> An unplanned renounce is not a theft alert; it is a
bricking alert, and it needs to fire just as fast.

### Pause state

`Paused` and `Unpaused` from `Pausable`.<Cite src="oz-pausable" /> Both directions. A pause you did
not initiate is an incident; an *unpause* you did not initiate during an incident is a much worse
one, because it is the attacker undoing your only containment step. The response-ladder design that
makes unpausing harder than pausing is treated properly in the incident-response material — here the
requirement is only that both transitions page.

### Timelock

Queue and execute both, with the queue as the one that matters. Watch the operation lifecycle on your
`TimelockController` and note its role constants — `PROPOSER_ROLE`, `EXECUTOR_ROLE`, `CANCELLER_ROLE`
and `DEFAULT_ADMIN_ROLE` — each of which is itself a grantable role and therefore already covered by
the `RoleGranted` watcher above.<Cite src="oz-docs-access-control" /> One configuration deserves its
own alert: granting the executor role to the zero address opens execution to anyone once the timelock
has expired, which OpenZeppelin describes as useful but to be used with
caution.<Cite src="oz-docs-access-control" />

### Protocol-specific privileged setters

Oracle source changes, fee parameters, collateral factors, cap adjustments, supported-asset
additions. There is no standard for these, which means **the event has to exist because you decided
it would**. If your `setOracle` function does not emit, no monitor can watch it without polling
storage, and you have created work for your future self at the moment of highest leverage and lowest
cost. The academic work on administrated tokens exists because privileged setters in deployed
contracts are numerous, varied and frequently undocumented — enumerating your own is not a
formality.<Cite src="arxiv-administrated-erc20" />

## Alert on the queue, not the execution

A timelock's entire value is the interval between an operation becoming scheduled and becoming
executable. OpenZeppelin says what that interval is for: the delay protects the users of the smart
contract by giving them time to review the maintenance operation and exit the system if they consider
it is in their best interest to do so.<Cite src="oz-docs-access-control" /><Cite src="oz-governance-docs" />

Time to review. Which means an alert wired to the *execute* event tells you about the change at the
exact moment your review window closed. The delay ran, and nobody used it.

This sounds obvious written down and it is one of the most common configurations in the wild, for a
sympathetic reason: execution is the moment the state actually changes, so it looks like the event of
interest. It is the event of *record*. The event of *interest* happened days earlier.

<Misconception
  belief="I should alert when the timelock executes — that is when the change lands."
  reality="By execution the delay is already spent. Queueing is the alert that preserves the entire response window, and the window is the only thing the timelock was ever buying you."
  why="Execution is the state transition, and state transitions are what monitoring is usually about. The timelock inverts the usual relationship between the interesting moment and the visible one."
  src="oz-docs-access-control"
/>

The queue alert has a job attached, and it is the reason this matters most for timelocks you do not
control. When a queue event fires on a dependency's governance, you have a fixed number of hours to
decode the payload, simulate it against forked state, and decide whether it changes an assumption you
depend on. That is a rehearsed procedure or it is nothing — the simulation machinery for it is
[eth_simulateV1](/c/eth-simulatev1) and forked-state execution, and the calldata decoding is exactly
the pre-execution verification the simulation module
covers.<Cite src="eth-simulatev1-spec" /><Cite src="tenderly-simulations" />

For your own governance you have a vote and a cancel path. For somebody else's, the delay is
literally all the notice you are going to get.

## Building the watcher

### Subgoal — enumerate addresses, not contracts, because the interesting ones are not the ones you think of

Write the list before you write any code, and be pedantic about it. For a single upgradeable
protocol the list is longer than most people's first draft:

- the proxy (where `Upgraded` fires)
- the current implementation (where your own privileged setters live)
- the ProxyAdmin, if the proxy is transparent rather than [UUPS](/c/uups-proxy)
- the timelock
- the multisig that holds the proposer role
- every module, adapter, strategy or plugin the protocol registers
- every one of the above for each chain you are deployed on

The failure mode here is not subtle and it is nearly universal: teams enumerate the contracts that
hold funds and miss the contracts that hold *authority*. A ProxyAdmin holds no value and can replace
every line of code in the system.

### Subgoal — subscribe by topic across the whole address set, so a new contract cannot fall out of coverage

One filter over the address set, keyed on the topic hashes, rather than one watcher per contract.
Fewer subscriptions, one place to add an address, and no chance of a contract being added to the
protocol and forgotten in the monitoring config.

```ts
const PRIVILEGED_EVENTS = parseAbi([
  'event Upgraded(address indexed implementation)',
  'event AdminChanged(address previousAdmin, address newAdmin)',
  'event BeaconUpgraded(address indexed beacon)',
  'event OwnershipTransferred(address indexed previousOwner, address indexed newOwner)',
  'event RoleGranted(bytes32 indexed role, address indexed account, address indexed sender)',
  'event RoleRevoked(bytes32 indexed role, address indexed account, address indexed sender)',
  'event RoleAdminChanged(bytes32 indexed role, bytes32 previousAdminRole, bytes32 newAdminRole)',
  'event Paused(address account)',
  'event Unpaused(address account)',
]);

client.watchEvent({
  address: PRIVILEGED_ADDRESSES,   // the full authority set, not just the value-holding contracts
  events: PRIVILEGED_EVENTS,
  onLogs: (logs) => logs.forEach(enrichAndPage),
});
```

Two operational notes. A `watchEvent` subscription over WebSocket can drop and reconnect, and a
reconnect that resumes from the current head has a gap in it — so reconcile on a slower timer with an
`eth_getLogs` sweep over the last N blocks and deduplicate on the
[log's natural key](/c/log-natural-key).<Cite src="viem-watch-contract-event" /> And keep this monitor
on a different provider from your invariant monitor, so that one provider's outage does not take both
layers of your detection with it.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-tools" />

### Subgoal — decode to a sentence a woken human can act on

`RoleGranted(0x9f2df0…, 0x4a1b…, 0x77c3…)` is three opaque hex strings. Every one of them can be
resolved by the detector, which already has the data.

The role is `keccak256("MINTER_ROLE")` or similar — precompute the hashes of every role constant in
your codebase and keep a reverse map, so the alert says `MINTER_ROLE` rather than a `bytes32`. The
account and the sender resolve against your address book: label every known address, and make an
*unlabelled* address the loudest part of the alert. "MINTER_ROLE granted to an address we have never
seen, by the timelock" is a decision; the raw log is a research project.

The Security Alliance's tooling guidance ranks channels by whether they escalate and confirm
delivery, and this is the tier where that matters most — these alerts page.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-tools" />

### Subgoal — alert when the authority set itself changes, not only when it acts

The subtlest and most valuable alert in this whole catalogue: fire when a privileged event names an
address that is not in your inventory, *and* when your inventory contains an address that no longer
holds the role you think it does.

The first catches a grant to an attacker. The second catches the drift that makes your incident
response wrong — the multisig signer who left the company, the deprecated module that still holds
`PAUSER_ROLE`, the deployer EOA that was supposed to renounce two years ago and did not. Reconcile
your written authority inventory against the on-chain event history on a schedule, and treat a
divergence as a finding. Trail of Bits' position is that key-centric controls are the thing to mature
past rather than to perfect, and reconciliation is how you find out how key-centric you still
are.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" />

## The contracts you do not control

Your protocol's failure modes are not bounded by your protocol's code. If you read a price from an
oracle, hold a yield-bearing token, route through a bridge or price against a pool, then that
contract's upgrade, pause, parameter change or depeg is your incident — and detecting exploits that
span contract boundaries is hard enough to be an open research problem, which tells you something
about how well the per-contract view serves you here.<Cite src="arxiv-cross-contract-detection" />

is one external contract, what you trust it for, and what breaks if it stops being trustworthy:

Teams consistently discover, during an incident, that the contract which failed was one nobody had
written into this table. The exercise takes an afternoon and its output is the input to everything
below.

**Subgoal — apply the same privileged-action alerting to every row.** Watch each dependency's
ERC-1967 slots and events, its role changes, its pause transitions and its timelock
queue.<Cite src="eip-1967" /><Cite src="oz-docs-access-control" /> They did not agree to tell you, but
they emit the standardised events, which is the entire reason the standard is worth knowing.

For a price feed specifically, the aggregator behind a feed proxy can be replaced, and that is a
change to what your protocol reads that involves no transaction against your protocol at
all.<Cite src="chainlink-historical-data" /> The responsibility split is stated plainly by the feed's
own documentation: developers are solely responsible for instituting the requisite risk mitigation
processes including data quality checks, circuit breakers and appropriate contingency
logic.<Cite src="chainlink-developer-responsibilities" />

**Subgoal — add the dependency's own liveness to the row.** An oracle that stops updating, a bridge
whose messages stop being delivered, a sequencer that stalls. These produce no event to subscribe to,
because the defining characteristic is the absence of one. That is the next lesson, and the
dependency table is where its alerts get their addresses.

<Answer>
**1 — page, and acknowledge in seconds.** This is the case for unconditional alerting rather than
against it. The grant is expected, the address is known, the sender is the timelock, and the enriched
alert says all three, so the page costs one glance. Filtering this out requires you to trust your
inventory of expected callers, and an attacker with the timelock's proposer role produces an
identically-shaped event. First sixty seconds: confirm the role name, the account label and the
sender against the change you were expecting, and close it.

**2 — page, loudly, and this is the sharpest item on the list.** The admin role for `PAUSER_ROLE` has
been moved to an address that cannot act. Nothing visible breaks today. What has happened is that
`PAUSER_ROLE` can now never be granted or revoked again, so if your pauser key is compromised or
lost you have no path to change it. `DEFAULT_ADMIN_ROLE` is the default admin for all roles and
carries significant risk precisely because of moves like this
one.<Cite src="oz-docs-access-control" /> First sixty seconds: establish whether this was a governance
action you authorised, and if not, treat every current `PAUSER_ROLE` holder as the complete and final
set forever.

**3 — review, not page.** A large withdrawal is a lagging, low-precision signal, and "40% of one
market" is a normal Tuesday for a market with three depositors. It belongs on the reviewed channel,
and if you want it to page it needs a second condition — a solvency predicate, a utilisation bound —
so that the conjunction is what fires.

**4 — page, and this is the one most teams have no alert for at all.** A dependency you did not write
just changed its implementation, and the change alters the numbers your liquidation engine acts on.
The event is standardised, so you can watch it without the feed operator's
cooperation.<Cite src="eip-1967" /> First sixty seconds: read the current answer and its timestamp,
compare against a second independent source, and decide whether to pause borrowing while somebody
reads the new implementation.

**5 — page, at the queue.** The delay exists to give you review time and that time only exists if
queueing wakes someone.<Cite src="oz-docs-access-control" /> First sixty seconds: confirm the decoded
target and calldata match the proposal that was discussed, then hand it to whoever simulates it
against forked state before the window closes.<Cite src="eth-simulatev1-spec" /> A fee change is the
benign case; the identical event shape carries an implementation swap.

**6 — page, and treat it as more serious than a pause would be.** The guardian address unpausing at
04:00 either means a human deliberately restored service, in which case one message confirms it, or
means the guardian key is not exclusively yours and somebody has just removed your containment. First
sixty seconds: reach the guardian holder on a channel that is not the one an attacker would have
access to.

**7 — page, and this is the item that justifies the storage poll.** The slot changed and no event
fired. ERC-1967 says the event SHOULD accompany the change, not MUST, so this is possible without any
bug.<Cite src="eip-1967" /> Two readings: a proxy implementation that legitimately writes the slot
without emitting, or a monitoring path that lost the log. Both matter. First sixty seconds: fetch the
code at the new implementation address and compare it to your expected deployment, then work out
which of the two readings is true — because if it is the second, your event coverage has a hole in it
right now.

**8 — neither, for this system.** A forum post is not on chain and this is an on-chain monitor. It

signals; that is how the review channel becomes a feed nobody reads.

**The pattern across the eight.** The three items with genuinely zero false-positive rate — 2, 5 and
7 — are also the three that most teams have no alert for, and all three are *changes to authority*
rather than changes to value. Value-shaped alerts are easy to imagine and expensive to run.
Authority-shaped alerts are hard to imagine and nearly free.

## What this unlocks

**You now have the two highest-precision layers running**: the state predicate from lesson two and
the privileged-action catalogue from this one. Between them they cover the failures that are
definitionally wrong and the failures that are invisible to every learned detector.

**Lesson five covers the third shape**, which is neither: things that fail by stopping. Your
dependency table is where those alerts come from, because a dependency's liveness is not visible in
any event it emits.

**Lesson six turns a queue alert into an action.** Knowing that a dependency's timelock has queued
something is worth exactly as much as your ability to decode it, simulate it and respond before the
delay expires — which means the simulation work and the pre-authorised response path are part of this
alert's design, not a separate project.<Cite src="tenderly-simulations" />

**On tooling:** everything in this lesson is standard events and standard slots, which means it runs
on anything that can filter logs and read storage — a self-hosted service with configurable trigger
conditions, a managed platform, or a hundred lines of viem.<Cite src="oz-monitor-docs" /> Run two, on
different providers, because the framework's redundancy recommendation applies most to the alerts you
cannot afford to miss and this is that tier.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring" />
