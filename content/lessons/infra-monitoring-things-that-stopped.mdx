At 02:11 a keeper process exits with a segfault. Nothing else happens.

No transaction is emitted, so no transaction-level alert fires. No storage value changes, so the
invariant from lesson two evaluates cleanly every block for the next six hours. Total value locked
does not move, so the economic layer sees a quiet night. Every detector you have built reports that
the protocol is healthy, and every one is telling the truth about what it can see.

At 08:30 somebody notices that nothing has been liquidated since before midnight and the market has
moved 9%.

This is the third shape of incident, and it needs a differently-shaped alert, because every monitoring
tool's default posture is to notice things rather than to notice their absence.

<Aside kind="warn">
This lesson's title is a rule of thumb, not a measurement. Nothing in this curriculum's source corpus
supports a specific split between theft incidents and liveness incidents, and inventing one would be
worse than useless — you would tune a rota against a number nobody measured. Take the ordering as
structural: liveness failures are a distinct category with a distinct detector shape, they are
commonly left out because of how detection tooling is built, and that is enough of an argument to
build them without a percentage attached to it.
</Aside>

## Every liveness alert is the same three fields

Once you see the shape, the whole category collapses into one small piece of code repeated with
different arguments.

**What is the last-observed timestamp of the thing that should keep happening?**
**What cadence do we expect?**
**How much slack before it is a problem?**

That is it. `now - lastSeen > tolerance` fires. The engineering is entirely in sourcing `lastSeen`
honestly and picking `tolerance` from something real, and both are more interesting than they look.

Note how much easier `tolerance` is here than in lesson three. A TVL threshold has to be derived from
your own measured baseline, because nobody publishes what normal looks like for your
protocol.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-thresholds" /> A liveness tolerance usually has a
**published cadence** behind it — a feed's heartbeat, a keeper's cron schedule, a bridge's stated
delivery window, a chain's block time — so the tolerance is that number plus a margin, and the margin
is the only judgement call. Liveness alerts are the cheapest high-precision alerts available for
exactly this reason, which makes leaving them out a bad trade.

### Oracles: the update timestamp is the signal, not the answer

A frozen price feed does not revert. It returns its last answer, correctly formatted, and your
contract will happily act on it.

Chainlink names two things that cause a feed to publish: a **deviation threshold**, where movement of
the underlying beyond a certain interval triggers all the nodes to update, and a **heartbeat
threshold**, where a feed whose value stays inside the deviation band still updates every so many
minutes or hours.<Cite src="chainlink-historical-data" /> Both of those are per-feed parameters, and
the second one is what gives you a `tolerance` with a defensible origin: if the feed's heartbeat is
one hour, an answer older than one hour plus a margin is outside the feed's own stated behaviour.

The responsibility for acting on it is assigned explicitly, and the wording leaves no room: developers
implementing Data Feeds are solely responsible for instituting the requisite risk mitigation
processes including, but not limited to, data quality checks, circuit breakers, and appropriate
contingency logic for their use case.<Cite src="chainlink-developer-responsibilities" />

So three things, not one. An on-chain staleness check that reverts or falls back —
[the contract-side control](/c/orac-staleness-check) — plus an off-chain monitor that fires *before*
the on-chain check starts reverting, plus a decided contingency: pause borrowing, widen a bound,
switch source. The monitor's job is to hand you the interval between "the feed is late" and "the
protocol is refusing transactions", and that interval is where every useful option lives.

### Keepers and relayers: watch the balance, because it is leading

A keeper that stopped is a lagging signal — you learn at the moment the work stopped happening. A
relayer wallet whose balance is falling toward zero is a *leading* one: you can compute the number of
transactions remaining at the current burn rate and alert hours before the first failure.

So instrument both, and understand that they are different alerts. The balance alert is a threshold
with a natural unit — hours of runway, not wei — and it belongs in the review tier if the runway is
long and the page tier when it is short. The liveness alert is the inverted one: no successful
transaction from this address in N minutes.

A third failure mode sits between them and neither alert catches it. Relayer implementations name it
because it bites: nonce management, keeping transaction order correct across concurrent
submissions.<Cite src="oz-relayer-docs" /> A relayer with a
[gap in its nonce sequence](/c/nonce-gap) is funded, alive and completing nothing, because every later
transaction queues behind a missing one. So watch a third thing: submitted-but-not-mined count, and
the age of the oldest one. The relayer is also privileged infrastructure holding a key, which puts it
in the previous lesson's inventory too. Three alerts, two tiers, one address.

### Cross-chain and rollup paths: the message that never arrived

A bridge message stuck in transit, a sequencer that stopped producing, a withdrawal proof never
submitted. Same shape — but sourcing `lastSeen` is harder, because "delivered" is a fact on a
different chain from "submitted". The practical form is a matched-pair check: for every send event on
the source chain older than the expected delivery window, assert a corresponding receive event on the
destination. Unmatched sends past the window are the alert, and the alert names the message rather
than the count. The framework lists unusual inflow or outflow volumes and bridge contract state
changes as their own monitoring track for the same
reason.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-guidelines" />

### Your own pipeline: gaps and lag

Your indexer is infrastructure whose failure is silent by construction, and
[the two predicates it needs](/c/gap-detection-monitoring) are the same ones you already know: no
missing block numbers in the ingested range, and cursor lag behind the chain head under a threshold.
Frameworks expose the second one directly through status endpoints and metrics, which means the alert
costs a scrape rather than a query.<Cite src="ponder-observability" />

A gap and a lag fail differently and both matter. Lag means you are late; gap means you are wrong,
because a fold with a hole in it produces a state that never existed.

### Nodes and endpoints

The guidelines put node and network monitoring in the same list as everything else: block propagation
times and node health, network latency affecting transaction confirmation, and RPC endpoint
availability.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-guidelines" /> This is not adjacent infrastructure
hygiene. If your RPC is unavailable, every detector above it is blind, and blindness looks exactly
like health. The reliability metric the framework asks you to evaluate for a provider — how quickly
after an on-chain event a notification reaches you — is the same number, measured from the other
end.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-tools" />

## Silence is ambiguous, and that is a design problem

Which brings us to the failure mode that undermines everything in this module.

Indexers lag. RPC endpoints fail. Bot processes exit. A monitoring stack in any of those states
produces exactly what a perfectly healthy protocol produces: nothing. And since alerting systems speak
only when something is wrong, their silence carries no information. You cannot distinguish "the
protocol is fine" from "the monitor died on Tuesday" by looking at your notifications channel, and
people try to do this constantly.

The fix is inversion. **The monitor emits a heartbeat on a fixed cadence, and something else pages
when the heartbeat goes stale.** Silence becomes a positive signal whose absence is detectable — the
same trick as every other alert in this lesson, applied to the alerting system itself.

Two constraints on the checker, and skipping either produces a dead-man's switch that dies with the
thing it watches.

**It must be separate infrastructure.** Not a separate thread, not a separate process on the same
host, not a container in the same compose file. Sharing a host means going down with the host; sharing
a region means going down with the region; sharing an RPC provider means going silent for the same
reason the monitor did. Every shared dependency is a correlated failure you just built in.

**It must be simpler than what it watches.** A heartbeat checker is a timestamp comparison and an HTTP
call. Give it an RPC client, a database or a decoder and it acquires the failure modes of the system
it is supposed to backstop.

### Say which thing is broken

*The monitor is not reporting* and *the protocol is broken* need different first actions — one starts
with a process and a provider, the other with reaching a guardian key holder. An alert that does not
distinguish them costs the first several minutes of an incident.

This is where lesson two's three-state result pays off. `FAIL` pages as an incident. `UNKNOWN` pages
as an infrastructure problem, with different text and often a different rota. Silence pages from the
independent checker. Three states, three sentences, three first actions.

## Three habits that keep the stack honest

**Test delivery, not configuration.** Verify periodically that an alert arrives end to end rather than
checking that the rule is configured, because a misconfigured webhook or an expired token silently
breaks alerting with no visible symptom.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-guidelines" /> A
scheduled synthetic alert routed through the real channel is the only version of this that stays true.

**Alert on alert tampering.** Configure alerts for the disabling or modification of existing alerts,
covering accidental misconfiguration and adversarial
tampering.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-guidelines" /> Note the shape: a liveness alert about
your configuration.

**Run two, independently.** No provider has perfect uptime or perfect detection, and the framework's
recommendation for critical systems is two independent providers watching the same invariants in
parallel, ideally one self-hosted and one
managed.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring" /><Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-tools" /> The
shared predicate module from lesson two makes the second deployment cheap, and a self-hosted option
makes it cheaper still.<Cite src="oz-monitor-docs" /> The reliability questions to ask of any managed
option are the framework's: what uptime is guaranteed and is there a status page, how quickly after an
on-chain event does a notification reach you, and is delivery at-least-once or
best-effort.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-tools" />

<Aside kind="note">
Redundancy at the detector level and at the RPC level are different problems, and it is easy to think
you solved both. viem's `fallback` transport ranks its transports every 10 seconds by pinging each,
scores the last 10 pings on stability and latency with weights of 0.7 and 0.3, and routes to the
best.<Cite src="viem-fallback-transport" /> Excellent for keeping the monitor running — and it means
your primary endpoint can be broken for a week without anyone finding out, because the failure was
handled rather than reported. **Fail over and alert on the failover.** The resilience feature is not
the observability feature.
</Aside>

<Aside kind="note">
On decentralised detection: Forta's documentation describes a monitoring network with data feeds and
bots you can run, alongside newer risk and firewall products.<Cite src="forta-docs" /> Claims
circulating in secondary write-ups about its 2026 adoption by named protocols were not verifiable from
a first-party source for this course, so check its operational standing yourself before depending on
it — which is the correct posture toward any single detector.
</Aside>

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="monitoring-the-monitor">
A team adds a dead-man's switch after an outage in which their invariant monitor was down for eleven
days. Here is what they shipped. Find the ways it fails to do its job.

```ts
// inside monitor/index.ts, the same process that runs the invariant loop
let lastHeartbeat = Date.now();

setInterval(() => { lastHeartbeat = Date.now(); }, 30_000);

setInterval(() => {
  if (Date.now() - lastHeartbeat > 5 * 60_000) {
    slack.post('#alerts', 'Monitor heartbeat stale');
  }
}, 60_000);
```

<Answer>
**1 — the checker lives inside the process it watches, so it dies with it.** This is the defining
error of the pattern and it is what the code does most visibly. The eleven-day outage that motivated
this work was a process that exited; after this change, a process that exits still produces exactly
nothing. Everything else below is secondary to this.

**2 — the heartbeat is a timer, not a proof of work.** `setInterval` updating a variable proves the
event loop is running. It does not prove the invariant loop evaluated anything or that the RPC
answered. A monitor whose RPC has rejected every request for three hours has a perfectly fresh
heartbeat. **The heartbeat must be written by the work, on completion of a successful evaluation, and
carry the block number it evaluated** — so the checker can also notice a heartbeat that is fresh in
wall-clock time but stuck at block 23,900,000.

**3 — the heartbeat lives in process memory, so a restart hides history.** A crash-loop restarting
every 45 seconds resets `lastHeartbeat` on every boot, so the staleness condition never triggers while
no useful work ever completes. The heartbeat must be durable and readable independently of the
monitor's lifetime.

**4 — the alert goes to a chat channel with no escalation.** The framework treats chat destinations as
useful for visibility but easy to miss with no delivery guarantees, reserving paging tools with
escalation and delivery receipts for severity.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-tools" /> "Our
detection is offline" invalidates every other alert; it belongs on the tier that escalates.

**5 — the alert text does not say which kind of failure this is**, leaving the reader to work out
whether the protocol is under attack or the box is down. Say it explicitly and include what the
checker knows: last heartbeat, last evaluated block, and distance from the chain head.

**6 — nothing ever tests the path.** Nobody discovers the expired webhook until the day the monitor
dies. Fire a synthetic heartbeat failure on a schedule and confirm the alert
lands.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-guidelines" />

**The corrected shape.** The invariant loop writes `{ timestamp, blockNumber }` to durable storage
after each successful evaluation. A separate minimal service, on different infrastructure with a
different provider, reads that record on a timer and pages via an escalating channel when the
timestamp is stale or the block number has stopped advancing. It does nothing else, has no RPC client,
and is exercised by a scheduled synthetic failure. The checker is now a single point of failure one
level up — a regress that terminates because each level is simpler than the last, which argues for
making the checker trivial rather than for adding a third level.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**Your detection stack is now complete in shape**, if not in coverage: state predicates for things
that are definitionally wrong, unconditional alerts for privileged actions, tiered thresholds for
things that look unusual, inverted alerts for things that stopped, and a heartbeat so that quiet means
something.

**The last lesson is the half of the system that is not detection.** An alert is not a control until
somebody can act on it, and measuring the pipeline end to end shows the detector is almost never the
slow part.

**Carry the liveness framing into it.** The response path has the same failure mode as everything
here: a guardian key not exercised in a year, a relayer with no gas, a runbook nobody has run. All of
them have quietly stopped working and none announce
it.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring" /> Detection latency measured against a response path that
does not execute is a number about nothing.

**One measurement worth taking this week.** The HOUSTON deployment reported concrete resource and
timing figures because the authors needed to show the system could keep pace with
mainnet.<Cite src="houston-ndss" /> Do the same at whatever scale you have: how long after a block is
available does your predicate finish evaluating, and what happens to that number under the worst load
you have seen.<Cite src="eth-pos-finality" />
