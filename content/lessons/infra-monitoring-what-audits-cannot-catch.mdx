At 03:14 a proxy you deployed emits `Upgraded(address)`.

The event is well-formed. The storage slot it reflects is the one ERC-1967 standardises, and the
event is the one the standard says a change to that slot should be notified
by.<Cite src="eip-1967" /> The transaction was signed by the address your access-control table lists
as the upgrader. It reverted nothing, consumed a normal amount of gas, and settled in a block like
any other.

Nothing in your repository is wrong. Every test still passes. The Slither run is still clean, the
Certora rules still hold, and the audit report published six weeks ago is still, in every particular,
accurate. The implementation address in that event is not one anybody on your team wrote.

This lesson is about the gap that transaction fell through, and about the fact that the gap is
structural rather than a matter of anyone having been careless.

## Two questions that sound like one

An audit answers a question about an artifact: *is this code correct?* It answers it on the day it is
read, against the version it was given, under a scope somebody wrote down. That is a genuinely
valuable answer and this course spent a whole track on how to produce one.

Monitoring answers a question about a system: *is this behaving correctly right now?* It asks it
every block, against whatever is actually deployed, under whatever conditions the world happens to be
supplying.

The two questions have different subjects. The first one's subject is a program. The second one's
subject is a running system plus its keys, its dependencies, its inputs and its users. Almost
everything interesting lives in the second subject and not the first, which is why the answer to
question one being "yes" tells you so much less than it feels like it should.

The Security Alliance's monitoring framework treats the second question as a standing requirement
with its own checklist rather than as an optional maturity add-on, and the checklist is worth reading
as a list of things an audit cannot cover: changes in contract ownership or admin roles, contract
upgrades and proxy implementation changes, access-control modifications such as role grants and
revocations, price-feed deviations beyond expected bounds, unexpected governance proposals or
accelerated vote execution, bridge state changes, and RPC endpoint
availability.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-guidelines" />

Read that list again with the audit question in mind. Not one entry on it is a defect in source code.
Every entry is a fact about the world at a particular moment.

## The four categories, and why each one is invisible to review

### A stolen key produces valid transactions

No source-level property distinguishes an upgrade signed by your operations lead from one signed by
whoever pulled her seed phrase off a compromised laptop. Both satisfy the same `onlyRole` check
because both present the same authorisation. The transaction is not malformed; it is unauthorised in a
sense your code has no representation for.

This is not merely a rhetorical point. It appears as an explicit scope exclusion in the state of the
art. HOUSTON, a real-time anomaly detector presented at NDSS 2026, considers out of scope threats
whose root cause is actions performed by entities such as protocol administrators with a high level of
privilege, and gives the reason directly: an anomaly detection system has limited efficacy against
such issues, because past actions that were considered normal — the paper's example is an
administrator updating an administration key — can now turn out to be
malicious.<Cite src="houston-ndss" />

A detector that learns from history cannot flag the thing whose entire signature is *this looked
normal last quarter*. Hence [lesson four](/c/admin-action-alerting): the privileged-action category is
the one you instrument by hand, because nothing is going to instrument it for you.

### A dependency upgrades underneath you

You read a price, hold a yield-bearing token, route through a bridge or price against a pool. The
auditor read your code. Nobody was engaged to read the other contract, and even if somebody was, it
can change tomorrow without asking you. Detecting exploits that cross contract boundaries is an active
research problem precisely because the per-contract view is
insufficient.<Cite src="arxiv-cross-contract-detection" /> Your audit's scope boundary and your risk
surface's boundary are different shapes, and the difference is
[a dependency graph you have probably not written down](/c/dependency-monitoring).

### An oracle stops updating without reverting

A price feed that has frozen does not throw. It returns its last answer, correctly formatted, with a
timestamp you were free to ignore. Chainlink names the two things that cause a feed to publish a new
answer: a deviation threshold, where real-world movement beyond an interval triggers an update, and a
heartbeat threshold, where a feed inside its deviation band still updates every so many minutes or
hours.<Cite src="chainlink-historical-data" /> Neither of those is a promise about the current
second, and the docs assign the consequence to you rather than to the feed: developers implementing
Data Feeds are solely responsible for instituting the requisite risk mitigation processes including,
but not limited to, data quality checks, circuit breakers, and appropriate contingency logic for
their use case.<Cite src="chainlink-developer-responsibilities" />

A reviewer can tell you that you failed to check the timestamp. A reviewer cannot tell you that the
feed stopped at 04:00 last Tuesday. The first is a finding; the second is an incident, and only one
of them has a start time.

### A parameter drifts until an assumption stops holding

Collateral factors, fee splits, caps, liquidation bonuses, supported asset lists. Each was correct
when set. Each was set against a market state, and market states move. There is no version of the
codebase in which this is a bug, because the value is a variable and putting a variable in range is
what governance is for. The failure is a slow one and it has no commit.

<Aside kind="note">
The four categories are not exotic. They are the ordinary operating conditions of a deployed
protocol. What makes them a coherent group is that all four produce *correct execution of correct
code* — which is exactly the thing a code review is built to certify and exactly the thing that
certification does not help you with.
</Aside>

## Three layers, because "monitoring" is three different engineering problems

Once you accept the second question is worth asking, the next mistake is to treat the answer as one
system. It is three, and they differ in latency, in precision and in what they need to run.

**Transaction-level.** A predicate over a single transaction or a single log: this event fired, this
selector was called, this address appeared. Fast — you can evaluate it the moment the log is
available, and in some designs before inclusion. Narrow — it fires only for the pattern you named,
which means it catches nothing you failed to anticipate. Precision is entirely a function of how
specific the pattern is: `Upgraded(address)` on your own proxy is essentially never a false positive;
"a transfer larger than X" is a false positive most of the time. Tooling is an event subscription and
a decoder.

**State-level.** A predicate over contract storage, evaluated against live state: total shares
against total assets, the sum of user balances against the contract's holdings, the pool's curve
equation. Slower, because you have to read state and read it consistently. Exactly as precise as the
predicate — no more and no less, which sounds like a limitation and is actually the whole selling
point, because a predicate that is definitionally false when something is wrong has no false-positive
rate to argue about. This is the layer [the next lesson](/c/invariant-monitoring) is entirely about,
and the empirical work on which invariants actually stop real exploits is the reason it goes
second rather than fifth.<Cite src="acm-invariant-effectiveness" /><Cite src="trace2inv-fse24" />

**Economic-level.** Aggregates over value, price and flow: total value locked, mint volume against a
baseline, price divergence from a reference, outsized flow to a single address. Broadest coverage —
this is the only layer that can notice something you never modelled. Also the noisiest by a wide
margin, and noisy for a reason worth stating precisely, because most people assume the noise comes
from markets being volatile.

It does, partly. But the data is also noisy *before* the market gets involved. A 2026 network-based
study of DeFi TVL dynamics reports that raw TVL data contains substantial irregularities due to
oracle disruptions, API glitches, contract upgrades or temporary reporting errors, and had to run a
cleaning pass before any analysis was possible.<Cite src="arxiv-tvl-fragility" /> Under their
cleaning rule, 3.31% of protocol-day observations were flagged as technical anomalies — typically
abrupt spikes followed by immediate reversals — and repaired by interpolation, while only 0.34% were
classified as large persistent changes and kept as economically meaningful liquidity
shifts.<Cite src="arxiv-tvl-fragility" />

<Aside kind="warn">
Those three values — a 5 million USD absolute change, a 200% relative change, and a median-absolute-deviation
score above 12 — are the study's **data-cleaning** cut-offs, chosen to decide which daily observations
to repair before running a systemic-risk model.<Cite src="arxiv-tvl-fragility" /> They are not
exploit-alerting thresholds and were never proposed as any. You will meet them, and other numbers that
disagree with them by orders of magnitude, quoted as recommended defaults. The published guidance
gives a *process* instead — establish a baseline from historical data, use multi-layered and adaptive
thresholds, and review them<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-thresholds" /> — and
[lesson three](/c/anomaly-heuristics) takes the disagreement apart properly. For now: there are no
defaults to copy.
</Aside>

### What the split buys you

A monitoring design that does not say which layer each alert lives on has no way to reason about its
own false-positive rate, because the three layers have structurally different ones. Naming the layer
forces three decisions at once — how fast this can possibly fire, how often it will be wrong, and
what infrastructure it needs — and those three decisions are what determine which channel the alert
belongs on.

The Security Alliance's guidance points at the same structure from the operational side, organising
coverage into tracks — transaction monitoring, contract event monitoring, bridge monitoring, oracle
and governance monitoring, node and network monitoring — rather than into a single
feed.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-guidelines" />

## Where the alerts go is part of the design, not an afterthought

Two sentences from the same framework do more work than most monitoring architectures. For every
alert you configure, answer: who receives this, and what do they do when it
fires.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring" /> And: an alert with no one responsible is
indistinguishable from no alert at all.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-guidelines" />

The guidance goes further into territory people rarely consider until they have been burned. It
prioritises dedicated paging tools with escalation policies and delivery receipts, treats chat
destinations as useful for visibility but easy to miss with no delivery guarantees, and says not to
rely on email as the sole channel for critical alerts because of latency and spam
filtering.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-tools" /> It asks you to test alert delivery end to
end periodically rather than only checking that the rule is configured, because a misconfigured
webhook or an expired token silently breaks
alerting.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-guidelines" /> And — paranoid until you think about who
benefits — configure alerts for the disabling or modification of existing
alerts.<Cite src="seal-frameworks-monitoring-guidelines" />

<Check type="explain" concept="monitoring-is-not-auditing">
Five things happened to five different protocols. For each one, say (a) whether a competent audit of
the protocol's own source code could plausibly have prevented it, and (b) which of the three signal
layers a detector for it would live on.

1. The multisig executes a proxy upgrade to an implementation with a `sweep()` function added.
2. A yield-bearing token the protocol accepts as collateral has its exchange rate inflated by an
   unpermissioned call on the token's own contract, and positions are liquidated against the new rate.
3. The protocol's price feed returns the same answer for nine hours while the market moves 20%.
4. Governance raises a collateral factor in March; in August the asset's liquidity has halved and the
   factor is now too high for safe liquidation.
5. A `redeem` path rounds in the user's favour by one wei, and an attacker calls it four million times.

<Answer>
**1 — audit: no. Layer: transaction.** The upgrade mechanism is presumably correct; the review would
have confirmed it works. What went wrong is *which implementation address was supplied*, which is a
runtime value. The detector is a subscription to `Upgraded(address)` on the proxy — the event
ERC-1967 says should notify changes to the implementation slot — and it fires on the log, before you
know anything about the new code.<Cite src="eip-1967" /> Note the second half of the response, which
is not monitoring: the alert is only useful if somebody can read the new implementation's code inside
your response window.

**2 — audit: partly, and this is the honest one.** A reviewer could have flagged that the protocol
consumes a rate movable by an unpermissioned call on someone else's contract — a real finding, about a
contract outside the audit's scope, so whether it appears depends on how the engagement was bounded.
**Layer: state, with a transaction-level assist.** The state detector is a sanity bound on the
imported rate; the assist is watching the dependency's own privileged surface, which is
[dependency monitoring](/c/dependency-monitoring).

**3 — audit: yes, one half of it.** A reviewer can absolutely find that you called the feed and never
compared its timestamp to a staleness bound, and should. What a reviewer cannot do is tell you the
feed is stale *now*. **Layer: this is the awkward one, because it is an absence.** No transaction
appears, no state changes, no aggregate moves — a frozen feed is perfectly still. It needs an
inverted alert that fires when the expected update has *not* happened inside a window, which is
[lesson five](/c/offchain-liveness-signals). Chainlink names the two update triggers and hands you
the responsibility for the contingency
logic.<Cite src="chainlink-historical-data" /><Cite src="chainlink-developer-responsibilities" />

**4 — audit: no, and no monitor catches this as an event either.** There is no moment at which
something happened; the parameter was correct when set and is incorrect now. **Layer: economic,
evaluated as a standing predicate rather than a change detector** — alerting when modelled liquidation
slippage at current position sizes and current pool depth exceeds the liquidation bonus. Periodic
re-evaluation of an assumption, not a reaction to an event, and the layer's best use.

**5 — audit: yes. Genuinely a code defect.** Rounding direction in a redemption path is what review,
[fuzzing](/c/invariant-testing) and formal methods are for. **Layer: state.** The accounting invariant
degrades by one wei per call and crosses any tolerance long before four million calls complete. This
is the only one of the five where the audit was the right primary control, and also the only one where
the invariant and the audit check the same statement.

**The pattern.** Four of five were not code defects. That is not a claim about the loss distribution
across the industry — this course does not have a defensible number for that and will not invent one.
It is a claim about *kind*: the failures a deployed protocol experiences are mostly facts about the
world, and facts about the world are not in the repository.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson** takes the state layer seriously and does the thing that makes this module worth
your time: the `invariant_` functions already sitting in your Foundry suite and the production monitor
you have not written yet are the same predicate with two state sources. Most of the work is done.

**Lesson three** is the discipline that keeps the result usable — precision as a safety property, with
numbers from a system that was deployed and measured rather than a rule of thumb. **Lesson four** is
where the ordering lands: given that the strongest published detector declares privileged actions out
of scope, hand-written alerts for role grants, ownership transfers, upgrades and timelock queueing are
the part nothing else covers.<Cite src="houston-ndss" /> **Lesson five** handles everything that fails
by ceasing, including your own stack. **Lesson six** connects a detector to a transaction, and
confronts the fact that the reference implementation most tutorials describe is no longer running —
Defender's hosted platform has been replaced by open-source
components.<Cite src="oz-defender-docs" /><Cite src="oz-monitor-docs" />

One thing to carry into all five. When you reach for `OwnershipTransferred`, `RoleGranted` or
`RoleRevoked` to find out who holds what, you are not improvising: OpenZeppelin's documentation says
the base `AccessControl` contract does not support on-chain enumeration of role members and that you
should rely on those events, processed off chain, to track
membership.<Cite src="oz-docs-access-control" /> For anything you did not write yourself, the
monitoring layer *is* the access-control design's read path.
