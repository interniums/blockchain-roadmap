The proposal is one slide and it is always the same slide. The provider invoice on the left, a server
on the right, the server smaller. Someone says "that is a ten-times saving", and nobody in the room
can immediately say why it is wrong — because on the numbers presented it is not wrong.

It is wrong because the slide compares the only two costs that have invoices attached. The thing that
actually decides this question — the attention of whoever will be paged when the node falls behind at
03:00 on a Sunday — has no line item anywhere.

This is the last lesson in the module and deliberately the least technical. Running a node is a solved
engineering problem with published requirements. Deciding whether to is a resourcing problem with a
countable answer, and the count is in engineer-hours.

## The side with a price list

Take the hardware seriously first, because it is real and because you can settle it in an afternoon.

Ethereum.org publishes a minimum — 2 or more CPU cores, 16 GB RAM with 32 GB recommended for
stability, a 2 TB NVMe SSD annotated as "likely exceeded by 2027", and 25+ Mbit/s of
bandwidth<Cite src="eth-run-a-node" /> — and a recommendation that now points at a spec of its own.
EIP-7870 is a Living Informational EIP giving hardware and bandwidth recommendations per node role,
specifying 4 TB NVMe, 32 GB RAM, 4 cores / 8 threads and 50 Mbps down / 15 Mbps up for a full
node.<Cite src="eth-run-a-node" /><Cite src="eip-7870" />

Two details in it matter more than those headline numbers.

**The storage requirement is an IOPS requirement wearing a capacity label.** EIP-7870 asks for at
least ~500 MB/s sequential read/write, ~50,000 random 4K read IOPS and ~15,000 random 4K write IOPS,
specifies NVMe over SATA for latency rather than throughput, and advises against DRAM-less and QLC
drives on endurance grounds.<Cite src="eip-7870" /> [Disk is the
bottleneck](/c/disk-is-the-bottleneck), and cloud dashboards advertise the wrong axis: a volume rated
in MB/s can be catastrophic at random 4K reads, which is the access pattern a state database has.

**The capacity guidance has a stated shelf life.** As of January 2025 2 TB can still work, "but daily
chain growth makes 4 TB more future-proof".<Cite src="eip-7870" /> That is a recommendation with an
expiry date printed on it.

And you need two processes — [an execution client and a consensus
client](/c/node-two-process-model) — so add roughly another 200 GB for beacon
data.<Cite src="eth-run-a-node" />

<Aside kind="stop">
**Do not carry a disk figure for an archive node out of this lesson, or out of anywhere else.** Three
published sources disagree by roughly an order of magnitude. Geth's own hardware page says a full
archive node keeping all state back to genesis "requires more than 12TB of
space".<Cite src="geth-hardware" /> Ethereum.org's per-client table gives 12TB+ for Besu, Geth and
Nethermind but 2.5TB+ for Erigon and 2.2TB+ for Reth, noting Erigon and Reth do not offer snap sync
though full pruning is possible.<Cite src="eth-run-a-node" /> A 2026 vendor article states that a full
Ethereum archive node needs 18–20 TB of fast NVMe and grows another ~1–2 TB per
month.<Cite src="chainstack-selfhost-myths" />

These cannot all describe the same thing. They differ on client, on state layout, on what "archive"
includes and on when they were written, and nobody has reconciled them. Pick the client first, read
*that client's* current release notes, and provision from there — the only procedure that will still
work next year.
</Aside>

## The side with no price list

Now the costs that decide it. None of these appear on the slide, and all of them are recurring.

**Fork upgrades.** Every network upgrade requires the client upgraded before the fork, on both layers,
or the node silently follows the wrong chain — a hard deadline set by someone else. The cadence is
what people underestimate: one vendor account puts Reth at 33 releases in 2025 and Prysm at 18, and
notes Fusaka landing on mainnet on December 3, 2025.<Cite src="chainstack-selfhost-myths" /> Not every
release is mandatory; enough are that keeping up is a standing calendar item, and multiplying it by
the number of chains you serve multiplies the calendar, not the benefit.

**Pruning, which takes the node offline.** Geth's own documentation is unusually direct. A snap-synced
node "currently requires more than 650 GB of disk space" and "with default cache size the database
grows by about 14 GB/week", so users "will rapidly run out of space on 1TB hard drives"; an offline
snapshot prune brings it back to roughly 650 GB, and "the pruning time depends on your hardware but it
can take upwards of 12 hours".<Cite src="geth-dbpruning" /> The same page recommends doing this
periodically — "e.g. every month or so for a 1TB disk" — requires at least 40 GB free to start, notes
failures at around 25 GB free, warns of a compaction stage lasting over an hour with no log output
that must not be interrupted, and states plainly that you cannot prune an archive
node.<Cite src="geth-dbpruning" />

Read that as an operations spec rather than a manual. It says: **this machine has a scheduled,
recurring, multi-hour outage, it cannot be started while it is happening, and the procedure has a
failure mode that looks identical to a hang.**

**Those numbers are already stale, in the direction that hurts.** Geth's hardware page carries "at the
time of writing (September 2022)" and was last edited in September
2023,<Cite src="geth-hardware" /> and the L1 gas limit has since been raised to 60 million by
EIP-7935.<Cite src="eip-7935" /> More gas per block means more state writes and receipts, so 14 GB/week
is a floor rather than a measurement — and every interval computed from it, pruning cadence included,
is optimistic.

**Resync after corruption.** Power loss, a bad shutdown, a disk that lies about flushes. The recovery
is a resync — hours to days depending on mode, during which the node serves nothing.

**The failure that has no alarm.** This is the one that should decide the argument, and it is exactly
the condition the previous lesson taught you to distrust: a node that loses the tip and keeps
answering is invisible to a process health check and visible to your users. The vendor article cites
Ethernodes' live tracker showing roughly a quarter of Ethereum execution-layer nodes out of sync at
any given time — 29% execution and 6.2% consensus as of early
2026.<Cite src="chainstack-selfhost-myths" /> Treat the exact figure as one company's citation of a
third-party tracker rather than a measurement you can rely on, and treat the *shape* as the point:
falling behind is a common steady state in this population, and the only thing that catches it is a
chain-aware health check somebody has to write and watch.

## Read the vendor's incentive, then read the number

Every cost estimate in this area is published by someone selling something, so the useful skill is
reading the incentive alongside the figure rather than discarding either.

The article quoted above puts the do-it-yourself cost at "roughly two weeks of senior engineer time to
stand up a production-grade Ethereum node, plus ~4 hours per week ongoing — around $6,000 upfront and
$15,000+ annually per protocol at typical senior engineer rates", and calls that "the honest DIY
figure".<Cite src="chainstack-selfhost-myths" /> Now the incentive: it is published by a company whose
product range includes managed RPC, dedicated nodes **and** a self-hosted node product, and the
argument the figure supports is that the ongoing hours collapse once the operational layer is bought
rather than built.<Cite src="chainstack-selfhost-myths" />

That does not make the number wrong. It makes it a number from a party with a reason to state the DIY
burden accurately and then sell a reduction of it — a shape to test against your own team, not an
input to copy. **The durable output is the unit, not the value:** this cost is denominated in
*engineer-weeks up front plus engineer-hours per week per chain*, and once you accept that
denomination the argument becomes arithmetic you can do at your own rates.

The same source is worth reading for what it concedes, because a vendor's list of "when you should not
buy my product" is the most credible paragraph on any vendor page. Managed infrastructure is the better
call, it says, when the team is under five engineers and blockchain infrastructure is not a core
competency; when the workload is a prototype or a temporary campaign; when traffic is bursty in ways
provisioned capacity would waste; when there is no in-house Kubernetes familiarity; and when the
application needs no custom tracers, exotic RPC extensions or data-residency
guarantees.<Cite src="chainstack-selfhost-myths" />

## When self-hosting actually wins

Three cases, and they are narrower and more specific than the general enthusiasm suggests.

**Sustained high volume, where the bill exceeds the rota.** The only case that turns on price, and note
what has to be true: *sustained*. Bursty traffic is the worst fit for a fixed machine, because you size
for the peak and pay for it continuously.

**Capabilities nobody sells.** Custom tracers, an unusual namespace, mempool access on your terms,
latency below what a shared fleet offers. This is where the previous lesson's audit becomes a direct
input: the non-standard column lists what a self-hosted node either gives you outright or cannot help
with at all, and the difference is whether the capability was ever chain data. A custom tracer was; a
token-metadata API never was, and running your own node does not get you one.

**Privacy.** Every read names an address and a block, and every broadcast passes through the provider
before the network sees it.<Cite src="execution-apis-spec" /> Ethereum.org makes this the argument for
running your own: with your own node "you can use dapps more securely and privately because you won't
have to leak your addresses and balances to
intermediaries".<Cite src="ethereum-org-nodes-clients" /> It is the one requirement here that no
contract, plan or configuration can satisfy — architectural or unmet.

Everything else loses, and it loses hardest for a small team, because the resource it consumes —
uninterrupted attention from whoever also owns the product — is the one a small team has least of.

## Self-hosting is additive, not a replacement

"Build versus buy" implies an exclusive choice and the operational reality does not support one.

Your node has scheduled unavailability designed into how it is run. Offline pruning stops the client
for hours;<Cite src="geth-dbpruning" /> a fork upgrade restarts it; a corrupted database resyncs it.
Something has to serve during those windows, and there are exactly two options: a second node —
doubling the hardware and roughly doubling the rota — or a commercial endpoint kept as failover. The
vendor arguing hardest for self-hosting builds this in, describing configurable failover to its own
cloud RPC for when a self-hosted node has to be taken
offline.<Cite src="chainstack-selfhost-myths" /> When the party selling you self-hosting puts a
provider in the design, that is not marketing; it is the shape of the problem.

Which means the realistic outcome is not a line removed from the spreadsheet. It is **a much smaller
provider plan and a much better worst case**: your own node serving the sustained, expensive,
privacy-sensitive traffic, and a provider behind a fallback transport serving everything else and
absorbing your maintenance windows.<Cite src="viem-fallback-transport" /> Budget for it that way and
the decision stops being ideological.

A five-engineer team runs a wallet on Ethereum, Base and Arbitrum. Their provider bill is $2,400 a
month and rising with users. One engineer proposes self-hosting: a machine per chain, "about $600 a
month all in", saving $1,800 a month or $21,600 a year.

Nobody on the team has run a node. There is no on-call rota; the founder answers pages.

Work through what the proposal is missing, and say what you would actually do.

<Answer>
**Start by fixing the denominator, because the saving is stated in the wrong unit.** Three chains is
three machines, three client pairs, three upgrade calendars and three pruning schedules. Using the
only published DIY estimate available — roughly two engineer-weeks up front and ~4 hours per week
ongoing *per protocol*<Cite src="chainstack-selfhost-myths" /> — this is six engineer-weeks of setup
and roughly twelve hours a week forever, on a five-person team. At any plausible loaded rate the
ongoing hours alone exceed the $1,800 monthly saving, and that is before the setup. **The proposal is
not a $21,600 saving; it is a proposal to convert $21,600 into most of a headcount.**

**Then check whether the hardware line is right.** "$600 a month all in" is a guess; price the
published requirements instead. EIP-7870's full-node recommendation is 4 TB NVMe, 32 GB RAM and
4 cores / 8 threads with 50/15 Mbps, plus IOPS floors of ~50,000 random 4K reads and ~15,000 random
4K writes that rule out most cheap cloud volumes,<Cite src="eip-7870" /> and each chain wants roughly
another 200 GB for beacon data.<Cite src="eth-run-a-node" /> L2 nodes are not "the same again" either
— each has its own client, sync story and cadence.

**Name the recurring outages the proposal does not staff.** Offline pruning is a stop-the-client
procedure that "can take upwards of 12 hours", is recommended periodically — every month or so for a
1 TB disk — and cannot be run against an archive node at all.<Cite src="geth-dbpruning" /> Three
chains means those windows land somewhere in every month, and something must serve during them. The
provider contract does not go away; at best it shrinks.

**Name the failure that has no alarm, because this is a wallet.** A node that falls behind keeps
answering, and one vendor's citation of a public tracker puts roughly a quarter of execution-layer
nodes out of sync at any given time.<Cite src="chainstack-selfhost-myths" /> A wallet serving balances
from a node two hundred blocks behind shows confidently wrong numbers and builds transactions on stale
nonces — the previous lesson's failure, now with nobody but the founder to page.

**Then ask the question nobody asked: why is the bill $2,400?** This is where the module's earlier
lessons pay for themselves. A wallet's traffic is overwhelmingly cheap latest-state reads, and the
usual finding is a loop: a contract read per token per refresh at 26 units
each,<Cite src="alchemy-compute-units" /> where one multicall would do. Pull last month's usage
grouped by method before provisioning anything. A 3× reduction from batching and a longer polling
interval is an afternoon's work and it beats the proposed saving outright, with no rota attached.

**What I would actually do, in order.**

1. **Measure and optimise the call mix first.** It is the highest ratio and it is reversible.
2. **Price the workload against a second provider's current published table**, since a near-flat
   two-tier rule and a per-method table price the same traffic very
   differently.<Cite src="chainstack-request-units" /> Wallet traffic at the tip is the profile that
   does well on a weighted table; confirm rather than assume.
3. **Add a second provider behind a fallback transport** — a few lines, and it buys the reliability
   the self-hosting proposal was implicitly reaching for.<Cite src="viem-fallback-transport" />
4. **Revisit self-hosting when a trigger fires, not on a hunch.** Write the triggers down now: the
   bill exceeding a loaded engineer-month; a capability no provider sells; or a privacy requirement
   that has to be architectural.<Cite src="ethereum-org-nodes-clients" />
5. **If it ever fires, run one node on one chain**, in front of the traffic that is both the largest
   and the least latency-sensitive, with the provider still behind it. Learn the rota on one machine
   before buying three.

**The part worth carrying.** The build-versus-buy question is almost never decided by the provider
bill. It is decided by whether your team has an engineer-week per chain to spend up front and a
few hours per chain per week to spend forever — and whether that is the most valuable thing those hours
could be doing. **For most product teams it is not, and the correct answer is a cheaper plan rather
than a server.**
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**This closes the module**, and the through-line is worth stating once: the endpoint is not
configuration, so price it, limit it, audit it and staff it like the dependency it is. The bill is a
function of your call mix. The throughput limit is independent of the volume cap. The log query and
the loop write the invoice. The fleet behind one URL does not agree with itself. The convenient
endpoint is a position. And the node you could run yourself is denominated in attention.

**Next in this track**, the layer above: indexing, event pipelines and monitoring. All three are
structurally the same problem you have been studying — they exist because [`eth_getLogs` cannot answer
product questions at product scale](/c/getlogs-is-the-cost-driver), and each is another dependency
with a pricing model, a failure mode and a migration cost.

**And two artifacts to leave behind.** The call inventory from the first lesson, with a cost per row.
The portability list from the last one, with hours per row. Between them they answer, on paper, every
question this module has raised — including this one, which is why the build-versus-buy decision
should take an hour rather than a quarter.
