The plan says ten million. The product does about two million reads a month. Someone divides, decides
there is four times the headroom needed, and moves on to a real problem.

Six weeks later the account is capped. Nothing changed about the traffic. What changed is that
somebody noticed the arithmetic never held: at 26 units for a single contract read, ten million units
buys closer to four hundred thousand of them, not ten million.<Cite src="alchemy-compute-units" />

This lesson is about closing that gap before it opens. The bill is not a mystery and it is not a
surprise — it is a function of your call mix, and you already have the call inventory from the
previous lesson. What is missing is the multiplier table, an honest account of how much of that table
you can actually trust, and the second pricing model that changes which workloads are expensive.

## A unit is a latency estimate wearing a price tag

Start with what the unit *measures*, because it explains the whole shape of the table.

Alchemy's own explainer says compute units "measure the total computational resources your apps use",
draws the analogy to paying a cloud provider for compute, and then gives the mechanism: "Each method
is assigned a quantity of compute units, derived from global average durations of each
method."<Cite src="alchemy-compute-units-explainer" />

Read that last clause again. The multiplier is a **measured average duration**, converted into a
billing unit. That is why the table looks the way it does, and it makes the numbers predictable rather
than arbitrary: a method's cost tracks how much work a node does to answer it. Reading one account
field is a point lookup. Executing a call is an EVM invocation. Re-executing a transaction with a
tracer attached is that plus instrumentation on every step. The pricing is not a pricing decision so
much as a physics decision that someone measured.

Which means two things you can act on. **The ranking is stable even when the numbers are not** —
providers re-measure and re-price, but a trace will never be cheaper than a block-number lookup.
And **the cheap way to make something cheaper is to make the node do less work**, not to negotiate.

Three observations before the caveats.

**The spread is real but not enormous, in this slice.** Cheapest to most expensive among these
methods is 10 to 40 — a factor of four. That is the ratio that decides whether re-engineering a
screen is worth doing.

**There is a second column, and it is a separate limit.** Throughput compute units "define how often
you can run the request within your throughput limits", and the explainer is explicit that "the actual
cost of the method is still reflected by the CU amount while the threshold CU allows you to increase
the number of requests before you hit your rate limit", with the rule that "if there is no throughput
CU listed, it defaults to the actual CU cost".<Cite src="alchemy-compute-units-explainer" /> So
`debug_traceTransaction` costs 40 against your volume and 1000 against your throughput — a factor of
25 between the two axes for a single method.<Cite src="alchemy-compute-units" /> A method can be
affordable and still be un-runnable at the rate you want. That is the whole of the next lesson.

**`eth_getBlockReceipts` is the sleeper.** 20 against volume, 500 against
throughput.<Cite src="alchemy-compute-units" /> It is the method an indexer reaches for to avoid N
receipt fetches per block, and on the volume axis that is a clear win — but it is priced as a
throughput hazard, which is exactly the axis a backfill loop saturates.

### The entry you must not memorise

`eth_getLogs` is missing from the table above on purpose, and the reason is worth walking through
because it is a lesson in how to read vendor documentation.

The cost reference lists it at 60 CU, and the method's own endpoint page repeats 60 in its
header.<Cite src="alchemy-compute-units" /><Cite src="alchemy-eth-getlogs" /> But the compute-units
explainer, on the same documentation site, gives a worked example of how per-second consumption adds
up: one `eth_blockNumber` (10 CUs), two `eth_getLogs` (75 CUs) and two `eth_call` (26 CUs) in the same
second, "you will have a total of 212 CUPS".<Cite src="alchemy-compute-units-explainer" />

Check the arithmetic. Ten, plus two of something, plus two twenty-sixes, equals 212. Two of something
equals 150. The example only works at **75** per log query, not 60. Separately, a secondary
cross-provider comparison in circulation reports 75 as a *per-log-returned* charge rather than a flat
one — a third reading, and a materially different cost model.<Cite src="dwellir-rpc-pricing" />

Three readings, one method, one vendor's own site supplying two of them. This has not been resolved,
so the number does not go in the table and it does not go in your head. What goes in your head is the
*shape*: the log query is the expensive standard read, it is somewhere in the region of two to three
times a contract call, and you look up the current figure on the day you need it, from the provider
you are actually on.

<Aside kind="stop">
**The cross-provider multiplier table is not something this curriculum will print.** You will find
tables comparing the same method's unit cost across five providers. They circulate widely, they are
published by market participants comparing themselves to competitors, and the figures in them are not
independently confirmable — the one entry that could be checked against a first-party source is the
26 for `eth_call`.<Cite src="alchemy-compute-units" /><Cite src="dwellir-rpc-pricing" /> A comparison
table is worth exactly as much as its worst row, and none of these tables is authoritative. Price your
own call mix against each provider's own current published table. That is the only method that
survives the numbers changing.
</Aside>

## Worked example: price a screen before you build it

A portfolio page. Balance, a dozen token positions, recent activity. Here is the discipline.

**Subgoal — count calls per unit of work, and pick the right unit.** Not per user, not per page
view: per *refresh cycle*, because a refresh is what repeats. One refresh issues one
`eth_blockNumber`, one `eth_getBalance`, twelve `eth_call`s for token balances, and one `eth_getLogs`
for the activity list. Fifteen requests.

**Subgoal — apply the provider's own table, method by method, and keep them separate.** Ten, plus
twenty, plus twelve twenty-sixes, is 342 units before the log query.<Cite src="alchemy-compute-units" />
Keeping the rows separate rather than summing immediately is what makes the next step possible.

**Subgoal — mark every entry you could not source, and carry it as a range.** The log query is the
one you cannot pin, so the refresh is 342 plus something in the 60-to-75 region: call it **402 to
417 units**, and write it down that way. A range you can defend beats a number you cannot.

**Subgoal — find the line that dominates, before optimising anything.** Twelve contract reads are 312
of those units — three quarters of the refresh. The log query, expensive per call, is one call. **The
cost is in the loop, not in the expensive method.** This inverts the intuition most people arrive
with, and it is the single most useful output of the exercise.

**Subgoal — multiply by a traffic model made of refreshes, not users.** A thousand sessions a day,
eight refreshes per session, is 8,000 refreshes — about 3.3 million units a day, near a hundred
million a month. Notice what just happened to the ten-million plan.

**Subgoal — find the change with the best ratio.** Twelve contract reads collapse into one
`eth_call` against a multicall aggregator: [viem's `multicall`](/c/viem-multicall-batching) batches them into a
single contract invocation.<Cite src="viem-multicall" /> Twelve calls at 26 become one at 26, and the
refresh falls from roughly 400 units to roughly 115 — a 3.5× reduction from one change that costs an
afternoon.<Cite src="alchemy-compute-units" /> Every subsequent optimisation on this screen is worth
less than this one.

**Subgoal — check the forecast against the dashboard after a day, and keep the delta.** If your
prediction is within ten percent, your model of the product is correct and you can use it for
planning. If it is off by three times, something is making calls you did not know about — a polling
interval you forgot, a component that mounts twice, a retry loop. **The gap between forecast and
invoice is a bug report about your own architecture**, and it is cheaper to read than a profiler.

## The second family, and why "flat" is the wrong word for it

Not every provider prices per method. The second model in wide use meters requests with far less
per-method weighting — but "flat" overstates it, and the difference matters.

Chainstack publishes a two-step rule rather than a multiplier table. A request against latest state
costs **1 request unit**; a request that needs archive state costs **2**. For most EVM chains, "127 or
more blocks behind" the tip is what makes a request archive rather than latest. All `debug_*`,
`trace_*` and `arbtrace_*` methods — the reference names `debug_traceTransaction`,
`debug_traceBlockByNumber` and `trace_block` — are always 2 regardless of block age. `eth_callMany` is
2 regardless. And for `eth_getLogs` and `eth_newFilter` the block evaluated for billing is `fromBlock`
if set, otherwise `toBlock` if set, otherwise `latest`.<Cite src="chainstack-request-units" />

That is not one price. It is two prices plus a handful of named exceptions, which is why the accurate
description is **near-flat**. Anyone who tells you a provider is on "flat pricing" has skipped the
part that will show up on your invoice: whether your reads are inside or outside the recent-state
window, and where your log queries start.

That last clause deserves a moment. A backfill that walks forward in ten-thousand-block chunks is
billed on `fromBlock` — the *older* end of each chunk — so the entire historical portion of the
backfill is charged at the archive rate, and only the final chunks near the tip drop to the latest
rate.<Cite src="chainstack-request-units" /> Reversing the direction of your loop does not change
that; the boundary is a property of the block number, not of your iteration order.

### What actually differs between the two models

Not the price. The **spread**.

On the weighted table, the confirmed methods above run from 10 to 40 — a 4× spread — and up to 25×
on the throughput axis for a single method.<Cite src="alchemy-compute-units" /> Under the near-flat
rule, the spread between the cheapest and the most expensive request is exactly
**2×**.<Cite src="chainstack-request-units" />

Make that concrete with two workloads.

**Workload A** is a product read path: nine parts cheap latest-state lookups to one part tracing.
**Workload B** is an analytics or reconstruction path: one part cheap lookups to nine parts tracing.

Take `eth_blockNumber` at 10 and `debug_traceTransaction` at 40 as the two ends of the weighted
table.<Cite src="alchemy-compute-units" /> Workload A averages 13 units per request; workload B
averages 37. Moving from A to B multiplies your unit consumption by **2.85**.

Now the same two workloads under the near-flat rule, at 1 and 2.<Cite src="chainstack-request-units" />
Workload A averages 1.1 units per request; workload B averages 1.9. The same move multiplies
consumption by **1.73**.

**Getting more expensive costs you 2.85× under one model and 1.73× under the other**, for identical
traffic. So if the two providers are priced such that workload A costs you about the same on each,
workload B costs roughly 1.65× more on the weighted one. Nothing about that conclusion requires
comparing their unit prices, which is exactly why it is a claim you can actually make.

The practical rule: **a workload dominated by cheap latest-state reads tends to do better where the
spread is wide; a workload dominated by tracing or archive reads tends to do better where the spread
is compressed.** Which one you are is a fact about your product, and it can change — the day you ship
an activity feed that reconstructs internal transfers, you have moved from A toward B without changing
a line of the pricing page.

## What this unlocks

**Next**, the two limits. You now know what a unit is; the next lesson is about the two completely
different ways of running out of them, why a dashboard showing five percent of your monthly quota
tells you nothing about whether the next burst succeeds, and why a naive retry turns a two-second
problem into a twenty-minute one.<Cite src="alchemy-throughput" />

**Two lessons out**, the three methods that write most of the bill get their own treatment: the log
query and its caps, what actually makes a read an archive read, and the two tracing namespaces that
are not the same API.

**And carry the discipline, not the table.** Every figure in this lesson has a shelf life; the method
does not. Count calls per unit of work, apply the provider's own current table, keep the unsourced
entries as ranges, find the line that dominates, and check the forecast against the invoice. That
loop is what turns the endpoint from a cost centre you discover into a number you chose.
