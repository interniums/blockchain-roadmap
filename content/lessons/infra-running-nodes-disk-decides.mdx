The node synced. It took the expected number of hours, the logs are clean, `eth_syncing` returns
`false`, and it has been running for three weeks without an error.

It is also four blocks behind head. Not sometimes. Always. When you check at nine in the morning it is
four blocks behind, and when you check at eleven at night it is four blocks behind, and the gap never
closes and never widens. If you stake on it, attestations land late. If you serve reads from it, users
occasionally see a transaction "not found" that a public endpoint found forty seconds ago.

Every dashboard is green. CPU is at twenty percent, memory has headroom, the network graph is flat and
boring, and the storage volume reports throughput far above anything the documentation asked for.

This is the characteristic failure of an underpowered node, and it is confusing precisely because it is
not a failure of any resource anyone thought to provision. This lesson is about the resource that is
actually binding, why the number your cloud console shows you is the wrong one, and about the recurring
chore that follows from the same underlying fact.

## The workload is not what the specification sheet measures

Start from what executing a block actually asks of the disk.

You know from the state trie material that [trie paths are hashes of keys](/c/merkle-patricia-trie),
which means logically adjacent state — two slots in the same mapping, two accounts owned by the same
user — lands in unrelated places in the structure. That is the property that makes the trie's
authenticated lookups possible and it is also the property that makes access
[random rather than sequential](/c/state-growth-problem).

Now execute a block against that. A transaction touches accounts and slots that were not chosen to be
near each other; the client resolves each one by walking a path of interior nodes; and those walks are
scattered small reads, not one large one. Multiply by the transactions in a block, and one block is
thousands of independent small reads at unpredictable offsets. Since EIP-7935 raised the default block
gas limit to 60M,<Cite src="eip-7935" /> each block can contain materially more of them than the same
workload produced a year earlier.

Then add the deadline. Blocks arrive on a fixed cadence, and your execution client is not free to take
as long as it likes: the consensus client hands it a payload over `engine_newPayload` and waits for a
`VALID` or `INVALID` answer.<Cite src="engine-api-paris" /> If answering takes longer than the gap
between blocks, the node does not crash. It just never catches up, because the next payload is already
waiting when it finishes the last one.

**That is the whole mechanism behind a node that is permanently four blocks behind.** Nothing is
broken. The service is arriving faster than it can be performed, and the queue depth has found its
equilibrium.

## Latency, and the two numbers that stand in for it

The practitioner sources are unusually direct about the metric, and worth quoting because the
distinction they draw is finer than "use an SSD".

The community SSD list that geth's own hardware page points readers to<Cite src="geth-hardware" /> opens
by stating that syncing a node is largely reliant on the latency of the storage, that budget drives
will struggle and some will not sync at all, and — crucially — that **IOPS can roughly be used as a
proxy for latency, while measuring latency directly is arguably better.**<Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" />
The eth-docker resource page says the same thing in one line: latency is what matters most to Ethereum
clients.<Cite src="eth-docker-resource-usage" />

So there is a hierarchy of metrics, and most people stop one or two rungs too early:

1. **Throughput (MB/s)** — measures the wrong axis entirely.
2. **IOPS** — correlated with the right answer, and the number vendors publish.
3. **Random-read latency under load** — the thing that decides it.

Both sources give a target on the third rung. The SSD list reports that geth is understood to benefit
from around 300 microseconds of latency, while noting that not all clients are alike — some need
faster, some tolerate slower.<Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" /> eth-docker states the requirement as
staying under 300 microseconds *maximum* for an execution client, and adds the consequence that matters
to a staker: high latency degrades attestation performance, and is particularly noticeable during sync
committee duties.<Cite src="eth-docker-resource-usage" />

### The measurement that settles the cloud-storage argument

The abstract argument convinces nobody who has just provisioned a volume with a large IOPS number on
it. The SSD list contains the concrete version, and it is the most useful paragraph in this lesson.

Measured while geth was state-healing, an AWS gp3 volume provisioned at 16,000 IOPS showed roughly
425 / 615 / 1300 microseconds minimum, average and maximum latency. A data-centre NVMe drive measured
25 / 75 / 150 microseconds with geth in steady state, and 25 / 80 / 265 microseconds on the same
hardware under synthetic load.<Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" />

Read the two averages next to each other. The provisioned-IOPS network volume is roughly eight times
slower per read than the directly attached drive, and its *best case* is worse than the attached
drive's worst case. Nothing about that gap is visible from the IOPS figure, which is the number the
volume was purchased on.

The reason is structural rather than a matter of tier: a network-attached volume puts a network hop
inside every read. Sequential throughput hides the hop by amortising it over large transfers. A
workload of thousands of small scattered reads pays it every time.

<Aside kind="warn">
This is the failure mode that most deserves to be recognised on sight, because it does not announce
itself. A node on high-latency storage syncs — slowly, but it finishes — and then runs indefinitely a
few blocks behind head while reporting healthy. Compare your head to an independent source and alarm on
the gap; `eth_syncing` will not tell you, because from the client's point of view it is not syncing, it
is keeping up as fast as it can.
</Aside>

## Measuring yours, in two commands

Both sources publish the same diagnostic pair, which is a good sign that it is the one practitioners
actually use.

**Subgoal: measure latency under realistic load rather than at rest.** Run
`sudo ioping -D -c 30 /dev/<ssd-device>` while the node is running as an Ethereum node, and read the
min/avg/max.<Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" /><Cite src="eth-docker-resource-usage" /> At rest, a
mediocre drive looks fine; the point of the measurement is to catch it while it is being asked for
scattered reads faster than it likes.

**Subgoal: manufacture that load if you do not have a node yet.** Both pages give the same fio
invocation — a 4k random read/write mix at queue depth 64 over a 150 GB file — and eth-docker explains
the size: 150 GB was chosen to break through any caching strategy the SSD uses for bursty writes,
because execution clients write steadily and an SSD's behaviour under sustained write is what
matters.<Cite src="eth-docker-resource-usage" /><Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" /> Run the ioping
while the fio job is still going.<Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" />

**Subgoal: read the answer off the node itself, not just the hardware.** The SSD list names a second
measurement that is better than either, because it is the actual thing you care about: the
`newPayload` quantile in your execution client's metrics during normal operation, where you want the
95th or even 99th percentile below 500 milliseconds.<Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" />

That last one deserves emphasis. It is the Engine API call from the first lesson in this module, timed.
It measures the disk, the CPU, the client's caches and the state size together, in the units the
protocol actually imposes — and it degrades before the node falls behind, which makes it a leading
indicator rather than a post-mortem. If you take one metric from this module into a dashboard, take
that one.

### Latency problems that are not the drive

A surprising share of "my node is slow" cases are configuration rather than hardware, and the same
community list enumerates them.<Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" />

- **Heat.** A drive above roughly 60 °C throttles; the list recommends checking with `smartctl -x` and
  keeping the temperature below that, ideally below 50 °C.<Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" />
- **TRIM not being allowed**, which happens with some hardware RAID controllers.<Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" />
- **Copy-on-write filesystems** — ZFS, BTRFS and similar.<Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" />
- **RAID 5 or 6**, where write amplification is severe.<Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" />
- **A SATA controller not set to AHCI** in firmware.<Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" />
- **`atime` left on**, which the list recommends disabling on the database volume; eth-docker notes its
  own test systems are configured with `noatime` and no swap for the same
  reason.<Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" /><Cite src="eth-docker-resource-usage" />
- **QLC and DRAM-less drives**, which are far slower than mainstream ones and have lower endurance —
  the list's blunt observation being that any saving disappears when the drive fails
  early.<Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" />

Check that list before buying anything. Two of those items are one-line changes.

## The treadmill, and what it is actually a property of

The second half of this lesson is the cost line that does not appear in a hardware budget, because it
is measured in your time.

A hash-based state database accumulates trie nodes that are no longer reachable from any recent state
root — every superseded interior node from the previous lesson's arithmetic. Reclaiming that space
means taking the node **offline** and running a pruning pass. Geth's documentation describes the loop
without euphemism: users will rapidly run out of space, an offline snapshot prune brings storage back
down, and this has to be done periodically to keep total disk within the bounds of the local hardware —
on the order of every month or so for a small disk.<Cite src="geth-dbpruning" />

Three details turn that from an inconvenience into something you schedule.

**It takes real downtime.** The page gives figures of upwards of twelve hours depending on hardware, and
four to five hours for the documented command sequence, with a stretch of over an hour during the
compaction stage that produces **no log output at all** — which is normal, and must not be interrupted;
the run is finished when a "State pruning successful" message
appears.<Cite src="geth-dbpruning" />

**It needs headroom you must reserve in advance.** At least 40 GB of free space is recommended, with
failures reported around 25 GB, which means **pruning cannot rescue a disk that has already
filled**.<Cite src="geth-dbpruning" /> The rule of thumb is to prune before the node reaches about 80%
of available disk.<Cite src="geth-dbpruning" /> A monitor on free space is not a nice-to-have here; it
is the thing that keeps the escape hatch usable.

**It is not available to every node.** Geth's pruning rules begin with: do not try to prune an archive
node, because archive nodes need to maintain all historic data by definition.<Cite src="geth-dbpruning" />
Nor is retention adjustable in place — Erigon requires deleting `chaindata` and re-syncing to change
mode.<Cite src="erigon-pruning-modes" />

Now the part that keeps this lesson from ageing badly. **The treadmill is a property of the storage
scheme, not of Ethereum.** Geth's own pruning page carries a note at the top saying offline pruning is
only for the hash-based state scheme, that the path-based scheme enables pruning by default, and that
once hash-based is no longer supported, offline pruning will be
deprecated.<Cite src="geth-dbpruning" /> The archive documentation describes the path-based design that
replaces it — one persistent state plus reverse diffs rather than every historical trie
node.<Cite src="geth-archive-mode" /> Practitioner tables record the same transition across the
ecosystem, noting which clients continuously prune state and which need an explicit
pass.<Cite src="eth-docker-resource-usage" />

So the question to ask about a client is not "how often must I prune" but **"does this client's storage
engine require a maintenance window at all"** — and the answer is changing, per client, per version.

### The growth rate, and why this lesson will not give you one

You will want a gigabytes-per-week number to plan the schedule against. The sources disagree, and the
disagreement is not resolved.

Geth's own documentation states, in two places, that a snap-synced full node's database grows about
14 GB per week at the default cache size.<Cite src="geth-hardware" /><Cite src="geth-dbpruning" />
Vendor material circulating in 2026 puts the figure closer to a gigabyte a day. Nothing in this
curriculum's research resolved which is right, and there are good reasons to expect *both* to be stale:
the geth hardware page prefaces its disk section with "at the time of writing (September 2022)" and
carries a last-edited date of September 2023,<Cite src="geth-hardware" /> and the default block gas
limit has since risen to 60M,<Cite src="eip-7935" /> which means more transactions, more receipts and
more state churn per block than any pre-2026 measurement captured.

The honest procedure is short and gives you a number that is actually about your node:

1. Record the datadir size and `eth_blockNumber` on a schedule for a week.
2. Divide. That is your growth rate, on your client, your version, your sync mode, your workload.
3. From that and your free space, compute the date you will hit 80%, and put the pruning window in the
   calendar before it.
4. Re-measure after every client upgrade, because storage engines are exactly the thing client teams
   keep changing.<Cite src="eth-docker-resource-usage" />

**Four defects. The first three combine into one failure, and the fourth removes the way out of it.**

**1 — The storage is chosen on the wrong metric, and the right metric is measured.** Throughput is the
wrong axis for thousands of small scattered reads; latency is what
decides.<Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" /><Cite src="eth-docker-resource-usage" /> The published
measurement is specific: a gp3 volume at 16,000 IOPS showed roughly 425 / 615 / 1300 microseconds
min/avg/max under geth state-healing, against 25 / 75 / 150 for a directly attached data-centre
NVMe.<Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" /> Against a target of staying under about 300
microseconds,<Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" /><Cite src="eth-docker-resource-usage" /> this volume
misses on its *minimum*. Observed: the node syncs, reports healthy, and sits permanently a few blocks
behind head.

**2 — ZFS and RAID-5 both add latency to the exact operation that is already the constraint.**
Copy-on-write filesystems and RAID 5/6 are both named as things that increase
latency, RAID 5/6 for write amplification.<Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" /> Observed: the gap in
defect 1, made worse, with the cause split across three layers so that no single dashboard shows it.
The snapshot requirement is real, but it should be met by a mechanism that is not in the node's hot
read path.

**3 — The alerting cannot detect the failure this deployment will actually have.** `eth_syncing`
distinguishes "catching up" from "not catching up"; a node that is keeping pace as fast as it can,
four blocks behind, reports `false`. Observed: every alert stays green while the product serves stale
reads. The correct check compares your head against an independent source and alarms on the gap, and
the leading indicator is the `newPayload` quantile — 95th or 99th percentile under 500
milliseconds.<Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" /> That degrades *before* the node falls behind.

**4 — Alerting at 95% full means the prune will fail.** Offline pruning needs at least 40 GB free, with
failures reported around 25 GB, so it cannot rescue a disk that has already filled; the recommended
trigger is about 80%.<Cite src="geth-dbpruning" /> Observed: the alert fires, the operator runs the
documented command, it fails for lack of space, and the only remaining option is a resync of a
production node. Note also that the prune itself is not a quick job — figures of four to five hours and
upwards of twelve are documented, including an hour or more of complete log silence during compaction
that must not be interrupted.<Cite src="geth-dbpruning" /> That is a maintenance window, not a
command.

**The corrected plan.** Directly attached NVMe, verified by `ioping` under load rather than by the
console's IOPS figure.<Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" /> A plain filesystem with `atime` disabled;
take upgrade safety from a second node rather than from a copy-on-write layer under the
database.<Cite src="ethereum-node-ssd-list" /><Cite src="eth-docker-resource-usage" /> Alert on head lag
against an independent source and on the `newPayload` quantile, not on `eth_syncing`. Alert on free
space at 80% and schedule the pruning window from your own measured growth rate — and check first
whether the client and version you run still needs offline pruning at all, since path-based schemes
prune inline and geth states offline pruning will be deprecated when hash-based support
ends.<Cite src="geth-dbpruning" />

## What this unlocks

You can now name the binding constraint on a node — random-read latency under a per-block deadline —
measure it three ways, and recognise its signature failure, which is a healthy-looking node that is
permanently slightly behind. You can also state the recurring cost of ownership without hand-waving:
a monitored free-space threshold, a scheduled downtime window, a re-measurement after every upgrade,
and a client-specific answer to whether any of that is still required.

That completes the module. You chose two clients knowing that the popular choice is the risky one,
chose a sync mode knowing which questions it makes permanently unanswerable, decomposed a disk into
history and state and blob data, and put a number on what keeping it alive costs in attention.

The next module spends that knowledge. It prices RPC access — per method, per provider, per compute
unit — and asks when running your own node is the cheaper answer. You now have the honest side of that
comparison, which almost no build-versus-buy spreadsheet includes: not the price of the machine, but
the pruning window, the resync after a corrupted database, the upgrade before a fork, and the fact that
the retention decision you made on day one cannot be revised without doing all of it
again.<Cite src="erigon-pruning-modes" />
