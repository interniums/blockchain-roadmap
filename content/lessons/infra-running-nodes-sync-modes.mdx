Three weeks after your node reached head, a product requirement arrives. The monthly statement should
show each user their balance at the first block of the previous month, not just today's.

You try it. `eth_getBalance` with a block tag from four weeks ago comes back as a failure about a
missing piece of the state database rather than as anything resembling "we do not keep that". You check
the flags. You did not do anything wrong. You chose the default sync mode, which is the right default,
and the default made this question permanently unanswerable on this node.

Not slow. Not expensive. **Unanswerable**, and not fixable in place: Erigon's documentation says that
to change what a node retains "you must first delete the `/chaindata` folder in the chosen `--datadir`
directory and re-sync from scratch",<Cite src="erigon-pruning-modes" /> and Nethermind warns "Do not
enable snap sync on a previously synced node. Only do so when syncing to the network for the first
time."<Cite src="nethermind-sync" />

Sync mode is the most consequential single decision in this module because it is made once, made
early, and made before anyone has told you what the product will need.

## Four questions, not one axis

Sync modes are usually drawn as a line from cheap to expensive. That framing loses the thing you
actually need. Ask four separate questions instead, because the modes score differently on each and
the differences are not correlated in the way "cheap to expensive" implies.

1. **What did this node verify for itself**, as opposed to accepting from somebody else?
2. **What does it retain** once it is at head?
3. **How long until it is useful?**
4. **Which questions can it answer** — and which can it never answer?

Question 4 is the one that reaches your product requirements, and question 1 is the one that
determines what your node's answers are worth. Question 3 is the one everybody optimises for.

## Snap sync: the default, and what it actually assumes

Snap sync fetches the header chain and a **state snapshot** at a recent pivot block, then executes
only the blocks after that pivot to reach head. Nethermind describes it as allowing "a node to perform
the initial synchronization and download of network state up to 10 times faster than using fast sync",
with the node downloading leaf nodes of the trie and generating the remaining nodes
locally.<Cite src="nethermind-sync" /> Hours instead of days, which is why it is the default and why it
should usually stay the default.

Now the part that is worth getting exactly right, because the popular summary of it is wrong in both
directions.

**What snap sync does not do:** it does not re-execute the blocks before the pivot. So it has not
personally checked that the state at the pivot is the correct *result* of applying every transaction
since genesis. If every client on the network shared a transition bug, a snap-synced node would inherit
its output without noticing.

**What snap sync does do, and gets no credit for:** it checks that the state it downloaded hashes to
the [state root](/c/state-root) committed in the pivot block's header. That is not a weak check. A peer
handing you fabricated account data cannot produce a set of trie nodes that hashes to a root some other
header already commits to, for the same reason nobody can produce a second preimage on demand. Snap
sync is not "trust a stranger's database". It is "trust that this header is on the canonical chain,
then verify the state against it cryptographically."

Which raises the obvious question, and it has a satisfying answer: where does the header's authority
come from? Not from the execution client, which has no opinion about canonicality. From the consensus
client — which got its own starting point from checkpoint sync, four sections down. **The whole trust
story of a freshly synced node terminates in one place, and it is not on the execution side.**

### Sync is several phases, and only one of them is a download

The word "sync" hides a pipeline, and knowing its shape is what lets you tell "slow" from "stuck".
Nethermind documents its own stages plainly: before fetching state at all, the node syncs headers in two
directions — backward from the pivot to block zero, and forward to the head of the chain for headers,
bodies and receipts — and only then begins downloading the latest state trie
nodes.<Cite src="nethermind-sync" /> That forward pass "might be very slow (5 to 50 blocks per second)",
which is why the documentation says a fresh pivot block is crucial.<Cite src="nethermind-sync" />

Two operational facts fall out of that pipeline, and both surprise people.

**Progress can exceed 100%, and it means what you fear.** If the network or file system pushes state sync
below roughly 1.5 Mbps on average, Nethermind "will start downloading some parts of the tree repeatedly",
and you may see something like `58000MB / 53000MB (100%)` — meaning it downloaded around 5 GB of data no
longer needed.<Cite src="nethermind-sync" /> The reason is worth stating because it is the single most
useful mental model in this module: **the chain does not wait for you.** State you fetched at the start of
the pass has already been overwritten by the time you finish it, so you fetch it again. A slow node is
not late by a fixed amount. It is fighting a moving target, and past a threshold it stops converging at
all — which is why the documentation says a sync extended beyond two days will likely never catch
up.<Cite src="nethermind-sync" />

**Restarting costs more than the time you were running.** A single restart during fast sync may extend
total sync time by up to two hours, because the client has to rebuild caches by reading millions of values
back out of the database.<Cite src="nethermind-sync" /> "Just restart it" is the reflex from every other
service you have operated, and here it is often the most expensive thing you can do.

Both facts point at the same underlying resource, and the last lesson in this module is about it.

## Full sync, and the word that misleads everyone

A full node in the strict sense executes **every block from genesis**. That is the difference, and it
is entirely a difference in question 1: the node verified every state transition itself rather than
accepting a snapshot. It takes days to weeks and the payoff is epistemic, not functional.

Here is what it does not buy, and this is the single most reliably misunderstood term in node
operation. Once synced, a full node retains all blocks and receipts but only a **short window of
recent state**. Ethereum.org puts the classic figure in an example: an execution client configured as a
full node "only caches the past few states, e.g., the state associated with the last 128 blocks, so it
can handle chain reorgs and provide fast access to recent data."<Cite src="eth-archive-nodes" />

So "full" describes the verification, not the retention. A full node holds every transaction that ever
produced the balance you are asking about and cannot tell you the balance.

### The retention window is being specified, and the number is not 128

The 128 figure is folklore that ethereum.org offers as an example, and in 2026 it is being replaced by
something both larger and more precisely defined.

EIP-8252 defines `REORG_RETENTION_WINDOW = 262_144` blocks — 8192 epochs times 32 slots, roughly 36.4
days — and requires execution clients to keep recent state **reconstructible** (via snapshots, reverse
state diffs, or any equivalent representation) for every canonical block in that window, so that any
reorg up to the inactivity-leak-bounded non-finality window can be processed
locally.<Cite src="eip-8252" /> The reasoning connects straight to material you already have: the
[inactivity leak](/c/inactivity-leak) bounds how long finality can stall, so it also bounds how deep a
reorg can be, so it sets how much state a node must be able to get back to without asking anyone.

Two cautions before you write it down. **EIP-8252 is Draft and Informational**<Cite src="eip-8252" /> —
it is not in a named fork, and it documents a requirement rather than changing consensus. And
"reconstructible" is doing real work in that sentence: a client that can rebuild a 30-day-old state by
walking reverse diffs satisfies it, while being nothing like an archive node for query latency.

The direction of travel is nonetheless visible in shipped software. Erigon's default `full` mode retains
state within that same window, and its four modes — `archive`, `full`, `blocks`, `minimal` — differ
precisely in this dimension: `archive` keeps all historical state and all blocks, `blocks` keeps all
block history plus state in the window, and `minimal` keeps state and blocks for roughly the last
100,000 blocks.<Cite src="erigon-pruning-modes" />

The lesson to carry is not the constant. It is that **"recent state" is a client-and-version-specific
window with a lower bound now being written down**, and that the correct way to find yours is to read
your client's current documentation rather than to repeat a number from a tutorial.

## Archive: one difference, and it is not the one people name

An archive node retains historical state — the intermediate values and trie nodes for every past block —
so that `eth_call`, `eth_getBalance` and `eth_getStorageAt` work at an arbitrary historical block tag
rather than only near head.<Cite src="eth-archive-nodes" /><Cite src="quicknode-full-vs-archive" />

That retention is the entire difference. Everything else — the blocks, the receipts, the peers, the
JSON-RPC surface — is the same as a full node. Ethereum.org is explicit that the network does not depend
on archive nodes for historical *data*, because "all historical interim states can be derived on a full
node" and the transactions themselves are stored by any full node and can be replayed to build the whole
archive.<Cite src="eth-archive-nodes" /> An archive node is a node that has done that derivation in
advance and kept the results.

Nethermind describes archive sync as "the heaviest and slowest sync mode" but the one that "can answer
questions like 'What was my account balance 2 years ago?'", with the largest database of any
mode.<Cite src="nethermind-sync" /> Sync takes several days depending on hardware and
connection.<Cite src="nethermind-sync" />

One widely repeated claim about archive nodes deserves to be retired here, because it is now
client-specific. "Archive means re-executing from genesis" was true and is no longer universally true:
Erigon 3's own release notes lead with "Initial sync doesn't re-exec from 0: downloading 99% LatestState
and History."<Cite src="erigon-readme" /> Whether an archive node must replay the chain, and how long
that takes, is a property of the client you picked in the previous lesson — not of the mode.

<Aside kind="note">
Archive is also not a binary. Geth's hardware page notes that "partial archive nodes can also be created
by turning off the garbage collector after some initial sync — the storage requirement depends how much
state is saved."<Cite src="geth-hardware" /> If your requirement is "the last eighteen months", a full
archive from genesis is buying you fifteen years you will never query.
</Aside>

Read the third and fourth columns together. Full sync costs an order of magnitude more time than snap and
changes nothing in the last column. Archive costs more again and is the only row that changes it. If your
reason for choosing full over snap is "so we have the history", you have picked the one upgrade that does
not deliver it.

One more dial that does not appear in any of those rows: **how far back bodies and receipts are fetched at
all** is separately configurable. Nethermind exposes `Sync.AncientBodiesBarrier` and
`Sync.AncientReceiptsBarrier` alongside the pivot settings,<Cite src="nethermind-sync" /> and those
knobs — not the sync mode — are what decide whether an old `eth_getLogs` range has anything to read. The
next lesson is about why they exist.

## Checkpoint sync: the one on the other side of the seam

Everything above is an execution-client decision. The consensus client has its own, and it is the one
that turns a multi-day bring-up into a coffee break.

Rather than replaying the beacon chain from genesis, a consensus client normally starts from a recent
finalised checkpoint state fetched over HTTP — from a public endpoint or from a node you already
trust.<Cite src="eth-run-a-node" /> Prysm describes it as a feature that "significantly speeds up the
initial sync between your beacon node and the Beacon Chain",<Cite src="prysm-checkpoint-sync" /> and
Lighthouse puts the checkpoint-loading phase at "usually less than a
minute".<Cite src="lighthouse-checkpoint-sync" />

The instinct is that this is a security shortcut. It is worth correcting sharply, because both major
client teams say the opposite.

Lighthouse's documentation answers the "is this less secure" question with: "No, in fact it is more
secure! Checkpoint sync guards against long-range attacks that genesis sync does
not."<Cite src="lighthouse-checkpoint-sync" /> Prysm says the same thing in different words: "It's
actually considered more secure thanks to the protections against long-range attacks afforded by Weak
Subjectivity."<Cite src="prysm-checkpoint-sync" />

<Misconception
  belief="Checkpoint sync trades security for speed, so a serious operator syncs from genesis."
  reality="Syncing from genesis is the configuration with the long-range attack surface. Starting from a recent finalised state removes it, at the cost of one bounded, one-time, verifiable assumption about which state you were handed."
  why="Every other 'sync from a snapshot instead of replaying' choice you have made really was a trust-for-speed trade. Weak subjectivity inverts it: in proof of stake, the distant past is the part you cannot verify from first principles."
  src="lighthouse-checkpoint-sync"
/>

The assumption you *do* take is precise and small: that the state you were handed is on the canonical
chain. And both clients tell you how to discharge it in about a minute.

- Lighthouse: "You should cross-reference the `block_root` and `slot` of the loaded checkpoint against a
  trusted source like a friend's node, a block explorer or some public
  endpoints."<Cite src="lighthouse-checkpoint-sync" />
- Prysm: query your own node at `/eth/v1/beacon/headers/finalized`, take the slot and state root, and
  confirm they match on independent explorers.<Cite src="prysm-checkpoint-sync" />

The community-maintained list of public endpoints carries its own warning, and it is the right one to
internalise: "This list should not be treated as a single source of truth for these endpoints, or the
data they provide."<Cite src="eth-clients-checkpoint-sync-endpoints" /> Fetch from one, verify against
two others, and note that the assumption expires the moment you are following head yourself.

## Working the actual decision

Take the requirement from the opening and drive it to a choice.

**Subgoal: split the requirement into "now" questions and "then" questions.** Current balances are a
"now" question and every sync mode answers them. The monthly figure is a "then" question. Only "then"
questions have any bearing on this decision, and most product specs bury exactly one of them in a
sentence about reporting.

**Subgoal: bound the "then" horizon.** One month back, once a month. That is a horizon of roughly 216,000
blocks — inside the reorg-retention window EIP-8252 describes<Cite src="eip-8252" /> and inside Erigon's
default `full` retention,<Cite src="erigon-pruning-modes" /> and outside the 128-block cache the older
documentation describes.<Cite src="eth-archive-nodes" /> So the answer is genuinely
client-and-configuration-dependent rather than obvious, which is the honest finding: **check your
client's actual retention before concluding you need an archive node.**

**Subgoal: ask whether the question can be pre-answered.** This is the move most teams miss. You know a
month in advance which block will be the first block of next month. A "then" question whose subject is
predictable is not a historical-state problem — it is a *recording* problem. Read the balances when they
are current, write them to your own database, and the archive requirement evaporates. An archive node is
frequently the price of not having decided in advance what you would want to know.

**Subgoal: price the fallback for the questions you cannot pre-answer.** For genuinely unpredictable
historical queries — a support ticket about a balance on an arbitrary past date — the answer is not to
run an archive node for one query a week. It is to send that one query to a provider that runs one, and
to accept the latency and the per-call cost. Your node stays cheap and the rare question stays possible.

**Subgoal: re-run the whole thing at five years.** Now pre-answering does not help, because you did not
record anything five years ago and cannot go back. But notice that "run an archive node" still does not
follow. Five years of monthly balances for every user is a **dataset**, not a query pattern: you would
build it once by replaying, store it, and serve it from your own database forever. The node was the wrong
tool the moment the answer stopped being a single lookup. That is the reflection prompt for this module,
and it is worth writing your version down before you read anyone else's.

<Answer>
**Four defects, and only the last one is cosmetic.**

**1 — "Full sync means we keep everything, so historical `eth_call` will work" is false.** A full node
retains all blocks and receipts and only a recent window of state — ethereum.org's example is the state
associated with the last 128 blocks.<Cite src="eth-archive-nodes" /> Consequence: the node syncs
successfully over days or weeks, reports healthy, and the first historical query fails. The team has paid
the full-sync cost and received none of the thing they were paying for, because full sync buys
*verification*, not *retention*. Note the two halves of the sentence are separately wrong: "full sync"
and "keeps everything" are unrelated claims that happen to share a word.

**2 — Skipping checkpoint sync is not the paranoid-but-correct option; it is the one with the extra attack
surface.** Lighthouse states that checkpoint sync "guards against long-range attacks that genesis sync does
not",<Cite src="lighthouse-checkpoint-sync" /> and Prysm attributes the same protection to weak
subjectivity.<Cite src="prysm-checkpoint-sync" /> Consequence: days of avoidable sync time spent buying a
*worse* security property, plus a longer window during which the node is not usable at all. The correct
paranoid move is to checkpoint sync and then verify — cross-reference the `block_root` and `slot` against
a friend's node, an explorer and other public endpoints.<Cite src="lighthouse-checkpoint-sync" />

**3 — "Switch the flag and let it backfill" does not exist.** Erigon requires deleting `/chaindata` and
re-syncing from scratch to change node type,<Cite src="erigon-pruning-modes" /> and Nethermind warns
against enabling snap sync on an already-synced node.<Cite src="nethermind-sync" /> Consequence: the day the
archive requirement is confirmed, the plan is a multi-day resync of a machine that is currently serving
production traffic — a resource and downtime problem discovered at the worst possible moment, which is
exactly the problem the design document was supposed to prevent. Retention is chosen at sync time.

**4 — `eth_syncing` returning `false` proves less than they think.** It distinguishes "still catching up"
from "not catching up", and the previous lessons established that it reports `false` for a node that has
finished syncing *and* for a node that has quietly stopped being driven over the Engine API. Consequence:
this check passes for a node that has been frozen for an hour. The real check compares your head to an
independent one and alarms on the gap.

**The correction that makes the plan coherent.** Ask question 4 first — which historical questions must this
node answer, and how far back — then pick the mode. Here, "analytics" almost certainly means an archive node
or, better, an indexed dataset that was built once and is served from a database. Full sync from genesis is
the mode you choose when you want to verify the chain yourself, which is a legitimate goal and is not the
goal stated anywhere in this document.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

You can now name what a node retains, why, and which of your product's questions that makes impossible.
Two threads run straight out of here.

**The archive number is next, and it is stranger than it looks.** If a full node holds every transaction in
under 400 GB and an archive node holds the same transactions in terabytes, the difference is not the
history — it is the state, and the size of that premium depends almost entirely on how your client lays
state out on disk. The next lesson makes that arithmetic concrete and then explains why the published
figures disagree by a factor of six.

**And "the node is allowed to forget" is now a live rule rather than a proposal.** Since 2025 execution
clients may drop pre-Merge block bodies and receipts entirely while still serving every header. That
changes what you may assume about fetching old data from an arbitrary peer, and it does nothing whatsoever
to the archive premium — a distinction the next lesson exists to make sharp.
