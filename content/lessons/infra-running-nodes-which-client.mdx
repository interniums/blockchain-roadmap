Two install pages, side by side. One has more guides, more Docker examples, more answered questions,
more people who have already hit your exact error. The other has a smaller book and a quieter forum.

Every instinct an engineer has says pick the well-trodden one. That instinct is correct for almost
every dependency you have ever chosen, and it is inverted here — not slightly, and not as a matter of
ideology. The popularity of a client is a term in the network's safety condition, and being on the
popular side of it is the risky position.

This lesson is short because the argument is short. It is here because the argument is also easy to
nod at and then not act on.

## The thresholds, and what happens at each

You already know that Ethereum's finalisation rule is a two-thirds-of-stake supermajority, and that a
protocol which refuses to finalise without one is choosing safety over liveness when it cannot tell a
partition from a crash. Client share turns that abstract choice into a concrete failure budget,
because a consensus bug in one implementation partitions the network *along implementation lines*
without any network partition at all.

Ethereum.org states the two thresholds directly.

**Above roughly a third.** "A bug in a consensus client with over 33% of the Ethereum nodes could
prevent the consensus layer from finalizing, meaning users could not trust that transactions would not
be reverted or changed at some point."<Cite src="ethereum-org-client-diversity" /> The honest remainder
cannot reach two thirds, so nothing finalises. This is a liveness failure with safety intact — the
same shape as a chain that stalls rather than guesses, and recoverable in the same way: the chain keeps
producing blocks, nothing conflicting is finalised, and when the bug is fixed and nodes are patched,
finality resumes and the backlog clears.

**Above two thirds.** "A critical bug in a client with a two-thirds majority could cause the chain to
incorrectly split and finalize, leading to a large set of validators getting stuck on an invalid
chain."<Cite src="ethereum-org-client-diversity" /> Nothing heals here. Two histories are finalised
under their own rules and there is no protocol mechanism that reconciles them, because both sides
followed the rule they were running.

The second case has a consequence that is worth stating plainly, because it is the part that makes
this feel unfair. The validators stuck on the invalid chain did nothing wrong in any ordinary sense —
they ran conforming, widely recommended software. Ethereum.org's description of what happens to them
does not soften it: "these validators face slashing or a slow and expensive voluntary withdrawal and
reactivation. The magnitude of a slashing scales with the number of culpable nodes with a two-thirds
majority slashed maximally (32 ETH)."<Cite src="ethereum-org-client-diversity" />

Read the last clause again. **The penalty scales with how many others made the same choice.** A
correlation-priced punishment is precisely the mechanism that makes "everyone runs the popular one"
expensive, and it is the protocol saying, in the only language it has, that homogeneity is the thing
it is worried about.

<Aside kind="note">
The two thresholds apply **separately** to the execution layer and the consensus layer. A consensus bug
in a supermajority execution client produces a chain the supermajority accepts and the minority rejects,
just as surely as one in a supermajority consensus client. So the choice is two independent draws, and
a pair made of the most popular client on both sides is doubling down rather than hedging.<Cite src="eth-run-a-node" />
The normative artefacts are correspondingly split: the execution APIs and Engine API on one
side,<Cite src="execution-apis-spec" /> and `ethereum/consensus-specs` on the
other.<Cite src="consensus-specs" />
</Aside>

## Why nobody can tell you the current shares

You will want to look up which clients are in a dangerous position before choosing. You should. But
you will find that the published answers disagree with each other far more than you expect, and the
reason is methodological rather than sloppy.

There are two ways to measure client share, and they answer different questions.

**Crawl the network and count nodes.** A crawler connects to peers, reads whatever identifies them, and
reports a distribution over reachable nodes. One node, one vote. This is the number that is
straightforward to gather.

**Infer the client from behaviour, and weight by stake.** Consensus clients differ in observable timing
and packaging of attestations, so a client can be fingerprinted from what it does rather than from what
it says, and each validator's contribution weighted by the stake it controls.

The protocol's thresholds are denominated in **stake**, not in nodes. So the second methodology answers
the question the safety condition actually asks, and the first answers an easier one that is also worth
knowing — network-level censorship resistance and data availability depend on node counts, not on stake.
Neither is wrong. They are not measuring the same thing, and there is no exchange rate between them: one
staking operation running a thousand validators behind one node is one vote in the first and an enormous
weight in the second.

The gap this produces is not a rounding error. As read in August 2026, clientdiversity.org published two
datasets **on the same page** — a node crawl and a stake-weighted fingerprint — that did not agree on
which consensus client was the largest, and that placed the crawl's clear leader well down the
stake-weighted list.<Cite src="clientdiversity-org" /> The execution-layer picture had the same problem
from a different direction: the widely cited crawler and an independently conducted manual survey
disagreed by tens of percentage points on the leading client's share. No source resolved it.

**What to do with that.** Not nothing. Two things survive the disagreement and are enough to act on.
First, both methodologies agree there *is* a concentration problem on both layers — the argument is
about magnitude, not existence. Second, the operational rule does not need a number: if the client you
are about to install is the one everyone recommends by default, it is the one whose share you are
increasing. Check the dashboard for the ordering, on the day, from the page itself; ethereum.org points
at ethernodes.org and clientdiversity.org for exactly this.<Cite src="ethereum-org-client-diversity" />
Do not carry a percentage in your head, and be sceptical of any curriculum, blog post or slide that
does — including ones written recently.

## What it costs you, honestly

Running a minority client is not free, and pretending otherwise is how the advice gets ignored.

The real costs are: a smaller documentation set, fewer people who have already hit your error, flag and
log formats you have to learn from scratch, and — this one is rarely admitted — **bugs that affect you
are found later**, because fewer operators are exercising the same paths. Your individual reliability
may go down a little while the network's goes up. That is a genuine trade, not a rhetorical one.

What it does *not* cost you is anything structural, and that is the point of the previous lesson. The
Engine API is the only contract between the halves,<Cite src="engine-api-spec" /> and the `eth_`
namespace is specified rather than client-defined,<Cite src="execution-apis-spec" /> so swapping either
half is a configuration change plus a resync of that half. Your application code does not know which
client answered. The exception is the one flagged in the previous lesson: if your tooling depends on a
tracing namespace, that dependency is on a client and it moves with you.

<Aside kind="note">
There is a cheaper version of this decision available to almost everyone, and most teams miss it.
**You do not have to run a node to diversify.** If your product reads through providers, ask which
clients sit behind them and arrange that your primary and your fallback are not the same
implementation. A fallback that fails over from one endpoint to another endpoint running identical
software protects you against the machine and not against the bug — and the bug is the failure mode
that takes both endpoints at the same instant.
</Aside>

## The argument that applies even if you never stake

Everything above is framed around validators, which invites a product engineer to file it under
"somebody else's risk". Here is the version that is yours.

Suppose a supermajority execution client has a consensus bug and computes a state root the minority
clients reject. If your node runs the supermajority client, it computes the same wrong state, serves it
over `eth_call` and `eth_getLogs` without hesitation, and your application acts on it. Nothing in your
stack disagrees with anything else in your stack. You find out when the network does.

If your node runs a minority client, or if you read one query through two implementations, the two
answers diverge — and divergence is a signal you can alarm on. You do not need to know which one is
right. You need to know that they stopped agreeing, because that is the moment to stop taking
irreversible actions.

That reframes client diversity from a civic duty into an **error-detecting code**. You are not being
asked to sacrifice for the network. You are being offered a second independent implementation of the
thing your product's correctness rests on, and the only way to collect on it is to not be running the
same one as everyone else.

<Check type="explain" concept="client-diversity-risk">
A team runs one execution client and one consensus client, both the most popular in their layer, on a
single machine. They add a second machine, identically configured, behind a load balancer, and describe
the result to their board as "no single point of failure".

Name what this protects against and what it does not, and describe the cheapest change that would buy
them the thing they think they bought.

<Answer>
**What it protects against:** hardware failure, a full disk, a bad kernel upgrade, a network outage in
one rack, and the operational mistakes that hit one host. These are real and they are the majority of
incidents by count.

**What it does not protect against:** every failure that is a property of the software rather than the
machine. A consensus bug in either client computes the same wrong answer on both hosts at the same
instant, because both hosts are running the same program against the same input. So does a bug that
makes the client return a subtly wrong `eth_getLogs` result, or one that makes it accept a payload the
rest of the network rejects. The load balancer will report both backends healthy throughout, because
they agree — and they agree because they are identical.

Worse, the failover logic actively hides it: a balancer's job is to make two endpoints look like one, so
the one signal that would have caught the bug (the two disagreeing) is the exact signal the architecture
is designed to erase.

**The cheapest change:** make the second machine a different implementation — at minimum a different
execution client, since that is the half serving `eth_` traffic to the application. Then stop using the
pair only as failover and start using the disagreement. Send a sample of read traffic to both, compare
the results for a small set of queries whose answers must match, and alarm when they diverge.

**The point to carry.** Redundancy defends against *independent* failures. Two copies of the same
software fail identically by construction, so duplicating it buys availability and no correctness. A
second implementation is the only redundancy that is independent in the dimension that matters here —
and the network-level thresholds<Cite src="ethereum-org-client-diversity" /> are the same argument
scaled up to everyone at once.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

You have now made two choices — which execution client, which consensus client — and each of them fixed
some properties of your node that you cannot change later without a resync.

The next lesson is about the third such choice, and it is the one with the sharpest edge. Sync mode
determines not just how long you wait and how much disk you buy, but which questions your node will be
able to answer for the rest of its life. It is chosen once, it is not adjustable afterwards, and the
question it most commonly makes unanswerable is one that product requirements ask for constantly.
