Fetch `https://docs.tenderly.co/virtual-testnets` today<Cite src="tenderly-virtual-testnets-docs" /> and you get a `200`. Look at where you
landed and it is `https://docs.tenderly.co/virtual-environments/overview`, a page about a product
called Virtual Environments, whose overview states that they are available on the paid plan and that
you should contact sales to enable them.<Cite src="tenderly-virtual-environments" /><Cite src="measured-simulation-2026-08-26" />
Meanwhile the GitHub Action the docs point you at for CI is still listed on the marketplace as
**Tenderly Virtual TestNet Setup**, version 1.0.17.<Cite src="tenderly-vnet-github-action" /><Cite src="measured-simulation-2026-08-26" />

Two names for one thing, one of them in every tutorial written before the rename. This lesson is
partly about which rehearsal environment to choose, and it opens here because the first skill the
choice demands is reading what a page actually says rather than what its URL implies.

You already know how to fork. This is not that lesson. This is the lesson about the four places a
rehearsal can live, what each one adds, and the single axis that actually separates them — which is
not realism, and not price.

## The axis is reach, not realism

All four rungs execute the same EVM against the same mainnet state. On fidelity of execution they are
close to indistinguishable. What separates them is **how far the state you created can travel, and
how long it lives.**

**`eth_simulateV1` — the state lives in one HTTP response.** Nothing persists. There is no address
you can hand to anyone. If you did not save the payload and the result, it is gone. Reach: your
process, for the duration of one call.

**A Foundry fork test — the state lives inside one test function.** Every test starts from the fork's
state and its writes are discarded at the end; that isolation is the property that makes a suite
meaningful. Reach: the assertions in that function.

**A local forked node — the state lives on your machine, on a port.** It persists across commands, it
accepts transactions from anyone who can reach the port, and you can point `cast`, a script, or a
local frontend at it. Reach: localhost, until you kill it.

**A hosted forked network — the state lives at a URL on the internet.** A frontend deployed to a
preview environment, a backend running in staging, a keeper bot on a server, a multisig interface and
four colleagues can all point at the same chain at the same time. Reach: everyone you give the URL
to.

That progression is the whole decision. **Ask who has to see the result, and the rung picks itself.**
A question about one call: rung one. A regression you want to keep forever: rung two. An afternoon of
poking at a migration with `cast`: rung three. A rehearsal that involves people who do not run
Foundry: rung four.

## Rung two: what a test adds that a request cannot

The fork-testing mechanics are behind you — [why you fork at all](/c/fork-testing-purpose),
[the two ways to enter a fork](/c/fork-mode-cli),
[per-test state isolation](/c/fork-state-isolation), and
[why the pinned block is the cache key](/c/fork-cache-keying). What changes when the target is
production rather than your own contracts is not the mechanics; it is what the run is *for*.

A test asks "does my code behave". A rehearsal asks "does this exact action, against this exact
state, produce the outcome I am about to promise". Three practical differences follow.

**The calldata is not synthesised, it is the calldata you will send.** A test constructs arguments to
exercise a branch. A rehearsal takes the bytes that are going into the transaction and executes those
bytes. Any encoding step between "the rehearsal passed" and "the transaction was sent" is an
unverified step, and lesson six is largely about eliminating it.

**The privileged accounts are impersonated, not owned.** The fork-testing guide's own examples are
this shape: `vm.prank(whale)` followed by a real transfer, or `vm.startPrank` around a sequence of
calls.<Cite src="foundry-fork-testing" /> Every impersonation is a substitution, and every
substitution belongs in the write-up next to the result.

**Time is moved, not waited.** `vm.warp` sets `block.timestamp` and `vm.roll` sets `block.number`,
which is how a vesting cliff or a timelock delay becomes a line in a test rather than a week of
waiting.<Cite src="foundry-fork-testing" /> You know these as
[warp and roll](/c/warp-and-roll); the thing to notice here is that they are what makes rehearsing a
slow governance process *routine* instead of an event.

**Balances are dealt, not acquired.** `deal()` sets a token balance directly, and takes an `adjust`
parameter for tokens where the total supply must move with it.<Cite src="foundry-fork-testing" />
[The cheatcode](/c/deal-cheatcode) is one line where acquiring the token honestly would be a swap,
a bridge, or an email.

Two operational details from the current guide that matter more in a rehearsal than in a test suite.
Fork data is cached at `~/.foundry/cache/rpc/<chain>/<block>/`, which is what makes a repeated
pinned run cheap.<Cite src="foundry-fork-testing" /> And when the provider rate-limits you,
`--fork-retry-backoff <ms>` sets the initial retry delay.<Cite src="foundry-fork-testing" /> A
rehearsal at head, hitting an unfamiliar protocol's storage for the first time, is exactly the
workload that trips a rate limiter.

## Rung three: a whole chain you control, on one machine

A local forked node turns the fork into a *place* rather than an *invocation*. Anvil describes itself
as a fast local Ethereum node that runs entirely in memory and supports forking any EVM-compatible
chain, with forking at a specific block, mining control, state dump and load, custom RPC methods for
impersonation and time manipulation, and tracing.<Cite src="foundry-anvil-overview" />

Four flags on anvil 1.7.1 change what is possible, as opposed to what is
convenient:<Cite src="measured-simulation-2026-08-26" />

**`--fork-block-number`** — fetch state from a specific block over the remote endpoint. Everything
about reproducibility and cache reuse rests on this being set.

**`--auto-impersonate`** — documented as enabling automatic impersonation on startup, so any
transaction sender can be simulated as a different account. Aliased `--auto-unlock`. This is the flag
that turns the node into a rehearsal rig, and it is also the flag that makes the node
catastrophically unsafe to expose to anything you do not control.

**`--dump-state` / `--load-state`** — dump the chain state and block environment on exit to a file
(or `<dir>/state.json` if given a directory), and initialise a later run from it. `--state` is an
alias for both. This is how a rehearsal becomes a *fixture*: set up the world once, save it, and
start every subsequent run from the interesting moment instead of rebuilding it.

**`--compute-units-per-second`** (default 330) and **`--no-rate-limit`** — anvil throttles itself
against your provider on the assumption that you are metered, and both knobs point at the same
compute-unit documentation. If your fork start is mysteriously slow against a private node you own,
this is the reason.

There is also a genuine offline mode: `--fork-chain-id` skips fetching the chain id from the remote
endpoint, but you still must pass both `--fork-url` and `--fork-block-number` and already have the
required state cached, and anything missing locally is still fetched.<Cite src="measured-simulation-2026-08-26" />
Worth knowing precisely, because "we cached it, so CI can run offline" is a claim people make and the
flag's own help text refuses.

### The cheat methods, measured

The interesting half is what you can do to a running fork over JSON-RPC. Measured on anvil 1.7.1,
forked at block 25,839,000:<Cite src="measured-simulation-2026-08-26" />

```text
anvil_impersonateAccount 0x807a…95d2      → null      (succeeded)
anvil_setBalance         0x807a…95d2 1e18 → null
anvil_setStorageAt       USDC slot 1 = 0xdead → true
evm_increaseTime         172800           → 170461
evm_mine                                  → "0x0"
evm_snapshot                              → "0x0"
evm_revert               0x0              → true
```

Two observations from that run are worth carrying.

**Snapshot and revert restore storage exactly.** After `anvil_setStorageAt` wrote `0xdead` into
USDC's slot 1, `evm_revert` put back `0x4914f61d25e5c567143774b76edbf4d5109a8566` — the real
value.<Cite src="measured-simulation-2026-08-26" /> That makes "try the destructive thing, then undo
it" a first-class workflow on a shared local fork, which is precisely what a rehearsal needs.

**Time does not move by exactly what you asked.** The block timestamp before the jump was
1,787,742,758; after `evm_increaseTime(172800)` and `evm_mine` it was 1,787,915,795 — a move of
**173,037 seconds against a requested 172,800**, and the method itself returned
170,461.<Cite src="measured-simulation-2026-08-26" /> The extra is real elapsed wall-clock since the
node started. Practical consequence: **never assert on an exact timestamp after warping a local
fork.** Assert on the property — the timelock has elapsed, the cliff has passed — not on the number.

<Aside kind="warn">
A forked node with impersonation enabled will execute any transaction from any address, including
the protocol admin and including yours. It has no authentication. Binding it to anything other than
loopback, or forwarding its port, hands anyone who finds it the ability to write arbitrary state into
an environment your team is treating as the truth. Rehearsal environments get trusted precisely
because they look real, which is what makes tampering with one worth an attacker's time.
</Aside>

## Rung four: a fork with a URL the whole team can use

The hosted option exists for one reason, and it is the reach axis. A Virtual Environment is described
as a private blockchain environment that forks a live network and behaves like a mainnet you control,
reached through the same JSON-RPC your tools already speak, plus an enriched Admin RPC for
cheatcodes, plus a visual dashboard with a block explorer.<Cite src="tenderly-virtual-environments" />
The documented use cases are the ones a local fork cannot serve: a local-node replacement for a whole
dapp including UI, backend and indexing layer; a fresh environment provisioned per pull request and
torn down after; staging and demo environments for manual QA, community testing, audits and sales
demos; and cross-chain testing with multiple networks attached to one
environment.<Cite src="tenderly-virtual-environments" />

Four details worth knowing before you build a workflow on it.

**The sync mode is chosen once and cannot be changed.** Three modes are documented: *Static*
(default), which stays at the forked block with no live updates; *State Only Sync*, where storage
slots keep tracking live parent-network values until your environment writes to them; and *Network
Mirror*, a full block-by-block replay of the parent network with a visible mempool and time controls.
The docs state that the mode is fixed at creation and cannot be changed afterwards from the dashboard
or the API — to use a different one, create a new environment.<Cite src="tenderly-virtual-environments" />
That is a real design decision, not a setting: State Only Sync gives you a rehearsal whose oracle
prices keep moving until you touch them, which is either exactly what you want or a source of
irreproducibility, depending on what you are rehearsing.

**There are two RPC endpoints and they are not interchangeable.** A Public RPC serving standard
methods, for the dapp UI, the backend and external testers; and an Admin RPC that adds cheatcodes for
setting balances, overriding storage, fast-forwarding time and deploying arbitrary bytecode, with the
full set documented in an Admin RPC reference.<Cite src="tenderly-admin-rpc" /> The
documentation is explicit that when you share the environment or expose it through a UI you hand out
the Public RPC, not the Admin RPC, because the Admin RPC lets the caller mutate balances, storage,
time and other network state.<Cite src="tenderly-virtual-environments" /> This is the same hazard as
an exposed `--auto-impersonate` node, with the difference that here it is one URL substitution away
and the URLs look similar.

**It is a paid product.** The overview states that Virtual Environments are available on the paid
plan and directs you to sales to enable them.<Cite src="tenderly-virtual-environments" /> Any
material that presents this as the default way to simulate is describing a purchase decision as a
technical one.

**Snapshots, forking and a faucet come with it.** Reverting state via snapshots, branching an
existing environment into a new one at its current state, and topping up native or ERC-20 balances on
any account are all documented as first-class operations,<Cite src="tenderly-virtual-environments" />
as is a REST API for creating, forking and deleting environments from scripts and CI
pipelines.<Cite src="tenderly-virtual-environments" />

What you are buying, stated without enthusiasm in either direction: **a network-reachable endpoint,
persistence, a shared explorer, decoded traces — call trees with the method name, the decoded inputs
and the decoded outputs on every frame<Cite src="tenderly-simulations" /> — and an API to create and
destroy environments from CI.** What you are not buying is the ability to simulate — that is standardised now, and rungs one
through three are free.<Cite src="eth-simulatev1-spec" />

## The failure mode that only exists at the top rung

Rungs one and two are isolated by construction: a request ends, a test function ends, and the state
goes away. That isolation is why nobody ever debugs them by asking "who else was on this fork".

A shared environment removes it. What you gain in reach you pay for in **shared mutable state**, and
the resulting failures are organisational rather than technical.

**Somebody else's rehearsal invalidates yours.** A colleague warps the clock forward eleven days to
test a timelock; your keeper bot, pointed at the same chain, wakes up in a world where every deadline
has passed and dutifully liquidates the positions you spent an hour setting up. Nothing malfunctioned
and no error was raised.

**"It worked on the fork" stops being reproducible.** The chain has accumulated a month of other
people's transactions. Nobody can say which of them the result depended on, and there is no commit
to bisect. The moment a shared environment has been alive longer than the memory of what was done to
it, its results have the epistemic status of anecdote.

**Environments outlive their purpose and quietly become production.** A staging fork that a frontend
has pointed at for six months, with an address book nobody has re-derived, is a dependency. When it
is torn down or its parent block drifts far enough from mainnet to matter, things break in ways that
take a day to trace.

Three habits make the top rung behave. None of them are technical.

**One environment per question, named after the question.** Not `staging`, not `dev-fork`. `migration-rehearsal-2026-08-26`. The hosted product supports forking an existing environment into a new
one at its current state and attaching a fixed, human-readable RPC name that survives a
re-fork,<Cite src="tenderly-virtual-environments" /> which is exactly the machinery for branching per
question rather than sharing one long-lived chain.

**A stated owner and a stated expiry.** Whoever created it says when it dies. Per-pull-request
provisioning and teardown is a documented pattern precisely because it makes the expiry
automatic.<Cite src="tenderly-virtual-environments" />

**Re-derive the important claims at rung two before anyone acts on them.** If the shared environment
produced a finding you are going to send a transaction on the strength of, reproduce it as a pinned
fork test. The shared run is how the team saw it; the pinned test is how you will still be able to
show it next month, and how CI will notice when it stops being true.

The trap this table exists to prevent is picking the top rung out of ambition. A hosted environment
does not make a rehearsal more correct; it makes it more *shareable*. If the finding you need is "the
upgrade does not break live storage", rung two produces it, keeps it, and re-runs it on every commit.
If the finding you need is "the treasury signers can complete the flow in the Safe UI without
improvising", no amount of Solidity will produce it.

## Worked example: staging a migration the whole team can rehearse

The migration moves user positions from an old vault to a new one. Contracts, a frontend, a keeper
bot and a five-of-nine multisig are all involved.

**Subgoal — establish the mechanical claim first, cheaply.** Before anything is provisioned, run the
migration calldata as a fork test at a pinned recent block: impersonate the admin, execute, then run
the existing integration suite against the migrated state. This is rung two, it costs one cached
fork, and it answers the only question that can cancel the whole plan.

**Subgoal — freeze the interesting moment as a fixture.** Once the setup is non-trivial — positions
opened, oracle in the right place, time advanced past a cooldown — start a local fork, build that
state once, and `--dump-state` it.<Cite src="measured-simulation-2026-08-26" /> Everything after this
starts from the file, which is the difference between a rehearsal you can repeat and one you
reconstruct from memory each time.

**Subgoal — decide who needs an endpoint, and only then pick a rung.** The keeper bot needs a chain
that persists and mines. The frontend needs an RPC URL its preview deployment can reach. The
multisig signers need a UI that can see the environment. That third requirement is the one that
forces rung four; the first two do not, on their own.

**Subgoal — hand out the right URL.** If you go hosted, the dapp and the testers get the Public RPC.
The Admin RPC stays in your tooling.<Cite src="tenderly-virtual-environments" /> If you stay local,
nothing gets exposed beyond loopback and you accept that the multisig rehearsal happens some other
way.

**Subgoal — snapshot before every destructive attempt.** Take a snapshot, run the migration, look at
the damage, revert, fix, repeat. Measured on anvil, `evm_revert` restored an overwritten storage slot
to its real value exactly;<Cite src="measured-simulation-2026-08-26" /> hosted environments document
the same operation.<Cite src="tenderly-virtual-environments" /> A rehearsal you can only run once is
a rehearsal you will be reluctant to run honestly.

**Subgoal — write down which rung produced which claim.** "The storage layout survives" came from the
fork test. "The signers can complete it" came from the shared environment. Two different pieces of
evidence with two different scopes, and conflating them is how a team ends up believing it rehearsed
something it did not.

**Disagree on the redundancy claim, agree on the value of the hosted environment. They answer

**What the hosted environment is genuinely better at.** Reach and persistence. A frontend, a keeper
bot, a backend and a multisig UI pointing at one chain simultaneously is the documented use case, and

Foundry, this is the rung.

**Why the fork tests stay.**

*They are the regression, not the demo.* A rehearsal performed in a shared environment is evidence
about one afternoon. A pinned fork test runs on every commit and fails the day someone else's
governance changes a parameter you depend on. Losing that is losing the standing check, which is
lesson seven's entire subject.

*They are where the assertions live.* A shared environment shows you what happened; a test says what
must be true. "Every existing holder's balance is unchanged across the upgrade" is a line of Solidity
with a message attached, and it is the artifact that survives the person who ran it.

*They are free and fast.* One cached pinned fork, sub-second re-runs, no plan required — the hosted
product is documented as available on the paid plan.<Cite src="tenderly-virtual-environments" />

*They are reproducible by anyone who clones the repository.* A shared environment is a place; a

## What this unlocks

You have the ladder, and the axis that orders it: **who can reach the state, and how long it lives.**
You also have the operational grit — pinned blocks, dumped state, snapshots, the two RPC endpoints
you must not mix up, and a time cheatcode that does not move time by exactly what you asked.

Everything so far has been apparatus. The next two lessons are what it is for.

**Next**: rehearsing a proxy upgrade against live storage — where a real upgrade, executed against
the real state of a real token, succeeds, returns `status 1`, and destroys every balance in the
contract without a single revert to warn you.
