An RPC provider hands you a block. It is a JSON object with a `hash` field, a `transactions` array,
and about twenty other keys. You want to be sure the provider is not lying to you.

The obvious move is to hash the thing you were given and compare. It does not work, and the reason it
does not work is the entire subject of this lesson: **nothing in that transactions array is an input
to that `hash`.** You can hand the same `hash` a completely different transaction list and the two
values remain, on their own terms, perfectly consistent. Something else has to catch the swap.

That something is a root, and the root lives in a part of the block that most tooling never shows you
separately.

## The block has two halves and only one of them is the chain

A block is a header plus a body. The body is the transaction list, the withdrawal list, and — for
historical reasons the fifth lesson in this module is about — an ommers list that is always
empty.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-blocks" />

The header is a fixed list of scalar values and 32-byte hashes. Several of those hashes are
*commitments to the body*: the transactions root, the receipts root, the withdrawals root, the state
root, and a hash over execution-layer requests. You already know from Track 01 what a commitment is
and what
[a canonical root buys you](/c/mpt-canonical-root) — one set of key-value pairs, one root, no
implementation freedom anywhere in between.

The consequence is a clean division of labour:

- **The header participates in consensus.** It is hashed, it is signed over, it is what a peer sends
  you first, and it is what "the chain" is a chain of.
- **The body is checked against the header.** Nothing in it is trusted for being present in the
  message. It is trusted because recomputing a root over it reproduces a value the header already
  committed to.

That is why people who work on clients say "block" and "header" interchangeably and never bother to
disambiguate. Operationally they are the same object: the body is an attachment whose only job is to
reproduce numbers the header already fixed.

## The block hash is the header hash

Say it precisely, because the precise version is the one that survives contact with an
implementation.

The identifier everyone calls the block hash is `keccak256(rlp(header))`. Not of the block. Not of
the body. Of the header, RLP-encoded as an ordered list.

You can watch the specification do it. When a client validates an incoming header, one of the checks
it performs is that the header's `parent_hash` field equals `keccak256(rlp.encode(parent_header))` —
the same construction, applied to the header it already holds.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-fork" />
There is no second hashing path anywhere. If a value is not in the header, it is not in the hash,
and it can only reach the hash by being folded into one of the roots that is.

This is not a pedantic distinction. It determines what you have to do to check a block, and the
answer is: **recompute the roots.** Given a header you trust and a body you do not, you rebuild the
transactions trie from the body's transactions and compare its root against the header's; you rebuild
the withdrawals trie and compare that; you re-execute and compare the state root. The specification
performs exactly this sequence and rejects the block on the first
mismatch.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-fork" />

### Order is part of the encoding

The header is an RLP list, and
[RLP lists are positional](/c/rlp) — there are no field names on the wire, only a sequence. Two
consequences follow, and both bite in practice.

**A reordered field is a different hash.** Not a slightly wrong hash that you can detect and repair.
A completely unrelated 32 bytes. An encoder that swaps two adjacent fields produces a value that
matches nothing, and the failure surfaces as "my hash does not match" with no hint about which field
is misplaced.

**A removed field shifts everything after it.** This one is why the fifth lesson exists at all: three
fields in the modern header carry no information whatsoever and are retained purely because deleting
them would have renumbered the list.<Cite src="eip-3675" />

So the ordering is normative, and the normative statement of it lives in exactly one place: the
`Header` dataclass in `ethereum/execution-specs`, in the directory for the fork your block belongs
to. **Read it there, for your fork, and treat every other listing — including the grouping below —
as commentary.**<Cite src="execution-specs" /> On 26 August 2026 the Osaka fork's `Header` declares
21 fields.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-blocks" />

### What is in there, grouped by what it does

The grouping below is *not* the encoding order. It is a way to hold twenty-one fields in your head.
Take the order from the source file.

**One field links.** `parent_hash`, the subject of the rest of this lesson.

**Six commit to the body.** The state root, transactions root, receipt root, logs bloom, withdrawals
root, and requests hash. Lessons two, three and four take these one at a time. Note already that they
do not all work the same way: most are Merkle-Patricia roots, one is a bloom filter, and one is a
SHA-256 accumulator that is not a trie root at all.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-blocks" />

**Seven meter and schedule.** Block number, timestamp, gas limit, gas used, base fee per gas, and the
two blob-gas counters. These are the block's accounting, and the last lesson in this module shows
that they are running two independent auctions.

**Three are dead.** Difficulty, the header nonce, and the ommers hash — frozen constants whose values
a client actively *checks*, so they are not merely ignored.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-fork" />

**Four connect to something outside the execution layer.** The coinbase, the free-form extra data
field, `prev_randao` carrying the beacon chain's randomness, and `parent_beacon_block_root` carrying
a commitment to the consensus layer. Lessons five and seven.

That accounting — one, six, seven, three, four — is twenty-one. If your fork's file gives a different
count, your fork's file is right and this paragraph is stale.

## The parent hash is the only thing making this a chain

Strip everything else away. What makes a sequence of blocks a *chain* rather than a set of records
with numbers on them is one 32-byte field: each header names its predecessor by hash.

The property that buys is worth stating carefully, because it is the property every other guarantee
in this system is eventually built on. **Changing anything about an ancestor changes every descendant
hash.** Edit a transaction in block N and its transactions root changes; the changed root changes
block N's header hash; block N+1's `parent_hash` no longer matches, so block N+1's own hash must
change too; and so on to the head. There is no way to alter history at depth without producing a
different chain of hashes all the way forward. The [preimage and second-preimage
resistance](/c/second-preimage-resistance) of keccak is what makes "produce a different chain that
still hashes to the same values" not an available move.

The specification adds one more binding alongside the hash: a header is invalid unless its `number`
is exactly the parent's `number` plus one.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-fork" /> Block numbers are
therefore contiguous by construction. They never skip. Hold on to that — the sixth lesson is entirely
about a counter that *does* skip, and about how much product code confuses the two.

### What the parent hash does not do

Here is where the mental model of most people who have read one blog post about blockchains goes
wrong, and it is worth being blunt about.

**A parent hash permits branching.** Nothing in the structure says a header may have only one child.
Two proposers can each build on the same parent, producing two valid headers with the same
`parent_hash` and the same `number`. Both are structurally correct. Both hash correctly. Both chain
correctly.

**Choosing between them is a completely separate mechanism.** The fork-choice rule — on Ethereum,
[LMD-GHOST](/c/lmd-ghost) weighing attestations, which Track 01 introduced — is what makes one of
those branches canonical. It is consensus policy layered on top of a data structure that by itself
has no opinion.

So "the hashes all link up" is a much weaker statement than beginners take it for. It means *this
history is internally consistent*. It does not mean *this history is the one the network agrees on*.
An attacker with a laptop can produce a perfectly linked chain of a million blocks; what they cannot
produce is the attestation weight behind it.

  belief="If every parent hash checks out, I am on the canonical chain."
  reality="Hash linkage proves internal consistency, nothing more. Two conflicting chains can each be perfectly linked. Canonicity comes from the fork-choice rule and, above that, from finality."
  why="The word 'chain' does a lot of unearned work. It suggests there is only one, and the linkage check is the only check most tutorials ever show you performing."

<Aside kind="note">
This split is exactly why a light client is possible. Follow headers — a few hundred bytes each —
verify the linkage and the consensus signatures over them, and you have a trustworthy sequence of
roots without ever downloading a body. When you later need a fact from a body, you ask for it plus a
Merkle proof against the relevant root, and check the proof against a header you already trust. The
header stream is the trust anchor; everything else is a proof against it.<Cite src="ethereum-docs-blocks" />
</Aside>

## Bodies are bounded, but not by gas

One practical asymmetry: the header is a fixed number of fields, so its encoded size barely moves.
The body is not. Since EIP-7934 the protocol caps the RLP-encoded size of a whole block at
`MAX_BLOCK_SIZE` of 10 MiB minus a `SAFETY_MARGIN` of 2 MiB, giving an effective 8 MiB ceiling; a
block whose encoding exceeds it is invalid.<Cite src="eip-7934" /> The motivation is propagation:
oversized blocks travel badly and blocks above 10 MiB are not gossiped by the consensus layer at
all.<Cite src="eip-7934" />

Note what that means for a builder. Gas is not the only budget. A block can be well under its gas
limit and still be invalid for being too many bytes — which is a constraint on
[calldata-heavy](/c/propagation-latency) block construction that did not exist before.

## The header is not finished growing

Every field in the current header arrived in some fork, and fields are still arriving. Two additions
are already declared in the in-development Amsterdam fork directory of execution-specs, which carries
a 23-field `Header`: a block access list hash and a slot number.<Cite src="execution-specs-amsterdam-blocks" />
Those correspond to EIP-7928 and EIP-7843 respectively, both listed as scheduled for Glamsterdam in
the upgrade's meta EIP.<Cite src="eip-7928" /><Cite src="eip-7843" />

Treat that as a warning, not a forecast. The meta EIP is a Draft, the fork has no activation epoch on
mainnet, and specification directories for unshipped forks change. The durable lesson is structural:
**any code that hard-codes a header layout has a fork-shaped expiry date on it**, and the only
maintainable version reads the field list per fork.

<Check type="explain" concept="block-hash-is-header-hash">
A provider returns a block. You already trust its `hash`, because you got that hash from a source you
trust — say, the `parent_hash` of a later header you verified. You do not trust the body.

Someone on your team proposes this validation: "hash the whole JSON response and compare it against
`hash`." Say what is wrong with it, and describe what you would do instead. Then say which single
substitution in the body their check would *also* miss even if it somehow worked.

<Answer>
**Why it fails.** `hash` is `keccak256(rlp(header))` — the RLP encoding of twenty-one specific
header fields, in the fork's declared order. It is not a hash of a JSON document, not a hash of the
body, and not a hash of the two concatenated. Hashing the response yields an unrelated value that
will never equal `hash` for any block, valid or not. The check does not have false negatives; it has
no true positives.

**What to do instead — three steps, and only the first involves the header.**

1. Take the header fields out of the response, RLP-encode them in the order your fork's `Header`
   dataclass declares, keccak-256 the result, and compare against the trusted `hash`. That
   establishes the header is the header you trusted.
2. Rebuild the transactions trie from the body's transaction list — key `rlp(index)`, value the
   transaction envelope — and compare its root against the header's transactions root. That
   establishes the body's transactions are the ones this header committed to, *in this order*.
3. Do the same for the withdrawals list against the withdrawals root.

Only after step 1 is the header trustworthy; only after steps 2 and 3 is the body.

**The substitution their check would still miss.** Even a hypothetical correct hash over the whole
response would not tell you the body is *the* body — it would only tell you the response has not been
altered since it was hashed, which is a statement about the provider's own serialisation, not about
consensus. The provider can serve you a self-consistent response containing a different transaction
list all day. The only thing tying the body to the network's agreement is the root comparison, and
that comparison has to be performed by you, against a header whose hash you obtained independently.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**Rebuilding a block hash yourself** is this module's first practice, and it is deliberately
unforgiving: omit one field or swap two, and the hash is wrong with no diagnostic. That failure mode
is the lesson.

**Every root in the next three lessons** is an instance of the pattern established here. The state
root is the one that cannot be checked without re-executing. The receipts root is the one that makes
a log provable to somebody who was not there. The withdrawals root and requests hash cover balance
changes that never appear in the transactions trie at all.

**Reorgs and finality** become tractable once you have separated linkage from canonicity. A reorg is
not a broken chain; it is two correctly linked chains and a rule that picked the other one.

**Light clients, bridges and proof systems** all rest on the split this lesson made: a small,
verifiable header stream, and everything else supplied as a proof against a root inside it.

The next lesson takes the one root you cannot verify by looking, and asks why the protocol chose to
make verification that expensive.
