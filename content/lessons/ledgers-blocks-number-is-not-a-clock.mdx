A governance timelock ships with this in it:

```solidity
// 30 days at 12 seconds per block
uint256 constant DELAY_BLOCKS = 216_000;
```

It is off. Not by seconds — by hours, and by an amount that grows the longer the delay is and that
nobody can predict in advance. The arithmetic is fine. The premise is not: **blocks do not happen on
a schedule. Slots do.**

Those are two different counters, they run at different rates, and an explorer prints them side by
side in a way that makes them look interchangeable. This lesson separates them, and then uses the
separation to say something precise about how long finality actually takes.

## The heartbeat and the thing that may or may not happen

Consensus time is divided into **slots** of fixed duration. The mainnet configuration sets
`SLOT_DURATION_MS` to 12000 — twelve seconds — and the phase 0 preset sets `SLOTS_PER_EPOCH` to
32.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-preset" /> Each slot
has exactly one proposer, assigned in advance by the beacon chain's shuffling.<Cite src="ethereum-docs-pos" />

The slot happens whether or not that proposer does anything. The clock does not wait. If the assigned
proposer is offline, misconfigured, mid-restart, on the wrong side of a network problem, or has
decided a particular RANDAO outcome does not suit them, the slot passes and **no block exists for
it**. There is no placeholder, no empty block, no orphan. The slot number advances and nothing
was appended to the execution chain.

Meanwhile, on the execution side, block numbers are contiguous by rule. Header validation rejects any
header whose `number` is not exactly the parent's `number` plus one.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-fork" />
There is no mechanism by which a block number can be skipped, ever.

Put those two facts together and the whole lesson follows:

**Slot numbers skip. Block numbers do not. Therefore the gap between them grows by exactly one for
every missed slot, and never shrinks.**

The direction of the error deserves a moment. A deadline expressed in block numbers always fires
**late**, never early, and by an unbounded amount. Your thirty-day timelock is at least thirty days
and possibly thirty days plus several hours, depending entirely on how many proposers were absent
during the period. If a large staking operator has an outage during your delay window, your delay
gets longer. That is a dependency nobody signs up for on purpose.

## Three clocks, and which one to use for what

There are three counters attached to any block and they answer three different questions.

**Block number** answers *how many blocks deep*. It is the right unit for confirmation depth, for
reorg depth, for "have I waited N blocks", and for ordering. It is contiguous, monotonic, and says
nothing about time.

**Slot number** answers *what consensus period is this*. It is the unit for attestations, committees,
epochs and finality. It is not present in the execution header at all — you get it from a beacon API,
or you derive it, as the next section explains. It is the only counter that maps directly to
wall-clock intervals.

**`block.timestamp`** answers *what time is it*. And here the story is more precise than most
material admits.

### The timestamp is pinned harder than its reputation suggests

Read only the execution layer's rules and `block.timestamp` looks weak: the sole constraint header
validation imposes is that it be strictly greater than the parent's.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-fork" />
Nothing about slots, nothing about wall-clock accuracy. That rule is where the field's reputation as
a loosely bounded, proposer-influenced quantity comes from, and on a chain where it is the only rule,
that reputation is deserved.

On Ethereum mainnet after the Merge it is not the only rule. The consensus layer asserts, when
processing an execution payload:<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" />

```python
# Verify timestamp
assert payload.timestamp == compute_time_at_slot(state, state.slot)
```

Equality, not a range. A block's timestamp *is* its slot's scheduled time, computed from genesis and
the slot number. A proposer who writes anything else produces a payload the consensus layer refuses.

Two consequences worth holding on to.

**Timestamps move in exact multiples of the slot duration.** Consecutive blocks are 12 seconds apart
when no slot was missed, 24 when one was, 36 when two were. If you have ever seen a chain of
timestamps differing by exactly 12 and wondered whether that was coincidence: it is not.

**The slot number is recoverable from the timestamp** — subtract the genesis time and divide by the
slot duration. That is exactly the first of the two techniques EIP-7843's motivation section
describes for getting the slot number on-chain today, and its stated drawback is that it forces a
contract to hard-code the slot length.<Cite src="eip-7843" />

So the practical guidance is unchanged from what you have probably heard — **use timestamps, not
block numbers, for anything time-shaped** — but the reason is stronger than "timestamps are roughly
right". On this chain they are exactly the slot clock. What you are actually accepting when you use
them is a different dependency: that the slot duration stays what it is.

<Aside kind="note">
The same assertion block pins `prev_randao` to the beacon state's RANDAO mix, by
equality.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" /> Neither field is proposer-chosen in the
sense of "the proposer writes a number of their choosing". The proposer's influence over the
randomness, as the previous lesson set out, is not in what they write — it is in whether they propose
at all.
</Aside>

## Epochs, and how long finality actually takes

Thirty-two slots make an **epoch**.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-preset" /> At twelve seconds a
slot that is 384 seconds — **6.4 minutes** — and the epoch boundary is where the consensus layer does
its periodic work: rewards, penalties, committee shuffling, and the checkpoint accounting that
produces finality.

You met [Casper FFG](/c/casper-ffg) in Track 01. The part that matters here is its two-step shape.
Checkpoints sit at epoch boundaries. A checkpoint is first **justified** — a supermajority of stake
has attested linking it — and becomes **finalised** only once the *next* checkpoint above it has been
justified in turn.<Cite src="eth2book-casper-ffg" />

That two-step structure is what sets the floor. You cannot finalise a checkpoint in the same epoch it
appears, because finalising it requires a justification that has not happened yet and cannot happen
until the following epoch's boundary. So finality trails the head by roughly two epochs:

```
32 slots × 12 s = 384 s = 6.4 min per epoch
2 epochs        = 768 s = 12.8 min
```

<Aside kind="warn">
Treat 12.8 minutes as **the mechanism's floor, not the observed number.** It is what the structure
requires under ideal participation. Third-party benchmarks measuring wall-clock finality on mainnet
have reported medians noticeably higher — one such measurement put the median above fifteen minutes —
and the sources do not agree on a single figure. Do not publish one. If your product needs a number,
measure it against your own definition of "finalised" over a window you can date, and re-measure it
after every upgrade.
</Aside>

The gap between the head and the finalised checkpoint is a live quantity you can read from any node,
and Track 01's lesson on the availability–finality dilemma already told you what a *growing* gap
means. Here you have the arithmetic that says what a healthy gap looks like: a couple of epochs,
steady. The [head, justified and finalised](/c/head-vs-justified-vs-finalized) triple gets its own
treatment in the finality module.

## Slot time is twelve seconds and is not scheduled to change

This is the part of the lesson most likely to be stale in material you read elsewhere, so here is the
state of it as of 26 August 2026, verified rather than recalled.

EIP-7782 proposes halving the slot to six seconds, which would halve epoch duration and therefore
Casper FFG's finality floor.<Cite src="eip-7782" /> It is **Draft**, and the Glamsterdam meta EIP
lists it under **Declined for Inclusion**.<Cite src="eip-7773" /> The consensus-specs mainnet
configuration still carries `SLOT_DURATION_MS: 12000`, and the fork version that would follow Fusaka
has no activation epoch set — its entry is the maximum `uint64` sentinel that means
unscheduled.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config" />

So: twelve seconds, not scheduled to change, and any material telling you six-second slots are coming
is describing a proposal rather than a plan. Note also that the meta EIP itself is a Draft with no
mainnet activation row filled in,<Cite src="eip-7773" /> so its declinations are provisional too. The
honest summary is that nothing about slot timing is settled beyond what is running today.

What *is* scheduled — provisionally, in a Draft meta EIP, for an unscheduled fork — is EIP-7843, an
opcode returning the slot number directly, in Review status.<Cite src="eip-7843" /><Cite src="eip-7773" />
Its existence is the best evidence that the problem this lesson describes is real: its motivation
section says outright that the only two ways to get the slot number on-chain today are to hard-code
the slot length and derive it from the timestamp, or to prove it against the beacon block root at
significant gas cost.<Cite src="eip-7843" />

<Check type="predict" concept="slot-vs-block">
You are asked to review a contract for a token sale. Bidding opens at deployment and the contract
computes its own deadline as `block.number + 7_200`, described in a comment as "24 hours". A separate
off-chain service polls every 30 seconds and emails bidders "one hour remaining" when
`deadline - block.number < 300`.

Predict what goes wrong, quantify the direction and rough scale of the error, and rewrite both the
contract's deadline and the notifier's trigger. Then answer a follow-up: the team says "fine, we will
use `block.timestamp`, but our auditor says timestamps are proposer-manipulable — how much should we
worry?"

<Answer>
**What goes wrong.** 7,200 blocks is 24 hours only if every slot in the window produces a block.
Every missed slot pushes the deadline later in wall-clock terms without changing the block count, so
the sale runs **longer** than 24 hours, by 12 seconds per missed slot. The error is always in that
direction — a missed slot can never make blocks arrive faster.

**Scale.** Over 7,200 slots, a miss rate of one percent is 72 missed slots, which is about
fourteen and a half minutes of overrun; a bad day for a large operator can push it further. Note the
shape of the risk: it is not a fixed fudge factor you can pad for. It is a dependency on other
people's uptime during your window, which is precisely the kind of coupling you do not want between
an auction close and an unrelated infrastructure incident.

**The notifier is worse than the deadline.** `deadline - block.number < 300` is not "one hour
remaining" — it is "300 blocks remaining", which is *at least* one hour and possibly considerably
more. Bidders receive a final-hour warning and then get more than an hour, which sounds harmless
until you consider the reverse framing: your email made a commitment about wall-clock time that the
contract does not enforce.

**The rewrite.** Store an absolute timestamp:

```solidity
uint256 public immutable deadline = block.timestamp + 24 hours;
```

and have the notifier compare against wall-clock time rather than any chain counter — it is an
off-chain service with a real clock, and it should use it. Chain counters belong in the notifier only
where it needs to agree with the contract about *which* block crossed the line.

**On timestamp manipulability.** The auditor is repeating advice that is correct in general and
overstated for Ethereum mainnet today. The execution layer's own rule is weak — a timestamp need only
exceed the parent's — but the consensus layer asserts equality between the payload timestamp and the
slot's computed time, so a proposer cannot write a timestamp of their choosing and have the block
accepted. The residual influence is not over the value; it is over whether a block exists in that
slot at all, which shifts a deadline by whole slot durations, in the same direction as any missed
slot, and is bounded by how many consecutive slots the adversary is assigned.

Which gives you the real guidance: worry about timestamp precision **at the granularity of one slot,
not at the granularity of arbitrary manipulation.** A mechanism whose outcome flips on a
sub-12-second timing difference is fragile; a 24-hour auction is not. Where the sub-slot difference
does matter — a liquidation ordering, a first-mover advantage — the problem is not the timestamp, it
is that you have built a race, and the proposer wins races.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The missed-slot practice** in this module has you sample real slots and demonstrate the divergence
yourself, and report a miss rate with a date attached. That number is the input to every argument in
this lesson, and inherited numbers go stale silently.

**Confirmation policy**, in the finality module, is this lesson with a risk model bolted on: how many
blocks, or which checkpoint, before you take an irreversible action.

**Anything with a deadline** — vesting, timelocks, auctions, options, challenge windows, sequencer
inactivity timers on an L2 — inherits this problem. Rollup challenge periods in particular are
usually expressed in L1 time, and the same reasoning applies one layer up.

**The next lesson** is about the header field that lets a contract stop guessing at consensus-layer
facts and verify them instead — including, if it is willing to pay for a proof, the slot number this
lesson has been circling.
