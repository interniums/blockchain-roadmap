An L2 team builds a cost model. Gas price times gas used, sampled hourly, extrapolated to a monthly
L1 bill. It is careful work. It is also wrong by a category, and the error shows up as an
unexplainable line item: some months the model is over, some months it is badly under, and the misses
do not correlate with anything the team is tracking.

The reason is sitting in the header they are already reading. **The block meters two entirely
separate resources, prices them with two separate curves, and calls both of them gas.** One is
execution. The other is blob data. They share a name, a header, and almost nothing else.

This lesson is about the header fields that run both auctions — what a proposer actually controls,
what is computed and merely verified, and the one place where the two markets were deliberately
connected in 2025.

## The gas market: three fields, one of which is a vote

Track 01 covered [EIP-1559 as a mechanism](/c/eip-1559-tfm) — why the base fee is burned, why the
design is incentive-compatible for users, where it is not. This is the plumbing underneath it.

**`gasLimit` is a vote, not a constant.** A proposer chooses it, within a bound relative to the
parent. The specification computes `max_adjustment_delta` as the parent's gas limit divided by
`LIMIT_ADJUSTMENT_FACTOR`, which is 1024, and rejects any limit that reaches or exceeds
`parent + delta`, any limit at or below `parent - delta`, and anything below `LIMIT_MINIMUM` of
5000.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-fork" /><Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-gas" />

So the ceiling moves by under one tenth of one percent per block. Nobody changes it unilaterally; a
material change is a slow, visible, coordinated act by a large fraction of proposers over hours.
That is the whole point of the design — capacity is a network decision, made continuously, rather
than a constant somebody edits.

**`gasUsed` is what actually happened**, and a header claiming a `gasUsed` greater than its
`gasLimit` is invalid.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-fork" />

**`baseFeePerGas` is not a choice at all.** It is derived from the parent's gas limit, gas used and
base fee, and header validation recomputes it and rejects the block on
mismatch.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-fork" /> Two constants shape that derivation:
`ELASTICITY_MULTIPLIER` of 2, which puts the target at half the limit, and
`BASE_FEE_MAX_CHANGE_DENOMINATOR` of 8, which caps the per-block move at one
eighth.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-fork" /><Cite src="eip-1559" />

That last point is worth pausing on, because it recurs in the blob market and is a genuinely useful
mental filter: **of the fields in the header that concern fees, the proposer chooses exactly one.**
The gas limit is a vote. Everything else in the pricing path is recomputed by every verifier and
would fail validation if it were anything else.

### What the limit is right now, and why the question is oddly hard

EIP-7935 is the source everyone cites for "60 million", and reading it changes what you should
conclude. It is an **Informational** EIP whose specification section says only that execution clients
should update the gas limit value generated in their default configurations to 60M, tied to the
Fusaka release so that the change could be coordinated and tested. Its abstract records that mainnet
was at 36M at the time of writing.<Cite src="eip-7935" />

So 60M is a **recommended client default**, not a protocol constant. The live figure is whatever
proposers have actually voted their configurations toward, which drifts within the ±1/1024 rule and
which you can read directly from a recent header. The one confident statement available is negative:
any material quoting 30 million is describing the value that held from 2021 until late 2025 and is
out of date.<Cite src="eip-7935" />

The direction of travel is that even the default stops being a client constant. EIP-8261, currently
in Review, proposes sourcing the gas-limit preference from a schedule in the consensus-layer
configuration rather than from hard-coded, release-scoped defaults — and states plainly that the
±1/1024 elasticity rule remains the only gas-limit *validity* rule, with the schedule being a
recommendation clients may warn about but must honour operator overrides
against.<Cite src="eip-8261" /> It is listed as scheduled in the Glamsterdam meta EIP, which is
itself a Draft with no mainnet activation.<Cite src="eip-7773" />

### Two more budgets that are not the gas limit

A block has at least three independent ceilings, and confusing them produces designs that fail for
reasons the designer was not measuring.

**A per-transaction gas cap.** EIP-7825, Final, caps any single transaction at 16,777,216 gas —
`2**24` — enforced both at transaction-pool admission and at block validation, and explicitly
independent of the block gas limit.<Cite src="eip-7825" /> No single transaction can occupy a whole
block any more, however large the block limit becomes.

**A block byte-size cap.** EIP-7934 caps the RLP-encoded size of a block at `MAX_BLOCK_SIZE` of 10
MiB minus a `SAFETY_MARGIN` of 2 MiB, giving 8 MiB.<Cite src="eip-7934" /> A block can be well under
its gas limit and invalid for being too many bytes.

**A per-transaction blob count.** The Osaka specification rejects a blob transaction carrying more
than six blobs, and rejects one carrying none.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-fork" />

## The blob market: the same shape, a different curve, its own dials

EIP-4844 added two header fields, and they run a second EIP-1559-style auction over a completely
different resource.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

**`blobGasUsed`** is this block's blob gas consumption. Blob gas is not execution gas and is not
convertible to it: each blob costs `PER_BLOB` blob gas, which is `2**17` —
131,072.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-gas" />

**`excessBlobGas`** is the running total of blob gas consumed above target, carried forward from
block to block. Like the base fee, it is derived rather than chosen: header validation recomputes it
from the parent and rejects any mismatch.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-fork" />

The blob base fee is then an exponential function of that accumulator — a Taylor-series exponential
of `excess_blob_gas` over `BLOB_BASE_FEE_UPDATE_FRACTION`, which is 5,007,716, floored at
`BLOB_MIN_GASPRICE` of 1 wei.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-gas" /> Consumption above target pushes
the accumulator up and the price up with it; consumption below target drains it and the price falls
back toward one wei.

### The target and maximum are not where you think, and probably not where I think either

The blob schedule is the one place in this lesson where I am going to decline to give you a number
for mainnet, and the reason is instructive.

The Osaka specification file carries a `BLOB_SCHEDULE_TARGET` of 6 and a `BLOB_SCHEDULE_MAX` of
9.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-gas" /> Those are the values in the executable spec's own
configuration. They are not necessarily mainnet's live values, because EIP-7892 introduced
**blob-parameter-only hardforks** — a lightweight upgrade class that changes blob parameters without
a full named hardfork, precisely so that capacity can be stepped up on telemetry rather than on a
release train.<Cite src="eip-7892" /> Several such forks have run, sources disagree about which
target/max pair is current as of August 2026, and no source I could verify settles it.

The correct engineering habit follows directly: **read the blob schedule out of your client's chain
configuration for the fork you are on**, and treat any target/max pair in any teaching material,
including this one, as a snapshot with an expiry date. If a cost model depends on the number, the
model should read it rather than embed it.

## The coupling that was added on purpose

Now the qualification, because "the two markets are completely independent" was true when EIP-4844
shipped and needs one amendment today.

The problem it fixes is stated cleanly in EIP-7918's own motivation. The blob auction only controls
behaviour while the blob base fee is a meaningful share of what a blob actually costs its consumer.
When execution costs dominate, the price signal is lost: the mechanism keeps lowering the blob base
fee, and it can settle at 1 wei, at which point a ten percent change in the blob fee moves a
consumer's total cost by a rounding error. Demand then has to be absorbed by near-full blocks for an
extended period before a market-clearing fee is restored, with the mechanism intermittently degrading
into a first-price auction.<Cite src="eip-7918" />

The fix assigns blobs a **reserve price of `BLOB_BASE_COST * base_fee_per_gas`** — an execution-gas
denominated floor. You can see it in the excess-blob-gas calculation: the spec compares that reserve
against `PER_BLOB` multiplied by the current blob gas price, and when the reserve is higher it stops
subtracting the full target from the accumulator, letting excess rise with usage
instead.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-gas" /><Cite src="eip-7918" /> `BLOB_BASE_COST` is
`2**13`.<Cite src="execution-specs-osaka-gas" />

State the resulting relationship carefully, because both over- and under-claiming it are common:

- The two markets remain **separate mechanisms with separate inputs**. The blob base fee still
  responds to blob consumption; the execution base fee still responds to gas consumption. Neither
  drives the other in the ordinary regime.
- There is now a **one-way floor**. The execution base fee sets a reserve under the blob price. It
  binds only when the blob fee is low relative to execution costs, and it never works in the other
  direction: heavy blob usage does not raise the execution base fee.
- Therefore the two prices can still diverge dramatically and routinely — they just cannot diverge
  arbitrarily far downward on the blob side.

**They are two line items, not one.** An L2 posting data pays a blob bill driven by `excessBlobGas`
and an execution bill driven by `baseFeePerGas`, and the ratio between them moves. A model that
tracks one and scales it is not a model.

**The correlation is an empirical question with a date on it.** Not a constant, not a rule of thumb.
This module's second practice has you chart `gasUsed / gasLimit`, `baseFeePerGas`, `blobGasUsed` and
the derived blob base fee over the same block range and find a block where they diverge sharply. Do
that against current data rather than trusting a figure — including any figure in this lesson.

**Capacity changes arrive through three different doors.** The gas limit through a proposer vote
bounded by ±1/1024. The blob schedule through blob-parameter-only forks that do not carry an upgrade
name.<Cite src="eip-7892" /> The per-transaction and byte-size caps through ordinary
EIPs.<Cite src="eip-7825" /><Cite src="eip-7934" /> If you monitor only named hardforks, you will
miss the door blob capacity actually comes through.

A rollup team is preparing a launch. Their L1 cost forecast assumes: gas limit 30,000,000; blob costs
scale with `baseFeePerGas` because "it is all gas"; and a single large batch-submission transaction
consuming 22,000,000 gas at peak.

Predict which assumptions break, in what direction, and what a correct forecast reads from the chain
instead of assuming. Then answer: on a day when execution gas is very cheap and blob demand is
heavy, which bill dominates, and which of their assumptions would have hidden that?

<Answer>
**Assumption one — gas limit 30,000,000 — is stale, and stale in the safe direction.** The
recommended client default was raised to 60M around Fusaka, and the live value is a proposer vote
that drifts by at most ±1/1024 per block. Their forecast therefore understates available capacity,
which makes their inclusion assumptions pessimistic rather than dangerous. The fix is to read
`gasLimit` from recent headers rather than to substitute 60M for 30M, since the number they want is
whatever proposers are actually voting.

**Assumption two — blob costs scale with `baseFeePerGas` — is the category error and breaks in both
directions.** Blob pricing is driven by `excessBlobGas`, its own accumulator, through an exponential
curve with its own update fraction and its own floor of 1 wei. Blob costs can be near that floor
while execution gas is expensive, and can spike while execution gas is cheap. The single coupling
that exists — EIP-7918's reserve price of `BLOB_BASE_COST * base_fee_per_gas` — runs the opposite way
from what they assume: it is a floor on the blob fee derived from execution costs, not a
proportionality between the two, and it binds only when the blob fee is low.

**Assumption three — a 22,000,000-gas transaction — is now invalid outright.** EIP-7825 caps any
single transaction at 16,777,216 gas, enforced at pool admission and at block validation and
independent of the block gas limit. That transaction is not expensive; it cannot be included. The
batch must be split, and splitting changes the cost model because each piece pays its own intrinsic
cost. There is also a byte-size ceiling to respect: a block's RLP encoding cannot exceed 8 MiB.

**What a correct forecast reads rather than assumes.** `gasLimit` and `baseFeePerGas` from recent
headers; `blobGasUsed` and `excessBlobGas` from the same headers, with the blob base fee derived from
the second; the blob target and maximum from the client's chain configuration for the current fork,
because blob-parameter-only forks change them without a named upgrade; and the per-transaction gas
cap as a hard constraint on batch shape.

**Cheap execution gas, heavy blob demand.** The blob bill dominates — possibly by a wide margin,
because the blob fee is exponential in accumulated excess while the execution base fee is capped at
one-eighth movement per block. Their second assumption hides this completely: tying blob costs to
`baseFeePerGas` means a cheap-gas day is forecast as a cheap day overall, which is exactly backwards.
The failure is silent, too — the forecast does not error, it just reports a number that is confidently
too low on precisely the days the finance team will ask about.
</Answer>

## Where this pays off

**Rollup economics**, on the scaling track, is this lesson with volume attached. Every design
question about batching, compression and posting cadence is a trade between two bills that move
independently.

**The blob and data-availability module** takes `excessBlobGas` further into how blob data is
actually distributed, retained and sampled — and why the resource being priced is bandwidth and
storage rather than computation.

**Reading any capacity claim critically.** "The gas limit is X" and "blobs are Y per block" are both
questions whose answer is a configuration value with a date on it, not a constant. That habit is the
most portable thing in this module.

**And this closes the header.** Twenty-one fields: one that links, six that commit to the body, seven
that meter and schedule, three that are dead, and four that reach outside the execution layer. You
have now read every one of them and can say what it commits to, when it arrived, and whether it is
still alive.
