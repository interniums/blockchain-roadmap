Two engineers are arguing in a pull request.

One wants to wait twelve blocks, because that is what the exchange integration guide they read says.
The other wants to wait for `finalized`, because anything less "isn't really settled". They are
arguing about a number, and they will resolve it by seniority or by fatigue, because neither of them
has stated the thing a number would have to be derived from.

Both positions are defensible for some action in some product. Neither is defensible as stated,
because a confirmation policy is not a constant. It is the output of a comparison, and this lesson is
about getting the two sides of that comparison right — including the side almost everyone gets wrong.

## The comparison

The wait should be long enough that **the cost of reversing the chain to that depth exceeds what
reversing it would earn an attacker against you.**

Track 01 established the cost side. Reversing a finalized checkpoint requires validators holding at
least a third of the total stake to sign provably contradictory messages, and at that participation
level the correlation penalty takes each offender's entire effective balance — so the attack destroys
roughly a third of all staked ETH rather than risking it.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /><Cite src="mikeneuder-slashings" />
Denominate that in ETH and compute it on the day you need it. Published totals for staked ETH in
2026 do not agree: one widely cited dataset puts it near 34% of supply,<Cite src="theblock-staking-share" />
while other reporting from the same period gives an absolute figure several million ETH away from
it. The research register behind this curriculum records the discrepancy as unresolved, and a spread
that wide moves the answer by more than a million ETH. Pull a live figure, state the date beside it,
and re-derive on a schedule.

The previous lesson established the other end of the cost side, and it is the end that matters far
more often: **short reorgs cost an attacker nothing.** One or two slots violates no slashing
condition, and reorg attacks are achievable well below the one-third threshold at no slashing
cost.<Cite src="arxiv-low-cost-attacks" />

So the cost side has two values, not a curve: approximately free, and approximately a third of all
staked ETH. Which of the two applies depends entirely on which side of the finality boundary you sit.

## The side everyone gets wrong

Now the profit side, where most policies quietly fall apart.

The naive version reads: "our largest single payment is ten thousand dollars, and the attack costs
billions, therefore we are safe." That argument is wrong twice.

**Wrong once: the attacker amortises.** A reversal is not aimed at you. One reversed history changes
the outcome for every party that acted on the discarded blocks — every exchange crediting deposits,
every bridge releasing on the far side, every payment processor that already paid out. The prize is
the aggregate across everyone acting on the same marker at the same moment, not your slice of it. Your
question is therefore not "is my payout worth an attack" but "is my payout in a class of payouts that,
summed, is worth one". You do not control that sum and you cannot observe it.

**Wrong twice: you compared against the wrong cost.** If your action sits on the head block, the
attacker does not need a finality reversal at all. They need a one-slot reorg, which is free, and
which happens without any attacker at all often enough to be routine. Comparing a ten-thousand-dollar
payout to the cost of destroying a third of the stake is comparing it to a threat you are not exposed
to.

The correct shape of the argument depends on which regime the action sits in, and the two regimes take
genuinely different reasoning:

**For actions gated on finality, the question is security.** Cost of corruption versus the largest
extractable prize across the whole ecosystem in one reversal. You are relying on that comparison
holding for everyone, not only for you — which is exactly why bridges holding very large balances are
a structural concern rather than a metric.

**For actions gated on the head or a few slots, the question is expected loss.** Reorgs at that depth
are ordinary events with a measurable rate. Multiply the rate by what one costs you and compare
against what the wait costs you in conversion, latency and support. That is an ordinary product
trade-off, and it is entirely legitimate to conclude "we will be wrong twice a month and eat it".
What is not legitimate is to conclude it without knowing the rate.

## What you can actually wait for

Four things, in increasing order of price to an attacker.

**The head block.** Free to reverse, available immediately. Correct for anything you can un-render.

**N slots of depth.** Buys you very little on this chain in security terms — depth is not what makes a
reversal expensive here — but it does buy you distance from the ordinary timing reorgs, which are
almost entirely one slot deep. Treat it as a latency filter, not a security level, and say so in the
policy.

**The `safe` tag.** Available as an execution-layer block tag alongside `latest` and
`finalized`.<Cite src="ethereum-org-json-rpc" /> Read the previous lesson's warning before you build
on it: the rule behind it is defined in the consensus specification, has been revised, and the current
formulation buys speed by assuming honest attestations arrive within their slot. The specification
states plainly that if that assumption breaks, confirmed blocks can be reorganised with nobody
behaving adversarially and nobody slashed.<Cite src="consensus-specs" /><Cite src="fcr-paper" /> That
is a real and useful guarantee with a named assumption attached, which is more than most policies
manage — but the assumption is part of the guarantee and belongs in your document.

**The `finalized` tag.** Roughly two epochs behind the head at best, which is about 12.8
minutes.<Cite src="consensus-specs" /> Reversal costs approximately a third of all staked ETH,
destroyed and attributable.

<Aside kind="warn">
Waiting longer is not monotonically safer, and this is the failure that catches sophisticated teams.
Depth is only evidence when it is depth on the chain the rest of the network is building. A node whose
peer set an adversary controls will report a private chain with any depth you care to require, and
will validate every block on it correctly — the *False Friends* work put the resource requirement for
that at two hosts in distinct subnets against an unpatched client.<Cite src="eclipse-eth-paper" /> If
your policy's only lever is depth, an eclipse defeats it entirely, and the defence is independent
observation rather than patience.
</Aside>

## The four things a policy has to say

Track 01 gave you the structure: name the action rather than the event, state the price you are
buying, state what happens during the wait, and state what changes the answer. Two additions now that
you know the mechanism.

**Say which regime you are in, explicitly.** A policy that says "we wait for finality" and a policy
that says "we accept one-slot reorgs at an observed rate of X per week, costing us Y each" are both
complete. A policy that says "we wait 12 confirmations" is neither, because it does not say which
threat it is buying protection from, and on this chain twelve blocks is not a threshold in any
model.

**Say what happens when the wait does not end.** Finalization is not guaranteed to arrive on a
schedule. If the gap between head and finalized grows for an hour, a policy of "wait for finalized"
becomes a policy of "queue indefinitely" unless you decided otherwise in advance. Decide in advance:
does the queue drain manually, does a threshold-gated override exist, who holds it, and what does the
user see. This is the paragraph that separates a policy from an aspiration, and it is almost always
missing.

<Aside kind="note">
One structural point worth carrying into any design review. The correct wait is a property of the
irreversible act, and irreversibility is a property of *your* system, not of the chain. Releasing a
downloadable file is more irreversible than a bank transfer, because the transfer can at least in
principle be clawed back and the file cannot. Teams routinely gate the payment rail carefully and the
file not at all, because the payment rail is where the money is and the file is where the value is.
</Aside>

<Check type="explain" concept="confirmation-policy">
You are reviewing a policy for a cross-chain bridge. It reads, in full:

> "We release funds on the destination chain once the source-chain deposit has 64 confirmations. 64
> blocks is more than two epochs, so the deposit is finalized by then."

Three separate things are wrong with this. Find them, and write the replacement clause for each.

<Answer>
**1 — depth is not finality, even when the arithmetic looks right.** Two epochs is 64 slots and about
12.8 minutes,<Cite src="consensus-specs" /> so the intuition behind the number is not silly. But slots
and blocks are not the same counter: a missed slot produces no block, so 64 blocks is always *at
least* 64 slots of elapsed time and the mapping drifts. More importantly, finalization is an event the
chain publishes, not a duration you approximate. Replacement: *release only when the deposit's block
is at or below the finalized checkpoint, read from the `finalized` tag.*<Cite src="ethereum-org-json-rpc" />

**2 — the prize side is missing entirely, and for a bridge it is the whole argument.** A bridge is the
canonical case where an attacker's payoff is not bounded by any one user's action: the balance the
bridge holds is the prize, it is public, and it grows without anyone deciding it should. A policy for
a bridge has to state the maximum value a single reversal can extract and compare it against the cost
of corruption in ETH on a stated date. Replacement: *state the destination-side exposure per source
block, state the finality-reversal cost in ETH with its date and source, and state the review trigger
if exposure grows past a fraction of that.*<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

**3 — there is no behaviour defined for the case where finality does not arrive.** If the source chain
stops finalizing, this policy says "wait", and a bridge that silently stops releasing funds during a
consensus incident is a liquidity crisis on the destination side. Replacement: *if the finalized
checkpoint has not advanced for N epochs, the bridge halts new deposits, surfaces the state to users,
and requires a named human decision to release anything — and that decision is recorded.*

**The one it did not ask about**, which you should raise in the review anyway: 64 confirmations of
depth read from a single RPC endpoint is 64 confirmations *on whatever chain that endpoint is
following*.<Cite src="eclipse-eth-paper" /> A bridge is exactly the kind of target where independent
observation is worth its operational cost, and no depth requirement substitutes for it.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The module's written practice** asks you to do this for three products with genuinely different
exposure and to defend each against a cost stated in ETH. The part it will press you on is the one
this lesson flagged as usually absent: what your system does to already-written data when a reorg
lands after you acted.

**Extractable value**, from the next lesson onward, supplies the missing piece of the profit side.
Reorg incentives are not only about attacking somebody — there is a standing economic pull toward
reorganising blocks whose contents are worth more than the reward for building
honestly.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

**Rollup confirmation tiers** in the scaling track are this same reasoning applied to a chain where
"confirmed" is issued by a single sequencer and means something considerably weaker than a head
block on L1. The structure of the argument transfers exactly; the numbers do not.

**Threat modelling** in the security track is where the aggregation point above becomes a habit: for
any guarantee you rely on, ask who else relies on it at the same moment, because the attacker's
budget is compared against the sum and not against your line item.<Cite src="eth2book-slashing" />
