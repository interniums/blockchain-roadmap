Open a recent mainnet block in an explorer and scroll to the bottom.

The last transaction is very often a plain ETH transfer, from an address with no name, to the block's
fee recipient. No contract call, no data, nothing interesting. It is the least remarkable transaction
in the block.

It is also the auction settling. That transfer is a block builder paying the validator whose name is
on the block for the right to have decided what is in it.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-builders" />
The validator did not choose the contents. In most cases the validator had not seen the contents at
the moment it signed.

Roughly nine mainnet blocks in ten are produced this way. This lesson is the pipeline, hop by hop,
and the specific place in it where a party outside the protocol holds something hostage by design.

## Four roles

**Searchers** look for orderings worth money — an arbitrage across two venues, a liquidation that has
become available, a backrun of somebody else's trade. They express the opportunity as a *bundle*: a
sequence of transactions that must execute together, in order, at a particular position. They submit
bundles to builders and bid for placement.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

**Builders** assemble complete blocks. Their input is bundles from searchers, transactions from the
public pool, and — increasingly, and decisively — private order flow routed to them
directly.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-builders" /> They run ordering algorithms and simulations over
that input to produce an execution payload that maximises value, then bid for the
slot.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-builders" />

**Relays** sit between builders and proposers. They receive blocks from builders, validate them,
hold them, and offer the proposer only the best bid's header.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-relay-docs" />

**Proposers** are validators with a slot. They receive bids and headers, sign the best one, and
publish the resulting block. Their consensus client's own execution client is still building a block
locally the whole time, as a fallback.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-proposers" />

The software that wires a validator into this is **MEV-Boost**: open-source middleware run as a
sidecar alongside the consensus client, which queries relays and outsources block
building.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-docs" /><Cite src="mev-boost-repo" /> It is not part of the
Ethereum protocol. A validator can not run it, in which case its execution client builds the block
and the pipeline above does not apply to that slot.

## The wire protocol

The pipeline is small enough to hold in your head, which is worth doing, because the failure modes
fall out of it directly. Three endpoints do the work, and they are standardised in the builder
specification rather than invented per relay.<Cite src="builder-specs" />

**`registerValidator`** — `POST /eth/v1/builder/validators`. Ahead of time, a validator registers with
each relay it uses, telling it where to send payment and what gas limit it
wants.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-relay-docs" />

**`getHeader`** — `GET /eth/v1/builder/header/{slot}/{parent_hash}/{pubkey}`. At the start of its slot,
the proposer asks for an execution payload *header* and a bid. MEV-Boost asks every configured relay
and presents the best answer; the relay docs describe MEV-Boost as effectively a relay of
relays.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-relay-docs" />

**`submitBlindedBlock`** — `POST /eth/v1/builder/blinded_blocks`. The proposer signs a *blinded* beacon
block — its own signature plus the winning header, and no transactions — and submits it. The relay
responds with the full execution payload, which the proposer's client assembles into a complete
signed block and publishes to the network.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-relay-docs" /><Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

All of it has to complete early in the slot. A slot is 12,000 milliseconds, and the attestation
deadline is set at 3333 basis points of that — a third of the slot, four
seconds.<Cite src="consensus-specs" /> A block that arrives after the committee has voted has already
lost most of its weight, with the consequences the reorg lesson described.

<Aside kind="note">
Read the shape of `getHeader` again. The proposer's own consensus client is asking an external party
for a commitment, on a per-slot deadline, with a locally built fallback ready. That is the same
structure as a cache with a timeout in front of an unreliable upstream — which is exactly what it is,
and it is why the operational failure modes will feel familiar even though the domain does not.
</Aside>

### One slot, walked through

The endpoints are easier to trust once you have followed a slot through them in order.

**Subgoal: get the proposer's terms on file, well before the slot.** The validator registers with each
relay it uses, supplying the fee recipient it wants paid and the gas limit it
prefers.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-relay-docs" /> Relays distribute those registrations to
builders, so a builder assembling a block for that slot already knows who to pay and how large the
block may be. Nothing here is time-critical, which is precisely why it is separated out.

**Subgoal: accumulate the order flow the block will be made of.** Searchers submit bundles with
sealed-price bids for inclusion, and those bundles pass through the relay for validation before they
reach a proposer, which is the mechanism that protects proposers from spam and invalid
transactions.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> Alongside bundles, builders take the public pool and
whatever private flow is routed to them directly.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-builders" />

**Subgoal: hold an auction the proposer never sees.** Builders bid; a relay can aggregate many
builders and surfaces only the highest *valid* bid.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-relay-docs" /> The
competition among builders is invisible from the proposer's side, which is the design intent — the
proposer is meant to face one number, not a market.

**Subgoal: ask, at the top of the slot.** The proposer's consensus client asks MEV-Boost for a header;
MEV-Boost calls `getHeader` on every configured relay and returns the best of what comes
back.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-relay-docs" /> Meanwhile the proposer's own execution client has
been building a block locally the whole time, which is the fallback if nothing usable
arrives.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-proposers" />

**Subgoal: commit.** The proposer signs a blinded beacon block containing the winning header. This is
the point of no return, and the next section is about why.

**Subgoal: reveal and publish.** `submitBlindedBlock` goes to the relay; the relay returns the payload;
the client assembles the full signed block and gossips it.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> All of that
has to land before the committee votes, four seconds into the slot.<Cite src="consensus-specs" />

**Subgoal: settle.** The last transaction in the published block moves ETH from the builder to the
proposer's registered fee recipient.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-builders" /> The auction is now
recorded on the chain it was conducted about, which is the only part of the whole sequence that any
outside observer can see directly.

## Why there is a relay at all

The obvious design has builders bidding directly to proposers. It does not work, and the reason is a
mutual distrust that neither side can resolve alone.

**Show the proposer the full block and the proposer can steal it.** Nothing stops a proposer from
reading a builder's payload, discarding the bid, and proposing that ordering as its own. The value the
builder spent effort finding is now free.

**Show the proposer nothing and the proposer is exposed.** A header is a commitment to contents. If
the contents turn out to be invalid, or to not pay what the bid promised, the proposer has signed
something worthless and missed its slot.

The relay is the escrow that resolves both. It receives the builder's full block, **simulates it**,
and checks specific properties before it will offer it: that the correct fee is paid to the
validator's registered fee recipient, that the block's attributes and transactions are what they claim
to be, and that gas is within the limit the validator requested.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-relay-docs" />
Having validated it, the relay gives the proposer only the header, and releases the payload once the
header is signed.

The relay documentation's own phrase for its position is **doubly trusted**: trusted by builders for
fair payload routing, and trusted by proposers for block validity, accuracy and data
availability.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-relay-docs" />

### What the escrow costs

Here is the part that most descriptions of this pipeline skip, and it is the reason the whole
arrangement is described by the canonical documentation as a temporary implementation carrying higher
trust assumptions than in-protocol PBS would.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

Once the proposer has signed the blinded block, it is committed. If the relay never returns the
payload — because it crashed, because it is under attack, or because it chose not to — the proposer
cannot fall back to the block its own execution client built. Signing a second block for the same slot
is equivocation, which is one of the two slashable consensus offences.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /><Cite src="eth2book-slashing" />

So the proposer's options at that moment are: propose the block the relay is holding, or propose
nothing. The slot is missed, the rewards are lost, and there is no recourse. The trust is not
metaphorical.

## The mitigations, and their shape

Three defences exist against the escrow risk. Notice that all three are operational rather than
protocol-level, which is precisely the complaint that in-protocol PBS is meant to answer.

**Multiple relays.** A validator connects to several. MEV-Boost aggregates their bids, and a proposer
that submitted a blinded block whose header came from more than one relay can obtain the payload from
whichever responds.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-relay-docs" /> This is why measurements of relay
share count the same block more than once: on a single 24-hour window read from relayscan on 26 August
2026, the eight monitored relays reported around 15,900 payload deliveries against roughly 6,480
distinct blocks, which is about two and a half relays per block.<Cite src="relayscan-io" />

**Circuit breakers.** Consensus clients implement conditions under which they stop sourcing blocks
externally and fall back to local building — for example when the chain looks unhealthy. Each client
defines its own conditions and its own resumption behaviour, so this is client
configuration rather than a protocol guarantee.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-relay-docs" />

**Relay monitoring.** Because running a relay is permissionless while the role is trusted, the
proposed mitigation is external observation: a monitor that forms a view of relay behaviour and
performance from public data.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-relay-docs" /> Reputation, in other words.

Relay operators are not all commercially aligned, and one of them says so at length. Aestus runs as a
neutral, non-censoring relay explicitly framed as a public good, on the argument that several relays
are operated by parties with direct financial interests in the value flowing through
them.<Cite src="aestus-relay" /> Its own account, as of February 2026, is that it had been operating
continuously for over three years, served over 650,000 validators, and negotiated between three and
five percent of daily mainnet block auctions.<Cite src="aestus-relay" /> The same page notes that its
founders originally expected the relay to be a short-term bridge until in-protocol PBS
arrived.<Cite src="aestus-relay" />

## Following the money

Two mechanics are worth knowing precisely, because they are how you verify any of this yourself rather
than taking it on description.

**The builder pays in-band.** The builder sets its own address as the fee recipient of the payload it
constructs, then includes a transaction at the end of that block paying ETH to the *proposer's*
registered fee recipient.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-builders" /> That is the unremarkable transfer
at the bottom of the block from the opening of this lesson.

**Block value is a balance delta.** The agreed method for scoring a block's value is block-level
scoring: the difference in the fee recipient account's balance before and after the block
executes.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-builders" /> Simple, checkable by anyone, and it does not
require trusting the builder's own accounting — which is exactly why it was chosen over
transaction-level alternatives.

<Check type="explain" concept="relay-escrow">
A relay serving a meaningful share of mainnet goes down for ninety seconds — connections time out
rather than erroring cleanly.

Walk through what happens to a proposer whose slot falls in that window, for each of these three
moments: before it called `getHeader`, after `getHeader` but before signing, and after signing the
blinded block. Then say which of the three is a protocol-level problem and which are not.

<Answer>
**Before `getHeader`.** Nothing bad happens. MEV-Boost queries every configured relay and the dead
one does not answer in time. If other relays respond, the proposer takes the best of those. If
none do, the fallback path is the proposer's own execution client, which has been building a block
locally the whole time.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-proposers" /> The proposer earns less — the
locally built block captures only what the public pool offers — and the slot is filled. This is the
designed degradation and it works.

**After `getHeader`, before signing.** Also fine, and for the same reason: the proposer has received a
header and a bid but has committed to nothing. If the relay stops responding it can discard that bid
and either take another relay's or use the local block. The only cost is the time already spent inside
a four-second budget.<Cite src="consensus-specs" />

**After signing the blinded block.** This is the failure. The proposer has signed a beacon block
containing that header and cannot sign a different one for the same slot, because two signed blocks
for one slot is equivocation and slashable.<Cite src="eth2book-slashing" /> Its own locally built
block is unusable. If no relay returns the payload, the proposer's only outcomes are the relay's block
appearing or the slot being missed entirely.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> Redundant submission to
every relay that supplied the winning header is the mitigation, and it is a mitigation rather than a
fix — it helps when one relay fails and not when the block itself is unavailable.

**Which of these is a protocol problem.** None of them, and that is the answer worth taking away. The
protocol is behaving exactly as specified in all three cases: the slot passes, the fork choice
proceeds, an empty slot is a normal event, and the equivocation rule is doing precisely its job. The
entire failure lives in a market layer the protocol cannot see. This is why "make PBS in-protocol" is
a live line of work rather than a purity argument — EIP-7732 exists to move this hop inside the
consensus specification, where the assumption becomes enforceable instead of
contractual.<Cite src="eip-7732" /><Cite src="ethereum-roadmap-pbs" />

A secondary effect worth naming: a missed slot is also a fork-choice event. Late and missing blocks
are the ordinary cause of the short reorgs from earlier in this module, so a relay outage does not
only cost one validator its rewards — it perturbs the head for everyone reading it.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**Builder concentration** is the last lesson of this module. You now have the pipeline; the question
is who is actually at the builder hop, and the answer is a very short list with a structural reason
behind it.<Cite src="arxiv-private-orderflow" />

**The module's measurement practice** asks you to trace one real block through exactly this path —
find it on a relay, identify the builder, find the payment to the proposer, and say where in the block
that payment sits. Everything you need to do that is in this lesson.

**Enshrined PBS** in Track 11 is where the relay hop gets specified away, and where you will see the
full cost of doing so: a beacon block that may carry no execution payload at all, a separate committee
attesting to payload availability, and a breaking change for every tool that parses a beacon
block.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

**Node operation** in the infrastructure track turns all of this into configuration you will actually
touch — which relays to connect to, what a circuit breaker does, and what your fallback behaviour is
when the market layer stops answering.
