Start with a disagreement, because it is the most honest way into this topic.

Two figures for Ethereum builder concentration were in circulation when this curriculum's research was
done in 2026. One put the top two builders at roughly 73% of blocks and named them as Titan and
Quasar. The other put two builders at roughly 80% and named them as Titan and BuilderNet. They cannot
both be current, and this course's research register records the conflict as unresolved with an
instruction attached: do not publish either number — measure it.

An earlier datapoint from an interested party sharpens the picture rather than settling it. BuilderNet's
own launch post, in November 2024, opened by stating that two parties built 90% of Ethereum blocks and
that concentration in the market had tripled since 2022.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" /> That is a
vendor making an argument for its own product, and it is also the clearest statement of the trend
anyone published.

So: three claims, three different numbers, and no way to reconcile them from reading. This lesson does
the other thing.

## A measurement, with its method attached

On 26 August 2026, one read of relayscan's 24-hour overview reported the following shares of
relay-delivered mainnet blocks: Titan around 55%, Quasar around 20%, BuilderNet around 14%, and two
further builders at roughly 6% and 4%.<Cite src="relayscan-io" /> Top two, about three quarters. Top
four, about 95%.

Every one of the following caveats matters more than the digits.

**The denominator is relay-delivered blocks, not all blocks.** Locally built blocks do not appear.
That is the right denominator for a question about the builder market and the wrong one for "what
share of Ethereum does Titan order".

**It is one window from one dashboard.** The same page's shorter windows disagreed with the 24-hour
figure by more than ten percentage points on the leader alone, which tells you the variance is large
at that timescale. A second dashboard exists and should give a broadly similar picture from a
different pipeline.<Cite src="mevboost-pics" />

**It moves.** Builder share is a market outcome measured daily. The figure above is dated and sourced
so it can be discarded, which is the entire point of writing it that way.

<Aside kind="note">
The same read is a useful cross-check on the other headline figure in this module. Relayscan's
24-hour window covered roughly 6,480 relay-delivered blocks.<Cite src="relayscan-io" /> A day is 7,200
slots of 12 seconds, so relay-delivered blocks were close to nine in ten of all slots — which is the
arithmetic behind "roughly 90% of blocks are built through this pipeline". Treat it as a floor: a
dashboard only counts the relays it monitors.
</Aside>

## The shape that does not move

Point the measurement at any recent window and the digits change while the structure does not.

**One builder holds more than half.** Not a plurality — a majority of the market, alone.

**The top two hold roughly three quarters.** Which two has changed over the years. That it is two has
not.

**The top four hold nearly everything.** Below them is a long tail of builders winning fractions of a
percent, several of which the same dashboard shows operating at a *loss* over the window, subsidising
blocks to stay in the market at all.<Cite src="relayscan-io" />

That is the fact to carry: two firms decide the contents and ordering of most Ethereum blocks. Not the
validity — validity is checked by everyone, and that part of the system works. The contents.

## Why it concentrated, and why software will not fix it

The instinct is to assume a technical moat: the leaders must have faster simulation, better ordering
algorithms, more hardware. Some of that is real and none of it is the mechanism.

The mechanism is **exclusive order flow**. A builder that receives transactions nobody else can see
can construct a block nobody else can match. Its bid is higher for a reason its competitors cannot
diagnose, let alone counter. Formal analysis of builder bidding under private order flow makes exactly
this argument: exclusive flow tends the market toward monopoly rather than toward a stable
oligopoly.<Cite src="arxiv-private-orderflow" />

The loop closes on itself. Exclusive flow produces winning bids. Winning bids produce slots won.
Slots won make a builder the obvious destination for the next wallet or application deciding where to
route its users' transactions — because routing to a builder that rarely wins a slot means slower
inclusion. So flow concentrates further.

Notice what is *not* in that loop. No agreement between builders. No misbehaviour. No technical
advantage that a competitor could replicate by writing better code. BuilderNet's launch post attributes
the entrenchment directly to opaque off-chain orderflow deals rather than to
engineering.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" />

And notice where the flow comes from: users routing through private RPCs to avoid being sandwiched.
The individually correct decision — protect this user's transaction — aggregates into the
concentration. That trade-off has been measured, and it is a trade-off rather than a free
win.<Cite src="arxiv-private-rpc-benchmark" /> Published estimates of what share of order flow is now
private range from around 15% in a 2023 baseline to claims near 80% in 2026, but those figures measure
different things — share of transactions, share of gas, share of value — and the research behind this
curriculum did not reconcile them. Take the direction as settled and treat any single percentage as
unsupported.

## What is actually at stake

Two specific things, and it is worth being precise because the topic attracts vagueness.

**Censorship.** Recall the strongest argument for PBS from two lessons ago: centralised block
production is tolerable because verification stays decentralised. Verification catches an *invalid*
block. It does not catch a valid block that omits your transaction. The roadmap's own answer to this
is inclusion lists — a mechanism letting proposers force transactions they can see into a
block.<Cite src="ethereum-roadmap-pbs" /> The concrete proposal, EIP-7805, is a draft that was declined
for the Glamsterdam upgrade and deferred.<Cite src="eip-7805" /> So as of this writing there is no
protocol-level mechanism forcing a builder to include anything.

**Fragility.** Two builders and a handful of relays are a small number of places for something to go
wrong at once. The previous lesson covered what one relay failing does to one proposer. Concentration
is the same failure with a much larger blast radius, and the mitigations available — multiple relays,
client-side circuit breakers, external monitoring — are all operational rather than
protocol-level.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-relay-docs" />

## Three responses, and what each actually addresses

**Enshrined PBS (EIP-7732).** Moves the proposer–builder handoff inside the consensus specification,
which removes the relay as a trusted party in the critical path.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> It has not
shipped, and sources disagree on its timing badly enough that no date should be quoted. Read what it
targets carefully: it addresses the *relay* trust assumption. It does not, by itself, make block
building less concentrated.

**Inclusion lists (EIP-7805).** Targets censorship directly, by giving the proposer a way to force
public-mempool transactions into a block. Declined for Glamsterdam and deferred.<Cite src="eip-7805" />

**BuilderNet.** The structural attempt, and the only one of the three aimed at the actual mechanism.
Many operators run bit-identical builder software inside trusted execution environments; each instance
shares the order flow it receives with the others, which is safe precisely because no operator can read
inside the enclave; blocks go out through ordinary relays; and the winning block's value is refunded to
order-flow providers according to their measured contribution.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" /> It is an
attack on the exclusivity of order flow rather than on extraction — the stated goal is to neutralise
exclusive orderflow deals.<Cite src="buildernet-intro" /> It runs on hardware trust, which is a
different assumption rather than no assumption, and the relayscan read above shows it as a meaningful
but not dominant share.<Cite src="relayscan-io" />

<Check type="explain" concept="builder-centralization">
Your team is asked, in a security review, whether builder concentration is a risk to your application.
The application is a lending protocol whose liquidations depend on liquidator transactions landing
promptly.

Give the answer you would defend, naming what is and is not a risk, and say what you would actually
do about it.

<Answer>
**Not a risk: your transactions being invalidated or your state being corrupted.** Builders cannot
produce invalid blocks — the relay simulates them and every node validates them. Concentration does
not weaken correctness at all, and a review answer that implies otherwise will be wrong on the
technical merits.

**A real risk: inclusion, and specifically inclusion timing.** Your protocol's solvency depends on
liquidations landing within some window. Inclusion is at a builder's discretion, there is no
protocol-level obligation to include anything today, and the concrete proposal to create one was
declined for the current upgrade and deferred.<Cite src="eip-7805" /> A small number of builders is a
small number of parties whose policy — legal, commercial, or accidental — determines whether your
liquidator lands.

**A real risk that reviews routinely omit: correlated failure.** If most blocks come through a handful of
builders and relays, an outage or a policy change at one of them shifts inclusion latency for
everybody at once, exactly when volatility is making your liquidations urgent. Your liquidation
parameters were probably calibrated against normal-case inclusion.

**What to actually do.** Three things, in order of value. Measure inclusion latency for your own
liquidation transactions across routes, rather than assuming it — the practice exercise for this
module is the smaller version of that work. Make the liquidation incentive large enough that multiple
independent liquidators compete for it, since the defence against one route failing is several
liquidators on several routes. And set the health-factor buffer against observed p99 inclusion latency
under stress rather than the median, because the median is the number that will be fine on the day
nothing happens.

**What not to do.** Do not build a private relationship with one builder to guarantee your inclusion.
It solves your problem, deepens everyone's, and makes you dependent on a counterparty with no
obligation to you — which is the same loop described in this lesson, entered voluntarily.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The module's measurement practice** is now the obvious next step: measure the distribution over a
thousand blocks yourself, compare it against the two published claims and against the figure above,
and note which — if any — your measurement supports.

**Enshrined PBS and inclusion lists** in Track 11 take the two protocol responses seriously, including
the uncomfortable part: what ePBS changes about the structure of a beacon block, and why removing the
relay does not remove the concentration.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

**The DeFi track's MEV module** goes at the application layer — order-flow auctions, intents and
solver competition — where the same trade-off recurs one level up: your protection is the
competitiveness of a market you cannot inspect.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

**The reflection prompt for this module** asks you to take a product you would actually ship and decide
what one user action must wait for, then defend it against the cost of reversing the chain at that
depth. Everything in these six lessons feeds that one paragraph. The three markers give you the
options; the two reorg regimes give you the prices; the pipeline and its concentration tell you who is
between your user and the chain, and what they are free to do.
