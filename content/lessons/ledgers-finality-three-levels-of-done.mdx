Ask a beacon node one question and it answers three times.

`GET /eth/v1/beacon/states/head/finality_checkpoints` returns an object with three fields:
`previous_justified`, `current_justified` and `finalized`.<Cite src="beacon-apis" /> The path you
asked along — `head` — is itself a fourth answer, and the response carries a fifth thing you did not
ask for: a boolean called `execution_optimistic`, which is the node telling you whether it has
actually verified the execution payloads underneath the state it has reported.<Cite src="beacon-apis" />

Track 01 established that [confirmation is a cost curve rather than a boolean](/c/finality-is-a-spectrum).
This lesson is about the three points on that curve the protocol *itself* computes and publishes,
where each one comes from, and why the gap between the second and the third is not what most people
assume it is.

## The head is an opinion, and it says so

Start with the one that is not a checkpoint at all.

The head is whatever block the node's fork choice currently selects. The beacon API's own parameter
documentation is unusually candid about this: `head` is defined as the canonical head *in the node's
view*.<Cite src="beacon-apis" /> Not the canonical head. The node's.

That view is a function of messages received. Fork choice walks down from the last justified
checkpoint and, at each step, follows the child whose subtree carries the most attesting validator
weight — weight measured in effective balance, counting only each validator's most recent
message.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> Feed two nodes different attestations, or the same
attestations in a different order relative to the slot boundary, and they compute different heads
without either being wrong.

There is a second reason the head moves that has nothing to do with disagreement. A block proposed on
time receives a temporary weight bonus during its own slot: `PROPOSER_SCORE_BOOST` is set to `40`,
applied as forty percent of one committee's weight, and it is removed when the slot
ends.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> So the fork-choice weight attached to the newest block is
different at second three of a slot than it is at second thirteen, by construction. A head that
"changed for no reason" between two of your RPC calls may have changed because a constant expired.

<Aside kind="note">
The `execution_optimistic` flag is worth knowing about before you meet it in an incident. A consensus
client can follow the chain while its execution client is still catching up, in which case it is
reporting head and checkpoints it has not fully validated at the execution layer, and it marks the
response accordingly.<Cite src="beacon-apis" /> A node in that state will happily answer questions
about balances and receipts. Treat an optimistic response as a different, weaker claim than a
non-optimistic one.
</Aside>

## Where justification comes from

The other two markers are checkpoints — specific blocks at epoch boundaries — and they are produced
by voting rather than by weighing.

Every attestation a validator makes carries three votes at once: the head it sees, a **source**
checkpoint, and a **target** checkpoint.<Cite src="ethereum-org-attestations" /> The head vote feeds
the fork choice described above. The source and target votes feed the finality gadget, and they are
what the rest of this lesson is about.

**A checkpoint becomes justified** when attestations from at least two thirds of the total stake name
it as their target.<Cite src="eth2book-casper-ffg" /> That is a supermajority statement about one
epoch boundary, and on its own it is a claim nobody can be punished for retracting.

**A checkpoint becomes finalized** when it is justified *and* the next checkpoint is justified with a
supermajority link whose source is it.<Cite src="eth2book-casper-ffg" /><Cite src="casper-ffg-paper" />
Two supermajorities, chained. That is the whole difference, and the whole difference is the point.

### Why the second supermajority is worth so much

Here is the part that gets flattened in most explanations, including ones that are otherwise careful.

The gap between justified and finalized is not primarily *latency*. It is *attributability*.

A single supermajority for a checkpoint tells you a lot of stake voted for it. It does not, by
itself, create a signed contradiction if that stake later votes for something incompatible — the
validators can move on to a different target in a later epoch, which is normal behaviour when
a chain fails to progress. Nothing about that sequence is provably malicious.

The chained pair is different. Once a checkpoint is finalized, producing a conflicting finalized
checkpoint forces validators holding at least a third of the stake to have signed messages that
violate one of the two slashing conditions — a double vote, or a vote whose source-to-target span
surrounds one of their own earlier votes.<Cite src="eth2book-slashing" /> Those messages are
self-incriminating and anyone can publish them. The protocol does not merely disapprove of the
reversal; it can identify who did it and destroy their stake.

That is **accountable safety**, and it is why the third rung costs an extra epoch and is worth it.
The cost of reversing a finalized checkpoint is approximately one third of all staked ETH, destroyed
rather than spent, and the arithmetic behind that figure is the subject of a Track 01 lesson you have
already done.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

### The timing, and why it is a floor rather than a schedule

An epoch is 32 slots and a slot is 12 seconds, so an epoch is 6 minutes 24
seconds.<Cite src="consensus-specs" /> Finalization needs a checkpoint justified and then the next
one justified, so the shortest path from a transaction landing to the checkpoint above it finalizing
is on the order of two epochs — roughly 12.8 minutes.<Cite src="consensus-specs" />

Two things about that number.

It is a **floor derived from arithmetic on two constants**, not a measured expectation. Where in an
epoch your transaction lands changes the answer by minutes, and the research register behind this
curriculum records published estimates that do not agree with each other. Measure the lag against the
chain and the provider you actually use, and record the date.

It is also **not guaranteed to arrive**. Finalization can stall — that is the entire point of the
availability–finality dilemma from Track 01 — and when it does, the gap between head and finalized
grows without bound until the network recovers or the inactivity leak drains the absent side. A
policy of "wait for finalized" is therefore also a policy about what your product does during an
outage of unknown length.

## What your infrastructure exposes

You do not have to compute any of this. Both layers hand you the markers directly, and the two APIs
do not expose quite the same set — which is a detail worth knowing before you design around it.

On the **consensus layer**, a `state_id` may be `head`, `genesis`, `finalized`, `justified`, a slot
number, or a state root.<Cite src="beacon-apis" /> A `block_id` accepts the same values *except*
`justified` — head, genesis, finalized, a slot, or a block root.<Cite src="beacon-apis" /> If you
want the justified checkpoint as a block, you read it out of the finality-checkpoints response and
look it up by root.

On the **execution layer**, the JSON-RPC default block parameter accepts `latest`, `safe` and
`finalized` alongside a block number.<Cite src="ethereum-org-json-rpc" /> Any call that takes a block
number takes a tag, so "is this receipt under a finalized checkpoint" is a query you make, not a
depth you count.

<Aside kind="warn">
`safe` is the tag that has quietly changed meaning. A great deal of material describes it as "the
justified checkpoint", and the rule clients follow is defined in the consensus specification and has
been revised — the current specification derives a confirmation from a fast confirmation rule rather
than straightforwardly from justification.<Cite src="consensus-specs" /> The fast rule buys a much
shorter confirmation by *assuming* that honest attestations arrive within their slot, and the
specification is explicit that if that synchrony assumption breaks, confirmed blocks can be
reorganised with no adversarial behaviour and nobody slashed.<Cite src="fcr-paper" /><Cite src="consensus-specs" />
If your policy turns on the difference between `safe` and `finalized`, read the current spec and your
client's release notes rather than an explainer.
</Aside>

<Check type="explain" concept="head-vs-justified-vs-finalized">
A backend polls a beacon node every slot and logs four numbers: the head slot, the `current_justified`
epoch, the `finalized` epoch, and the value of `execution_optimistic`.

Over a ten-minute window it records:

1. The head slot going *backwards* by one, twice.
2. `current_justified` advancing while `finalized` stays where it is, for four consecutive epochs.
3. `execution_optimistic` flipping to true for about ninety seconds.

For each, say whether it indicates a problem, and what it tells you about the chain versus about
your own infrastructure.

<Answer>
**1 — almost certainly your infrastructure, not the chain.** The head is the node's view, and a
backend talking to a pooled endpoint is talking to several nodes behind a load balancer. Two
consecutive calls can land on nodes whose views differ by a block, producing a non-monotonic head
with nothing wrong anywhere. It can also be a genuine one-slot reorg, which is likewise not an
incident. Either way, the correct response is that code must never assume the head slot is monotonic.
Distinguishing the two cases requires pinning to one node, which is precisely what a pooled endpoint
prevents.

**2 — the chain, and it is the one to alert on.** Justification advancing while finalization does not
means checkpoints are getting their first supermajority but the chained pair is not completing. That
is a finalization stall: safety intact, liveness of the strongest guarantee degraded. Four epochs is
roughly 25 minutes, and it is also the threshold at which the protocol's inactivity leak begins to
act.<Cite src="consensus-specs" /> The right product response is to stop taking irreversible actions
that depend on finalization and keep serving reads — not to page everyone, and not to fall back to
the head.

**3 — your node, and it invalidates the other two readings while it lasts.** An optimistic response
means the consensus client is following the chain without its execution client having verified the
payloads.<Cite src="beacon-apis" /> Anything you concluded about balances or receipts during that
window rests on unverified execution. The correct handling is to treat optimistic responses as a
distinct state in your own state machine rather than as ordinary answers, because the API will keep
answering normally throughout.

The through-line: two of these three readings are about the observer, and only one is about the
chain. Most "the chain is broken" reports resolve at the observer.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**Reorgs** are the next lesson, and they are what the first rung actually costs you. Head changes are
routine, cheap and occasionally deliberate, and the shape of the risk curve between "in the head
block" and "under a finalized checkpoint" is steep and non-linear rather than gradual.

**Confirmation policy** is the lesson after that: given three markers with three prices, which one
does a specific action in your product wait for, and how do you defend the choice to somebody who
will ask.

**Fork choice in depth** — LMD-GHOST, proposer boost as a tunable dial with two opposing failure
modes, and the confirmation rules built on top of it — is a Track 11 module. What you need here is
that the head is computed, the checkpoints are voted, and the two mechanisms have different
guarantees rather than different speeds.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" />

And when you meet a rollup later, the same three-level structure reappears one layer up with
different names and considerably weaker guarantees on the first two rungs. Knowing which rung a
marker corresponds to is how you avoid taking an L2's "confirmed" for L1 finality.
