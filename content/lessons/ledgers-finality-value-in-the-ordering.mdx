Pull up a block and read three consecutive transactions.

Position 14: an address you have never heard of buys a token. Position 15: a stranger swaps a large
amount of the same token, at a price slightly worse than they would have got a second earlier.
Position 16: the address from position 14 sells, at the price position 15 created.

Nothing in that sequence broke a rule. Every transaction was validly signed, correctly executed and
correctly charged. The profit in positions 14 and 16 did not come from a bug, from a stolen key, or
from a flaw in the exchange contract. It came from the *order*, and the order was somebody's to
choose.

You already know that [pending is a set rather than a queue](/c/mempool-pending-is-not-a-queue) and
that [the public pool is a broadcast of intent](/c/mempool-is-a-public-broadcast). This lesson is
about the consequence: because ordering is a discretionary resource, it has a price, and that price
has reshaped how Ethereum blocks are produced.

## Where the value comes from

Two properties, together.

**Shared mutable state.** Ethereum's accounts model means transactions touch common state, so the
result of executing one depends on what executed before it. That is precisely what
[makes stateful contracts possible](/c/accounts-enable-stateful-contracts), and it is also what makes
order matter. A ledger built on [disjoint unspent outputs](/c/utxo-parallelism) has far less of this
property, which is one of the honest costs of the account model.

**An ordering monopoly per block.** Exactly one party decides the contents and sequence of each block.
For the duration of that block, that party holds a monopoly on a resource everyone else needs.

Put them together and some value is available to whoever holds the monopoly — value that exists
independently of the fees anyone paid and independently of what the transactions were trying to do.
That is **maximal extractable value**, and its existence is a structural consequence rather than a
defect. There is no patch that removes it, because removing it would mean removing one of the two
properties above.

### The catalogue

The canonical documentation names the well-known categories, and it is worth having them in one place
because they behave very differently.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

**Cross-venue arbitrage.** The same asset is priced differently on two exchanges. A searcher buys on
one and sells on the other inside a single transaction, and reverts if the opportunity has closed.
Risk-free within the transaction, so the competition is entirely on latency and bid.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

**Liquidations.** A lending position falls below its collateral requirement and someone repays the
debt in exchange for discounted collateral. This is a payment the protocol deliberately offers,
because the alternative to a fast liquidation is bad debt.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

**Sandwiching.** A searcher watches for a large swap, buys ahead of it to move the price, lets the
victim execute at the worse price, and sells behind it. Unlike arbitrage this is not atomic and
carries real risk for the attacker.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

**The long tail.** NFT mints and mispriced listings, and a large, poorly documented set of
opportunities that are individually small.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

The axis that matters when you are designing something is not "good or bad" but whether the
extraction requires a *specific user to get a worse outcome*. Arbitrage and liquidation capture a
public inconsistency; a sandwich manufactures a private loss. They need different remedies, and
conflating them produces designs that break the mechanisms DeFi depends on while leaving the
extractive ones intact.

## The two consequences that reach consensus

Application-layer extraction is where MEV is most visible. The reason it ended up restructuring
Ethereum's block production is that it does not stay at the application layer.

**It destabilises the fork choice.** If the extractable value in a block significantly exceeds the
reward for building the next one, a proposer is incentivised to reorganise that block and take the
contents instead of extending it.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> That is the standing economic pull
behind the cheap-reorg regime from the previous lesson: not an attacker with a plan, but an ordinary
proposer for whom the arithmetic came out the wrong way.

**It centralises staking.** This is the consequence that actually drove the design response, and the
argument is worth following because it is a pure economies-of-scale story.

Extracting value well requires sophistication: simulation infrastructure, latency engineering,
relationships with the parties who generate profitable order flow. A large staking operator can
amortise all of that across many validators. A solo staker cannot. So the operator earns more per
validator, invests the surplus in extracting better, and earns more still.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />
The canonical documentation is explicit that this creates a centralising force pushing individuals to
stake through large pools rather than on their own.<Cite src="ethereum-roadmap-pbs" />

Notice what makes this dangerous. It is not that a large operator behaves badly. It is that the
protocol's security rests on stake being distributed, and MEV creates a persistent gradient pulling
stake toward whoever is best at extraction. Nobody has to do anything wrong for the property to erode.

### The auction used to be on-chain, and everyone paid for it

There is a piece of history here that explains why the current arrangement looks the way it does, and
it is worth knowing because the same pattern recurs.

Before any of this infrastructure existed, competition for position was expressed the only way the
protocol offered: by bidding gas. Generalised frontrunners — bots that watch for any profitable
transaction and copy it with a higher fee — competed by progressively raising each other's gas
prices, and the canonical account is blunt about the effect. Extraction ballooned in early 2021 and
produced extremely high gas prices across the first months of that year, and the auctions themselves
caused network congestion and high fees for everyone else trying to
transact.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

Note the structure of that cost. The bidding war was between searchers, but the price was paid by
every unrelated user of the chain, because the auction was being conducted in the one venue the
protocol provides and that venue is shared. It is a textbook negative externality, and it was large.

What changed it was not a protocol upgrade. A private channel between searchers and block producers
appeared, and the auction moved into it. The canonical documentation records the outcome: the
emergence of a relay reduced the effectiveness of generalised frontrunners and took gas-price
auctions off-chain, lowering gas prices for ordinary users.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

That is a genuine improvement and it is worth holding onto, because it is easy to lose in the
criticism that follows. It is also the moment the pattern established itself: **a real externality was
removed by moving an activity out of the protocol and into a private venue run by an intermediary.**
Everything in the next two lessons is a consequence of that trade, including the parts that are now
the largest structural concern about Ethereum block production.

The same documentation notes the other side of the ledger, which is where the value goes over time. As
opportunities become better known and more searchers compete for them, more of the total extracted
value ends up captured by block producers rather than by searchers — because the auctions still
happen, privately, and the producer is the one selling.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

## Proposer-builder separation

The response is a role split, and once you see the argument it is hard to unsee.

The centralising force comes from the fact that *the same party* both orders the block and proposes
it. So separate them. Let specialists compete to assemble the most valuable block and bid for the
slot; let the proposer's entire job be to pick the highest bid.

The proposer then needs no sophistication at all. It runs no simulations, holds no relationships,
employs no strategists. It sells the ordering right in an auction and keeps the proceeds. The
canonical framing is exactly this: even if a small pool of specialised builders dominates extraction,
the *reward* flows to whichever validator holds the slot — including a solo staker at
home.<Cite src="ethereum-roadmap-pbs" />

### The commit–reveal at the centre

The mechanism has one non-obvious piece, and it is the piece that makes the rest work.

A builder cannot hand the proposer a finished block. If it did, the proposer could read the
contents, discard the bid, and propose the profitable ordering as its own. Nor can the builder be
asked to trust the proposer's promise, because there is nothing to enforce it with.

So the bid is a **commitment**. The builder publishes a cryptographic commitment to the block's
contents — the execution payload header — along with the amount it will pay. The proposer picks the
highest bid and signs a block proposal containing that header. Only then is the body
revealed.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /> The proposer commits to contents it has not seen, and the
builder reveals contents to a proposer that can no longer steal them.

Read that again with the incentives in mind, because the same structure will reappear in the next
lesson with an extra party inserted into it, and the extra party is where the real trust assumption
lives.

### The strongest argument for it, stated honestly

The Ethereum roadmap makes an argument for PBS that goes further than "it helps solo stakers", and it
is worth engaging with because it is the serious case.

Centralised block *production* is acceptable, the argument runs, as long as block *verification* is
maximally decentralised. Building a block is hard and getting harder; checking that a block is honest
is cheap and stays cheap. This is prover–verifier asymmetry, and the roadmap draws the conclusion
directly: decentralisation is a means, not an end — what is wanted is honest blocks.<Cite src="ethereum-roadmap-pbs" />

It is a good argument and it is not a complete one. Verification catches a block that is *invalid*. It
does not catch a block that is valid and omits you. Censorship is the failure mode that survives the
asymmetry argument intact, which is why the same roadmap page pairs PBS with inclusion
lists — a mechanism letting proposers force transactions they can see into the next
block.<Cite src="ethereum-roadmap-pbs" /> Keep that pairing in mind: the argument for tolerating
concentrated building depends on a censorship remedy existing, and the concrete proposal for one,
EIP-7805, is a draft that was declined for the Glamsterdam upgrade and deferred.<Cite src="eip-7805" />

## What is actually live

Here is the thing that surprises people most, and it sets up the rest of the module.

In-protocol proposer-builder separation **has not shipped**. It requires changes to the consensus
protocol — the fork choice rule itself has to change — and the roadmap page states that there is no
finalised specification yet.<Cite src="ethereum-roadmap-pbs" /> The current vehicle for enshrining it
is EIP-7732, and it has not shipped either; sources disagree on its timing and none of them should be
treated as a date.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

What exists instead is an out-of-protocol implementation: middleware run alongside a validator's own
clients, connected to a market of builders through intermediaries, which the canonical documentation
describes as a temporary working implementation of PBS carried out with higher trust
assumptions.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" /><Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-docs" />

"Temporary" has now been the state of affairs for years, and this middleware builds the large
majority of Ethereum blocks. The next lesson takes that pipeline apart hop by hop.

<Check type="predict" concept="pbs">
A team is designing a new L2 with a single sequencer that they control. Their reasoning:

> "Because we run the only sequencer, there is no builder market, no auction and no relay. So there
> is no MEV on our chain, and we do not need PBS."

Say which parts of that are true, which are false, and what has actually happened to the extractable
value.

<Answer>
**True: there is no builder market, no auction and no relay.** Those are all consequences of a
competitive ordering market, and a single sequencer has no competitors to run an auction among.

**True, in a narrow and real sense: sandwiching largely disappears.** Sandwiching needs advance
visibility of the victim's transaction, and a sequencer with no public pool does not provide it. This
is a genuine user-facing improvement, and rollups without public mempools do measurably better on this
specific attack.

**False: there is no MEV.** The two ingredients are unchanged — shared mutable state and an ordering
monopoly per block. Cross-venue arbitrage still exists, liquidations still race, and the ordering is
still worth money. Nothing has been removed.

**What actually happened: the monopoly was consolidated rather than dissolved.** On L1 the ordering
right is auctioned every twelve seconds to whoever bids most, and the proceeds flow to a rotating
proposer. On this L2 it is held permanently by one party that also wrote the software, and there is no
auction, no disclosure and no rotation. That is a *larger* concentration than L1's, not a smaller one,
and calling it "no MEV" describes the absence of a market, not the absence of value.

**What follows for the design.** The honest version of the team's position is a policy statement:
"ordering on our chain is at our discretion; here is the rule we follow, here is how you can check we
followed it, and here is what happens when we stop." First-come-first-served, a private pool with a
stated non-extraction commitment, or an encrypted ordering scheme are all defensible answers with
different trade-offs. "There is no MEV" is not one of them, and a reviewer who knows this material
will read it as either a misunderstanding or a claim the team has not tested.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The block-building pipeline** is the next lesson: four roles, a wire protocol with three endpoints,
and one hop where a party outside the protocol holds a block hostage by design.

**Builder concentration** is the lesson after, and it is where the argument in this lesson gets its
scorecard. PBS was meant to decentralise the reward from extraction. It succeeded at that, and it
concentrated the extraction itself into very few hands — both things are true, and the second is now
the main argument for enshrining PBS in-protocol.<Cite src="arxiv-private-orderflow" />

**The DeFi track's MEV module** goes after the application layer properly: what a sandwich costs a
specific user, why slippage tolerance is a security parameter that wallets present as a convenience
dial, why arbitrage profit is paid by liquidity providers rather than by traders, and what intents and
order-flow auctions actually change.

**Rollup sequencing** in the scaling track picks up exactly where the check above left off: what an
ordering monopoly looks like when it is held permanently by one party, and what a chain can offer in
place of an auction.<Cite src="ethereum-docs-pos" />
