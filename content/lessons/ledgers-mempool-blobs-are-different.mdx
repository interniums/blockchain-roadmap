A rollup's batcher is ported from posting calldata to posting blobs. The submission path is
unchanged, the signing is unchanged, the fee-bump helper — battle-tested over two years and thousands
of batches — is unchanged.

Batches stop landing. The bump helper returns `replacement transaction underpriced` on every attempt,
regardless of how far the fees are raised. A transaction sent slightly out of order disappears within
a minute rather than waiting. And when the team adds a small ordinary transaction from the same
address to nudge things along, that is rejected too.

None of this is a bug in their code. Type-3 transactions are not admitted to the pool everything else
uses. They go to a separate pool with a separate policy, and the policy differs in at least six ways
that break tooling written against the ordinary one.

## Why the rules are different at all

The blob pool's design rationale is written out in go-ethereum's package documentation, and it is
worth reading as design rather than as trivia, because every rule below follows from one of these
lines.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" />

Blob transactions are large — the initial design targets 128 KB blobs — so they must traverse the
network as few times as possible.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" /> Block blob space is small, so pool
churn is expected to be low and the pool can afford a persistent disk backend rather than living in
memory.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" /> Blobs exist for layer twos committing to their own state, which
means there is essentially no legitimate reason to cancel or replace one apart from a fee
adjustment.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" /> Replacements are therefore expensive and should be
"aggressively discouraged", and evicting an already-propagated blob transaction is described directly
as "a huge DoS vector".<Cite src="geth-blobpool" />

The through-line: the ordinary pool is optimised for a chaotic market of small transactions from
strangers. The blob pool is optimised for a handful of well-behaved industrial senders posting large
objects on a schedule. Nearly every difference is that assumption made concrete.

## Rule one: three fee fields, all of them doubled

The ordinary pool compares two fee fields against a threshold of the incumbent plus ten percent. The
blob pool compares three, against a threshold of the incumbent plus one hundred percent.

The default is explicit in the pool's configuration: `PriceBump: 100`, with the source comment
"either have patience or be aggressive, no mushy ground".<Cite src="geth-blobpool-config" />

The check runs in two stages, mirroring the ordinary pool's structure but over three
dimensions.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" /> First, strict improvement: if the candidate's execution fee
cap, execution tip cap, or blob gas fee cap is less than or equal to the incumbent's corresponding
field, it is rejected.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" /> Then the threshold: each of the three minimums is
computed as the incumbent's value multiplied by 100 plus the bump, divided by 100, and the candidate
must meet all three.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" /> Failing any single one returns
`replacement transaction underpriced`, with a message naming which field
fell short.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" /><Cite src="geth-txpool-errors" />

At the default bump that means: to replace a blob transaction you must **double** `maxFeePerGas`,
**double** `maxPriorityFeePerGas`, and **double** `maxFeePerBlobGas`, all in the same
transaction.<Cite src="geth-blobpool-config" /> A helper that raises two fields by ten percent fails
at the first gate and never reaches the arithmetic.

This is the rule that most often shows up as a mysterious production incident, because the bump
helper is usually shared code and usually correct for everything else the service sends.

## Rule two: a nonce gap is measured in minutes, not hours

The design principle is stated flatly in the package documentation: nonce-gapped blob transactions
are disallowed, as one of the measures against cancellation being used as a denial-of-service
vector.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" />

The current implementation is slightly softer than the principle, and the exact shape matters if you
are writing a batcher.

Gapped blob transactions are not admitted to the pool proper. They are held in a small in-memory
buffer that exists purely to absorb out-of-order arrival, with three bounds. The buffer holds at most
128 gapped transactions across the whole pool.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" /> Entries are evicted after
a lifetime of one minute.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" /> And per-account admission to the buffer is
governed by an allowance computed as the base-ten logarithm of the account's current nonce plus one,
capped by the account's remaining pool slots and reduced by whatever it already has
buffered.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" />

Work the allowance through, because it produces a rule you can act on. An account that has never
transacted has a nonce of 0, so its allowance is the logarithm of 1, which is 0: **no gapped blob
transaction from a fresh account is buffered at all.** An account with a nonce in the tens gets 1. In
the hundreds, 2. In the thousands, 3.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" /> Gap tolerance is earned by
history, and even a long-running batcher earns only a handful of slots.

Compare that with the ordinary pool: 1024 global non-executable slots, 64 per account, and a
three-hour lifetime.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" /> A batcher that tolerates a brief nonce gap and
recovers within minutes survives on the legacy pool and loses the transaction on the blob pool.

**The operational rule: treat any out-of-order blob transaction as gone.** Do not build recovery
logic that assumes it will still be there when the missing nonce lands.

## Rule three: a hard cap per account, and only one pool at a time

Two more constraints that have no analogue in the ordinary pool.

**Sixteen blob transactions per account, full stop.** The cap exists to bound how much data one
account can push onto the network before cancelling it with an ordinary transaction, and the source
notes that transactions resurrected by a reorg count against the same limit — so pushing it lower
would break reorg recovery.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" /> There is no per-account guarantee-versus-cap
distinction as there is in the legacy pool. Sixteen is the ceiling.

**An address may use one subpool at a time.** go-ethereum's subpools share a reservation tracker
whose stated purpose is to ensure that one address "cannot initiate transactions, authorizations, and
other state-changing behaviors in different pools at the same
time".<Cite src="geth-txpool-reserver" /> A sender holding blob transactions in the blob pool cannot
also have ordinary transactions pending in the legacy pool from the same address, which is the
enforcement of the design note that the presence of blob transactions excludes non-blob
ones.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" />

That is why the "nudge it along with a small ordinary transaction" instinct fails, and it is a good
reason to give a batcher its own address rather than sharing one with an operations wallet.

**Delegated senders get one in-flight transaction.** If the sender has a delegation designator or a
pending authorization, the blob pool permits a single in-flight executable transaction from that
address, allowing a replacement at the same nonce but nothing stacked or gapped behind
it.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" /> The comment gives the reason: without it, an attacker could endlessly
stack blob transactions from a delegated sender.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" /><Cite src="eip-7702" />

## Rule four: nobody broadcasts your blob, and most nodes never fetch it

The ordinary pool pushes complete transaction objects to a random fraction of peers and announces
hashes to the rest.<Cite src="devp2p-eth-wire" /> For blob transactions that is forbidden at the
specification level: nodes "MUST NOT automatically broadcast blob transactions to their peers", and
must announce them by hash only, to be requested explicitly.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

Since Fusaka this has gone considerably further, and the direction is towards fewer nodes holding
your data rather than more. Blobs are erasure-coded into cells, and as of eth/72 the announcement
message itself carries the announcing peer's per-cell availability.<Cite src="devp2p-eth-wire" /> On
receiving an announcement, a node makes a probabilistic choice: with probability `p`, no smaller than
`MIN_P` of 0.15, it fetches the full blobs; otherwise it fetches only the cells in its own consensus
node's custody set, and only after seeing `AVAILABILITY_THRESHOLD` of 2 distinct full-availability
announcements.<Cite src="devp2p-eth-wire" /> EIP-8070, which introduced this, gives the arithmetic: a
validator downloading as little as one eighth of the data brings average bandwidth to roughly a
quarter of the full blobpool's, a fourfold reduction, while keeping a connected backbone of nodes
that hold complete payloads.<Cite src="eip-8070" /> go-ethereum's blob pool carries the corresponding
knob as a `FetchProbability` configuration field.<Cite src="geth-blobpool-config" />

Two consequences for anyone reasoning about a blob transaction's whereabouts. **"Is it propagated" is
not a yes-or-no question** — most nodes deliberately hold only a fraction of it. And the pre-Fusaka
mental model of one proof per blob is wrong: blob transactions now carry per-cell proofs, and library
code written against the original wrapper does not parse them.<Cite src="eip-7594" /><Cite src="geth-blobpool" />

<Aside kind="warn">
There is one more silence to know about. The pool deliberately suppresses announcement of blob
transactions whose fee caps sit far below the current base fee and blob fee, delaying the
announcement until fees come down, on the stated principle that it is not the blob pool's job to
store limit orders below market.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" /> So a blob transaction can be accepted by
your node, stored on its disk, and never mentioned to a single peer. Local acceptance is not evidence
of anything.
</Aside>

## Rule five: they leave the pool late, and they cannot be creations

Two smaller differences that catch specific classes of code.

**Retention past inclusion.** An ordinary transaction is dropped from the pool the moment it appears
in a block, because a reorg can recover it from that block. Blob data is not in the execution chain,
so a reorg cannot re-pool it — which is why blobs are retained on disk until finalised, and why the
Engine API has to hand back blobs from blocks the node never saw on the
network.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" /> A "still in the pool" signal means something different here.

**No contract creation.** EIP-4844 specifies that a blob transaction's `to` field must not be nil and
must always be a 20-byte address, so a blob transaction can never take the form of a contract
creation.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Any generic transaction builder that treats a missing `to` as
"deploy" produces an invalid type-3 transaction.

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="blob-pool-different-rules">
An L2 batcher has this retry policy, which has worked correctly for two years against calldata
batches:

```ts
// Bump both fee fields by 15% (comfortably over the 10% minimum) and resend.
const bumped = {
  ...original,
  maxFeePerGas: (original.maxFeePerGas * 115n) / 100n,
  maxPriorityFeePerGas: (original.maxPriorityFeePerGas * 115n) / 100n,
};
await client.sendRawTransaction(sign(bumped));
```

It is now used for type-3 blob transactions. Name every way this fails, and give the policy that
replaces it.

<Answer>
**Failure 1 — the third field is not bumped, and it is not optional.** A blob transaction carries
`maxFeePerBlobGas` alongside the two execution fee fields.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> The blob pool checks
all three: strict improvement on each, then a threshold on each.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" /> Spreading
`...original` carries the old blob fee cap through unchanged, so the strict-improvement stage rejects
the candidate before any percentage is evaluated.

**Failure 2 — 15% is nowhere near enough.** The blob pool's default bump is 100, not
10.<Cite src="geth-blobpool-config" /> The thresholds are the incumbent's values doubled, on all three
fields.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" /> Even with the third field included, a 15% bump fails all three
threshold checks.

**Failure 3 — the error is uninformative unless you read it.** The rejection is
`replacement transaction underpriced` with a message naming the specific field and the required
penalty.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" /><Cite src="geth-txpool-errors" /> Retry logic that matches on the
error class and escalates blindly will loop, because the field that is short may not be the field
being escalated.

**Failure 4 — the retry loop's structure is wrong for this pool.** Doubling three fees is expensive
enough that a naive escalation loop is a real financial exposure rather than a nuisance. The blob
pool's documentation is explicit that this is the intent: replacements should be aggressively
discouraged, and layer twos are expected to have no reason to replace apart from a fee
adjustment.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" />

**Failure 5 — the surrounding recovery logic probably assumes the wrong gap semantics.** If a retry
is ever sent at a nonce ahead of an outstanding one, the ordinary pool would park it for up to three
hours<Cite src="geth-legacypool" /> while the blob pool buffers it for about a minute, within an
allowance of at most the base-ten logarithm of the account's nonce, and drops it
afterwards.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" />

**The replacement policy.** Bump all three fields by the pool's configured factor with a margin,
discovering that factor from the endpoint's rejection message rather than hard-coding it. Cap total
escalations and the absolute fee, and alert rather than escalate past the cap. Serialise submissions
so a gap is never created, and treat any gapped blob transaction as lost rather than waiting for it.
Give the batcher a dedicated address, because a sender using the blob pool cannot simultaneously have
ordinary transactions pending from the same address.<Cite src="geth-txpool-reserver" /> And never
conclude anything from the transaction being absent at a peer, since most nodes now hold only their
custody cells by design.<Cite src="devp2p-eth-wire" />
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**Rollup economics** rest on batch posting landing predictably. The difference between a batcher that
recovers from congestion and one that stalls is almost entirely the policy in this lesson.

**The blob fee market** is a second EIP-1559-style mechanism running independently of the execution
one, which is why `maxFeePerBlobGas` can be the binding constraint while your execution fees are
generous.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

**Data availability sampling** is where the fetch-probability rule comes from. The blob pool is
becoming a sampling network rather than a replication network, and the same idea drives how the
consensus layer distributes blob data.<Cite src="eip-8070" /><Cite src="eip-7594" />

**Nothing guarantees inclusion**, the last lesson in this module, applies here with sharper edges: a
batcher's transaction can be silently unannounced, silently dropped from a reorder buffer, or simply
not selected, and none of those produce an error you receive.
