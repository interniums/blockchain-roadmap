A transaction is gone.

Not reverted — there is no receipt. Not replaced — no other transaction from that account shares its
nonce, and the account nonce has not moved. It was pending on three different providers an hour ago
and it is `null` on all of them now. Nothing in any log records a rejection, because nothing sent
one.

This is the ordinary end of a transaction's life, and it is worth treating as ordinary rather than as
an anomaly to be explained away. This lesson closes the module with the complete list of ways a
transaction leaves without landing, and then with the harder fact underneath it: **even a perfect
transaction has no right to be included.** No fee buys that right, and as of today no protocol
mechanism provides it.

## Eight ways out, none of which notify you

Read from the client, in roughly the order you will meet them.

**1 — Replaced.** Another transaction at the same sender and nonce cleared the price-bump gates, and
the pool overwrote the entry.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" /> This is the only exit on the list you
caused deliberately.

**2 — Refused on admission because the pool is full and you are cheap.** When occupied slots would
exceed `GlobalSlots` plus `GlobalQueue`, go-ethereum checks whether the arriving transaction is
underpriced relative to the pool's contents and returns `transaction underpriced` if
so.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" /><Cite src="geth-txpool-errors" /> Your transaction never entered.

**3 — Refused because the pool has churned too much recently.** Even a competitively priced
transaction is rejected with a pool-overflow error if more than a quarter of `GlobalSlots` worth of
changes have already occurred since the last reorg — a throttle that limits how much work one
inter-reorg window can do.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" /> You did nothing wrong and were still turned
away.

**4 — Refused because it would displace a pending transaction with a future one.** If the arriving
transaction is itself gapped and making room for it would evict something currently executable, the
pool restores the would-be victims and rejects the newcomer instead.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" />

**5 — Evicted on price after admission.** The pool discards the cheapest entries to make room for
better-paying arrivals.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" />

**6 — Evicted on fairness, ignoring price entirely.** `truncatePending` penalises accounts holding
more than `AccountSlots` pending transactions and removes their highest-nonce entries first,
consulting no fee field at any point.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" /> A well-priced transaction from a
busy sender is dropped here, and the drop manufactures a nonce gap in that sender's sequence.

**7 — Expired.** Queued transactions carry a lifetime — three hours by default — enforced by an
eviction ticker that runs once a minute.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" /> In the blob pool the
equivalent window is about one minute.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" />

**8 — Invalidated by state.** On each new head the pool demotes transactions that have become
unexecutable and deletes those invalidated by low nonce or low
balance.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" /> A balance that fell below `gas_limit` multiplied by
`max_fee_per_gas` is enough, since that is the reservation the fee mechanism
requires.<Cite src="eip-1559" />

And a ninth that is not an exit at all, because there was never an entry: **never announced.** The
blob pool suppresses announcements for transactions whose fee caps sit far below current
fees.<Cite src="geth-blobpool" /> A transaction submitted to a private endpoint is not gossiped by
design.<Cite src="flashbots-protect-docs" /> In both cases it exists somewhere and is invisible
everywhere you would look.

### Why none of them tell you

There is no rejection message in the protocol, and the specification chose that deliberately.
Implementations "must not disconnect peers for sending invalid transactions, and should simply
discard them instead", because the peer "might be operating under slightly different validation
rules".<Cite src="devp2p-eth-wire" /> The design assumption is that nodes disagree, so a node
declining a transaction is not evidence of anything and is not worth reporting.

The consequence for your system: **the only negative signal that exists is the absence of a positive
one, and that absence never becomes conclusive.** You get an error from the node you submitted
to — synchronously, at submission — and after that, silence in both directions.

## The harder fact: nobody owes you a slot

Everything above is client policy, and client policy can be argued with. The next part cannot.

There is no rule of the Ethereum protocol that obliges a block proposer to include any particular
transaction. A block containing only the proposer's own transactions is valid. A block that omits a
specific address indefinitely is valid. Blocks are checked for what they contain, never for what they
left out.

For most of Ethereum's history this was a theoretical exposure held in check by numbers: thousands of
proposers, each seeing roughly the same public pool, meant that a transaction excluded by one would
be picked up by the next. Censorship required coordination across a large, changing set.

Proposer-builder separation moved the choice. The proposer signs a header for the block that pays it
the most; the block itself is assembled by a builder.<Cite src="mev-boost-docs" /> The decision about
what a block contains is now made by a small number of specialists, and the previous lesson gave the
mechanism that keeps their number small: exclusive order flow raises a builder's block valuation,
which wins more auctions, which attracts more exclusive flow.<Cite src="arxiv-private-orderflow" />

So the practical question changed shape. It is no longer "would thousands of validators coordinate to
exclude you", which is hard. It is "would the handful of parties who assemble most blocks decline to
include you", which is considerably less hard, and which requires no coordination at all if they
happen to share a constraint.

## FOCIL: the mechanism that would fix this, and its status

The response the protocol has designed is EIP-7805, fork-choice enforced inclusion lists. Reading how
it works is worthwhile even though it is not live, because its structure is a direct answer to
everything in this module.

The mechanism.<Cite src="eip-7805" /> In each slot a committee of validators — `IL_COMMITTEE_SIZE` is
16 — is selected. Each member builds one inclusion list from its own view of the mempool and gossips
it. The next slot's proposer and attesters store and forward the lists they see. The proposer, or the
builder assembling on its behalf, must include the listed transactions. Attesters vote for the block
only if it satisfies every inclusion list they stored.

Three design choices are worth pulling out.

**The enforcement is in the fork choice, not in block validity.** The execution-layer check runs after
the payload executes and asks whether any listed transaction *not* present could have been validly
appended — nonce and balance passing, and space remaining. If any could have, an error is returned to
the consensus layer, and although the block remains valid, attesters will not vote for
it.<Cite src="eip-7805" /> The punishment for censoring is that your block loses the fork choice, not
that it is rejected.

**The lists are subjective by design.** Each member builds "according to their subjective view of the
mempool", and lists are propagated with no execution-layer validity checking at all, to avoid the
computational overhead — so a list may contain invalid transactions.<Cite src="eip-7805" /> This is
the first lesson of this module turned into a security property. Because there is no canonical
mempool and 16 independent members each hold a different one, an adversary wanting a transaction
excluded must suppress it from many independent views at once.

**It is bounded to stay cheap.** A list may not exceed `MAX_BYTES_PER_INCLUSION_LIST`, 8192 bytes,
and the validation rules exist specifically to bound the bandwidth that list propagation
consumes.<Cite src="eip-7805" />

The motivation section names the problem in the same terms as the previous lesson: block building was
auctioned to specialised entities, this led to a few sophisticated builders dominating block
production, and censorship resistance deteriorated as a result.<Cite src="eip-7805" />

### And it is not shipping

EIP-7805 is a Draft.<Cite src="eip-7805" /> The Glamsterdam hardfork meta EIP lists the upgrade's
scheduled, considered and declined proposals, and EIP-7805 appears under **Declined for
Inclusion**.<Cite src="eip-7773" /> It is not alone there: EIP-7782, which would have cut slot time
from 12 seconds to 6, was also declined.<Cite src="eip-7773" /><Cite src="eip-7782" />

The same document lists EIP-7732, enshrined proposer-builder separation, as **Scheduled for
Inclusion**.<Cite src="eip-7773" /> Hold those two facts together, because the combination is the
current state of this problem: the upgrade that moves block building into the protocol is going ahead,
and the mechanism that would force those blocks to include eligible transactions is not.

**So as of August 2026, there is no protocol-level inclusion guarantee on Ethereum, and none is
scheduled for the next upgrade.** Any material telling you inclusion lists are arriving in the next
fork is out of date.

<Aside kind="note">
Nothing here says censorship is currently widespread. It says the property is not guaranteed by the
protocol, that it currently rests on the commercial behaviour of a small number of builders, and that
the mechanism intended to make it structural has been deferred. Those are the terms on which to
design, and they are unchanged by how benign this month's behaviour is.
</Aside>

## Designing for a system that does not promise inclusion

Four rules, in order of how much money they save.

**A submitted transaction is not a completed action, and no timeout changes that.** The only
authoritative facts are the receipt and the account nonce. A transaction with no receipt whose nonce
has not been consumed is still live and may land at any time, including after your system decided it
had failed.

**Give every intent a deadline the chain enforces, not one your service enforces.** A contract-level
deadline parameter, a signed message with an expiry, or a nonce you will deliberately burn are all
mechanisms that make lateness *safe* rather than merely detected. This is why every serious swap
interface carries a deadline: not because it makes inclusion faster, but because it converts an
unbounded wait into a bounded failure.

**Give up by replacement, never by re-issue.** Abandoning a transaction and signing a new one at a
fresh nonce makes the two additive: if both land, both execute. Replacing at the same nonce makes
them mutually exclusive. The cost of the second option is that it can lose the race; the cost of the
first is that the user pays twice.

**Instrument the exits you can distinguish.** You cannot detect eviction, and you should stop trying.
You can detect a great deal else: submission errors, which arrive synchronously and name their
reason<Cite src="geth-txpool-errors" />; your transaction sitting in the `queued` half of a pool you
can query<Cite src="geth-rpc-txpool" />; a `maxFeePerGas` now below the current base fee, which is a
one-line comparison<Cite src="eip-1559" />; and blocks elapsed since submission, which is a fact
about your latency budget rather than about queue position.

You are designing the pending-transaction states for a payments product. Payments are initiated from
a hot wallet, and a confirmed payment triggers an irreversible action: releasing a digital good to the
recipient.

Specify what the system does at 15 seconds, 2 minutes and 10 minutes after submission, and say what a
"Cancel payment" button does at the protocol level. Then name the thing your design cannot honestly
tell the user.

<Answer>
**At 15 seconds — do nothing, and say nothing definite.** One or two blocks have passed. A
transaction not yet included after two blocks is entirely ordinary. The correct user-facing state is
"submitted", the correct internal state is "awaiting receipt", and the correct action is to poll for a
receipt rather than for pool presence, since pool absence is a statement about one node and nothing
more.<Cite src="devp2p-eth-wire" />

**At 2 minutes — diagnose, do not escalate blindly.** Roughly ten blocks. Run the two checks that
actually distinguish causes. Compare `maxFeePerGas` against the current base fee: if the ceiling is
lower, the transaction is ineligible for every block until the base fee falls, and no tip
helps.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> Compare the `latest` and `pending` transaction counts for the sending
address, and read the `queued` half of a pool if you can: a nonce gap means the transaction is not
competing at all and the fix is to land the missing nonce.<Cite src="geth-rpc-txpool" />

Then act on the diagnosis, not on the clock. Ineligible on price means a replacement raising both fee
fields past the price-bump gates. A nonce gap means a new transaction at the missing nonce. Neither is
"try again", and re-issuing at a fresh nonce is the one action that can make the user pay twice.

**At 10 minutes — replace with an explicit cancel and stop the clock.** Fifty blocks. The transaction
may still be live in some pool, so abandoning it is unsafe: the only way to make it impossible is to
consume its nonce. Send a zero-value self-transfer at that nonce with fees bumped past the
thresholds, and treat the payment as terminal only when the account nonce has advanced past it and
you know which transaction advanced it. Note that this can lose — the original may already be in a
builder's block — so the terminal state is decided by which transaction got the receipt, not by which
one you sent last.

**What the design cannot honestly tell the user, and this is the important half.** It cannot tell them
their payment failed. Absence of a receipt is never conclusive: the transaction may be sitting in a
pool you cannot see, it may have been evicted from every pool with no notification of any
kind<Cite src="geth-legacypool" />, or it may have been routed privately and be invisible by
construction<Cite src="flashbots-protect-docs" />. The system can honestly say "we stopped waiting"
and, after the cancel confirms, "this payment can no longer execute". Any interface that says "failed"
before the nonce is consumed is asserting something it does not know.

**The irreversible action is where this becomes expensive.** Release the digital good on a receipt
with a stated number of confirmations behind it, never on a timeout and never on an optimistic
pending state. The whole point of the list of exits in this lesson is that "it has been a while" is
not evidence about anything, and a design that treats it as evidence is choosing a threshold with no
meaning.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**Censorship resistance** stops being a slogan once you have seen that it is currently a property of
builder behaviour rather than of the protocol, and that the mechanism to make it structural has been
deferred.<Cite src="eip-7773" />

**Layer-two escape hatches** are this problem one level up, and they are why every credible rollup has
a forced-inclusion path that does not depend on its sequencer's goodwill. Reading those designs after
this module, you will recognise them as inclusion lists built by an application because the base
layer does not provide one.

**Enshrined proposer-builder separation** changes who the builder is and what they are accountable
for, and it is scheduled.<Cite src="eip-7773" /> Watching how it interacts with the concentration
mechanism from the previous lesson is the most interesting open question in this part of the protocol.

**FOCIL** itself is worth revisiting when it is next proposed, and the thing to carry until then is
its shape: enforcement through the fork choice rather than block validity, 16 subjective views rather
than one canonical mempool, and a bounded byte budget so the mechanism cannot be turned into a
bandwidth attack.<Cite src="eip-7805" />

And the practice exercises for this module both ask you to *measure* rather than to look up — the
nonce gap in a live pool, and the bump threshold on the endpoint you actually use. Every number in
these eight lessons is either a specification constant or a client default, and the client defaults
are exactly the kind of number that is true until someone changes a flag.
