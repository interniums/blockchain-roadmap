Two transactions are eligible for the next block. One has been in the pool for forty minutes. The
other arrived two hundred milliseconds ago.

The older one has no advantage. Not a small advantage, not a tie-breaking advantage — none. There is
no field anywhere recording when it arrived, no structure ordered by that field, and nobody
consulting it. If the newer transaction offers a better effective tip, or arrives inside a bundle
that pays the builder more, it goes in and the older one waits again.

This is the mental model that costs teams the most money, because first-in-first-out makes every
failure look like a delay. If you believe there is a line, then a transaction that has not landed is
a transaction that is *nearly there*, and the correct response is patience. There is no line, so
waiting is not progress, and the correct response is to change something.

## The pool is indexed by sender and sorted by price

Look at the structures. `txpool_content` returns transactions keyed by sender address and then by
nonce.<Cite src="geth-rpc-txpool" /> That is the only ordering the pool imposes on your
transactions, and it is a *constraint* — nonce 8 cannot execute before nonce 7 — rather than a
position.

Across senders there is no order at all except price, and the price ordering is not fixed. On every
new head, go-ethereum computes the base fee that will apply to the *next* block, sets it on the
pool's priced list, and re-heaps.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" /> In the same pass it demotes anything
that has become unexecutable and promotes anything that has become executable, deleting transactions
invalidated by low nonce or low balance.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" />

Read that as a claim about your transaction: **its rank is recomputed from scratch every slot —
twelve seconds at the current slot time<Cite src="eip-7782" /> — against a base fee that has
changed.** Nothing about the transaction changed. Its position
did, because position is derived rather than stored.

The reason the re-computation matters is the arithmetic from the previous lesson. What the pool
sorts on is the effective tip — the smaller of your priority fee and the gap between your ceiling
and the base fee.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> When the base fee moves, every transaction's effective tip
moves, and they do not move together: a transaction with a generous ceiling barely notices while one
sitting near its ceiling loses most of its bid. Relative ordering reshuffles without a single new
transaction arriving.

## Selection belongs to whoever builds the block

The pool does not decide anything. The wire specification's own framing is that nodes exchange
pending transactions in order to relay them to block producers, "which will pick them for inclusion
into the blockchain".<Cite src="devp2p-eth-wire" /> *Pick* is the operative verb, and everything
downstream of it is outside the pool's control.

In 2026 the picker is usually not the validator. Under proposer-builder separation the right to
assemble a block is auctioned to specialised builders, and the proposer signs a header for the
winning bid.<Cite src="ethereum-roadmap-pbs" /><Cite src="mev-boost-docs" /> A builder is running an
optimiser whose objective is the value it can pay the proposer, over a candidate set that includes
the public pool *and* whatever reached it privately.

So the selection rule is not "highest tip first". It is "the combination of transactions, in the
order that maximises what I can bid". Those coincide when every candidate is an independent
fee-paying transaction. They diverge as soon as any candidate's value depends on its position
relative to another — which is what the searcher economy is built on, and which is why a
higher-tipping transaction can lose to a lower-tipping one that completes somebody's profitable
sequence.

## Eviction is not FIFO either, and it is not always about price

When capacity binds, the natural guess is that the pool drops the cheapest transactions. It does that
in one place and something quite different in another, and the difference is what turns a batch
sender's bad day into a nonce gap.

**On admission, when the pool is over capacity, price decides.** If the total occupied slots would
exceed `GlobalSlots` plus `GlobalQueue`, go-ethereum first checks whether the arriving transaction is
underpriced relative to the pool's current contents and rejects it outright with `transaction
underpriced` if so. Otherwise it discards enough of the cheapest entries to make
room.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" /> There is a throttle on top: if more than a quarter of
`GlobalSlots` worth of changes have already happened since the last reorg, the pool stops evicting
and returns a pool-overflow error instead.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" />

**On truncation, when the pending set is over its limit, fairness decides — and it is price-blind.**
`truncatePending` assembles a "spam order" of accounts holding more than `AccountSlots` pending
transactions, penalises the largest transactors first, and removes their **highest-nonce**
transactions, on the reasoning that these are the ones executing furthest in the
future.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" /> Fee fields are not consulted at any point in that
procedure.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" />

Sit with the consequence, because it is genuinely counter-intuitive. A service running one hot EOA
with thirty pending transactions is, by definition, over the sixteen-slot per-account guarantee and
therefore in the spam order. Under global pressure it loses the *tail* of its own nonce sequence
first — regardless of how well those transactions are priced. And the tail is exactly where a gap
does the most damage: everything the service sends next sits behind a hole it did not
create.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" />

The design intent is defensible: without it, one well-funded account could occupy the whole pool. The
operational lesson is that **paying more does not protect you from every eviction path**, and that
"spread across several sending accounts" is a real availability measure rather than an optimisation.

## The other half: the pool is a broadcast

Everything above concerns what happens to your transaction. The second property of the public pool
concerns what happens to your *information*, and it operates from the instant you broadcast.

A transaction in the public pool is fully specified and fully readable. Recipient, calldata, value,
gas limit, both fee fields, and a signature proving the sender authorised exactly this. Any node that
asks a peer for it receives the complete object.<Cite src="devp2p-eth-wire" /> Pool synchronisation
is designed to spread it widely and quickly: on connection both sides exchange hashes for their
entire pools, and new transactions are pushed in full to a random fraction of peers and announced by
hash to the rest.<Cite src="devp2p-eth-wire" />

So the honest description of `eth_sendRawTransaction` against a public endpoint is: **publish a
signed, machine-readable statement of what you are about to do, to everyone who will listen, before
it happens.**

That is the precondition for everything in the extractable-value literature. An observer who can read
your intent before execution, and who can pay a builder to place transactions around yours, can
profit from the difference between the price you expected and the price you get. Ethereum's own
documentation describes maximal extractable value as the value that can be extracted from block
production in excess of the block reward and fees, by including, excluding and changing the order of
transactions.<Cite src="ethereum-org-mev" />

Two things follow that are worth stating flatly, because they are usually softened.

**Frontrunning is not an exploit of a bug.** It is the correct use of published information combined
with a selection process that was never promising you an order. Nothing is being violated. If your
design assumed otherwise, the assumption was the defect.

**Visibility is not the only ingredient — ordering power is.** Seeing your transaction is worthless
without the ability to be placed relative to it. That ability is bought from builders, which is why
the answer to this problem in practice has been to change *who sees the transaction* rather than to
change how blocks are ordered. That is the next lesson, and it is a trade rather than a fix.

## What this forbids in your product

Four rules, all of them things teams ship anyway.

**Never display a queue position or a place in line.** There is no such quantity. Any number you show
is invented, and users will hold you to it.

**Never derive an estimate from elapsed time.** "Usually confirms in thirty seconds, it has been
twenty-five, nearly there" is reasoning about a queue. The correct inputs are the current base fee
against your ceiling, and the current distribution of effective tips against yours — quantities that
say whether you are eligible and competitive right now, which is the only thing that determines the
next block.

**Never treat elapsed time as evidence of anything except elapsed time.** A transaction pending for
ten minutes is not closer to inclusion than one pending for ten seconds. It is, however, closer to
its pool's lifetime limit and more likely to have seen an eviction event.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" />
Time makes things worse, not better.

**Never design a flow whose safety depends on ordering you did not enforce.** If two of your own
transactions must execute in order, they must share a sender and use consecutive nonces, or be
atomic within one transaction. Submitting them a second apart and hoping is not a design.

<Check type="explain" concept="mempool-pending-is-not-a-queue">
A team is asked why their exchange withdrawal, submitted at 14:02, is still pending at 14:11 while
withdrawals submitted at 14:09 have confirmed. Their support macro says: "the network is congested
and transactions are processed in order; yours is next."

Rewrite that explanation truthfully in a way a non-technical user can act on. Then name the one
timing consideration that genuinely does exist, and say why it does not rescue the original macro.

<Answer>
**The truthful version.** Transactions are not processed in order. Every twelve
seconds<Cite src="eip-7782" />, whoever builds the next block selects from all currently valid
transactions and picks the set that pays them the most. Your withdrawal has been offered for
selection roughly forty-five times and not chosen. The
later withdrawals were not ahead of it — they were priced for the market as it stood when they were
signed, and yours was priced for the market nine minutes ago.

What the user can do: authorise a replacement at a higher fee, or wait for the network price to fall
back below the maximum they already agreed to. Those are the only two levers, and which one applies
depends on whether the transaction is currently ineligible or merely outbid.

**Why "yours is next" is worse than wrong.** It is falsifiable in the user's favour and then
immediately falsified, which converts one support ticket into an escalation. It also removes the
user's agency: it tells them to wait, when waiting is the one action with no effect.

**The one real timing consideration.** Eligibility is evaluated per block, and a block's contents are
fixed at some point before it is proposed — a builder is assembling and bidding on a deadline. So a
transaction that reaches a builder after it has frozen its candidate set is competing for the block
after that one. Arriving in time to be *considered* is a real constraint measured in hundreds of
milliseconds.

That does not rescue the macro, for two reasons. It is a threshold, not a queue: being considered
confers no ranking, and a transaction that has been considered forty-five times has exactly the same
standing as one being considered for the first time. And it operates on a timescale of milliseconds
before each block, which has nothing to do with the nine minutes the user has been waiting.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**Private order flow** is the next lesson, and it is what happens when a large share of the network
concludes that publishing intent to everyone is a bad trade. Everything there follows from the two
halves of this one: selection is a revenue calculation, and the public pool is a broadcast.

**Extractable value** as a field is this lesson monetised. Nothing in it is a new mechanism —
sandwiching is visibility plus ordering power, and both were established here.

**Order flow auctions** invert the same two facts: if your intent is worth something to somebody,
that value can be auctioned on your behalf instead of taken from you.

**Inclusion guarantees.** The last lesson in this module asks what obliges a builder to include you
at all. Once you accept that selection is discretionary and revenue-driven, the answer stops being
surprising and becomes a design question the protocol has not yet answered.
