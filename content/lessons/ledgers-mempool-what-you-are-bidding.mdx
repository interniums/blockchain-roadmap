Two transactions sit in the same pool. Both set `maxFeePerGas` to 20 gwei. One sets
`maxPriorityFeePerGas` to 1 gwei; the other sets it to 5 gwei — a bid five times higher, and the
wallet that produced it charged the user accordingly.

The base fee climbs to 19.6 gwei. Both transactions are now bidding **0.4 gwei**. They are
indistinguishable to a block builder, the five-times-higher bid bought nothing, and neither wallet
will tell its user that this happened.

That is not an edge case. It is the arithmetic of the fee fields working exactly as specified, and it
is the single most consequential thing about EIP-1559 that does not appear in the diagrams. Track 01
established *why* the base fee is burned and how it moves — see
[the base-fee burn](/c/base-fee-burn) and [the fee mechanism](/c/eip-1559-tfm). This lesson is about
what the two numbers on your transaction do once they meet a block.

## The fields, and the arithmetic that consumes them

A type-2 transaction's payload is
`rlp([chain_id, nonce, max_priority_fee_per_gas, max_fee_per_gas, gas_limit, destination, amount, data, access_list, signature_y_parity, signature_r, signature_s])`.<Cite src="eip-1559" />
Four of those are prices or quantities you choose, and they do not do what their names suggest.

EIP-1559's reference implementation is short enough to follow line by line, and every claim below is
one of its lines.<Cite src="eip-1559" />

**The balance check reserves the whole ceiling.** Before anything else, the sender must be able to
afford `gas_limit` multiplied by `max_fee_per_gas` — the maximum, not the expected
cost.<Cite src="eip-1559" />

**Eligibility is a hard comparison against the block.** The transaction asserts that
`max_fee_per_gas` is at least the block's `base_fee_per_gas`.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> A transaction
whose ceiling sits below a block's base fee is not underpriced in that block. It is invalid for it.

**The tip is squeezed, not paid as written.**
`priority_fee_per_gas = min(max_priority_fee_per_gas, max_fee_per_gas - base_fee_per_gas)`.<Cite src="eip-1559" />
This is the line that produced the scene at the top of the lesson, and it is the one almost nobody
carries in their head.

**The price actually charged is the sum.**
`effective_gas_price = priority_fee_per_gas + base_fee_per_gas`, and the `GASPRICE` opcode returns
that value.<Cite src="eip-1559" />

**Unused gas is refunded at that price.** The signer is charged `gas_limit` times the effective price
up front and refunded `gas_limit - gas_used` times the effective price
afterwards.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> The base-fee portion is burned and the producer receives only the
priority fee.<Cite src="eip-1559" />

Read as a set, those lines say something a fee slider cannot express: **your ceiling and your bid are
not independent.** The ceiling caps the bid. The base fee is filled first, and your tip is whatever
survives underneath the ceiling.

## The squeeze, worked through

Take one transaction and hold it fixed: `maxFeePerGas` 25 gwei, `maxPriorityFeePerGas` 2 gwei, and
watch four blocks go by.

**Subgoal — establish the calm case, so the contrast is visible.** Base fee 10 gwei. The gap between
ceiling and base fee is 15 gwei, which is comfortably more than 2, so the tip is
`min(2, 15) = 2` gwei and the effective price is 12 gwei.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> Everything behaves
as the interface implies.

**Subgoal — approach the ceiling and watch the bid decay.** Base fee 23.5 gwei. The gap is 1.5 gwei,
which is less than 2, so the tip is `min(2, 1.5) = 1.5` gwei — a 25% cut to your bid that no field on
your transaction records. Effective price 25 gwei, which is your entire ceiling.<Cite src="eip-1559" />

**Subgoal — reach the ceiling exactly.** Base fee 25 gwei. The gap is 0, so the tip is 0. The
transaction is still *eligible*: the assertion is that the ceiling is at least the base
fee.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> It is eligible and bidding nothing, which in a contested block means it
will not be chosen, and in an uncontested one it may still be included.

**Subgoal — cross the ceiling.** Base fee 26 gwei. The assertion fails. The transaction cannot appear
in this block at all, at any price, and no property of the transaction can change that. When the base
fee falls back below 25 it becomes eligible again with no action from you.

**Subgoal — name what the user saw.** One spinner, for the whole sequence. From outside, a decaying
bid, a zero bid and an ineligible transaction are the same event: nothing happening.

## Two failure modes that look identical and need opposite fixes

This is the practical payoff, and it belongs in your error handling rather than your documentation.

**Ineligible.** The ceiling is below the base fee. The transaction is invalid for every block whose
base fee exceeds it. Raising the tip does nothing whatsoever — the tip is capped by a gap that is
negative. The fix is to raise `maxFeePerGas`, which means a replacement, which means clearing the
price-bump gates from the previous lesson on **both** fields.

**Outbid.** The ceiling is fine, the transaction is eligible, and other transactions offer builders
more. The fix is to raise the tip — and, because of the squeeze, usually to raise the ceiling
alongside it so the higher tip is actually reachable.

Distinguishing them takes one comparison: read the current base fee from the latest block header and
compare it to your `maxFeePerGas`. If your ceiling is lower, you are ineligible and no amount of tip
helps. If it is higher, compute the gap and check whether your tip is being squeezed before you
conclude anything about the market.

## What the ceiling costs when it is never used

A generous ceiling looks free. Under the arithmetic above the difference between your ceiling and the
realised price is refunded, so setting it high seems to cost nothing but caution.

It costs one specific thing, and it shows up as a bug in exactly one place. The balance assertion
requires `gas_limit` multiplied by `max_fee_per_gas` to be available *before* execution, in
addition to the value being transferred.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> That is a reservation against the
account's balance at the moment of validation, computed from the ceiling and not from the expected
cost.

So an account holding a modest balance, with one transaction in flight that names a very high
ceiling and a large gas limit, can fail the balance check on the *next* transaction — a transaction
that is cheap, well priced and entirely affordable at the price it will actually pay. Pools apply the
same accounting when deciding what a sender can support, so the second transaction may be rejected on
admission rather than at execution.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" />

The rule that falls out: size the ceiling for the volatility you need to survive, not for comfort,
and never let a batch sender pick ceilings independently per transaction from one funded account.

### How much ceiling is enough

There is real arithmetic behind the choice, and it comes from a constant you already met. The base
fee changes by at most one eighth — 12.5% — from one block to the next, because the adjustment is
divided by a maximum-change denominator of 8.<Cite src="eip-1559" />

So a ceiling that survives `n` consecutive blocks of maximum increase must exceed the current base
fee by a factor of 1.125 raised to the power `n`. One block needs 1.13 times the current base fee.
Six blocks need roughly 2 times, which at
the current twelve-second slot is about a minute and a quarter.<Cite src="eip-7782" /> Twelve blocks
need roughly 4 times. Twenty-four need roughly 17 times.

That is arithmetic on a cited constant rather than a measurement, and its value is in the shape
rather than the digits: the cost of surviving a sustained rise grows geometrically, so "just set it
very high" and "set it for the next two minutes" are quantitatively different decisions and the first
one is what breaks the balance check above.

## The other fee fields

Two more numbers belong in the picture, because they interact with the two above in ways that catch
people out.

**`gasLimit` is a quantity, not a price, and it is not free to overstate.** It multiplies into the
balance reservation, and since Fusaka a protocol-level cap applies: EIP-7825 limits any single
transaction to 16,777,216 gas — two to the power twenty-four — as a denial-of-service and
block-verification-time defence.<Cite src="eip-7825" /> A transaction naming more than that is
invalid, so an estimator that pads generously against a large block gas limit can produce a
transaction that no block can contain. The context for that cap is a much larger block: EIP-7935
directs execution clients to set 60M as the default gas limit in their
configurations.<Cite src="eip-7935" /> That EIP is Informational and changes client *defaults* — the
limit remains something block producers signal, not a constant you can read off a
specification.<Cite src="eip-7935" />

**`maxFeePerBlobGas` is a third, independent price.** A type-3 blob transaction's payload is
`[chain_id, nonce, max_priority_fee_per_gas, max_fee_per_gas, gas_limit, to, value, data, access_list, max_fee_per_blob_gas, blob_versioned_hashes, y_parity, r, s]`,
where the first fields keep their EIP-1559 semantics and `max_fee_per_blob_gas` is a separate ceiling
against a separate base fee.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Three prices, three independent ways to be
ineligible, and — as the blob lesson will show — three fields that a replacement must clear
simultaneously.

Both of those live under the same typed-transaction envelope: a one-byte type prefix followed by a
type-specific payload.<Cite src="eip-2718" /> A wallet that assumes "the fee fields" is a fixed pair
is already wrong for two of the five types on mainnet.

<Check type="predict" concept="eip-1559-fee-fields">
A wallet estimates fees as follows: it reads the latest block's base fee `b`, sets
`maxPriorityFeePerGas` to 2 gwei, and sets `maxFeePerGas` to `b + 2` gwei — "the base fee plus the
tip, exactly what it will cost."

Predict the behaviour of a transaction built this way (a) in the very next block if the base fee rises
at all, and (b) two blocks later if the base fee has risen the maximum amount each time. Then say what
the wallet should do instead.

<Answer>
**(a) The transaction is bidding zero, or is ineligible.** The base fee is a property of the block
your transaction lands in, not the block you read. If it rises even slightly, say to `b + 0.5` gwei,
then the gap between your ceiling and the base fee is 1.5 gwei and your tip is squeezed from 2 to
1.5.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> If it rises by more than 2 gwei, the ceiling assertion fails and the
transaction cannot be in that block at all.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> The wallet's estimate is not
conservative and not aggressive — it is an estimate with no headroom in a mechanism designed to move
between blocks.

**(b) After two blocks of maximum increase the ceiling is almost certainly breached.** The maximum
per-block change is one eighth<Cite src="eip-1559" />, so two blocks give a factor of about 1.27. For
any base fee above roughly 7.5 gwei, a 27% rise exceeds the 2 gwei of headroom this construction
provides, and the transaction is ineligible until the base fee comes back down. The user sees a
spinner, and nothing about the transaction is wrong except a number chosen from a stale block.

**What the wallet should do.** Separate the two decisions, because they answer different questions.

The **tip** answers "how much am I willing to pay to be chosen among eligible transactions", and it
should come from recent realised priority fees rather than from a constant. Two gwei was reasonable
at some point in the past and is a magic number now.

The **ceiling** answers "how long am I willing to remain eligible while the base fee moves", and it
should be derived from that duration. Multiply the current base fee by 1.125 raised to the number of
blocks you want to survive, then add the tip.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> Surviving a minute or so of
sustained maximum increase means roughly doubling the base-fee component.

Then bound it, for the reason in this lesson: the ceiling times the gas limit is reserved against
the sender's balance before execution, so an unbounded ceiling on a modestly funded account starves
that account's next transaction.<Cite src="eip-1559" /><Cite src="geth-legacypool" />

And surface the distinction the arithmetic creates. If a transaction is not landing, the wallet can
tell the user which of two things is true — "the network price passed the maximum you agreed to" or
"others are bidding more than you" — because it can compare the ceiling to the current base fee. One
needs a higher ceiling, the other a higher tip, and both need a replacement that clears the price-bump
gates on both fields.<Cite src="geth-txpool-errors" />
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**Pending is a set** is the next lesson, and it explains what your bid is competing *against*: not a
queue position, but a builder's revenue calculation over the whole eligible set, re-run every block
against a new base fee.

**Replacement** now has a second gate you understand rather than memorise. Both fields must rise
because both fields do different work, and a replacement that raises only the ceiling has not raised
the bid at all.

**Blob transactions** add a third price on a separate market, and the blob pool requires a
replacement to clear its threshold on all three at once.

**Fee estimation as a product surface.** The two questions to hold apart are how long you want to
stay eligible, and how much you want to bid while you are. Every fee UI that presents a single
slider has collapsed them, and every one of those UIs is lying about one of the two.
