You run your own node. You subscribe to pending transactions, you have tuned your peer count, your
machine sits in the same datacentre region as half the network, and your liquidation bot is faster
than your competitors'.

It still loses, and not occasionally. The reason is not latency and no amount of engineering on your
side addresses it: **the transactions that matter are not in the stream you are watching.** They went
straight to a builder. By the time you can see them, they are in a block.

This lesson is about where transactions actually travel in 2026, why so many of them skip the pool
you have spent four lessons learning to reason about, and what that route costs — for the user who
takes it, and for the network that has come to depend on it.

## The supply chain, named

The path from a signed transaction to a block has more stages than "broadcast and wait", and each one
is a party with its own incentives.

A **user** signs. Their **wallet** chooses a submission route: gossip it to the public pool, or send
it to a private endpoint. A **searcher** looks for profitable sequences involving other people's
transactions and assembles bundles. A **builder** assembles complete blocks from everything it can
see and bids for the right to have its block used. A **relay** holds the block body and gives the
proposer only the header, so the proposer cannot steal the contents. The **proposer** signs the
header of the winning bid.<Cite src="mev-boost-docs" /><Cite src="ethereum-roadmap-pbs" />

The wallet's routing choice at step two is the whole lesson. Everything downstream is a consequence
of it.

The public route is what the previous lessons described: full objects to a random fraction of peers,
hashes to the rest, and a pool synchronisation protocol that works hard to spread your transaction
everywhere.<Cite src="devp2p-eth-wire" /> The private route is mechanically much simpler. It is the
same `eth_sendRawTransaction` call, pointed at a different URL, which forwards the transaction to one
or more builders instead of gossiping it.

## What a private endpoint actually offers

Take one and read what it promises, because the promises are specific and the trade-offs are in them.

Flashbots Protect describes itself as sending transactions to a private mempool where they are hidden
from frontrunning and sandwich bots; it advertises refunds when a transaction generates extractable
value or pays high priority fees; and it states that transactions are only included if they do not
revert, so users do not pay for failed transactions.<Cite src="flashbots-protect-docs" /> Submission
is an ordinary RPC call to the operator's endpoint.<Cite src="flashbots-protect-docs" />

Three of those are worth translating out of marketing language.

**"Hidden from frontrunning bots" means hidden from everyone except the operator and whoever they
choose to show it to.** By default, Protect shares hints about the transaction with searchers through
MEV-Share: the documentation lists the default configuration as sharing the transaction hash and
partial logs — the pool id and the fact that a swap occurred — for several classes of
trade.<Cite src="flashbots-protect-mev-refunds" /> A user can request maximum privacy by setting only
the hash hint, and the documentation is candid that doing so makes opportunities harder for searchers
to spot and will very likely reduce the refund.<Cite src="flashbots-protect-mev-refunds" /> Privacy
and rebate are explicitly traded against each other, by a query parameter.

**"Only included if they do not revert" is a real feature with a real cost.** It removes the class of
failure where a user pays for a transaction that accomplished nothing. It also means a transaction
that would have reverted is simply never included, which from the outside is indistinguishable from
a transaction that vanished — and the previous lessons' diagnostics do not apply, because it is not
in any pool to be found.

**Operator custody makes some things possible that the protocol cannot do.** Protect exposes a real
cancellation: same address, same nonce, `from` and `to` both set to the sender, empty `data`. The
documentation states it costs nothing, because the cancellation "serves solely for authentication
purposes" and is never included on-chain, and that transactions may sit at the operator for up to six
minutes.<Cite src="flashbots-protect-cancellations" /> Correspondingly, the operator treats the
derived nonce as sensitive: a transaction it holds appears in `eth_getTransactionCount` only for the
`pending` tag and only when the request is signed with the sender's own
key.<Cite src="flashbots-protect-nonce-management" />

Every one of those behaviours exists because one party holds the transaction. That is the trade in a
sentence: **you exchange broadcast for custody.** Broadcast means everyone can see it and nobody can
recall it. Custody means one party can see it, can recall it, and can decide what to do with it
within whatever rules they publish.

## Order flow auctions: selling the intent instead of leaking it

The second-generation version of this does not merely hide the transaction. It sells access to it and
gives the proceeds back.

MEV Blocker's documentation describes the mechanism concretely. User transactions go to a private
pool of searchers rather than the public mempool. Those searchers compete to backrun the
transaction — to trade immediately after it, in the state it created — and the winning bid returns up
to 90% of its value to the user, with the remaining 10% used to reward builders and
validators.<Cite src="mev-blocker-ofa" /> Builders who want access post a bond and pay weekly dues
calculated on the value attributable to that flow.<Cite src="mev-blocker-ofa" /> By default the
service shares the transaction without its signature and mixes real transactions with generated fake
ones, so that a searcher reading the stream cannot reliably tell which intents are
real.<Cite src="mev-blocker-ofa" />

Read the shape of that rather than the numbers. An order flow auction takes the thing that was
previously extracted from users — advance knowledge of what they are about to do — and sells it on
their behalf. The user's position changes from victim of an information leak to seller of an
information asset.

It is still a trade, and the terms are legible if you look. The user grants one operator exclusive
early sight of their intent. The operator monetises that sight under published rules and returns a
share. The rebate is real; so is the fact that the operator now knows every transaction that user
sends before anyone else does, including the ones that generate no rebate at all.

## The number you have read, and why this lesson will not repeat it

You will encounter a percentage for how much of Ethereum's transaction flow is private. You should
not trust any of them, including from otherwise excellent sources, and it is worth seeing exactly why
because the failure mode generalises.

Published figures measure at least three different quantities:

- **Share of transactions** — what fraction of transaction *count* arrived privately.
- **Share of gas** — what fraction of the gas consumed in blocks came from private flow.
- **Share of block value** — what fraction of what builders could pay proposers came from
  transactions others could not see.

These are not close to each other, and they cannot be, because private flow is skewed toward
high-value activity. A route carrying a small fraction of transactions can carry a large fraction of
value. Quoting one number as though it answered the others is the error, and it is extremely common.

The most widely repeated figure appears in the introduction of a 2025 benchmark study of private
RPCs — where it is a citation to another work rather than anything those authors
measured.<Cite src="arxiv-private-rpc-benchmark" /> The measurement that *is* first-hand answers a
different question entirely: it covers block value, over Ethereum blocks from January 2023 to May
2024, and attributes the majority of block value in that sample to private order
flow.<Cite src="arxiv-private-orderflow" /> Both are legitimate. They are answers to different
questions from different periods, and neither of them is "the share of private order flow".

So this lesson states the direction, which is settled and well supported, and refuses the magnitude,
which is not:

**Private submission has gone from a niche route to a primary one, and value-bearing flow is more
private than transaction count.**<Cite src="arxiv-private-orderflow" /><Cite src="arxiv-private-rpc-benchmark" />

If you need a number, measure it against the definition your decision depends on, date it, and treat
it as perishable.

<Aside kind="note">
The same discipline applies to builder market share, which you will also see quoted. Published pairs
for the top two builders disagree materially, and they name different builders — a consequence of a
market that reshuffles faster than analyses are published. The concentration is high; the specific
figure is contested. Pull it from a live relay dashboard on the day you need it rather than from any
document, including this one.
</Aside>

## The measured costs, and how to read a vendor benchmark

Private routing is often presented as costless. There is real empirical work, and reading it properly
is a lesson in itself.

The 2025 benchmark study compared four order flow auctions — MEV Blocker, Flashbots Protect, Blink
and Merkle — by submitting identical transactions to each simultaneously and comparing
outcomes.<Cite src="arxiv-private-rpc-benchmark" /> Its measures are the right ones: success rate,
defined as landing on-chain without reverting; time to inclusion, measured in *blocks* rather than
seconds because block times are discrete and arriving a second earlier often changes nothing; and
execution quality, measured as relative swap price against a reference
provider.<Cite src="arxiv-private-rpc-benchmark" /> The sample was 273 transactions over blocks
spanning December 2024 to January 2025, comprising approvals, transfers and swaps on both liquid and
illiquid pairs.<Cite src="arxiv-private-rpc-benchmark" />

Three things to notice, in order of importance.

**The method is worth copying and the rankings are not worth carrying.** Submitting the same
transaction to every candidate route simultaneously and comparing outcomes is exactly right, and it
is the only way to get an answer that applies to your traffic. The rankings are from a sample of a
few hundred transactions during one two-month window, in a market that has since moved.

**The authors work for one of the four services benchmarked.** The paper is authored from CoW DAO,
which operates MEV Blocker, and MEV Blocker is used as the reference against which the others are
compared.<Cite src="arxiv-private-rpc-benchmark" /> That does not make the work dishonest — the
methodology is stated plainly enough that you can see the choice — but it does mean the ranking is a
vendor's ranking of its competitors and should be read as such.

**Inclusion latency is a real cost and it is measurable in blocks.** Time to inclusion varied between
providers, with the paper reporting a single outlier at seven blocks against a second-worst of
four.<Cite src="arxiv-private-rpc-benchmark" /> For a swap, several blocks of delay is a price
movement you did not choose. This is the concrete form of "private RPCs are not strictly better".

## Why exclusivity decides who builds blocks

Now the structural claim, which is the most important thing in this lesson and the least intuitive.

Block building is an auction. Builders assemble candidate blocks and bid for the right to have theirs
used; the highest bid wins.<Cite src="mev-boost-docs" /> Set up the comparison carefully:

**Subgoal — establish the symmetric case.** Suppose every builder sees exactly the same candidate
set, namely the public pool. Then every builder can construct approximately the same most-valuable
block, and competition drives bids toward the full value of that block. Nobody has an advantage
because nobody has information the others lack. This is the outcome proposer-builder separation was
designed to produce: block value flowing to proposers rather than accumulating with
specialists.<Cite src="ethereum-roadmap-pbs" />

**Subgoal — introduce one asymmetry.** Now let one builder receive transactions the others cannot
see. Its candidate set is a strict superset. Its most-valuable block is therefore worth at least as
much as anyone else's, and usually more.

**Subgoal — follow the bidding.** A builder holding a more valuable block can win while bidding a
smaller *fraction* of that block's value. Competitors bidding their full value still lose. So the
advantaged builder wins more often **and** keeps more per win — an outcome the empirical work states
directly: builders with more private order flow have higher block valuations, are more likely to win,
and retain a larger proportion of profits.<Cite src="arxiv-private-orderflow" />

**Subgoal — close the loop.** Order flow is sent to whoever is most likely to include it, and the
builder winning most often is the safest place to send it. So winning attracts flow, and flow causes
winning. The paper's own framing of where this leads is in its title: the road to monopoly in
Ethereum's block building market.<Cite src="arxiv-private-orderflow" />

**Subgoal — state what this is not.** It is not a side effect of concentration; it is the mechanism
producing it. And it is not a failure of proposer-builder separation to work as specified. The
authors' conclusion is sharper than that: PBS in its current stage does not balance profit
distribution, it transfers the centralisation of block profits from institutional stakers to a
monopolistic builder.<Cite src="arxiv-private-orderflow" />

Sit with the uncomfortable part. The best available protection for an individual user is private
routing. Its aggregate effect is to concentrate the power to decide what goes into blocks. Each
individual choice is correct and the sum is a property nobody chose — which is why the answers being
worked on are protocol-level rather than advisory.<Cite src="eip-7805" />

## What to do with this when you are shipping something

**Choose the route deliberately, per transaction class.** A transaction with no extractable value —
an approval, a claim, a small transfer — gains little from private routing and pays the latency. A
large swap or a liquidation-triggering action gains a lot. Routing everything one way is a decision
you should be able to defend.

**Never build a product feature on public pool observation.** "See your transaction in the mempool",
"watch pending activity", "detect incoming transfers before confirmation" — all of these are
sampling a biased subset that is missing exactly the transactions users care about most.

**Treat the operator as a dependency with an SLA you do not have.** They can be down, rate-limited,
or slow. They see everything you send. Have a fallback route and know what it changes about your
transaction's behaviour, including whether your cancellation logic still works.

**Instrument your own inclusion latency.** It is the one number in this lesson you can obtain
honestly: submit through each route you are considering, in parallel, and measure blocks to
inclusion. The benchmark study's method transfers directly, and your traffic is the only sample that
is definitely relevant to you.<Cite src="arxiv-private-rpc-benchmark" />

<Answer>
**The strongest argument against is not about the users, and that is what makes it hard.** For almost
every individual user, on almost every transaction, private routing is the better choice. Sandwich
protection is real, the rebate is real, and paying nothing for a reverted transaction is
real.<Cite src="flashbots-protect-docs" /><Cite src="mev-blocker-ofa" /> An argument that pretends
otherwise will not survive contact with the data.

The argument that does survive has three parts.

*It concentrates block building, and your wallet is a large enough share of flow to matter.* Exclusive
order flow is the input that lets one builder outbid the rest while keeping more of the
value, which attracts more flow, which compounds.<Cite src="arxiv-private-orderflow" /> A few hundred
thousand users' default is not a personal choice; it is a policy decision about where a meaningful
volume of flow goes. If you route it all to one operator you are buying your users protection with a
property — many independent parties able to build blocks — that those same users depend on for
censorship resistance.

*It creates a dependency your users cannot see and cannot route around.* The operator sees every
transaction before anyone else, decides what to do with it under rules they can change, and holds it
for a period they specify — up to six minutes in one documented
case.<Cite src="flashbots-protect-cancellations" /> If they are down, your wallet is down in a way
your users will attribute to you.

*It costs latency, sometimes.* Measured time to inclusion differs between routes, with outliers of
several blocks.<Cite src="arxiv-private-rpc-benchmark" /> For a swap, blocks are price movement.

**What I would ship: private by default, with three qualifications.**

*Route by transaction class rather than uniformly.* Transactions with no plausible extractable value
go public — they gain nothing from privacy and they are the ones where latency is pure loss. Swaps,
large transfers and anything touching a lending position go private. This also keeps some of your
flow contributing to the public set that inclusion-list designs depend on.

*Use more than one operator and rotate.* This is a decision with a cost — you cannot optimise rebates
across operators you split between — and it is the only lever a wallet has against the
concentration dynamic. Measure both, honestly, and choose with the trade-off visible.

*Do not let the routing choice leak into your correctness assumptions.* A privately routed
transaction is absent from every public pool, so your status logic cannot depend on seeing it there,
your nonce management must use the operator's signed-query path rather than a public `pending`
count<Cite src="flashbots-protect-nonce-management" />, and your cancel flow is operator-specific
rather than a same-nonce replacement. Each of those is a source of production bugs when a route is
changed later by someone who did not know they were load-bearing.

**And disclose it.** The user is entering a relationship with a third party who sees their intent
before anyone else. That is a reasonable default and it is not one to make silently.

## Where this pays off

**Nothing guarantees inclusion** is the next and final lesson in this module, and it starts exactly
where this one ends: if builders choose, and a few builders do most of the choosing, what obliges
anyone to include you?

**Inclusion lists** are the protocol's answer to the concentration mechanism described here. EIP-7805
would have a committee force-include eligible transactions, and it names the cause in its own
motivation: the right to build blocks was auctioned to specialised entities, a few sophisticated
builders came to dominate, and censorship resistance deteriorated.<Cite src="eip-7805" /> It was
declined for the Glamsterdam upgrade.<Cite src="eip-7773" />

**The MEV track** takes each mechanism referenced here — searchers, bundles, backruns, sandwiches,
rebates — and works through the economics properly. Everything there rests on the two facts this
module established: intent is visible, and ordering is discretionary.

**Enshrined proposer-builder separation** is the other half of the response, moving the builder role
into the protocol rather than leaving it to out-of-protocol software. Unlike inclusion lists, it is
scheduled for Glamsterdam.<Cite src="eip-7773" />
