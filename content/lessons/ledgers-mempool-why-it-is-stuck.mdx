A payout service fires fifty transfers from one address in a loop. Seven confirm. Forty-three do
not, and stay that way.

The on-call engineer does the obvious thing: resubmits all forty-three with triple the priority fee.
Nothing changes. Triples it again. Nothing changes. Three hours later the forty-three are gone from
every endpoint the team can query, no receipts exist, and the account nonce has moved by exactly
seven.

Every step of that debugging session was reasonable and every step was aimed at the wrong problem.
The forty-three transactions were never competing for inclusion, so no bid could have helped. They
were not slow. They were **ineligible**, and ineligibility and slowness are indistinguishable in
every wallet interface ever shipped.

## The pool has two halves, and only one of them is in the game

go-ethereum splits its pool into two structures, and the JSON-RPC surface exposes the split directly:
`txpool_content` returns an object with two fields, `pending` and `queued`, each mapping sender
address to nonce to transaction.<Cite src="geth-rpc-txpool" />

**Pending** means executable right now. The transaction's nonce follows contiguously from the
account's current nonce, given whatever else the pool already holds for that sender. Nothing stands
between it and a block except price and someone's decision to include it.

**Queued** means held but not executable. Something is missing in front of it — a nonce the pool has
never seen. The geth documentation describes these as transactions "being scheduled for future
execution only".<Cite src="geth-rpc-txpool" />

The distinction is not cosmetic. A queued transaction is:

- **not propagated.** The wire protocol's guidance is that a node should announce hashes the peer
  probably lacks, and it adds a revealing preference — "it is better to return more transactions than
  to have a nonce gap in the pool".<Cite src="devp2p-eth-wire" /> Nodes work to avoid holes, because
  a hole makes everything behind it dead weight.
- **not available to a block producer.** Whoever builds the next block selects from executable
  transactions. A queued one cannot be executed, so it cannot be selected.
- **on a much smaller and shorter-lived shelf.** go-ethereum's defaults are 1024 non-executable slots
  globally and 64 per account, against 4096 plus 1024 executable slots and 16 guaranteed per
  account.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" /> Queued transactions also carry a `Lifetime`, three hours by
  default, enforced by an eviction ticker that runs once a
  minute.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" /><Cite src="geth-cli-options" />

So the forty-three were sitting in a 1024-slot shared parking area, invisible to peers, with a
three-hour clock running. That is the entire story of the incident.

## The protocol does not define this — clients do

Worth pausing on, because it determines how much of this you may assume.

The wire specification's transaction validity rules cover signature, intrinsic gas, sender balance,
and the requirement that the nonce "must be equal or greater than the current nonce of the sender
account".<Cite src="devp2p-eth-wire" /> Note what that permits: a transaction with a nonce far in the
future is a *valid* transaction. It is not garbage and nodes do not treat it as an attack.

What happens to it next is explicitly delegated: it is "up to implementations to determine how many
'future' transactions with nonce greater than the current account nonce are valid, and to which
degree 'nonce gaps' are acceptable".<Cite src="devp2p-eth-wire" />

Two consequences you can act on. First, there is no consensus rule to appeal to and no EIP to cite —
"queued" is a go-ethereum concept, and another client may hold, drop or bound future transactions
differently. Second, the amount of gap tolerance you get is a property of whichever node received
your transaction, which by the previous lesson you do not know.

## Why a fee bump cannot help

Run it as a decision procedure, in the order the node applies it.

**Subgoal 1 — establish eligibility, before price is considered at all.** The pool asks whether this
transaction's nonce is reachable from the account's current nonce through transactions it already
holds. If the answer is no, the transaction goes to the queued structure. Price has not entered the
question.

**Subgoal 2 — notice that the queued structure is not an input to block building.** Builders select
from executable transactions. A transaction that is not executable is not in the candidate set, so
its fee fields are not compared against anything.

**Subgoal 3 — see what a bump actually does.** Raising the fees on a queued transaction and
rebroadcasting produces a replacement of a queued transaction with a more expensive queued
transaction. It is still behind the same hole. The only thing that changed is the maximum you have
committed to pay if it ever becomes eligible.

**Subgoal 4 — identify the only move that works.** Land the missing nonce. The instant the gap
closes, everything behind it is promoted to pending in nonce order, in one step, and only then does
price start to matter.

## Where gaps come from when nobody made an obvious mistake

The textbook case — deliberately sending nonce N+1 before N — is rare in production. The five below
are not.

**Reading `latest` and caching it.** `eth_getTransactionCount` with the `latest` tag returns the
nonce as of the last block, which ignores everything you have in flight. A service that reads it once
and increments locally is fine; a service that re-reads it per transaction under concurrency issues
duplicate nonces. Some are rejected as `already known` or replacement-underpriced, some
replace each other, and the surviving set has holes.<Cite src="geth-txpool-errors" />

**Concurrency without an allocator.** Fifty goroutines, fifty calls to "get next nonce", no
serialisation. This is the failure at the top of this lesson. The fix is a single-writer nonce
allocator that hands out numbers and can reclaim one when a send fails before broadcast.

**A transaction in the middle disappearing.** This is the cruel one, because it opens a gap *after*
you did everything correctly. If nonce N is dropped — evicted on capacity, expired, or rejected on
price at the node that mattered — then N+1 through N+9 were pending a moment ago and are queued now.
Your sequence developed a hole without you doing anything, and the next lesson's list of ways a
transaction leaves a pool is the list of ways this happens.

**Private submission invisible to the nonce query.** A transaction sent to a private endpoint is not
in any public pool, so a `pending` nonce query answered by a public node cannot know about it.
Operators go further and treat the derived nonce as sensitive: Flashbots Protect includes such
transactions in `eth_getTransactionCount` only for the `pending` tag, and only when the request is
signed by the sender's key and carries the signature in an `X-Flashbots-Signature`
header.<Cite src="flashbots-protect-nonce-management" /> Query it unsigned and you get a nonce that
counts only public transactions — then you sign at that number and collide with your own in-flight
transaction.

**EIP-7702 authorizations moving the nonce.** Since Pectra, a type-4 transaction carries an
`authorization_list`, and processing an authorization increments the **authority's** nonce as part of
execution — which is exactly what makes self-sponsored delegation
work.<Cite src="eip-7702" /> A wallet that models the nonce as "count of transactions I sent" is
wrong for any address that has ever authorised a delegation, and computes a next nonce one too low.

That last one has a second half that is easy to miss and expensive to discover. go-ethereum's pool
treats delegated accounts as a churn risk and applies a much tighter limit to them: any account with
a deployed delegation, or with an in-flight authorization to deploy one, is allowed a **single**
transaction slot rather than the standard number, because the account can be swept by an unrelated
party.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" /> Within that single slot, replacement at the same nonce is
allowed and stacking is not, and a gapped-nonce transaction from such an account is refused outright
with `gapped-nonce tx from delegated accounts`.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" /> A batch sender whose
address is delegated does not get sixteen executable slots. It gets one, and the second concurrent
send is rejected rather than queued.

## Diagnosing it in ninety seconds

The procedure is short because there are only two numbers.

**Compare the two nonce views.** `eth_getTransactionCount(address, "latest")` and the same call with
`"pending"`. If they are equal, the node believes you have nothing in flight — which means either you
genuinely do not, or everything you sent is queued rather than pending, or it went somewhere private.
If `pending` exceeds `latest` by less than the number of transactions you sent, the difference is the
size of your visible in-flight set and the rest are unaccounted for.

**Read the queued half directly.** `txpool_content` gives you `queued` keyed by address and
nonce.<Cite src="geth-rpc-txpool" /> Your address appearing there settles the diagnosis instantly.
The lowest nonce present in `queued` minus one is the transaction you actually need to land. Note the
caveat from the previous lesson: this answers for whichever node you reached, so a negative result
proves nothing.

**Then act on the gap, not on the backlog.** Sign and broadcast the missing nonce with fees that
clear the current base fee comfortably. Do not touch the queued transactions; they promote on their
own.

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="nonce-gap">
A batch service manages one hot EOA. Its nonce allocator is:

```ts
let next: number | null = null;

async function nextNonce(): Promise<number> {
  if (next === null) {
    next = await client.getTransactionCount({ address, blockTag: "pending" });
  }
  return next++;
}

async function send(tx: Tx) {
  const nonce = await nextNonce();
  try {
    return await client.sendRawTransaction({ ...tx, nonce });
  } catch (e) {
    next = null;            // resync on any failure
    throw e;
  }
}
```

It works in testing and produces long-lived nonce gaps in production. Identify the two defects and
say what each one does at the protocol level.

<Answer>
**Defect 1 — the failure path abandons a nonce it already allocated.** When `sendRawTransaction`
throws, the allocated nonce is discarded and `next` is reset. The reset re-reads the `pending` count,
which is computed from what that node holds — and it holds every transaction you successfully sent
*after* the failed one, if any were in flight concurrently. The abandoned nonce is now a hole that
nothing will ever fill, and everything above it is queued: parked in a 1024-slot shared structure,
not announced to peers, on a three-hour clock.<Cite src="geth-legacypool" /><Cite src="devp2p-eth-wire" />

Correct behaviour is to distinguish two error classes. If the node rejected the transaction before
accepting it into a pool — malformed, underpriced, `already known`<Cite src="geth-txpool-errors" /> —
the nonce was never consumed anywhere and belongs back at the head of your allocator's free list. If
the send failed on a network timeout, you do not know whether it was accepted, so the nonce must be
treated as spent and retried at the same nonce as a replacement.

**Defect 2 — resyncing from `pending` is resyncing from a cache.** The `pending` count is that node's
opinion. Against a load-balanced endpoint the resync may hit a node that has none of your in-flight
transactions and return the `latest` value, at which point the allocator hands out nonces you have
already used. If any of your flow is submitted privately, the public `pending` count cannot include
it at all unless the query is signed to the operator's
requirements.<Cite src="flashbots-protect-nonce-management" />

The allocator must be authoritative locally and reconcile against the chain, not against a pool. The
shape that works: a single writer, durable local record of every allocated nonce and its signed
transaction, promotion driven by receipts, and `latest` — the on-chain count, which is a consensus
fact — as the only external input. `pending` is useful for a warning banner and useless as a source
of truth.

**Bonus defect worth naming.** If this address ever signs an EIP-7702 authorization, its nonce
advances by one during that transaction's execution without a transaction of its own appearing, and
an allocator that models the nonce as "transactions I sent" drifts one behind
permanently.<Cite src="eip-7702" />
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**Replacement** is the next lesson, and it is the operation you reach for when the missing nonce is
itself stuck. The two are easy to confuse and have opposite fixes: a gap wants a *new* transaction at
a *lower* nonce, a stuck pending transaction wants a *different* transaction at the *same* nonce.

**The blob pool** treats gaps far more harshly than the legacy pool does, and an L2 batcher that
tolerates a gap for three hours on the ordinary pool loses the transaction in about a minute on the
blob pool.

**Eviction and disappearance** is the last lesson in this module, and it is the direct cause of the
third gap source above — the one where a hole opens in a sequence you built correctly.

**Account abstraction** exists partly to escape this. A smart account's nonce space is its own design
choice rather than a single strictly incrementing counter, which is why parallel nonce keys are one
of the first things every account-abstraction standard specifies.
