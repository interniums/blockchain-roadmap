A validator is slashed at 09:14 on a Tuesday. The operator opens an incident, calculates the immediate
charge — a fraction of an ETH — and wants to close the ticket.

They cannot. **Nobody knows yet what the offence cost**, including the protocol. The larger of the two
charges will not be computed for another eighteen days, and its size depends on something that has not
happened yet: how much other stake gets slashed between now and then.

That is not an implementation quirk. It is the central design decision of Ethereum's punishment
layer, and reading it correctly changes what you think slashing is *for*.

## The timeline is three events, not one

Everything below comes from two functions and two constants, so it is worth walking the calendar
before interpreting it.

**Day 0 — evidence is included.** `slash_validator` marks the validator slashed, initiates its exit,
pushes its withdrawable epoch out by `EPOCHS_PER_SLASHINGS_VECTOR`, adds its effective balance to a
rolling vector at the index for the current epoch, and charges the immediate penalty of effective
balance over `MIN_SLASHING_PENALTY_QUOTIENT_ELECTRA`.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" />
The mainnet presets set that vector length to 8192 epochs and that quotient to
4096.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" />

**Day ~18 — the correlation charge.** `process_slashings` runs at every epoch boundary and charges a
validator when the current epoch plus half of `EPOCHS_PER_SLASHINGS_VECTOR` equals its withdrawable
epoch — that is, exactly 4096 epochs after the
slashing.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" /> At 32 slots of 12 seconds, 4096 epochs
is 18.2 days.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-config-mainnet" />

**Day ~36 — the balance becomes withdrawable.** 8192 epochs after the slashing, which is 36.4
days.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-config-mainnet" />

Now notice why the middle event lands where it does. The charge is applied at the *midpoint* of the
window, so the sum it reads covers 18 days before the offence and 18 days after it. The reference
derivation states this directly: the correlation penalty is based on the total stake slashed during
the 18 days preceding and the 18 days following.<Cite src="eth2book-slashing" />

**The window is centred on you.** You are not being charged for company you already had. You are being
charged for company you may yet acquire — which is why the bill cannot be computed on the day, and
why an operator who is slashed cannot honestly tell their board what the loss was for two and a half
weeks. The canonical documentation states the shape in one sentence: the slashed validator's ether
drains across the exit period, and on day 18 it receives a correlation penalty that is larger when
more validators were slashed around the same
time.<Cite src="ethereum-docs-pos-penalties" />

<Aside kind="note">
The window slides rather than resetting. `process_slashings_reset` zeroes the next epoch's slot in
the rolling vector at every epoch boundary, so the vector always holds exactly the last 8192 epochs of
slashed effective balance.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" /> Nothing accumulates
forever; the state stays a fixed size regardless of how many slashings the chain has ever seen.
</Aside>

## The one number the security argument needs

Here is the endpoint, and it is the only part of the curve you should rely on.

**When the balance slashed within the window reaches one third of the total active stake, the
correlation penalty consumes the offender's entire effective
balance.**<Cite src="mikeneuder-slashing-7251" /><Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

That number is not a coincidence and it is not a round figure someone liked. It is the same one third
that appears in [accountable safety](/c/accountable-safety): two conflicting checkpoints cannot both
finalise unless at least a third of the stake signed provably contradictory
messages.<Cite src="eth2book-casper-ffg" /> The penalty curve is calibrated so that the exact
participation level required to break finality is the participation level at which every participant
loses everything.

Read that as a sentence about pricing rather than about punishment. The protocol is not claiming a
finality reversal is impossible. It is quoting a price for one, and the correlation penalty is the
mechanism that makes the quote binding.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

<Aside kind="stop">
**The shape of the curve between zero and one third is contested, and this course does not teach it.**

One analysis presents a linear form as current and a quadratic form as proposed; a 2026 secondary
source reports the quadratic version as having shipped. That disagreement was not resolved against
the specification, and it is recorded in this course's research register as
open.<Cite src="mikeneuder-slashing-7251" />

What can be said, because it is readable directly: Electra's `process_slashings` multiplies the summed
slashed balance by `PROPORTIONAL_SLASHING_MULTIPLIER_BELLATRIX`, which the mainnet preset sets to 3,
caps the product at the total active balance, and then charges each offender in proportion to its own
effective balance. That is linear in the slashed fraction, and no later fork in the repository
redefines the function.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" />

Both candidate forms agree at the one-third point. If you need the middle of the curve rather than its
endpoint, read `process_slashings` for the fork you are actually running against. Do not inherit the
formula from an article, including this one.
</Aside>

## What "alone" actually costs

Turn the dial the other way and the same mechanism produces the opposite extreme.

Under the phase 0 form of the function, the arithmetic rounded the correlation penalty down to a whole
unit of `EFFECTIVE_BALANCE_INCREMENT`, so an isolated slashing produced a correlation charge of
exactly zero. The reference derivation notes this explicitly, and observes that no correlated slashing
had ever incurred a penalty greater than zero under that
mechanism.<Cite src="eth2book-slashing" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" />

Electra restructured the computation: it divides first, deriving a penalty *per effective-balance
increment*, and then multiplies by the offender's increments.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" />
The rounding therefore happens at a finer granularity than a whole ETH. The direction is unchanged —
a lone offender's correlation charge is a very small number — but "zero" is a claim about the older
form of the function, not a general property.

Combine that with the previous lesson's invoice and the whole picture resolves: for an isolated
slashing, both explicit penalties are small, and what the operator actually loses is 36 days of a
force-exited validator earning nothing.<Cite src="eth2book-slashing" /> For a correlated event at
scale, the second charge dominates everything else by orders of magnitude.

**Same offence. Same signatures. Same code path. Two outcomes separated by a factor of thousands.**

## What the design is actually pricing

The dossier's most useful framing: this is not a punishment schedule, it is a **statement about which
failures the chain can survive**.

One validator equivocating is noise. It cannot move a checkpoint, cannot reverse anything, and cannot
even meaningfully shift a fork choice. The protocol prices it as noise, deliberately, so that honest
staking is not a lottery in which one bad Tuesday ends the business.

A third of the stake equivocating simultaneously is a finality attack, whether or not anyone intended
it. It is indistinguishable from one, it does the same damage, and it is priced as
one.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

The variable the mechanism actually measures is neither severity nor intent. It is **how many others
failed in the same way at the same time.**

<Misconception
  belief="The correlation penalty punishes large operators."
  reality="It prices simultaneous failure. A large operator whose infrastructure genuinely fails independently is not the target; a thousand small operators running the same client image, on the same cloud provider, in the same region, are."
  why="'The penalty scales with how many were slashed' reads as a size penalty, because size is the usual proxy for how many of anything you have. That second misconception is the one worth carrying, because it inverts an intuition about
decentralisation. A hundred thousand independent hobbyists all running the same consensus client are
a *correlated* set as far as this mechanism is concerned. Counting operators tells you nothing. What
the curve responds to is whether their failure modes are shared.

## Translating the curve into three decisions

The reflection prompt for this module asks what operational choices this changes. Here is the honest
version, including why each one is hard.

**Client diversity.** A consensus bug that causes equivocation in a client running one tenth of the
stake produces a correlated event one tenth the size of the same bug in a client running a third of
it, and the penalty is steep in exactly that variable. This is why published consensus-client share is
a security metric rather than a popularity contest — with the caveat that the figures are
methodology-dependent: clientdiversity.org publishes both a node-crawl measurement and a
stake-weighted one side by side, and they do not
agree.<Cite src="clientdiversity-org" /> This course's research register leaves the two unreconciled,
because they measure different things and the gap between them is large. Diversity is hard to act on
because the majority client is usually the best-documented one, and every guide you find assumes it.

**Failure-domain diversity.** Same host, same rack, same region, same orchestrator, same
configuration-management run all convert an independent failure into a correlated one. Hard because
running one thing well is genuinely easier than running three things adequately, and the correlated
choice looks *more* reliable on every uptime dashboard right up until the moment it does not.

**Key custody.** Never two live processes for one key, and never resume from an unknown signing
history. Hard because this is the exact operational instinct — hot standby, fast failover — that every
other system you have run rewards.

The mechanism does not care which of these you get wrong. It only measures whether you got it wrong at
the same moment as everybody else.

**Plan A:** one client, one cloud provider, one region, automated failover, targeting five nines.
**Plan B:** three clients, two providers, three regions, manual failover, expecting a few hours of

Assume A truly is more reliable in the ordinary sense. Argue for B using the protocol's own price

<Answer>
**Compare distributions, not expected values.**

Plan A optimises the common case and concentrates the tail. Its ordinary failures cost inactivity
penalties, which are approximately symmetric to forgone rewards and fully recoverable. Its rare
failure — a consensus bug in the single client, a region-wide event that resurrects a stale instance,
an orchestrator that duplicates a signer — produces equivocation across the entire holding at once. If
the same client and the same provider are common across the network, the network-wide slashed balance
in that window is large, and the correlation charge is computed against
it.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" />

Plan B accepts a worse common case to buy an uncorrelated tail. Its downtime is charged at roughly
what the missed duties would have earned. Its bug scenario affects one third of its stake, and — more
importantly — is far less likely to coincide with everybody else's failure, which is the variable the
penalty is actually a function of.<Cite src="mikeneuder-slashing-7251" />

**Where A is genuinely better:** when the stake at risk is small relative to the operational cost of
plan B, and when the client and provider choices are *minority* ones. The curve responds to
correlation with the rest of the network, not to plan A's internal architecture. A single-client setup
on a client running a small share of stake, in an unusual region, is a less correlated bet than a
three-client setup where all three run in the same datacentre on the same
image.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

**The honest closing caveat:** none of this can be priced precisely, because the intermediate shape of
the curve is contested and because the correlation term depends on other people's future failures.
What you are choosing is not an expected loss — it is which tail you are exposed to. Say that
explicitly in the memo, rather than inventing a number.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

The one-third endpoint is the input to the finality module's cost model. "Reversing a finalised
checkpoint costs about a third of the total staked ETH, destroyed" is a sentence that only means
something once you know why that fraction and not another, and that the destruction is a mechanism
rather than a metaphor.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

The eighteen-day gap is what makes a confirmation policy an economic decision rather than a
mechanical one. Finality is not the moment nothing can change; it is the moment at which changing it
would put a third of the stake into that rolling vector and trigger this curve at its expensive
end.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" />

And the general shape — price correlation, not error — is the transferable idea. You will meet it
again wherever a system has to tolerate individual failure while making systemic failure
unaffordable: restaking's slashing conditions, rollup bonds, and every design where the question is
not "was this participant wrong" but "how many were wrong together".
