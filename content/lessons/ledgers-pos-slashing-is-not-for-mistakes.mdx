The question staking operators ask most often is some version of: *we had fourteen hours of downtime
last month — how close did we come to being slashed?*

The answer is that you did not come close, and that the question has the risk model inverted. Downtime
is a bill. Slashing is a verdict. They are produced by different mechanisms, triggered by different
evidence, and priced on completely different scales — and an operator optimising against the wrong
one will buy failover hardware that makes the real risk *worse*.

This lesson is about the axis those two mechanisms are separated along. It is not severity, and it is
not intent.

## The axis is provability

You met the counting argument in
[accountable safety](/c/accountable-safety): a conflicting finalisation forces a third of the stake to
have signed self-incriminating messages. The design decision downstream of that argument is where
this lesson starts.

**Slashing requires a self-contained proof.** A slashing is triggered by an object included in a
block that carries the two contradictory signed messages themselves. The state transition verifies
both signatures, confirms the contradiction against a predicate, and applies the
penalty.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" /> No judgement, no context, no observer's
testimony. Two objects, one predicate, a boolean.

**Absence produces nothing to verify.** A validator that did not attest emitted no message. There is
no artefact, and — this is the part that matters — no way to distinguish the cases. A validator that
is offline, a validator behind a partition, a validator whose messages were dropped by a peer, and a
validator whose messages arrived somewhere just after the deadline all look identical from every
vantage point in the network.

So the protocol cannot punish absence as an offence without punishing the network's behaviour as
though it were the operator's. It uses a different instrument instead: one that is small, symmetric,
and does not require anyone to be at fault.

## Two different inactivity mechanisms, routinely conflated

Before pricing slashing, separate the two things that get called "the inactivity penalty", because
they behave completely differently and only one of them is an emergency.

**The ordinary penalty** applies whenever the chain is finalising normally. Rewards are accounted per
participation flag — timely source, timely target, timely head, with weights of 14, 26 and 14 out of a
`WEIGHT_DENOMINATOR` of 64 — and a validator that misses a flag is charged a penalty computed from the
same base reward and the same weight.<Cite src="consensus-specs-altair-beacon-chain" /> Missing a
duty costs you roughly what performing it would have earned. It is symmetric by construction, and it
is a rounding error against a validator's balance for anything short of a sustained outage.

**The inactivity leak** is the emergency mechanism, and it only engages when the chain is failing to
finalise. Every validator carries an inactivity score; the score rises by `INACTIVITY_SCORE_BIAS`,
which the config sets to 4, for each epoch in which the validator's timely-target vote is missing,
falls by 1 for each epoch it is present, and — only while the chain is *not* leaking — falls by a
further `INACTIVITY_SCORE_RECOVERY_RATE` of
16.<Cite src="consensus-specs-altair-beacon-chain" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-config-mainnet" />
The leak itself begins after finalisation has been delayed more than `MIN_EPOCHS_TO_INACTIVITY_PENALTY`
epochs, which the preset sets to 4.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" />

The recovery rate of 16 against a bias of 4 is worth sitting with. In normal operation the score
decays four times faster than it accumulates, so an operator with intermittent trouble never builds a
meaningful score. It is only when finalisation itself has stalled that the recovery term switches off
and the score compounds — which is exactly the design described in
[the inactivity leak](/c/inactivity-leak): the mechanism is aimed at a stalled chain, not at an
unreliable operator.

## What being slashed actually costs, itemised

Track 01 derived the arithmetic. Here is the same event read as a line-item invoice from the
operator's seat, because the item most people quote is not the item that dominates.

**Line one — the immediate charge.** `slash_validator` deducts the validator's effective balance
divided by `MIN_SLASHING_PENALTY_QUOTIENT_ELECTRA`, which the preset sets to
4096.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" />
For a 32 ETH validator that is under a hundredth of an ETH. This is the number every article leads
with, and post-Electra it is close to noise.<Cite src="eip-7251" />

**Line two — the correlation charge**, applied much later and computed from how much other stake was
slashed around the same time. For an isolated event it is small. It is the entire subject of the last
lesson in this module, and it is the only line that can reach the whole balance.<Cite src="mikeneuder-slashing-7251" />

**Line three — thirty-six days of being treated as absent.** This is the one nobody quotes.
`slash_validator` pushes the withdrawable epoch out by `EPOCHS_PER_SLASHINGS_VECTOR` — 8192 epochs, a
little over 36 days at 32 slots of 12 seconds
each.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-config-mainnet" />
Throughout that period the validator earns nothing and is penalised as if it were failing to
participate, whatever it actually does — the reward accounting excludes slashed validators from the
participating set entirely.<Cite src="consensus-specs-altair-beacon-chain" /><Cite src="eth2book-slashing" />
The reference derivation puts this third charge at roughly 0.08 ETH for a 32 ETH validator under its
stated assumption of a 500,000-validator network, and notes it is much higher if an inactivity leak is
running at the same time.<Cite src="eth2book-slashing" />

**Line four — the exit is not optional and not fast.** The exit is initiated for you, it enters the
same balance-denominated churn queue every other exit uses, and the funds are withdrawable only after
the 36 days have elapsed.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" />

Add the lines up for a single isolated slashing and the shape is counter-intuitive: **the penalty
named "the slashing penalty" is roughly an order of magnitude smaller than the cost of the ejection
that comes with it.** What an isolated slashing destroys is mostly opportunity, not principal.

<Aside kind="warn">
Sources written before 2025 quote a much harsher immediate charge — the quotient was 32 under
Bellatrix, giving about 1 ETH for a 32 ETH
validator.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" /><Cite src="eth2book-slashing" /> This is not
only a problem with blog posts. The canonical reference on slashing still describes the Bellatrix
regime on its own page, and ethereum.org's rewards-and-penalties documentation still says a slashed
validator has up to 1 ETH
burned.<Cite src="eth2book-slashing" /><Cite src="ethereum-docs-pos-penalties" /> Before reusing any
slashing figure, check which fork's preset the source is reading; this is one of the few constants
that has moved by two orders of magnitude in a single upgrade.<Cite src="eip-7251" />
</Aside>

## Nobody is watching, and inclusion is rate-limited

Two operational facts about how a slashing actually reaches the chain.

**Someone has to file.** The protocol runs no surveillance. The evidence must be constructed by a
participant, gossiped, and included by a proposer, and the incentive to bother is a whistleblower
reward: `WHISTLEBLOWER_REWARD_QUOTIENT_ELECTRA` is 4096, and the including proposer keeps
`PROPOSER_WEIGHT` over `WEIGHT_DENOMINATOR` — 8 of 64, one eighth — of
it.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-altair-beacon-chain" />

**Blocks carry very few slashing objects.** The preset allows `MAX_PROPOSER_SLASHINGS` of 16 per block
but only `MAX_ATTESTER_SLASHINGS_ELECTRA` of 1.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" /> That
looks like a bottleneck until you notice what a single attester-slashing object contains: two
*aggregate* attestations, and `process_attester_slashing` slashes every validator in the intersection
of their attesting index sets.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" /> One object can
therefore slash an entire committee's worth of validators at once. The rate limit is on evidence
objects, not on offenders — which is precisely the property a mass correlated event needs.

Put the two mechanisms side by side and an operational conclusion falls out that is worth stating
before the next lesson gives you the mechanics.

Downtime is punished mildly and recovers automatically. Equivocation is punished by ejection and
recovers never. So the correct engineering response to "our validator was down" is *not* the response
that instinct suggests, which is a hot standby that can take over quickly.

A hot standby holding the same signing key is a machine whose entire purpose is to sign the same
duties as another machine. If both are ever live at once — a failed health check, a network partition
between them, a restore from a backup that did not know the original was still running — they will
produce exactly the pair of messages the slashing predicate is looking for. Lighthouse's own
documentation is blunt about the limit here: the slashing-protection database cannot help across two
instances, because it is a per-instance record and each instance has its own; ensuring keys never run
simultaneously is the operator's responsibility, not the
client's.<Cite src="lighthouse-slashing-protection" />

The instrument that punishes downtime is calibrated so that accepting some downtime is the cheap
choice. Taking that trade is not laziness; it is reading the price list correctly.

<Check type="explain" concept="slashing">
An operator is choosing between two architectures for a validator with a 32 ETH effective balance.

**A:** a single signer, with a documented manual failover that takes about four hours.
**B:** two signers behind an automatic health check, with a documented failover of about ten seconds.

Assume B's health check misfires roughly once a year and briefly runs both signers. Argue which is
the better design using the protocol's own price list, and name the assumption that could flip your
answer.

<Answer>
**A is better, and the argument is a comparison of two very different distributions.**

A's failure mode is four hours of missed attestations, roughly once per incident. Missed duties are
charged at approximately the reward they would have earned, computed per participation flag against
the same base reward.<Cite src="consensus-specs-altair-beacon-chain" /> Four hours is around 37
epochs of forgone rewards plus a symmetric penalty. It is recoverable, bounded, and leaves the
validator active throughout.

B's failure mode is a double signature — the exact object
`is_slashable_attestation_data` matches.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" /> The
immediate charge is small, but the validator is force-exited and spends over 36 days earning nothing
while being penalised as though absent, and its capital is locked for that whole
period.<Cite src="consensus-specs-electra-beacon-chain" /><Cite src="eth2book-slashing" /> One
misfire per year costs more than several four-hour outages, and B's redundancy does not merely fail to
prevent this — it is the mechanism that causes it.

**The assumption that flips the answer is correlation, and it is a bigger deal than the arithmetic
above.** Everything so far prices an isolated event. If the same automatic failover design is running
across a large fraction of the network — the same client, the same orchestrator, the same cloud
region — then a single triggering event does not produce one slashing, it produces thousands
simultaneously. At that point line two of the invoice stops being small and becomes the only line
that matters.<Cite src="mikeneuder-slashing-7251" /> The last lesson in this module is about exactly
that curve, and it is the reason this decision is a network-level question and not only a
site-reliability one.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

The provability axis is what makes the next lesson enumerable at all: there are exactly three
slashable offences because there are exactly three shapes of contradiction the protocol can verify
from two signed messages and nothing else.

The itemised invoice is what makes the correlation penalty legible when you meet it — you will already
know that three of the four lines are fixed and small, so the one that varies is the one carrying the
entire security argument.

And in the finality module, this distinction is the difference between a chain that has stalled and a
chain that has been attacked. One of those has a self-correcting mechanism running. The other has a
bill being drawn up.
