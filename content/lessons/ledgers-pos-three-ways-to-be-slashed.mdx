Three post-mortems, from three different operators, all ending in the same sentence.

The first: a container orchestrator decided a healthy validator was unhealthy, started a second copy,
and for eleven minutes two processes held the same key.

The second: a host's clock was corrected forward by several hours during a maintenance window, and the
validator client woke up believing it was in an epoch it had already voted in.

The third: a machine died, was rebuilt from a keystore backup, and came back with no record of
anything it had ever signed.

None of these operators attacked anything. All three produced the pair of messages the protocol
defines as proof of an attempt to have it both ways, and all three were slashed. This lesson is about
exactly what that proof looks like, because once you can construct each offence by hand you can also
recognise the ordinary operational accident that produces it.

## The whole definition, in two functions

There is no discretion anywhere in this. The protocol's entire notion of a slashable consensus
offence is two predicates over signed objects.

For attestations, `is_slashable_attestation_data` is four lines and covers two of the three
offences:<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" />

```python
def is_slashable_attestation_data(data_1: AttestationData, data_2: AttestationData) -> bool:
    """
    Check if ``data_1`` and ``data_2`` are slashable according to Casper FFG rules.
    """
    return (
        # Double vote
        (data_1 != data_2 and data_1.target.epoch == data_2.target.epoch)
        or
        # Surround vote
        (data_1.source.epoch < data_2.source.epoch and data_2.target.epoch < data_1.target.epoch)
    )
```

For blocks, `process_proposer_slashing` covers the third: it asserts the two headers share a slot,
share a proposer index, and are not identical — then verifies both
signatures.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" />

Everything below is commentary on those two definitions. Note what is absent from both: no notion of
harm, no notion of intent, no notion of which message came first, and no reference to any state
outside the two objects themselves. That is what makes the evidence self-contained and the accusation
permissionless.

## Offence one: double proposal

**The rule.** A proposer assigned to a slot may sign exactly one `BeaconBlockHeader` for it. Two
distinct signed headers with the same slot and the same proposer index are
slashable.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" />

**Worked example.**

```text
slot          = 9,412,096
proposer      = validator index 401,337

header_1.slot           = 9,412,096
header_1.proposer_index = 401,337
header_1.body_root      = 0x8f2c…41ab

header_2.slot           = 9,412,096
header_2.proposer_index = 401,337
header_2.body_root      = 0x1d7e…c093
```

Slots equal, proposer equal, headers different. The predicate is satisfied and the pair is complete
evidence — no context needed about which chain either block was on, or whether either was ever
included.

**Read the strictness carefully.** The assertion is `header_1 != header_2`, on the whole header. Two
headers that differ only in the graffiti field, or only in the state root because they were built
against slightly different mempool views, are two distinct headers. "I built the same block twice"
is not a defence, because the protocol never compares block *contents* — only header identity.

**How it happens by accident.** Almost always: two processes holding one key, both convinced it is
their turn. The honest-validator specification's countermeasure is an ordering constraint, not a
clever algorithm — save a record to disk that a block has been signed for this slot, *then* generate
and broadcast it, so that a crash mid-routine leaves evidence of a *potentially* signed
block.<Cite src="consensus-specs-validator" />

<Aside kind="note">
That ordering — record first, sign second — is the same discipline as writing to a write-ahead log
before applying a mutation, and for the same reason. The failure you are defending against is not
"the signature was wrong", it is "the process died between deciding and remembering".
</Aside>

## Offence two: double vote

**The rule.** Two attestations whose `AttestationData` differ in any way, but whose target epochs are
equal.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" />

**Worked example.**

```text
attestation_A.source.epoch = 293,880
attestation_A.target.epoch = 293,881
attestation_A.beacon_block_root = 0x77c1…9e40

attestation_B.source.epoch = 293,880
attestation_B.target.epoch = 293,881
attestation_B.beacon_block_root = 0xa30f…2255
```

Same target epoch, different data. Slashable.

**This is broader than it first reads, and the breadth is the trap.** The clause does not say "two
different target checkpoints". It says the two `AttestationData` objects are unequal and their target
epochs match. In the example above the source and target *epochs* are identical; the only difference
is the head vote. That is still a double vote.

So the operational surface is larger than the name suggests. A validator that attests, sees a reorg
change its view of the head, and attests again in the same epoch has double-voted — even though
nothing about its opinion on finality changed. Lighthouse's documentation names this case explicitly:
a deep reorg can change the shuffling and prompt a validator to re-attest in an epoch where it has
already attested, and the protection database exists to refuse the second
signature.<Cite src="lighthouse-slashing-protection" />

The canonical reference on slashing groups the offences differently for this reason, splitting them
by which consensus mechanism they threaten: making two differing attestations for the same target
checkpoint and making a surround vote are the Casper FFG offences, while proposing two blocks for one
slot and attesting to two different head blocks with the same checkpoints are the LMD GHOST
ones.<Cite src="eth2book-slashing" /> Both descriptions are correct. The specification simply
implements the second FFG-style rule and the second LMD-style rule with a single predicate, because
the second is a special case of the first.

## Offence three: surround vote

This is the one that needs a picture, and the one that cannot be checked without memory.

**The rule.** `data_1` surrounds `data_2` when `data_1.source.epoch` is strictly less than
`data_2.source.epoch` **and** `data_2.target.epoch` is strictly less than
`data_1.target.epoch`.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" />

**Worked example.**

B strictly contains A on both ends, so `is_slashable_attestation_data(B, A)` is true.

**Why this is the offence.** A is a signed statement that the history from epoch 240 to 241 is the one
this validator supports. B is a signed statement that a *different, longer* stretch — from 236 all the
way to 245 — is the one it supports, and that stretch spans and therefore contradicts the earlier
claim. This is precisely the manoeuvre a validator would make to walk back an endorsement it had
already given, which is why the span relationship itself is the offence rather than any particular
block being wrong.<Cite src="eth2book-casper-ffg" />

**It is slashable in either direction, and the mechanism is worth being precise about.** The predicate
as written tests one ordering — whether `data_1` surrounds `data_2`. But the evidence submitter
constructs the `AttesterSlashing` object and chooses which attestation goes in the first
position.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" /> So if your later vote is surrounded by
your earlier one, a reporter simply puts the earlier one first. There is no ordering you can adopt
that escapes the rule.

**What is deliberately *not* slashable.** Two attestations with the *same* source epoch and different
target epochs — say 240 → 241 and 240 → 245 — satisfy neither clause. The source epochs are equal, so
the surround clause's strict inequality fails; the target epochs differ, so the double-vote clause
fails. And it must be that way: a validator whose justified checkpoint has not advanced for several
epochs will honestly cast exactly that pattern. The two clauses are the Casper FFG commandments and
nothing more; every additional pattern you might feel ought to be punished was left out on
purpose.<Cite src="eth2book-casper-ffg" />

## The offence is defined against your own past, so something has to remember

Double proposal and double vote can, in principle, be avoided by knowing what you did in the current
slot or epoch. Surround voting cannot: it is defined against the entire span of your history, and the
message that convicts you may be months old.

This is the reason a validator client keeps a **slashing protection database** — a persistent record
of every block slot and every source/target pair the key has signed, consulted before every signature
and updated after it.<Cite src="lighthouse-slashing-protection" /> The specification's protection
best-practices section states the requirement bluntly: the validator client should treat the beacon
node as untrusted, validate the data, check it against a local slashing database, and update that
database with the newly signed object.<Cite src="consensus-specs-validator" />

**EIP-3076 standardises the portable form of that record**, so a key's signing history can move
between clients. Its conditions are worth reading as the operational rules they
are:<Cite src="eip-3076" />

- Refuse to sign any block slashable against the imported blocks, and refuse to sign any block at a
  slot at or below the minimum slot in the imported history.
- Refuse to sign any attestation slashable against the imported attestations.
- Refuse to sign any attestation with a source epoch below the minimum source epoch in the history.
- Refuse to sign any attestation with a target epoch at or below the minimum target epoch in the
  history.

Those last two conditions are what makes a *minimal* database — only the latest messages, rather than
every message — safe to use: refusing anything at or before the recorded watermark is a conservative
over-approximation of the real predicate.<Cite src="eip-3076" />

The EIP's motivating example is not an attack either. A client whose clock is accidentally set a day
ahead — 225 epochs — votes on those epochs, and a validator that then migrates without carrying the
history stands to vote on all of them again as real time catches up. The EIP notes that such time-skew
bugs have been observed in the wild.<Cite src="eip-3076" />

<Aside kind="note">
One genuine narrowing worth knowing: signed data must be within a sequential fork context to
conflict. Messages cannot be slashed across diverging forks — if a chain splits from fork version 1
into versions 2 and 102, messages under 2 are not slashable against messages under 102, though both
are slashable against messages under 1.<Cite src="consensus-specs-validator" /> This is why testnet
keys and mainnet keys are not a slashing risk to each other, and it is the *only* place the rules
care about anything beyond the two objects.
</Aside>

## Losing the database is not a restart problem

The third post-mortem at the top of this lesson is the one that catches good operators, because the
instinct — rebuild from the keystore and resume — is exactly wrong.

A key with no signing history is a key that cannot answer the only question that matters before
signing: *what have I already claimed?* Any attestation it now casts might duplicate or surround one
it made before the loss, and it has no way to check. Clients treat this as a hard stop: Lighthouse
refuses to start when it finds validator keys not registered in its protection database, and
recovering a validator from a private key alone leaves an empty database that must be filled by
importing the history from a trusted source before signing
resumes.<Cite src="lighthouse-slashing-protection" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-validator" />

If the history genuinely cannot be recovered, the only safe action is to wait. What you are waiting
for is enough elapsed epochs that no vote you could now cast is capable of conflicting with a vote
from before the loss — a new attestation's source and target both being later than anything you could
have signed. Waiting costs inactivity penalties, which are the cheap instrument. Resuming costs a
slashing, which is not.

**Doppelganger protection is the other half of this**, and it deserves an honest description because
it is routinely oversold. On startup, the validator client stays silent for two to three epochs and
listens for messages from its own keys before signing anything
slashable.<Cite src="lighthouse-doppelganger" /> Lighthouse's own documentation is explicit that the
mechanism is imperfect and best-effort — your beacon node may simply not see the other instance's
messages — and that it always costs two to three epochs of missed duties on every restart. Its stated
guidance is that users should never rely on it and should treat duplicate validators with exactly the
caution they would if it did not exist.<Cite src="lighthouse-doppelganger" />

## Where this pays off

Every one of these offences carries the identical penalty — the protocol does not grade them, and the
reference on slashing says so directly: when it comes to the punishment, it does not matter which rule
was broken.<Cite src="eth2book-slashing" /> The variable that actually determines what a slashing
costs is not the offence at all, and the next lesson is about what it is instead.

The two FFG clauses are also the mechanical content of
[accountable safety](/c/accountable-safety). The counting argument says a third of the stake must
have signed contradictory messages for two conflicting checkpoints to finalise; these two clauses are
what "contradictory" means, and the fact that both are decidable from two objects alone is what turns
an economic argument into an enforceable one.

And in the security track, this is the template for a whole class of designs: define misbehaviour as
a locally-checkable predicate over signed artefacts, pay people to report it, and you get enforcement
without a watchdog. Fraud proofs on optimistic rollups are the same idea with a different predicate.
