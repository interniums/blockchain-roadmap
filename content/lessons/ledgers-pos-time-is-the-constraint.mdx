An indexer computes "time since deployment" by multiplying a block-number delta by twelve. It has been
running for two years and it is wrong by hours, in a direction that only ever gets worse.

Nothing is broken. The chain has two counters, and they are not the same counter. One of them is a
clock and the other is a tally of successes. The gap between them is the first thing this lesson
makes precise, because it is also the gap in which every timing attack, every missed duty and every
proposal to make Ethereum faster actually lives.

## The clock, exactly

Two constants define the whole schedule. The mainnet config sets `SLOT_DURATION_MS` to 12,000
milliseconds, and the mainnet preset sets `SLOTS_PER_EPOCH` to
32.<Cite src="consensus-specs-config-mainnet" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" />

So an epoch is 384 seconds: six minutes and twenty-four seconds. Every duty in the protocol is
scheduled against one of those two units, and the choice of which one is never arbitrary.

Each slot has **exactly one** validator eligible to propose, selected by the beacon
chain.<Cite src="ethereum-docs-pos" /> Note the word: eligible, not obliged in any enforceable sense.
The proposer may be offline, may be censored, may decide the block is not worth building. When that
happens the slot simply passes with nothing in it.

## A slot is a heartbeat; a block is an outcome

This is where the indexer went wrong. **Slot numbers advance whether or not anything happens. Block
numbers advance only when something does.**

The consequence compounds. Over a long enough sample, the execution layer's block number and the
consensus layer's slot number drift apart by exactly the number of missed slots, and that drift is
permanent — there is no catch-up mechanism, because there is nothing to catch up. The slot was not
delayed. It was empty, and then it was over.

Empirical work on the consensus layer measures participation and missed duties as first-class
quantities precisely because they are neither zero nor
constant.<Cite src="arxiv-pos-participation" /> This module's measurement practice asks you to
sample real slots and count the gaps for yourself, which is worth more than any figure a lesson could
quote, because the rate depends on the week.

## Inside twelve seconds: what the budget is spent on

A slot is not twelve idle seconds waiting for a block. It is a sequence of deadlines, and the
consensus layer states them as fractions of the slot rather than as absolute times — which is itself
a design statement, since it means the shape survives a change to the slot length.

The mainnet config sets three:<Cite src="consensus-specs-config-mainnet" />

```text
PROPOSER_REORG_CUTOFF_BPS  = 1667   ~17% of the slot, ~2.0s
ATTESTATION_DUE_BPS        = 3333   ~33% of the slot, ~4.0s
AGGREGATE_DUE_BPS          = 6667   ~67% of the slot, ~8.0s
```

Read them as three consecutive jobs.

**The first sixth belongs to the proposer.** The fork-choice specification's `is_proposing_on_time`
returns true only while the time into the slot is at or below the re-org cutoff, and that predicate is
one of the conditions `get_proposer_head` requires before a proposer may build on the previous block's
parent instead of on the previous block
itself.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fork-choice" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-config-mainnet" /> Past
the cutoff, a proposer that is late has forfeited the option to argue about history and can only
extend it.

**The first third is the propagation budget.** An attester must broadcast when it has received a valid
block from the expected proposer *or* when the attestation deadline passes — whichever comes
first.<Cite src="consensus-specs-validator" /> You met that rule in
[propagation latency](/c/propagation-latency); here is its consequence stated from the validator's
seat: **the protocol never waits for you.** If the block has not reached a validator by the deadline,
that validator votes for the previous head, and its vote is signed and gone.

**The second third is aggregation.** Individual attestations are collected into aggregates and
rebroadcast by the deadline at two thirds of the slot.<Cite src="consensus-specs-validator" />

**The last third is inclusion.** `MIN_ATTESTATION_INCLUSION_DELAY` is 1 slot, so the earliest an
attestation can appear on chain is the block after the slot it voted
in.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-presets" /> The votes cast during a slot are the next
proposer's raw material.

stops assuming two honest participants agree about what time it is — which puts a hard floor under
how finely any of these deadlines can be carved.
</Aside>

## The epoch is where consequences are computed

Slots are where messages happen. Epochs are where they turn into state.

At the end of every epoch, `process_epoch` runs a fixed sequence: justification and finalisation,
rewards and penalties, registry updates, slashings, effective-balance updates, and several resets of
rolling state.<Cite src="consensus-specs-phase0-beacon-chain" /> Nothing in that list happens
per-slot. Your rewards are not credited when you attest; your effective balance does not move when
your actual balance does; a validator queued for activation does not become active mid-epoch.

Two things follow that are easy to miss.

**Checkpoints are epoch-aligned.** A validator's FFG vote targets `epoch_boundary_block_root`, the root
of the block at the most recent epoch boundary.<Cite src="consensus-specs-validator" /> This is the
object [Casper FFG](/c/casper-ffg) justifies and finalises — not individual blocks. It is also why
the first slot of an epoch matters more than the other 31: if it is missed, the boundary root is the
last block *before* it, so the checkpoint everyone votes on is an older block wearing a newer epoch
number.<Cite src="consensus-specs-validator" />

**Duties are epoch-scheduled, with asymmetric warning.** The shuffling provides at least one epoch of
lookahead on attesting assignments, so a validator learns its committee and slot for the next epoch
at the start of the current one. That lookahead explicitly **does not apply to proposing**, which can
only be checked for the epoch in question.<Cite src="consensus-specs-validator" />

That asymmetry is a security property rather than an implementation shortcut. If proposers were known
an epoch ahead, an attacker would have six minutes of warning in which to locate and attack the one
machine that matters next.

## How far behind is "finalised"?

Justification and finalisation move one epoch at a time, so the finalised checkpoint trails the head
by roughly two epochs — 64 slots, about 12.8 minutes by the
clock above.<Cite src="eth2book-consensus" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-config-mainnet" />

Treat that as the mechanism's floor, not as a measured figure. This course's research register holds
a conflict here: the two-epoch arithmetic gives about 12.8 minutes, while one published benchmark
reported a median closer to 16 minutes over a 24-hour window. The sources disagree and neither was
adopted. If your product depends on the number, measure it from the position you will actually
occupy, and record the date.

## Why twelve seconds is hard to change

The obvious question is why the slot is not shorter. Twelve seconds of latency is a long time to hold
a user in front of a spinner, and the constants above are, on their face, just numbers in a YAML file.

EIP-7782 proposed exactly that — reducing the slot from twelve seconds to six — and it was declined
for the Glamsterdam upgrade and deferred.<Cite src="eip-7782" /> As of this writing the mainnet
config still reads `SLOT_DURATION_MS: 12000`, and the fork epochs for Gloas and the fork after it are
both set to the far-future sentinel, meaning neither is
scheduled.<Cite src="consensus-specs-config-mainnet" />

The reason it is hard is the sub-slot budget, not the slot. Halving the slot halves every interval
above it in absolute terms: the propagation budget, the aggregation window, the inclusion gap, and
the margin above that half-second of tolerated clock disparity. The physics of getting a block to
most of the world does not halve to
match.<Cite src="consensus-specs-p2p" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-validator" />

There is direct evidence in the config that the argument is about fractions rather than the total.
Alongside the current values, the mainnet config already carries a re-carved set for a future fork —
`ATTESTATION_DUE_BPS_GLOAS` at 2500, `AGGREGATE_DUE_BPS_GLOAS` at 5000, `PAYLOAD_DUE_BPS` at 5000 and
`PAYLOAD_ATTESTATION_DUE_BPS` at 7500.<Cite src="consensus-specs-config-mainnet" /> The slot stays
twelve seconds; the work inside it gets reorganised to make room for a separately-timed payload.

<Aside kind="warn">
Material written in 2025 that predicts six-second slots in the next upgrade is describing a proposal
that was not accepted for it.<Cite src="eip-7782" /> The general habit worth building: when a source
quotes a protocol constant, check it against the config or preset file rather than against another
article. The files are short and they are the thing clients actually read.
</Aside>

<Check type="predict" concept="slot-timing">
A proposer for slot *N* is running on a machine whose clock is 700 milliseconds fast. It builds
correctly and publishes what it believes is exactly on time.

Predict two distinct problems, and say which deadline each one collides with.

<Answer>
**Problem one: the block may be rejected on gossip as too early.** The consensus layer tolerates
`MAXIMUM_GOSSIP_CLOCK_DISPARITY` of 500 milliseconds when validating slot ranges on gossiped
messages.<Cite src="consensus-specs-p2p" /> A 700-millisecond lead exceeds that tolerance, so peers
whose clocks are correct can see a block for a slot that, by their reckoning, has not started.

**Problem two: the block is late relative to the attestation deadline even though it is early
relative to the proposer.** The proposer thinks it published at the start of slot *N*; in real time
it published 700 milliseconds *before* the slot began, which sounds harmless until you notice that
its notion of the 33% deadline is also 700 milliseconds early. Every subsequent judgement it makes
about timing — whether to re-org, when to give up waiting — is displaced by the same amount, against
attesters who are on time.<Cite src="consensus-specs-validator" />

**The general point:** in a system where the only synchronisation is a wall clock, clock skew is not
an operational annoyance, it is a consensus fault with a signature attached. This is why NTP
discipline is a first-class requirement for validator operators, and why the slashing-protection
material in the next lessons treats a clock jump as one of the classic ways an honest operator
signs something they should not have.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

The 32-slot epoch is the unit that makes the next lesson possible: committees are sized so that every
active validator attests exactly once per epoch, which only makes sense once you know the epoch has
32 slots to spread them across.

The one-epoch attesting lookahead is the security assumption behind committee sampling — an attacker
cannot pre-position against validators whose assignments are not yet known.

And the slot-versus-block distinction is load-bearing for the finality module. A reorg of one slot is
a normal outcome of a propagation wavefront; a missed slot is not a reorg at all; and a confirmation
policy that counts blocks rather than slots is measuring a quantity that quietly changes meaning
whenever the network has a bad hour.
