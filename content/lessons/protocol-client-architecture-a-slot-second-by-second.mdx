A validator's dashboard shows a missed **head vote** for slot 12,884,301. Not a missed attestation — the
attestation went out, on time, and got included. The source and target votes were correct and paid. Only
the head vote was wrong.

Pull up the block explorer for that slot and there is a block. It exists, it is canonical, it has been
finalised for hours. The validator voted for its parent instead.

Nothing malfunctioned. The block arrived at 4.6 seconds into the slot, and the deadline for deciding what
the head was had passed at 3.999. This lesson is about the deadlines inside a slot, where they come from
in the specification, and why "twelve seconds" is the least important number in the structure.

## The slot is a schedule, not a duration

The mainnet configuration sets `SLOT_DURATION_MS` to 12,000 — twelve seconds — and the phase0 preset sets
`SLOTS_PER_EPOCH` to 32, making an epoch 384 seconds, about six and a half
minutes.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-preset-phase0-v161" />

Both of those are true and neither tells you how the slot behaves. What governs behaviour is a set of
deadlines *inside* the slot, and the way the specification expresses them has changed in a way worth
noticing.

The current scheme is basis points. `BASIS_POINTS` is 10,000, and a helper converts a basis-point figure
into milliseconds:<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" />

```python
def get_slot_component_duration_ms(basis_points: uint64) -> uint64:
    """
    Calculate the duration of a slot component in milliseconds.
    """
    return basis_points * SLOT_DURATION_MS // BASIS_POINTS
```

The mainnet config then sets three of them:<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" />

- `PROPOSER_REORG_CUTOFF_BPS: 1667` — about 17% of the slot.
- `ATTESTATION_DUE_BPS: 3333` — about 33%.
- `AGGREGATE_DUE_BPS: 6667` — about 67%.

Do the arithmetic at the mainnet slot duration and the familiar numbers fall out, slightly bent:

- Proposer reorg cutoff: 1667 × 12,000 ÷ 10,000 = **2,000 ms**.
- Attestation due: 3333 × 12,000 ÷ 10,000 = **3,999 ms**.
- Aggregate due: 6667 × 12,000 ÷ 10,000 = **8,000 ms**.

Not four seconds. Three thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine milliseconds, because 3,333 basis points is
not exactly a third. The millisecond does not matter operationally. What matters is what the encoding
tells you: **these are fractions of a slot, not absolute times.** Change `SLOT_DURATION_MS` and every
deadline moves with it, without anyone editing a deadline.

That is a recent refactor and it left fossils behind. `SECONDS_PER_SLOT`, value 12, is still in the phase0
spec and marked *deprecated*.<Cite src="consensus-specs-beacon-chain-v161" /> `INTERVALS_PER_SLOT`, value
3, is still in the fork-choice spec and marked *deprecated*
too.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> That second one is where the folklore comes from: for years the slot
was described as three four-second intervals, and material written in that era will tell you the
attestation deadline is "one third of the way in" as though the third were the definition rather than the
current value of a configurable fraction.

## t = 0 — the proposer publishes

One validator is selected to propose per slot. Its job started before the slot did: it has already called
`engine_forkchoiceUpdated` with payload attributes to begin a build, and it collects the result with
`engine_getPayload` — which returns the most recent version of that build at the time of the
call.<Cite src="engine-api-paris" /> Later means fuller. Later also means the block reaches attesters
later.

The specification names the point where "later" turns expensive. `PROPOSER_REORG_CUTOFF_BPS` is 1,667
basis points — 2,000 ms on mainnet — and the fork-choice spec uses it in a helper called
`is_proposing_on_time`.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" />
A proposer that publishes after that point is, by the protocol's own definition, not on time, and one of
the consequences is that the *next* proposer is permitted to consider reorging it out.

So the proposer's twelve seconds are really two: build value until about two seconds in, then get out.

## t ≈ 4 s — the attestation deadline, and how a late block loses

Every active validator attests once per epoch, in an assigned slot, on an assigned
[committee](/c/committee).<Cite src="ethereum-org-attestations" /> The rule for *when* is one sentence in
the validator spec, and it is the sentence behind the missed head vote at the top of this lesson.

A validator should create and broadcast its attestation when either the validator has received a valid
block from the expected proposer for the assigned slot, **or** `get_attestation_due_ms(epoch)`
milliseconds have elapsed since the start of the slot — **whichever comes
first**.<Cite src="consensus-specs-validator-v161" />

Read the disjunction carefully. The deadline is not a wait; it is a ceiling. A validator that sees the
block at 900 ms attests at 900 ms. A validator that has seen nothing by 3,999 ms attests at 3,999 ms
anyway, because an attestation withheld is worth nothing.

And what does it attest *to*? The spec says: let `head_block` be the result of running fork choice during
the assigned slot.<Cite src="consensus-specs-validator-v161" /> Fork choice over the blocks it has
actually seen. If this slot's block is not among them, the heaviest thing it knows is the previous slot's
block — so that is the head vote.

That is how a late block loses. Not through a rule that punishes lateness, but through the ordinary
operation of a deadline against a proposer who missed it. The attesters were not wrong; they voted for the
head as of the moment they were required to decide. By the time the block arrived, the votes that would
have supported it had already been cast for its parent.

The declination is worth stating precisely, because half-remembered versions of it circulate. EIP-7782 is
titled "Reduce Block Latency" and proposes cutting the slot from 12s to 6s.<Cite src="eip-7782" /> The
Glamsterdam hardfork meta EIP lists it under **Declined for Inclusion**, alongside EIP-7805
(FOCIL).<Cite src="eip-7773" /> Twelve seconds, with the deadline at 3,999 ms, is what mainnet does. A
declined EIP is not a dead one — it can be picked up by a later upgrade — but it is not scheduled, and
nothing about its future has a date.

### The counterweight: proposer boost

If attesters vote for the parent when a block is late, an adversary who can delay a block by a second has
a cheap reorg. The fork choice compensates.

`PROPOSER_SCORE_BOOST` is 40, and the spec explains the unit: the boost and the reorg weight thresholds
are percentage values measured with respect to the weight of a single
committee.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" /> A timely
block gets a temporary weight bonus worth 40% of one committee's weight, applied during its own slot.
Three related constants sit beside it — `REORG_HEAD_WEIGHT_THRESHOLD` 20,
`REORG_PARENT_WEIGHT_THRESHOLD` 160, and `REORG_MAX_EPOCHS_SINCE_FINALIZATION` 2 — which gate when a
proposer may deliberately reorg a late head.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" />

[Proposer boost](/c/proposer-boost) and its trade-offs belong to the fork-choice module; what matters here
is that the boost exists *because* of the deadline. The deadline creates the attack, the boost prices it,
and the value of the boost is a dial with a failure mode at each end.

## t ≈ 8 s — aggregation

Individual attestations are too numerous to put in blocks one by one, so a subset of each committee
aggregates them. `TARGET_AGGREGATORS_PER_COMMITTEE` is 16.<Cite src="consensus-specs-validator-v161" />
Selected validators collect matching attestations from their subnet, combine the signatures, and publish
the aggregate by `AGGREGATE_DUE_BPS` — 8,000 ms into the
slot.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" />

The last four seconds of the slot are, in effect, the propagation budget for aggregates so that the next
proposer has something to include. `MIN_ATTESTATION_INCLUSION_DELAY` is 1 slot, so the earliest an
attestation can appear on chain is the slot after the one it
attests to.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-preset-phase0-v161" />

Stack the whole schedule and the twelve seconds are fully spoken for: two seconds for the proposer to
build and publish, two more for the block to reach attesters, four for aggregates to form, four to deliver
them. Everything else the protocol wants to do in a slot — block propagation, execution validation, data
availability sampling — competes inside the same budget. That is why measured attestation timings are a
live research subject rather than a solved one.<Cite src="rig-attestation-timings" />

### Where the Engine API calls land on this clock

The previous lesson gave you three methods with no timing attached. Put them on the schedule and the
pressure on each becomes obvious.

**For a proposer**, `forkchoiceUpdated` with payload attributes fires as early as the consensus client can
commit to a parent — typically as soon as it has processed the previous slot's block. `getPayload` fires
at the last tolerable moment before publishing, and the specification's 1-second timeout on that method is
not a generous allowance when the whole publish window is about two
seconds.<Cite src="engine-api-paris" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" />

**For everyone else**, `newPayload` fires the moment a beacon block arrives, and it is on the critical
path to the head vote. The attester cannot vote for a block it has not validated, so execution time for
that payload is spent out of the same budget that ends at 3,999 ms. The specification's 8-second timeout
on `newPayload` is a protocol-level upper bound, not a target — a payload that took eight seconds to
validate has already cost you the head vote and most of the next
slot.<Cite src="engine-api-paris" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" />

Then `forkchoiceUpdated` follows to move the head. That one is not on the attestation critical path, which
is why a node under load will often show head updates trailing payload validation without any effect on
voting.

There is one recurring exception to the rhythm. The beacon state transition decomposes into per-slot
processing, block processing, and epoch processing — and epoch processing runs only at epoch boundaries,
where it does the expensive accounting: justification, finalisation, rewards and
penalties.<Cite src="consensus-specs-beacon-chain-v161" /> Every 32nd slot therefore carries work the
other 31 do not, inside the same twelve seconds and against the same 3,999 ms deadline. If you are
sampling timings, sample enough slots to see epoch boundaries as their own population rather than as
outliers.

## What a validator actually does all day

The schedule above makes the job sound dramatic. It is not.

**Attesting is the constant duty.** One attestation per epoch, every epoch, forever. An epoch is 384
seconds, so that is 225 attestations per day, per
validator.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-preset-phase0-v161" />
Each carries three votes: source, target and head.<Cite src="ethereum-org-attestations" />

**Aggregating comes round occasionally** — 16 aggregators per committee per
slot.<Cite src="consensus-specs-validator-v161" />

**Sync-committee duty applies only if selected.** `SYNC_COMMITTEE_SIZE` is 512 and
`EPOCHS_PER_SYNC_COMMITTEE_PERIOD` is 256, so a selected validator serves a fixed stretch of 256 epochs —
a little over 27 hours.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-preset-altair-v161" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" />

**Proposing is rare.** There is exactly one proposal per slot — 7,200 per day, shared across the entire
active validator set — while every validator in that set attests 225 times a day. Whatever the size of the
set, that ratio is the whole story: proposals are a scarce resource divided among everyone, attestations
are a duty each of them performs.

The operational consequence is the whole point. Because income is dominated by attestations, and
attestations are graded on being **on time, every epoch**, the important property of a staking setup is
not how well it builds blocks. It is uptime and latency, sustained indefinitely. A setup that produces
brilliant blocks and misses attestations during every restart is a worse setup than a dull one that never
misses.

## The second split: beacon node and validator client

Everything above is the consensus client's work — but "the consensus client" is usually two processes as
well.

The **beacon node** handles peer-to-peer networking, keeps the beacon state, runs fork choice, drives the
Engine API, and exposes an HTTP API. The **validator client** holds the signing keys, asks the beacon node
what duties it has, and signs.<Cite src="lighthouse-book" /><Cite src="prysm-docs" />

Three things follow, and each is a real operational lever.

**The process holding the keys is not the process exposed to the public network.** The beacon node talks
to strangers. The validator client talks to the beacon node. That is a meaningful reduction in what an
attacker who compromises the networked process gets.

**One validator client can be pointed at more than one beacon node.** If one beacon node is resyncing,
restarting, or simply wrong, duties can still be fetched and signed against another.

**Slashing protection lives with the validator client.** It is a local database recording what these keys
have already signed, and it is what stops a restarted or duplicated process from producing a second
conflicting signature. There is an interchange format specified for moving it between
clients — EIP-3076 — precisely because moving keys without moving that history is how operators
[slash](/c/slashing) themselves.<Cite src="eip-3076" /><Cite src="lighthouse-slashing-protection" />

<Aside kind="warn">
"I copied the keystores to the new machine and started it up" is the sentence that precedes most
self-inflicted slashings. The keystores are not the state that matters; the slashing-protection database
is.<Cite src="lighthouse-slashing-protection" /> Clients ship a detection mechanism for the specific case
of the same keys running twice — doppelganger protection, which waits and listens for your own validators
attesting before it signs anything — and it costs a couple of epochs of downtime on every
start.<Cite src="lighthouse-doppelganger" /> That trade is almost always worth taking.
</Aside>

## What is proposed, and what is not scheduled

The mainnet configuration file contains constants for deadlines that are not in force. `ATTESTATION_DUE_BPS_GLOAS`
is 2,500 — 25% of the slot — with `AGGREGATE_DUE_BPS_GLOAS` at 5,000 and `PAYLOAD_ATTESTATION_DUE_BPS` at
7,500.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" /> A restructured slot, with the attestation
deadline pulled earlier to make room for a payload-attestation phase, is written down.

And in the same file, three lines above: `GLOAS_FORK_EPOCH: 18446744073709551615`.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" />
That is 2⁶⁴ − 1, the maximum value of a `uint64`, and the file's own comment explains the convention —
forks that are disabled are temporarily set to the max uint64 value.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" />

This is the cleanest answer available to "when does Glamsterdam ship". The specification carries the
constants and encodes the activation as *never, for now*. Anyone quoting a date is quoting something other
than the config, and the config is what clients read.

<Check type="predict" concept="slot-phases">
Your team runs validators. An engineer proposes moving them to a datacentre with better hardware but
roughly 400 ms more network latency to most beacon-chain peers. They argue: "The attestation deadline is
four seconds. Four hundred milliseconds is a tenth of that. It is well within budget."

Predict the effect, and say what you would measure before deciding.

<Answer>
**The budget framing is wrong, and the error is that 400 ms is not being spent against a four-second
allowance.**

**What the deadline actually is.** A validator attests when it has seen a valid block from the expected
proposer, *or* at `get_attestation_due_ms` — 3,999 ms on mainnet — whichever comes
first.<Cite src="consensus-specs-validator-v161" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" /> The
attestation goes out on time in either case. The deadline is not what you are at risk of missing.

**What you are at risk of missing is the block.** The quantity that matters is whether the block arrives
before your deadline, and blocks do not arrive at a fixed time. They arrive in a distribution, and the
proposer is allowed to publish as late as roughly 2,000 ms and still be "on
time".<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" /> Adding 400 ms
shifts your view of that entire distribution 400 ms later against a fixed cut-off.

**So the effect is on head votes specifically.** Source and target votes are about checkpoints and are
unaffected by whether you saw this slot's block. The head vote is the one that requires having seen it. If
you were previously seeing 1% of blocks after the deadline, and 400 ms of the arrival distribution sits
between the old and new positions, you now miss the head vote on every block in that band. Source and
target keep paying; head-vote rewards degrade. That is a small, permanent revenue loss that will not show
up as an error anywhere.

**The second-order effect nobody predicts.** Your validators voting for the parent is exactly the
mechanism that reorgs a late block.<Cite src="consensus-specs-validator-v161" /> At small scale that is
your problem alone. At large scale, a systematically late-viewing operator becomes a participant in
reorging honest proposers, which is a network externality rather than a private cost.

**What to measure before deciding, not after.**

*Block arrival time relative to slot start, as a distribution.* From the candidate location, over
thousands of slots. One number is useless; you need the tail, because the tail is what crosses 3,999 ms.

*The fraction already past 3,599 ms today.* That is the population that the extra 400 ms moves across the
line. If it is negligible, the move is cheap; if the distribution has a fat shoulder there, it is not.

*Your current head-vote inclusion rate as a baseline.* You cannot detect a regression in a number you were
not tracking, and this one will not alarm on its own.

*Aggregate publication timing.* If any of your validators aggregate, they are working against the 8,000 ms
deadline with the same shift applied.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" />

**The reframe for your engineer.** The deadline is fixed and the block arrival is a random variable. You
are not spending 400 ms out of 4,000; you are moving a distribution against a cut-off, and the only thing
that matters is how much probability mass crosses it.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

- **The next lesson** takes the same clock somewhere uncomfortable. Everything here assumed clients behave
  correctly. What happens when a third of them, or two thirds, behave incorrectly in the same way at the
  same moment is a quantitative question with two named
  breakpoints.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" />
- **The module's practice** asks you to sample a hundred consecutive slots and plot block arrival times
  with the 3,999 ms line drawn on the chart. The shape of that histogram explains more about validator
  economics than any amount of prose.<Cite src="rig-attestation-timings" />
- **The consensus-specs module** shows you where every constant in this lesson lives, and how to change
  one and watch the specification behave differently — including running epochs at a shrunken parameter
  set so that finalisation happens inside a REPL session rather than
  overnight.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-preset-phase0-v161" />

The habit worth forming: when a system quotes you a period, ask what the deadlines *inside* the period
are. Twelve seconds is a scheduling unit. 2,000, 3,999 and 8,000 milliseconds are what your infrastructure
is actually being graded against.
