Open the community client-diversity dashboard and look at the consensus-layer section. It shows three
datasets, one above the other, all labelled "updated daily".

The first says Teku has 99.83% and Lighthouse has 0.16%. The second says Lighthouse has 50.98% and Teku
has 7.84%. The third says Teku has 53.86% and Lighthouse has 20.6%.<Cite src="clientdiversity-org" />

These are not three views of the same number. They cannot all be approximately right; two of them cannot
even be approximately right at the same time. And every one of them is on the same page, under the same
heading, with the same freshness label.

That is the honest state of client-share data in 2026, and it is the reason this lesson is built around
the *thresholds* rather than around the numbers. The thresholds are stable, derivable and consequential.
The numbers are contested, and a curriculum that teaches a percentage teaches something that was wrong
before it was published.

## Two breakpoints, and why they are those numbers

Client diversity is usually argued as a value. It is better understood as a blast-radius calculation with
two named breakpoints, both of which fall out of the two-thirds supermajority that
[Casper FFG](/c/casper-ffg) requires.

### Above one third: finality stalls

A bug in a consensus client with over 33% of the Ethereum nodes could prevent the consensus layer from
finalizing, meaning users could not trust that transactions would not be reverted or changed at some
point — a situation ethereum.org calls out as particularly problematic for the applications built on
top.<Cite src="ethereum-org-client-diversity" />

The mechanism is arithmetic. Justification needs a two-thirds supermajority of stake attesting to a
checkpoint link. Remove more than a third of the validators — because their client crashed, or stopped
attesting, or attested to something the others reject — and the remaining set cannot reach two thirds no
matter how correctly it behaves. Finality stops advancing.

This is a [liveness](/c/liveness-property) failure. Blocks may still be produced; the chain may still
extend. What stops is the guarantee. And it is recoverable: the [inactivity leak](/c/inactivity-leak)
gradually bleeds the balances of validators that are not participating until the participating set once
again constitutes two thirds, at which point finality resumes. Unpleasant, expensive, survivable, and it
heals on its own.

### Above two thirds: an invalid chain finalises

A critical bug in a client with a two-thirds majority could cause the chain to incorrectly split and
finalize, leaving a large set of validators stuck on an invalid chain — and if they want to rejoin the
correct chain, those validators face slashing or a slow and expensive voluntary withdrawal and
reactivation.<Cite src="ethereum-org-client-diversity" />

This is a [safety](/c/safety-property) failure, and it does not heal. Finality is the property that makes
"this cannot be reverted" mean something; two thirds of the network asserting a chain the rest considers
invalid produces a finalised history that a correct implementation refuses to accept. There is no
protocol-level mechanism for un-finalising something. There is no fork choice that resolves it, because
fork choice only ever considers blocks descending from the last finalised checkpoint — and the two sides
no longer agree on what that is.

Resolution becomes a social process: humans deciding which chain is Ethereum, client teams shipping
patches, exchanges and bridges picking a side. That is a governance event, not an incident response.

## The asymmetry is what makes this urgent

Two failures, both bad. The reason diversity is argued with the intensity it is comes from *who* is
punished in each case, and the answer is backwards from intuition.

**If your client is a small minority and it breaks**, you go offline. You stop attesting, you accrue
inactivity penalties, and when the bug is fixed you come back. The loss is bounded, proportional to the
downtime, and entirely yours. Nobody else notices.

**If a supermajority client finalises an invalid chain, the validators who were right are the ones in
trouble.** They are on a minority fork the protocol cannot reconcile. Their options are to stay on a chain
almost nobody else recognises, or to move to the majority chain and be slashed for the conflicting
attestations that move requires.<Cite src="ethereum-org-client-diversity" />

And the penalty is worst precisely because it is correlated. The magnitude of a slashing scales with the
number of culpable validators, with a two-thirds majority slashed
maximally.<Cite src="ethereum-org-client-diversity" /> Mass simultaneous slashing is the case the penalty
schedule was designed to make catastrophic, because uncorrelated slashings are usually operator error
while correlated ones look like an attack.

<Aside kind="note">
Documentation from the pre-consolidation era quotes the maximum as 32 ETH per
validator.<Cite src="ethereum-org-client-diversity" /> Since Electra, `MIN_ACTIVATION_BALANCE` is still 32

</Aside>

So the incentives point the right way. Running the minority client is the individually cheaper insurance
*and* the collectively better outcome — an unusually favourable alignment, of the kind that is normally
missing from coordination problems.

It has still not been enough to move the numbers. That is worth sitting with, because it is a fact about
human systems rather than about protocols: a correctly aligned incentive that requires deliberate
migration work loses to defaults.

## Why nobody can tell you the current share

Now back to those three datasets, because understanding *why* they disagree is more durable than any of
them.

**They measure different populations.** One dataset comes from crawling the peer-to-peer network and
counting nodes. Another comes from fingerprinting attestation and block-production behaviour to attribute
stake. A node is not a validator, and one node can serve thousands of validators. A large custodian
running one client across an enormous stake barely moves a node count and dominates a stake weighting.
Two honest methods, two different questions, two different answers.

**At least one of them is visibly broken.** A dataset reporting 99.83% for one client and 0.0% for three
others is not measuring client share; it is a classifier that has collapsed, most likely because the
behavioural fingerprints it keys on stopped being distinctive.<Cite src="clientdiversity-org" /> That is a
useful thing to have seen, because it is what a broken measurement looks like when it is presented with
the same confidence as a working one.

**The execution-layer side has the same problem, and the dashboard says so about itself.** It carries two
sources: one giving Geth 50.13%, Nethermind 25.46%, Besu 9.45%, Reth 7.67% and Erigon 6.53%, and another
giving Nethermind 43%, Geth 43%, Besu 8%, Reth 3% and Erigon 3%.<Cite src="clientdiversity-org" /> The
page then editorialises about both: it states that the first dataset has been removed, on the grounds that
it represents what clients show to free peers and it is not well understood how that correlates with
actual usage — and it labels the second **stale**, noting it is largely manually gathered, that much of it
has not been updated in over a year, and that it rests on 59.4% self-reported coverage with the remainder
assumed to be mostly Geth.<Cite src="clientdiversity-org" />

A dashboard publishing a number alongside its own reasons for distrusting that number is doing the right
thing. It is also telling you plainly that "look up the current share" is not an available operation.

### What you can say honestly

Three things survive the uncertainty, and they are enough to act on.

**The thresholds are not in dispute.** 33% and 66%, with a liveness failure at the first and a safety
failure at the second.<Cite src="ethereum-org-client-diversity" />

**Concentration is real on both layers.** Every dataset, whatever it measures, shows one client
substantially ahead of the rest on both the consensus and execution
sides.<Cite src="clientdiversity-org" /> The disagreement is about *which* client and *how far* ahead,
not about whether concentration exists.

**The set of viable clients is larger than most material admits.** The dashboard lists seven stable
consensus clients and six stable execution clients, including Reth as a production execution
client.<Cite src="clientdiversity-org" /><Cite src="reth-repo" /> "Geth, Nethermind, Besu, Erigon" is a
list from an earlier era, and the practical objection that there is nothing to switch *to* is weaker than
it was.

## What each failure does to your product

You are reading this as someone who ships on Ethereum rather than someone who runs the chain, so the
useful exercise is to price each scenario in your own system.

**A finality stall — the 33% case.** The chain keeps producing blocks. Your transactions keep confirming.
What freezes is anything keyed on finalisation: the `finalized` block tag stops advancing, and every
consumer of it stalls with it — bridges that wait for finality before releasing funds, rollup settlement
that anchors to finalised L1 state, treasury operations that require a finalised confirmation before
booking. Meanwhile everything keyed on confirmation depth carries on as though nothing has happened, which
means half your system is running and half is stopped.

**This one you can engineer around**, and the engineering is unglamorous: know which of your flows depend
on finality and which depend on depth, make that dependency explicit rather than incidental, alert on
finality lag as a first-class metric, and have a decision written down in advance about which flows pause
and which continue. The chain recovers on its own; your job is to not make irreversible decisions during
the gap and to not have your users discover the distinction from a support ticket.

**A bad finalisation — the 66% case.** Your node follows one chain. Which one depends on which client you
happen to run, and you will not know which side is "correct" for hours or days, because correctness here
is being decided by people rather than by code. Balances differ across the split. A transaction that
finalised on one side did not exist on the other. Every reconciliation assumption you hold breaks
simultaneously.

**This one you cannot engineer around**, and pretending otherwise is the mistake. The only useful
preparations are detection and a stop: cross-check finalised roots against an independent node running a
different client, and have a documented halt — pause withdrawals, pause settlement, pause anything
irreversible — that a human can trigger in minutes. The value of running a second node on a different
client is not redundancy. It is that a disagreement between two clients is the *only* local signal you
will get that something of this kind is happening.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

<Check type="explain" concept="supermajority-client-risk">
Your staking operations lead proposes: "We run the same consensus client as most of the network. That is
actually the safe choice — if there is a bug, we are in the majority, so whatever happens is what happens
to everyone. Running a minority client means we break alone."

Say whether the reasoning holds, and what you would do.

<Answer>
**The reasoning inverts the risk. Being in the majority is where the unbounded losses are.**

**Walk the minority case, which they think is the bad one.** Your client has a bug and stops attesting.
You go offline. You accrue inactivity penalties for the duration. When a patch ships, you restart and
resume. The loss is bounded by downtime and it is fully recoverable — this is the cheap
failure.<Cite src="ethereum-org-client-diversity" />

**Now walk the majority case they think is the safe one.** If your supermajority client has a critical
bug and finalises an invalid chain, "what happens to everyone" is not shared safety. It is that your
validators have attested to and finalised a chain the correct implementations reject. Returning to the
chain the ecosystem settles on means slashing or a slow and expensive exit and
reactivation.<Cite src="ethereum-org-client-diversity" /> And because everyone in the supermajority does
it at once, the correlation is maximal and the slashing is maximal — the whole effective
balance.<Cite src="ethereum-org-client-diversity" /> Since Electra that is up to 2,048 ETH for a
consolidated validator rather than 32.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-preset-electra-v161" />

**Name the fallacy precisely.** "Everyone loses together so nobody loses" assumes losses are relative. The
slashing penalty is denominated in ETH and scales *with* correlation, so being one of many does not
dilute your loss — it maximises it. This is the one place in the protocol where the crowd is the hazard.

**The second error: the outcome is not symmetric between the two chains.** Validators on the correct
minority chain keep their stake and face a difficult recovery. Validators on the incorrectly finalised
majority chain face slashing to rejoin. The protocol punishes being confidently wrong at scale, and it is
built that way on purpose.

**What I would actually do.**

*Split the fleet across clients rather than switching wholesale.* You do not need to move everything to
the least-used client. Splitting across two or three consensus clients caps how much of your own stake any
single client bug can reach, which is the same blast-radius argument applied to your balance sheet instead
of to the network's.<Cite src="ethereum-org-client-diversity" />

*Split the execution side too.* The same thresholds apply to execution clients — an execution-client
consensus bug reaches validators just as surely as a consensus-client one, because a validator whose
execution client rejects a valid payload cannot attest to it.<Cite src="clientdiversity-org" />

*Keep an independent observer.* One node on a different client pair, not attesting, whose finalised root
you compare against your production fleet. That comparison is your only local detector for the scenario
that has no protocol remedy.

*Stop quoting a share number in the justification.* Write the decision as a blast-radius argument. The
share figures are contested — three consensus datasets on the same dashboard disagree, and the dashboard
itself flags one execution dataset as removed and the other as
stale<Cite src="clientdiversity-org" /> — so a plan that rests on a percentage will be relitigated the
next time somebody reads a different chart. A plan that rests on 33% and 66% will not.

**The one-line version.** In the minority you are exposed to downtime. In the supermajority you are
exposed to slashing. Only one of those has a floor.
</Answer>

## What this unlocks

- **The next module** takes you to the artifact all these clients implement against. Six independent
  implementations agreeing is not an accident of good review; it is produced by a shared, executable
  specification and a corpus of generated test vectors, and understanding that machinery is understanding
  why the diversity argument is even feasible.
- **This module's reflection** asks you to price both scenarios against your own product rather than
  against Ethereum. The two answers should look completely different — one is a runbook, the other is a
  halt switch.
- **Practically**, the cheapest action available to most teams is the second node on a different client
  pair. It is a small, boring piece of infrastructure that turns an unsolvable failure mode into a
  detectable one.<Cite src="ethereum-org-nodes-clients" />

The habit worth forming: when someone quotes you a market-share statistic about infrastructure, ask what
population it counted and when. On this dashboard that question has three different answers on one screen,
and the thresholds those numbers are being compared against are the only part of the picture that will
still be true next year.
