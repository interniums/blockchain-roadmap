The alert fires at 03:12. **Node healthy. Head stale 41 minutes.**

Somebody opens the runbook. The process is up. `eth_chainId` answers instantly. `eth_getBalance` answers
instantly, with a balance that was correct forty-one minutes ago. `eth_syncing` returns `false`, which the
runbook says means "fully synced". The logs scroll steadily. Nothing has crashed, nothing has restarted,
disk and memory are unremarkable.

The runbook has no next step, because the runbook was written about one program. There are two, and the
one that is broken is not the one being examined. It is not even the one serving the API that produced
the alert.

This module is about the inside of that machine. You have run a node, or at least talked to one across
an RPC boundary. This lesson establishes the thing every later lesson depends on: which of the two
processes owns which question, and — the part that surprises people — which of them is allowed to decide
what is true.

## The split, stated exactly

A full Ethereum node is two independently written programs running side by side on one machine, plus one
private channel between them.<Cite src="ethereum-org-nodes-clients" />

The **execution client** holds the world state, runs the EVM, keeps the public transaction mempool, and
serves the `eth_*` JSON-RPC that your application talks to.

The **consensus client** speaks the beacon-chain peer-to-peer protocol, keeps the beacon state and the
validator registry, collects attestations, runs [fork choice](/c/fork-choice-purpose), and tracks
justification and finality.

They are different codebases by different teams in different languages. They are configured separately,
they sync separately, they have separate ports, separate log files, separate data directories, separate
version numbers and separate release cadences. They agree on nothing except a shared secret and a wire
format.

That is not a deployment convenience. It is the architecture, and it has been since the Merge.

## What the execution client owns, and what it does not

Start with the half you already know from the inside, because you have been talking to it all along.

**It owns state.** Accounts, balances, nonces, contract code, storage. Every [Merkle-Patricia
trie](/c/merkle-patricia-trie) you learned to build a proof against lives here, and so does the
[state root](/c/state-as-keyvalue) that commits to all of it.

**It owns the machine that changes state.** The EVM, gas accounting, the precompiles, transaction
validity rules, receipt and log generation. Everything from the EVM track happens inside this process.

**It owns the public transaction mempool.** The devp2p transaction gossip network, the pending and queued
pools, replacement rules, blob pool policy. Your `eth_sendRawTransaction` lands here.

**It owns the user-facing API.** `eth_*`, `debug_*`, `trace_*` — the whole surface your indexer, your
wallet backend and your simulation harness consume.

Now the part that is easy to say and hard to feel: **it owns no opinion about which chain is real.**

The execution client does not choose a head. It does not know what an attestation is. It cannot compute
finality; it is *told* what is finalised. Handed a block, it executes it and reports a verdict — this
extends the state consistently, or it does not. Handed a head, it adopts that head. It is a very
sophisticated evaluator that has been deliberately stripped of authority.

This is a genuine amputation, not a design that was always this way. Before the Merge the execution
client ran fork choice itself, by total difficulty: the heaviest chain of proof-of-work won, and the
execution client could compute that quantity from block headers it already had. EIP-3675 replaced that
rule wholesale — the beacon chain becomes the source of the canonical view, and the execution layer's
fork choice is driven by events pushed down from consensus rather than computed
locally.<Cite src="eip-3675" /> The code that used to decide was removed. What is left is a process that
answers questions and never asks one.

## What the consensus client owns

The consensus client is the process with a clock and an opinion.

**It owns time.** Slots and epochs are its units. The mainnet configuration sets `SLOT_DURATION_MS` to
12,000 — one slot every twelve seconds — and the phase0 preset sets `SLOTS_PER_EPOCH` to
32.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-beacon-chain-v161" />
Every deadline any [validator](/c/validator) has to hit is enforced here.

**It owns the beacon state.** The validator registry, effective balances, the justification and
finalisation checkpoints, randomness, [committee](/c/committee) assignments. The whole beacon-chain
[state transition](/c/state-machine-replication) runs in this process.

**It owns the votes.** [Attestations](/c/attestation) arrive on gossip subnets, get aggregated, get
included in blocks, and get counted.

**It owns fork choice and finality.** [LMD-GHOST](/c/lmd-ghost) picks the head each slot;
[Casper FFG](/c/casper-ffg) justifies and finalises checkpoints. This process, and only this process,
knows what the canonical chain is.

**And it owns the authority to say so.** It is the only thing on the machine that can tell the execution
client what the head is.

Note what the consensus client does *not* own. It holds no account balances. It cannot execute a
transaction. It has never seen your contract's storage. When it needs to know whether an execution
payload is valid, it has to ask — it has no way to find out for itself.

## Why anyone would build it this way

Two processes is more operational surface than one. The reason it is worth it is that the split is drawn
along a line where two very different kinds of correctness live.

Execution correctness is *deterministic replay*. Given a pre-state and a block, there is exactly one
right answer, and any implementation that computes a different one is wrong in a way you can demonstrate
on a laptop.

Consensus correctness is *agreement under adversarial timing*. There is no single right answer to "which
of these two blocks is the head" that can be computed from local data alone; the answer depends on what
other nodes saw, when they saw it, and what they voted. Its failure modes are the [safety](/c/safety-property)
and [liveness](/c/liveness-property) failures from the fundamentals track, not "the number came out
wrong".

Splitting them lets each side be specified, implemented and tested in the terms that fit it. The
execution layer's specification is executable Python that replays blocks. The consensus layer's is
executable Python plus a fork-choice store plus a networking spec. Neither has to model the other.

There is a second payoff, and the Engine API specification names it outright: because the consensus
client drives and the execution client answers, one consensus client can legally drive *several*
execution clients — the spec calls this a supported one-to-many
configuration.<Cite src="engine-api-common" /> The reverse is not supported: the execution layer keeps
one chain head, so two consensus clients writing to it simultaneously is undefined
behaviour.<Cite src="engine-api-common" /> That asymmetry is a direct consequence of who holds authority,
and it is the technique behind running a candidate execution client alongside a production one on live
traffic.

### The consequence that bites first: they sync separately, and in an order

The two processes maintain separate databases and sync independently, which sounds like two parallel
tasks and is not.

The Engine API specification defines sync, from the execution client's point of view, as the process of
obtaining the data required to validate a payload — pulling data from remote peers, and passing a
payload's ancestors through validation to obtain a parent state.<Cite src="engine-api-paris" /> Read that
definition for what it presupposes. Sync is *toward a payload*. The execution client syncs toward a block
someone handed it.

Post-Merge, nobody hands it one except the consensus client. An execution client started next to a
consensus client that is not yet following the chain has no target. It is not stuck and it is not broken;
it has not been given a destination. The dependency runs one way: **the consensus client has to be
following the chain before the execution client can finish syncing to it.** This is why a fresh node's
progress reads as nothing, nothing, nothing, then everything at once, and why the useful first question
during a slow sync is what the *consensus* client thinks its head is.

The exact behaviour during sync is left to implementations — the specification says so
explicitly<Cite src="engine-api-paris" /> — so two execution clients can be at different points of the
same sync with entirely different log output and both be conformant.

## The 2026 landscape, without the stale list

Both halves have more implementations than most course material admits, and the execution-layer list in
particular has moved.

On the consensus side the community dashboard lists Lighthouse (Rust), Prysm (Go), Teku (Java), Nimbus
(Nim), Lodestar (TypeScript), Grandine (Rust) and Caplin (Go, shipped with Erigon) as stable, with
Lambda (Elixir) marked deprecated.<Cite src="clientdiversity-org" />

On the execution side it lists Geth (Go), Nethermind (.NET), Besu (Java), Erigon (Go), Reth (Rust) and
Ethrex (Rust) as stable, Nimbus's execution client as alpha and Silkworm (C++) as pre-alpha, with Akula
and EthereumJS deprecated.<Cite src="clientdiversity-org" /> Reth is a production execution client in
this list, not a research project<Cite src="reth-repo" />, and "Geth, Nethermind, Besu, Erigon" is no
longer the complete set — though Geth remains the oldest and most widely deployed of
them.<Cite src="go-ethereum-repo" />

You need one from each column. A Reth plus Lighthouse node and a Geth plus Prysm node are both full
Ethereum nodes and neither is a hybrid of anything — the pairing is free, because the interface between
the columns is specified.<Cite src="engine-api-spec" />

<Aside kind="note">
The consensus side splits again, into a beacon node that talks to the network and a validator client
that holds the signing keys.<Cite src="lighthouse-book" /><Cite src="prysm-docs" /> That second split is
a separate mechanism with its own failure modes, and it gets its own treatment in this module's fourth
lesson. For now: when this lesson says "consensus client", read it as the beacon node.
</Aside>

## Reading the 03:12 alert

Return to the incident with the split in hand.

**Subgoal 1 — identify which process produced the observation.** `eth_getBalance` and `eth_chainId` are
`eth_*` methods, served by the execution client. Everything in the alert came from the execution client,
and the execution client is behaving correctly: it is answering questions about the last state it was
told to adopt.

**Subgoal 2 — identify which process is responsible for the head advancing.** New heads arrive because
the consensus client received a beacon block, ran fork choice, and pushed the resulting head down. If
the head is not advancing, either no beacon blocks are arriving, or fork choice is not running, or the
push is failing. All three live on the consensus side or on the channel between the two.

**Subgoal 3 — notice what `eth_syncing` can and cannot mean here.** It reports the execution client's own
sync state. An execution client that has been given no new work has nothing to sync and says so. "Not
syncing" and "up to date with the network" are different claims, and only the first one is being made.

**Subgoal 4 — decide where to look.** The consensus client's logs, and the health of the channel between
them. Nothing in the execution client's logs will explain this, which is exactly why the runbook stalled.

The generalisation is the useful part: **every "which chain am I on" question belongs to the consensus
side, whatever the execution client's logs say.** Staleness, wrong head, a head that jumped backwards,
finality not advancing — all of them. And every "what does this state say" question belongs to the
execution side. Sorting an incident by that one cut eliminates half the search space before you read a
single line of logs.

A teammate is debugging a service that reads from your node. They report: "The node returned a receipt
for transaction `0xab…` with `status: 1` and a block number, and forty seconds later the same query
returned null. The execution client has a bug — it lost a receipt."

Say whether the execution client has a bug, what actually happened, and which process you would examine.

<Answer>
**The execution client almost certainly does not have a bug, and the behaviour is not a loss.** It is a
[reorg](/c/reorg) — the block containing that transaction stopped being on the canonical chain, so the
receipt stopped being reachable by that lookup.

**Locate the decision.** The execution client did not decide to drop the block. It cannot: it has no
fork choice, no attestations, and no view of what the rest of the network voted for.<Cite src="eip-3675" />
It was told the head is now a different block, and it re-derived what is reachable from that head. The
decision to change the head was made in the consensus client, by fork choice, on evidence — attestations
— that never crosses into the execution process at all.

**Why the execution client's logs will look boring.** From its side, this was a routine head update
followed by ordinary state re-derivation. There is no error, no warning, nothing anomalous to print. A
search through those logs for the moment the receipt "disappeared" finds nothing, which reads as a
cover-up and is in fact an accurate report that nothing went wrong here.

**Where to look.** The consensus client, for a head change at that timestamp and for its depth. If the
new head is a sibling of the old one, this was a one-block reorg at the tip and it is unremarkable. If
the reorg was several blocks deep, or if it crossed a finalised checkpoint, those are different and much
more serious stories — and the consensus client is the only process that can tell you which one you are
in, because finality is a quantity it computes and the execution client merely receives.

**The product lesson underneath.** Your teammate's service treated a receipt as a fact. A receipt is a
statement about the canonical chain, and which chain is canonical is a live decision being made in
another process on a twelve-second clock.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" /> The fix is
not in the node. It is to stop treating a receipt at the head as final, which is a question about
[confirmation depth](/c/finality-vs-confirmations) rather than about node health.

**The one-line version.** The execution client answers questions about a chain; it does not choose one.
Anything that looks like the chain changing its mind is a consensus-side event surfacing through an
execution-side API.
</Answer>
</Check>

## The mental model to carry

Two boxes.

The left box holds state, the EVM, the mempool and the public API. Arrows leave it outward to devp2p
peers and to your application. It answers.

The right box holds validators, attestations, fork choice and finality. Arrows leave it outward to
libp2p beacon peers. It decides.

Between them, exactly one arrow — private, authenticated, local, and pointing in one direction as far as
who initiates is concerned. Everything the two halves of your node ever say to each other goes through
it.<Cite src="engine-api-common" />

That arrow is the next lesson.

  match runs happily forever without following the chain.<Cite src="engine-api-common" />
- **Lesson three** puts the three calls that carry every block on a timeline, including the one whose
  return value is worth more the later you ask for it.<Cite src="engine-api-paris" />
- **The module's practice** has you pair two clients yourself and then break the pairing on purpose, so
  that the failure has a shape in your memory rather than a paragraph.

The habit worth forming: when something about a node looks wrong, ask *which process owns this question*
before asking *what went wrong*. Half the time the answer to the first question makes the second one
trivial, and the other half of the time it stops you reading the wrong log for an hour.
