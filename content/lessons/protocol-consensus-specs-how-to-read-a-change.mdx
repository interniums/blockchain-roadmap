Your team lead forwards a link and one sentence: *"Someone said this changes how we should structure our
indexer. Is that right?"*

The link is EIP-7928, Block-Level Access Lists. You could read it in ten minutes and answer. You would
probably be wrong, and not because the EIP is misleading — because an EIP answers one question out of four,
and the question it answers is not the one you were asked.

This lesson is the reading procedure. Four artifacts, in order, each answering something the others
cannot, plus a fifth that tells you whether any of it is happening at all.

## The four layers

**The EIP gives intent.** Why anyone wanted this, what problem it solves, what alternatives were
considered. It is an argument.

**The specs pull request gives the normative change.** What the protocol now says, in a form clients
implement.

**The test vectors give observable behaviour.** What a client must actually produce, case by case.

**The client pull requests give reality.** How it was really built, what turned out to be hard, and what
was argued about in review.

Each layer answers a question the others cannot, and skipping one produces a characteristic, predictable
kind of wrongness.

**EIP-only readers describe designs that were revised.** An EIP is a living document that reaches a status
and often stops moving while the specification keeps going. Its examples get stale. Its field names
change. Its motivation section is durable; its details may not be.

**Spec-only readers miss cost and motivation.** The specification tells you the rule with total precision
and is silent on why the rule exists, what it replaced, and what it costs anyone to implement.

**Vector-only readers see behaviour without structure.** A corpus tells you inputs and outputs. It will not
tell you which rule produced them or why it is shaped that way.

**Client-only readers generalise one implementation's choices.** The most seductive failure, because
reading real code feels like the most concrete thing you can do. One client's data structures, caching
strategy and error handling are engineering decisions, not protocol. Mistaking them for protocol is how
you build against something that another client does differently and legally.

## A worked trace: EIP-7928

Take the actual question. Follow the layers.

### Layer 1 — the EIP, for intent

EIP-7928 introduces block-level access lists recording all accounts and storage locations accessed during
block execution, along with their post-execution values, to enable parallel disk reads, parallel
transaction validation, parallel state root computation and executionless state
updates.<Cite src="eip-7928" />

Its motivation is specific: transaction execution cannot be parallelised without knowing in advance which
addresses and storage slots will be accessed, and EIP-2930's transaction-level access lists are optional
rather than enforced.<Cite src="eip-7928" />

**What this layer told you.** The purpose. Also, already, something relevant to the question you were
asked: *state reconstruction without executing transactions* is on the list of things this
enables.<Cite src="eip-7928" /> That is an indexing-shaped claim, and it is why someone thought to send
you the link.

**What it did not tell you.** Whether it is happening, when, or in what form. Its status is
**Review**.<Cite src="eip-7928" />

### Layer 2 — the specifications, for the normative change

This change lands on both layers, which is common and is where single-repository habits break.

**On the execution side**, the EIP introduces a `block_access_list_hash` field in the block header, holding
the Keccak-256 hash of the RLP-encoded block access list. The list itself is not in the block body — the
execution client stores it separately and transmits it as a field in the `ExecutionPayload` via the Engine
API.<Cite src="eip-7928" />

**On the interface**, that is already written down. The Engine API's Amsterdam document defines
`ExecutionPayloadV4` as `ExecutionPayloadV3` plus a `blockAccessList` field — `DATA`, an RLP-encoded block
access list as defined in EIP-7928.<Cite src="engine-api-amsterdam" /> And `engine_newPayloadV5`, which
carries that structure, **MUST** return `-32602: Invalid params` if the `blockAccessList` field is
missing.<Cite src="engine-api-amsterdam" />

**On the consensus side**, the specs repository carries `eip7928` as one of its executable
specifications, built alongside the fork specs.<Cite src="consensus-specs-makefile-v161" /> The mainnet
config gives it a fork version, `0x0b000000`, and a fork epoch of
`18446744073709551615`.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" />

**What this layer told you.** Something the EIP's abstract did not make obvious: the BAL is not part of the
block body, so it does not arrive the way transactions do. It travels over the Engine API and is stored
separately. For anyone building on top of a node, that is the single most consequential fact in the whole
trace, and it is in the specification rather than in the summary.

<Aside kind="note">
Look at `ExecutionPayloadV4` once more and notice it appends **two** new fields: `blockAccessList` and
`slotNumber`.<Cite src="engine-api-amsterdam" /> The second belongs to a different proposal entirely —
EIP-7843's SLOTNUM opcode, also listed as Scheduled for Inclusion in the same
upgrade.<Cite src="eip-7773" /> One structure, two EIPs. Reading a spec change means reading the
*structure*, because a field's presence tells you nothing about which proposal put it there.
</Aside>

### Layer 3 — the vectors, for observable behaviour

The vectors are where a change stops being a description and becomes a set of inputs with pinned outputs.

For a consensus-layer change, look at what the specs repository's generators added to
`consensus-spec-tests` for that fork — a new operation handler means a new entry in the operations table
mapping the operation to its processing function, and a directory of cases with `pre`, input and `post`
files.<Cite src="consensus-spec-tests" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-test-formats-operations" /> For an
execution-layer change, the equivalent corpus is `execution-spec-tests`.<Cite src="execution-spec-tests-repo" />
For behaviour that only appears when two real clients interact, it is Hive.<Cite src="hive-repo" />

**What this layer tells you.** The edge cases someone thought about. A test suite is a list of the
conditions the authors considered worth pinning, which is a remarkably good proxy for where they expected
implementations to differ. Reading the case names of a new suite is often faster than reading the spec
diff, and it tells you a different thing: not what the rule is, but where it is slippery.

**What it does not tell you.** Anything outside coverage — and, since the vectors are generated by running
the specification, nothing at all about whether the specification is right.

### Layer 4 — the client pull requests, for reality

Now go and read how Geth, Reth, Lighthouse or Prysm actually implemented
it.<Cite src="go-ethereum-repo" /><Cite src="reth-repo" /><Cite src="lighthouse-book" /><Cite src="prysm-docs" />

**What this layer tells you.** Cost, difficulty and disagreement. A change that reads as three paragraphs
in an EIP may be a six-month refactor in a client, and the review conversation is where you find out.
Comments on a client pull request are the most candid record in the ecosystem of what a protocol change
actually demands.

**How to read it without being misled.** Everything you find is one team's answer to a specification, and
another team's answer will differ. When you see a data structure, a cache, an error type or a
configuration flag, ask whether the spec required it. If not, it is engineering — real, important, and not
protocol.

### Layer 5 — the process, for whether this is happening at all

Do this one first in practice, even though it is last in the conceptual list, because it can end the
investigation in thirty seconds.

EIP-7723 defines the inclusion stages: Proposed, Considered, Declined, Scheduled.<Cite src="eip-7723" />
The Glamsterdam meta EIP lists EIP-7928 under **Scheduled for
Inclusion**.<Cite src="eip-7773" /> Its own status is Draft and it names no activation
date.<Cite src="eip-7773" /> The consensus config sets the corresponding fork epoch to max
uint64.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" />

And for the argument behind any of it, All Core Devs agendas and notes are in
`ethereum/pm`.<Cite src="ethereum-pm" /> That is where a declination has a reason attached rather than
just a bucket.

### The answer to the question you were actually asked

*"Does this change how we should structure our indexer?"*

**Eventually, and not yet, and not in the way the summary suggests.** Block-level access lists are
scheduled for Glamsterdam<Cite src="eip-7773" /> with no activation date and a fork epoch encoded as
disabled.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" /> When they ship, they enable state
reconstruction without executing transactions<Cite src="eip-7928" /> — genuinely interesting for an
indexer. But the list is not in the block body; it is stored separately by the execution client and moves
over the Engine API.<Cite src="eip-7928" /><Cite src="engine-api-amsterdam" /> So whether *you* can use it
depends on whether the RPC surface you consume exposes it, which is a question about
`execution-apis`<Cite src="engine-api-spec" /> and about your provider — not one the EIP answers, and not
one anybody has answered yet.

That answer took four artifacts. No one of them contains it.

### Why the order matters, and why it is not the order people use

The natural instinct is to read in the order of increasing concreteness: EIP, then spec, then code. That is
backwards for anything with a deadline attached, because the cheapest check is the one that most often ends
the investigation.

**Process first, because it is a thirty-second query with a binary answer.** A proposal that is Declined or
merely Proposed does not need a spec read, a vector read or a client read unless you are researching rather
than planning.<Cite src="eip-7723" />

**Interface second, if you build on a node rather than in one.** Whether a change reaches you at all is
decided by `execution-apis` and by your provider — not by the consensus
specification.<Cite src="engine-api-spec" /> Half the changes that matter enormously to client teams are
invisible from your side of the RPC boundary, and a few that read as internal plumbing change your data
model completely.

**Then the specification, the vectors and the client code, in that order**, because each one is more
expensive to read than the last and each is only worth reading once the previous one has told you where to
look.

## A checklist you can hand to your team

**1. Is it happening?** Meta EIP first — Scheduled, Considered, Declined or
Proposed.<Cite src="eip-7723" /> Then the config: a real fork epoch, or max
uint64.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" /> If it is not scheduled, stop unless you are
doing research.

**2. Which layer, or both?** Consensus, execution, or the interface between them. Determines which
repository you are reading and, often, whether it affects you at all.

**3. What does it actually say?** The specs pull request, not the EIP. For consensus-layer behaviour the
merged specs change is normative and the EIP frequently lags it.

**4. What must a client produce?** The added vectors. Their case names are a map of the edge
cases.<Cite src="consensus-spec-tests" />

**5. What did it cost?** One client's implementing pull request, read for difficulty rather than for
detail.

**6. What is the surface you touch?** For anyone building on a node rather than in one, this is the real
question, and it is usually `execution-apis` plus your provider's
documentation.<Cite src="engine-api-spec" />

**7. Date it and cite it.** Write down what you read, from which artifact, at which version, on which
day.<Cite src="consensus-specs-releases" /> All five layers move. A conclusion without a date is a
conclusion with an unknown expiry.

<Aside kind="warn">
Upgrade trackers and news aggregation are useful for noticing that something exists and unreliable for
anything else.<Cite src="eipsinsight-upgrades" /> Every wrong claim in the worked example above — "arriving
this year", "includes inclusion lists", "six-second slots" — is the kind of thing a secondary source
prints and a primary artifact contradicts in under a minute. Use trackers to build the list of things to
check. Never to answer.
</Aside>

<Check type="explain" concept="reading-a-spec-change">
A colleague sends you a message: "Big news — Ethereum is enshrining proposer-builder separation, which
kills the relays. We should remove our MEV-Boost relay monitoring; it will be dead code."

Work through which artifacts you would consult, in order, and say what you would tell them.

<Answer>
**Five checks, roughly ten minutes, and the answer changes the recommendation.**

**Check one — is it happening?** EIP-7732, Enshrined Proposer-Builder Separation, is listed under
Scheduled for Inclusion in the Glamsterdam meta EIP.<Cite src="eip-7773" /> So the headline is right: it
is scheduled, not speculative. Worth knowing that "scheduled" is a defined stage rather than a
figure of speech.<Cite src="eip-7723" />

**Check two — when?** The meta EIP's own status is Draft and it carries no activation
date.<Cite src="eip-7773" /> The consensus config sets `GLOAS_FORK_EPOCH` to `18446744073709551615`, the
convention for a disabled fork.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" /> **This alone settles the
recommendation.** You cannot remove monitoring for infrastructure that will be live for an unknown further
period, ending on a date nobody has set.

**Check three — what does the specification actually change?** Read the specs, and read the Engine API's
unshipped fork files, which are where the interface consequences show
up.<Cite src="consensus-specs" /><Cite src="engine-api-amsterdam" /> "Enshrined" means the protocol gains
its own mechanism for the proposer-builder relationship. Whether that eliminates the *out-of-protocol*
market, or changes the trust assumptions inside it, or leaves a role for relays in a different shape, is a
question the spec answers and the phrase "kills the relays" does not.

**Check four — what did the argument look like?** All Core Devs notes and the associated research threads
are where the operational consequences for existing infrastructure were
debated.<Cite src="ethereum-pm" /> If relays are expected to disappear entirely, someone said so there,
with reasoning. If the expectation is more nuanced, that is where the nuance is.

**Check five — what does it mean for a client?** The implementing pull requests, when they
appear.<Cite src="lighthouse-book" /><Cite src="prysm-docs" /> A change of this size arrives in clients
over multiple releases, and the shape of that rollout tells you how long the transition period will be —
which is the actual planning input.

**What I would tell them.**

*Keep the monitoring.* Glamsterdam has no date, the fork epoch is encoded as
disabled<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" />, and even after activation there will be a
transition during which both paths matter.

*Correct the framing, not the enthusiasm.* "Scheduled for a future upgrade with no announced date" is the
accurate version. It is still one of the two headline changes of that
upgrade.<Cite src="eip-7773" />

*Do the useful work now instead.* Read the specification and write down which of your assumptions about
block production it invalidates. That is a design document you will need, and it is available today —
unlike the removal, which is available on a date nobody has.

*Set a trigger rather than a deadline.* Watch the consensus-specs release line and the config's fork
epoch.<Cite src="consensus-specs-releases" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-v161" /> When that
epoch becomes a real number, you will have a known amount of time and can plan against it. Until then any
schedule you write is fiction.

**The general lesson to leave them with.** "X is being enshrined, so Y is dead code" compresses five
separate questions into one sentence — is it scheduled, when, what exactly does it change, what happens
during the transition, and what do clients do — and each of those has a different artifact. The
compression is where the error entered, not the enthusiasm.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

- **The module's reflection** asks you to list the artifacts you would consult on finding mainnet
  behaviour that contradicts what you were taught, and to say what each can and cannot settle. That is
  this lesson, applied to a case where you do not know the answer in advance.
- **Everything downstream in this track** — fork choice, ePBS, PeerDAS, the state-format debate — is a
  change you can now read for yourself rather than take on authority. That is the point of the module: the
  ability to answer your own protocol questions from primary artifacts, and to know which artifact to open.
- **The execution-layer counterpart** works identically: `execution-specs` for the normative change,
  `execution-spec-tests` for the vectors.<Cite src="execution-specs" /><Cite src="execution-spec-tests-repo" />
  Only the repository names differ.

The habit worth forming: before you repeat a protocol claim, name the artifact it came from and what kind
of question that artifact can settle. Almost every confident, wrong statement about Ethereum's roadmap is
a true statement from one layer, presented as though it came from another.
