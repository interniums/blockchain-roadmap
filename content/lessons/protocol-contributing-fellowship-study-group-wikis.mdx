Two of those are programmes and two are websites. One programme is competitive and currently closed;
the other is open and has no gate at all. One website you can contribute to today; the other you
enrol in.

Getting this wrong has a specific cost. People decide that protocol work is closed to them because
the fellowship's applications shut in May, when the thing they actually wanted was open all along and
required nothing but a browser. This lesson separates all four, with the dates and structures read
from the primary artefacts on 26 August 2026.

## The fellowship, and what it is not

The Ethereum Protocol Fellowship — formerly the Core Developer Apprentice Program — is described by
its own program guide as "a program designed to onboard developers to the process through which
protocol development happens".<Cite src="epf7-program-details" /> Note the object of that sentence.
Not the protocol; the *process*. It is an apprenticeship in how decisions get made and how work gets
coordinated, with a project attached.

Cohort 7 was announced on 30 April 2026. Applications were open until 13 May, an introductory town
hall was held on 6 May at 15:00 UTC, and the cohort runs from June through
November.<Cite src="epf7-announcement" /> The program guide describes it as a five-month programme
running "from June through mid-November", timed so that a physical meetup can happen at
Devconnect.<Cite src="epf7-program-details" />

So if you are reading this in the second half of 2026, **EPF7 is mid-flight and its application window
closed months ago.** The next opportunity is Cohort 8, whose timing has not been announced. Do not
plan against an inferred date: the pattern of previous announcements is a pattern, not a schedule.

<Aside kind="stop">
**Two first-party documents disagree about the money, so do not plan on it.**

The announcement says: "Select participants will be provided with a monthly stipend to focus on the
program."<Cite src="epf7-announcement" />

The program guide says: "Over the course of the 5 month program, EPF participants will receive project
support and mentorship as well as a monthly stipend while working on a project of their
choosing."<Cite src="epf7-program-details" />

The community wiki's own description splits the difference: "Funding support — Some fellows receive
stipends to focus on the program."<Cite src="epf-wiki-epf-page" />

Two of the three scope the stipend to a subset; one does not. No amount is published anywhere. The
honest reading is that a stipend exists, that it is not guaranteed to everyone accepted, and that
anyone treating this as a salaried position is planning against a number that has never been printed.
</Aside>

**What the fellowship explicitly is not** is stated more clearly than most programmes manage. The
guide carries a call-out: "This program will not teach or focus in any way on smart contract
development or creating blockchain applications."<Cite src="epf7-program-details" /> And on
mentorship: "mentors are there to unlock doors and remove barriers. There will be near-zero
'teaching'."<Cite src="epf7-program-details" />

Read those together with the participant profile the guide describes. Successful candidates have a
technical foundation, strong written and verbal communication, the ability to work in a self-directed
way, the ability to write code, and — flagged as "probably the most important one" — intrinsic
motivation.<Cite src="epf7-program-details" /> The technical foundation is defined generously:
software development experience, a computer science or mathematics background, or the ability to read
and loosely understand the yellow paper.<Cite src="epf7-program-details" />

The structure is four phases:<Cite src="epf7-program-details" />

- **Phase Zero** — the month before the cohort starts, spent getting up to speed on the current state
  of protocol development, with epf.wiki and the cohort's reading list named as the place to do it.
- **Phase One** — orientation, a group session on areas of focus, and a written topic of interest
  shaped by mentor input.
- **Phase Two** — deep dive into an area, connect with mentors, and write a project proposal with a
  roadmap. The repo guide recommends creating the project document around week four to six, once the
  project is mature enough to present.<Cite src="epf7-repo-guide" />
- **Phase Three** — execute the roadmap, publish development updates, and attend weekly standups.

Two calls a week, both public and both on a fixed clock: standups on Mondays at 15:00 UTC, and office
hours on Wednesdays at 15:00 UTC, which are mostly AMAs with core
developers.<Cite src="epf7-program-details" /> Anybody can propose a topic for office hours by
commenting on that call's tracking issue.<Cite src="epf7-program-details" />

<Aside kind="note">
**The cohort runs in public, so you can attend most of it without being in it.** The cohort
repository states its own purpose as four things: an informational resource for how the programme
operates, a coordination point for participants, a historical record of prior projects, and a
collection of resources for future participants.<Cite src="epf7-repo-guide" /> All four are readable
by anyone.

Concretely, on 26 August 2026 the current cohort's repository holds a program guide, a mentors list, a
reading list, a projects directory, a notes directory and the development-update
log.<Cite src="epf7-cohort-repo" /> The office-hours agenda lives in the repository's issues and
anybody may propose a topic by commenting on the tracking
issue.<Cite src="epf7-program-details" /> The calls are on public links at fixed
times.<Cite src="epf7-program-details" />

What acceptance buys is mentorship, a stipend for at least some participants, and the accountability
of a cohort. What it does not gate is the material, the agenda or the record.
</Aside>

## Protocol Studies: the open one

Ethereum Protocol Studies began as a ten-week study group ahead of the fifth fellowship cohort and
has, in its own words, "grown into the primary educational on-ramp for anyone looking to understand
Ethereum's core protocol".<Cite src="eps-2026" /><Cite src="epf-study-group-intro" /> There is no
application and no selection.

The 2026 edition kicked off on 23 February with a specific
shape:<Cite src="eps-2026" />

- An intro session on Monday 23 February.
- Two onboarding weeks working through foundational content on the new study platform.
- Five weeks of new content from 9 March, at two sessions per week.
- Live seminar-style discussions with core developers, researchers and past fellows.
- A dedicated Discord, and an invitation to contribute to the wiki.

The two **new** content tracks were Cryptography — elliptic curve pairings, KZG commitments, hash
functions, zero-knowledge proof systems and post-quantum signature schemes — and Lean Consensus and
zkEVM, covering how zkEVM implementations prove execution correctness and how that connects to
SNARKifying the protocol.<Cite src="eps-2026" />

That second track is itself a roadmap signal. A study group aimed at future core developers added a
track on hash-based cryptography and real-time proving, which tells you where the people running it
believe the protocol is going.

The scale is modest and worth knowing so you calibrate: the cohort-six recap reports the study group
ran February to April with "over 300 people registered".<Cite src="epf6-recap" /> This is a seminar
series, not a MOOC.

### Why the platform exists, and what it changed

The 2026 announcement is specific about what the new platform is and is not, and both halves matter.

It is "a dedicated FOSS learning management system built on Moodle", hosting the previous curriculum
as a structured self-paced course, in which "each module pairs video content from study group sessions
with interactive assessments in the form of comprehension checks embedded directly in lecture
recordings", with progress tracking and stated learning objectives per
section.<Cite src="eps-2026" />

It is explicitly not a replacement: "The learning platform complements the live program rather than
replacing it. Cohort participants still benefit from seminar-style discussions with protocol
engineers, real-time Q&A, and collaborative work through the EPF Wiki and
Discord."<Cite src="eps-2026" /> The stated purpose of the platform is that it "helps track your
progress, test your knowledge and ensures that the barrier to entry is as low as
possible".<Cite src="eps-2026" />

The reason to dwell on this is that it reverses a piece of advice that was correct until early 2026.
Before the platform, the honest guidance to someone arriving in August was: the study group runs on a
calendar, you have missed it, read the wiki and wait. After it, the recordings, the comprehension
checks and the structured progression are there whenever you arrive. The waiting answer is now wrong,
and it is still the answer most written material gives.

The lineage is worth knowing too. The study group was introduced in February 2024 as an on-ramp for
people who wanted to understand the protocol but were not yet ready to apply to the
fellowship.<Cite src="epf-study-group-intro" /> That original framing — *below* the fellowship,
deliberately — is still the right way to read the relationship between the two.

## The two websites, told apart

**epf.wiki is the wiki.** It is community-maintained, it is built from
`eth-protocol-fellows/protocol-studies`, and it is where fellowship applications and the reading list
are pointed.<Cite src="epf-wiki-site" /><Cite src="protocol-studies-repo" /><Cite src="epf7-program-details" />

Its contributing document is worth reading before you touch it, because its scope rules are unusually
explicit:<Cite src="epf-wiki-contributing" />

- The scope is technical resources for the Ethereum **core** protocol — specifications,
  implementations, testing, research and related tooling. In its own shorthand: "stuff which would be
  covered by EIPs, not ERCs."
- It explicitly does **not** cover on-chain protocols, applications, or layer 2s.
- "We are not aiming to rewrite other existing Ethereum documentation." If something is well explained
  elsewhere, link it and add context.
- The instruction for what to write is specific and unusual: write from what you learned along the
  way, and from "missing pieces that slowed you down".

Mechanically, the site is generated by Docsify from `/docs`, with `/docs/_sidebar.md` defining the
structure; the published site is served from a `wiki-pages` branch that is regularly updated from
`main`, so pull requests go to `main` or a topic branch rather than to the branch you can
see.<Cite src="epf-wiki-contributing" /> An aspell typo checker runs on every pull request, and the
repository ships `check_typos.sh` and a `wordlist.txt` so you can run it
locally.<Cite src="epf-wiki-contributing" /><Cite src="protocol-studies-repo" /> The source is also
mirrored on radicle and on an independent git host — a small, deliberate act of not depending on one
company.<Cite src="epf-wiki-contributing" />

The repository itself, on 26 August 2026: 275 stars, 193 forks, 26 open issues, and a last push of
19 May 2026.<Cite src="protocol-studies-repo" /> Read the staleness as opportunity rather than
warning. A wiki about a protocol that changed materially over the summer, with three months since the
last push, has pages that are wrong — and its contributing guide asks precisely for corrections
written by someone who recently found them confusing.

**study.epf.wiki is the course platform**, and it is a Moodle instance.<Cite src="study-epf-wiki-site" />
Read on 26 August 2026 without logging in, it lists three available courses and links to a study
group chat, a YouTube playlist and epf.wiki.<Cite src="study-epf-wiki-site" />

The three, and what is actually inside them:

**Ethereum Protocol 101** is the largest by a distance and is the previous EPS curriculum turned into
a structured self-paced course.<Cite src="eps-2026" /><Cite src="study-epf-protocol-101" /> Its
outline runs: a welcome and FAQ; five numbered lectures — Intro to Ethereum Protocol, Consensus
Layer, Execution Layer, CL and EL Specs Overview, and Testing and Security Overview — each paired with
a resources-and-exercises page; a node workshop; a page on the ACD development process; a local
prototyping exercise; then a consensus-layer deep dive covering Gasper, CL client architecture, the
validator client, beacon state, libp2p and the Engine API; and an execution-layer deep dive covering
the EVM, execution client architecture, EL data structures, devp2p and
precompiles.<Cite src="study-epf-protocol-101" /> Completion is tracked per item — some require
marking as done, the interactive lectures require receiving a
grade.<Cite src="study-epf-protocol-101" />

**Cryptography of Ethereum** runs five lectures across finite groups and fields, Diffie-Hellman to
Galois fields, FFT and field extensions, secret sharing and commitments, and curves and the group
law.<Cite src="study-epf-cryptography" />

**Lean Ethereum/zkEVM** runs five sections: a lean consensus overview, lean client architecture,
zkEVM fundamentals, leanSpec and tooling, and post-quantum
cryptography.<Cite src="study-epf-lean-zkevm" />

Three of those four stages have no gate. The programme's own posture matches: the study group is
described as the educational on-ramp for anyone, and the wiki explicitly welcomes contributions
"whether you are part of the Study Group or not".<Cite src="eps-2026" /><Cite src="epf-wiki-contributing" />
The wiki's own advice for getting involved lists joining the R&D Discord, following EIP discussions on
Ethereum Magicians and ethresear.ch, and exploring good first issues — with applying to a cohort as
the fourth item, not the first.<Cite src="epf-wiki-epf-page" />

The per-cohort repositories under the `eth-protocol-fellows` GitHub organisation hold every past
cohort's projects and update logs, going back to cohort
zero.<Cite src="eth-protocol-fellows-org" /><Cite src="epf7-cohort-repo" /> They are the best available
evidence of what a realistic project looks like, and the next lesson reads them.

### Which one answers which question

The four are not interchangeable, and picking the wrong one is how people end up reading a wiki page
for a normative answer or a course module for a current one.

**"How does this part of the protocol work, from first principles?"** → study.epf.wiki. It is
structured, sequenced, and has exercises. Its Protocol 101 course covers consensus and execution deep
dives in an order somebody thought about.<Cite src="study-epf-protocol-101" />

**"How do these pieces connect, and what does this term mean in context?"** → epf.wiki. It is
explicitly built as a cohesive collection that links rather than duplicates existing documentation,
and its stated ambition is connecting topics into a big picture.<Cite src="epf-wiki-contributing" />

**"What does the protocol actually specify?"** → neither. The specification repositories are
normative and the wiki is not; treat epf.wiki as a well-maintained community reference and go to
[the specs](/c/consensus-specs-repo) for anything you would defend in review. The wiki's own
contributing guide says as much when it declines to recreate documentation that exists
elsewhere.<Cite src="epf-wiki-contributing" />

**"What is being decided right now?"** → also neither. That is the All Core Devs record from the
previous lesson. A wiki page and a course module are both, structurally, snapshots. In a domain where
a headline design can be dropped between cohorts, a snapshot is the wrong instrument for a question
about the present.

**"What would I actually work on?"** → the cohort repositories, which is where the next lesson goes.

## What this unlocks

**The final lesson** reads the cohort repositories as data: thirty project files in the current
cohort, twenty-eight in the previous one, and a pattern in their names that tells you more about how
Ethereum gets built than any diagram. It also covers the development-update log — the programme's
accountability mechanism, and the artefact that outlasts it.

**Immediately**, you have four correctly separated things and a first move for each: enrol in a
course, correct a wiki page, claim a labelled issue, and stop waiting for an application window.

**And the habit worth keeping**: when two documents from the same organisation disagree about a
detail — a stipend's scope, a course count, an end date in November versus mid-November — that is
information, not noise. Report the range, name the sources, and go and look at the thing itself.
