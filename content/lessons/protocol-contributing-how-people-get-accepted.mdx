You do not have to speculate about what an accepted fellowship candidate looks like. The cohort
repository is public, and the `projects/` directory is a directory listing.

Here is a sample of the filenames sitting in the current cohort's projects folder on 26 August
2026:<Cite src="epf7-cohort-repo" />

```text
reth-eip8304-trustless-index.md
erigon-eip8304-trustless-index.md
nethermind-eip-8304-trustless-log-index.md
geth-ssz-execution-blocks-eip-7807.md

grandine-focil.md
lighthouse-focil.md
lodestar-eip-7732-builder.md
hive-spect-test-hardening.md
reth-partial-statefulness-and-state-expiry.md
Teku_Light_Client_Protocol.md
Grandine-Lean-Client.md

```

Read the left-hand side of those names. `reth-`, `erigon-`, `nethermind-`, `geth-`, `grandine-`,
`lighthouse-`, `lodestar-`, `hive-`, `ream-`. Now read the right-hand side: one EIP number appears
three times, a second appears twice, and one feature name appears twice more.

That is not duplicated effort. It is the single most important structural fact about how this protocol
gets built, and once you see it you know where a newcomer fits.

## One EIP, several clients, several people

A protocol change is not done when one client implements it. It is done when enough independent
implementations agree, which — as you know from the
[diversity thresholds](/c/client-diversity-thresholds) — is the entire point of having several. So
every scheduled change generates *N* similar-but-not-identical pieces of work, one per client, in *N*
different languages.

The pattern repeats across cohorts. The previous cohort's recap lists twenty-eight projects with
mentors from the Ethereum Foundation, Grandine, Erigon, Lighthouse, Lodestar, Nimbus, Prysm, Ream,
Reth and Besu.<Cite src="epf6-recap" /> Among them: fork-choice enforced inclusion lists implemented
separately in Erigon, Grandine, Reth and Nimbus; EIP-7919 implemented separately in Reth, Nimbus and
Erigon; ephemery testnet support in Besu; enshrined proposer-builder separation in Lighthouse;
block-level access lists in Reth.<Cite src="epf6-recap" />

For someone deciding what to work on, this shape has three consequences.

**The work is enumerable in advance.** You do not need an original idea. Take a change that is
scheduled or being considered, list which clients have implemented it, and the gaps are the available
projects. That list is derivable from the All Core Devs record and the spec repository's EIP branches
in an afternoon.

**Somebody has already solved your problem in another language.** The first client to implement a
change writes the hard part: the interpretation of the specification, the edge cases nobody thought
about, the test scaffolding. The fourth implementation is a genuinely different task — porting a
solved design into an unfamiliar codebase — and it is a much better first serious project than
inventing something.

**It is legible to a mentor.** "Implement EIP-8304 in Nethermind" has a definition of done, a
comparable prior implementation to review against, and a client team that wants it. Compare that to a
proposal to research something interesting.

Not everything is a port. The same two directories contain a lean client in Grandine, a Zig
implementation of libp2p with QUIC and Gossipsub, an SSZ query language with Merkle proofs, a
PostgreSQL backend for the Lighthouse beacon node, differential testing of clients using large
language models, and hardening of the cross-client test
harness.<Cite src="epf6-recap" /><Cite src="epf7-cohort-repo" /><Cite src="hive-repo" /> The point is
not that originality is unwelcome. It is that originality is not the entry requirement, and treating it as one is how people
talk themselves out of applying.

## What the selection criteria actually say

The programme publishes them, and they are short:<Cite src="epf7-program-details" />

- Your application and areas of interest.
- Your history of open-source contributions.
- Your existing contributions to the Ethereum ecosystem.
- Relevant prior work and educational experience.

Followed by: "We will be prioritizing candidates which have something that we consider a strong signal
of both interest and ability."<Cite src="epf7-program-details" />

Two of those four are things you can start producing this week and cannot fake retroactively. That is
the whole message of this module compressed into a bullet list.

There is also an explicit carve-out worth quoting, because it addresses the reader most likely to
self-reject: "There are life situations which may result in a capable candidate not being able to
easily demonstrate their ability. An example would be a long career in private industry working in a
closed-source environment. If this is your situation, you are encouraged to explain your situation and
make as compelling a case as possible for why you should be
considered."<Cite src="epf7-program-details" />

Cohort 7 was announced as "a smaller, more focused cohort, prioritizing depth of engagement over
breadth", so that fellows can work more closely with mentors and make higher-impact
contributions.<Cite src="epf7-announcement" /> The announcement closes with: "EPF7 is going to be a
small cohort and we're looking for people who are genuinely ready to dig
in."<Cite src="epf7-announcement" />

<Aside kind="stop">
**Numbers you will see quoted, and must not plan against.** Cohort sizes, acceptance rates and stipend
amounts are not published in any of the primary sources this module read. The announcement scopes the
stipend to "select participants" while the program guide describes participants receiving
one.<Cite src="epf7-announcement" /><Cite src="epf7-program-details" /> The wiki says "some fellows
receive stipends".<Cite src="epf-wiki-epf-page" />

The only quantities that are published are structural: a five-month programme, June through
mid-November; twenty-eight projects in the previous cohort; over three hundred registrations for the
study group in a February-to-April
window.<Cite src="epf7-program-details" /><Cite src="epf6-recap" /> If you read a percentage
acceptance rate somewhere, it was not sourced from these documents.
</Aside>

## The development update, and why it outlives the programme

Every fellow publishes development updates. The repository guide sets the cadence and the reasoning:
"One of the core goals of this program is to improve your technical writing and communication skills.
One key way to do this is to get comfortable telling people about what you are working
on."<Cite src="epf7-repo-guide" />

The mechanics, which are more instructive than they look:<Cite src="epf7-repo-guide" />

- **Cadence:** at least once every two weeks, for the duration of the programme. The first update goes
  out within the first week, covering initial research.
- **Published publicly, elsewhere.** An external blog, a shared document host, or your own site — then
  linked from the cohort repository's `development-updates.md` table.
- **Verified public.** The guide asks you to open the link in a private browsing window before adding
  it, which is a small instruction that reveals how often people link something only they can see.
- **Contributed by pull request** from a branch dedicated to that week, on your fork, with the guide
  recommending the git command line over the web interface on the grounds that it is a skill worth
  having.
- **Shared in the Ethereum R&D Discord** `#protocol-fellowship` channel.
- **Not generated.** "Not use an LLM or similar tech for generating your updates. The point is to
  exercise your technical writing and expressing ideas, generated updates won't be
  accepted."<Cite src="epf7-repo-guide" />

The programme guide's Phase Three description says "publish weekly development
updates".<Cite src="epf7-program-details" /> The repository guide says at least once every two
weeks.<Cite src="epf7-repo-guide" /> Two documents in one repository, two cadences; read the repository
guide as the floor and the programme guide as the aspiration.

On what a good update contains, the guidance is refreshingly low-ceremony: you do not need to explain
everything, a bulleted list of factual statements about what you worked on is a great place to start,
and links are great — forum posts, pull requests, documents, anything you have been studying or
creating.<Cite src="epf7-repo-guide" /> The reference model it points at is Danny Ryan's "finalized"
series of updates from the Merge devnet era.<Cite src="epf7-repo-guide" />

<Aside kind="note">
**A detail about collaborating in public that nothing else in this curriculum teaches.** The update
log is a single enormous markdown table that everyone edits, which means pull requests conflict
constantly — not because two people made contradictory edits, but because markdown column-width
whitespace shifts when anyone adds a row.<Cite src="epf7-repo-guide" />

The repository automates it away. A GitHub Action runs on every pull request touching the table,
merges the latest base branch using a semantic table merge, reformats, and pushes the result back to
your branch; it never deletes anyone's filled cells; and only genuine conflicts — two different links
in the *same* cell — make it fail, leaving explicit conflict markers to resolve. The same logic ships
as a dependency-free Python script you can run locally, plus a one-time git merge-driver
setup.<Cite src="epf7-repo-guide" />

The reason to notice this: somebody looked at a recurring human cost, decided it was a tooling
problem, and fixed it for everyone who comes after. That is the same instinct that makes a good
non-code contribution, applied to a coordination artefact rather than to a client.
</Aside>

Two other conventions in the same repository are worth stealing whether or not you ever join. A
project document lives at `projects/<project-name>.md` and should carry the description, scope, goals
and roadmap, every link to where development is actually happening, and the list of people working on
it — and the guide recommends writing it around week four to six, once the project is mature enough
to present rather than on day one.<Cite src="epf7-repo-guide" /> Separately, a
`notes/<your-name>.md` file exists for the messy middle: aggregated reading links, half-formed project
ideas, and details being worked out. The guide is explicit that this "isn't meant to be a
'profile'".<Cite src="epf7-repo-guide" />

The split is the useful part. One document is for other people and is written when there is something
to say; the other is for you and is written continuously. Most people invert that, publish the polished
thing too early, and then have nowhere to put the thinking.

**Why the log is the artefact that matters.** For the programme it is accountability. For you it is a
dated, public, link-rich record of real work on real problems, readable by anyone deciding whether to
hire you, fund you, or hand you a harder problem. A completed project is one line on a CV. Twenty
dated updates showing how you got there — including the weeks you were stuck — is evidence of how you
work, which is what anyone is actually trying to assess.

Previous cohorts' logs are also the best available research material for writing a proposal. The
organisation holds every cohort's repository, and the repo guide explicitly points at cohort six's
update log as an
example.<Cite src="eth-protocol-fellows-org" /><Cite src="epf6-cohort-repo" /><Cite src="epf7-repo-guide" />

That last row is the honest answer to what the fellowship is *for*. Access to a mentor from the core
development community, a cohort of peers on the same trajectory, and — for at least some participants
— money to focus, are genuinely hard to arrange
yourself.<Cite src="epf7-announcement" /><Cite src="epf7-program-details" /> Everything above it in
the table is a decision, not a privilege.

The wiki's own advice for getting involved orders it the same way: join the R&D Discord, follow EIP
discussions, explore good first issues, and — fourth — apply for the next
cohort.<Cite src="epf-wiki-epf-page" />

## What this unlocks

**This closes the module**, and the honest summary is that nothing in it required permission. The
codebases are public, the labelled issues are public, the All Core Devs record is public, the study
platform is open, the wiki takes pull requests, and the cohort repositories publish every fellow's
work as it happens.

**The one thing worth carrying into your own work** is the shape of the evidence. Protocol
development runs on a public, dated, linked record — call notes, spec pull requests, client
implementations, development updates — and the people who become known in it are the people who
contributed to that record rather than the people who read it fastest.

**And the honest inventory**, which the module reflection asks for: protocol work is mostly systems
engineering. Networking, databases, sync, concurrency, serialisation and performance, with
cryptography as a specialised minority. Measure yourself against that list rather than against the
version of this field that appears in marketing, then pick one repository and stay in it long enough
for the maintainers to learn your name.
