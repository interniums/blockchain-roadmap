Nothing about the software changed at those moments. What changed is that a curriculum that memorised
a list of URLs was quietly wrong, and its readers had no way to notice, because a redirect is not an
error.

That is the shape of this whole module. The list of Ethereum clients is not stable enough to
memorise. The *criteria* for choosing one are, and they are almost entirely unromantic: which
language you can already be productive in, and how tractable the project has made a first
contribution. This lesson establishes both, then reads the two execution clients that sit at opposite
ends of that second axis.

## There is no Ethereum codebase

You already know that [a node is two programs](/c/el-cl-split): an
[execution client](/c/execution-client-role) and a [consensus client](/c/consensus-client-role),
speaking to each other over the Engine API. What that split produces is not one codebase with two
halves. It produces two *ecosystems* of independent implementations, each written against the same
specification by a different team, in a different language, with a different review culture.

And the list is still growing. Ream describes itself as an Ethereum *lean* client written in Rust,

as a reading of one day, not as a fact about the protocol.

<Aside kind="note">
**Why so many, and why that is the point.** Six independent implementations of a consensus protocol
is not redundancy for its own sake — it is the mechanism behind the
[diversity thresholds](/c/client-diversity-thresholds) you already know. A bug that stalls finality
has to be present in more than a third of the network to do it, and a bug that finalises an invalid
chain has to be present in more than two thirds. Independent implementations are how those numbers
stay above the share of any single codebase. Every client on those two tables is somebody's answer to
"what if the others are wrong".
</Aside>

## Choose on two axes, and share is neither of them

The instinct is to pick the client with the largest share, on the theory that the most-used codebase
is the most important one to work on. Resist it, for two independent reasons.

**The first is that the numbers do not agree.** The community diversity dashboard publishes multiple
consensus-layer datasets on one page, under one heading, all labelled as freshly updated, and they
are mutually irreconcilable: one methodology puts Lighthouse above half the network and Teku under a
tenth, another puts Teku above half and Lighthouse at a fifth.<Cite src="clientdiversity-org" />
Node crawls and stake fingerprinting measure different populations. Neither is lying; they are
answering different questions. Any decision you make on a single published percentage is a decision
made on a coin flip you did not know you were making.

**The second reason is stronger, and holds even if the numbers were perfect.** Share tells you how
many people run the software. It tells you nothing about whether your pull request will be read. Those
are unrelated properties of a project, and only the second one determines whether you become a
contributor or a person with an abandoned branch.

So the two axes that actually decide it:

**Axis one — the language you are already fluent in.** Protocol code is systems code. You will be
reading concurrent state machines, database layers, peer-to-peer networking and hot loops that
somebody has profiled. Doing that in a language you are still learning means every bug looks like a
language problem, and you will not be able to tell the difference between "I do not understand
Ethereum" and "I do not understand borrow checking". Pick the layer *after* the language, not before.

**Axis two — how tractable the project has made a first contribution.** This is observable before you
write a line. Does the repository have a contributing guide that describes the actual workflow? Are
there labelled issues, and do they have unassigned ones? Is there a documented way to run the test
suite? Is there a stated review philosophy? Every one of those is a public artefact you can read this
afternoon.

  belief="Protocol work requires deep cryptography, so you need the mathematics before you can start."
  reality="Most client work is systems engineering: networking, databases, sync, concurrency, serialisation, performance. Cryptography is a specialised minority of it, and in most clients it is concentrated in a handful of crates or packages that most contributors never touch."
  why="Blockchain is marketed as a cryptography field, and the cryptography is the part that gets conference talks."

/>

That misconception is worth killing with evidence rather than assertion. Reth organises itself as a
set of crate groups, and on 26 August 2026 there are 32 directories under `crates/`. Their names:
`net`, `trie`, `storage`, `stages`, `transaction-pool`, `rpc`, `prune`, `static-file`, `engine`,
`evm`, `revm`, `payload`, `consensus`, `chainspec`, `chain-state`, `config`, `cli`, `node`, `tasks`,
`tokio-util`, `etl`, `era`, `era-utils`, `era-downloader`, `metrics`, `tracing`, `tracing-otlp`,
`errors`, `fs-util`, `exex`, `e2e-test-utils`, `ethereum`.<Cite src="reth-repo" /> Read that list as
a job description. It is a database, a network stack, a scheduler, an ETL pipeline and an
observability layer. There is no crate called `cryptography`.

## Geth: the one to read, not the one to start in

Geth is the reference implementation in the strongest sense of the phrase: a great deal of how
Ethereum actually behaves was decided in Geth's source before it was written down anywhere normative,
and clients have historically had to match its behaviour rather than the other way
round.<Cite src="go-ethereum-repo" /> That history is exactly what makes it enormous and exactly what
makes its review bar high.

The size is visible from outside. On 26 August 2026 the repository carries 51,314 stars, 22,125 forks
and 420 open issues.<Cite src="go-ethereum-repo" /> A fork count above twenty thousand is not a
measure of contributors; it is a measure of how many people have needed to modify Ethereum for their
own purposes. Every one of those is a potential source of pull requests the maintainers have to
triage.

The contribution norms are short and specific. Contributions are welcomed "from anyone on the
internet"; complex changes should be discussed with core devs first; code must pass `gofmt` and
follow Go commentary conventions; pull requests must be based on and opened against `master`; and
commit messages must be prefixed with the packages they modify, in the form `eth, rpc: make trace
configs optional`.<Cite src="geth-readme" /> Website changes go to an entirely separate `website`
branch.<Cite src="geth-readme" />

The label taxonomy tells you more than the guide does. Geth maintains 34 labels, and among them are
four that describe process state rather than subject matter: `status:triage`, `status:on-hold`,
`status:marinating` and `status:merge-at-meeting`.<Cite src="geth-labels" /> Read that last one
slowly. A pull request in this repository can be in a state whose name means *the team will decide
about this when they are next in a room together*. That is not dysfunction — it is what a
consequence-heavy codebase looks like when it takes review seriously. It is also a merge latency you
should know about before you spend a fortnight on a change.

And the entry points: on 26 August 2026, an aggregator that watches the `good first issue` label
across 31 Ethereum core repositories lists **zero** open good first issues in
go-ethereum.<Cite src="gfi-ethereum-aggregator" />

<Misconception
  belief="Contributing to Geth is the goal, because it is the reference client and therefore the real one."
  reality="Its size, its dependency footprint and the consequences of getting something wrong give it one of the highest review bars in the ecosystem. It is a superb codebase to read and a poor one to make your first contribution to."
  why="'Reference implementation' reads as 'the authoritative one', and authority reads as prestige."
  src="reth-repo"
/>

Read Geth anyway. When you want to know what Ethereum does rather than what it says, `core/`, `eth/`
and `trie/` are the answer, and no amount of specification reading substitutes for them. Just do not
confuse reading it with contributing to it.

## Reth: modular on purpose, and it shows in the guide

Reth is a Rust execution client whose stated first goal is modularity: "every component is built to
be used as a library: well-tested, documented and benchmarked", with a repository layout document
aimed at people reading the code rather than operating the
binary.<Cite src="reth-readme" /><Cite src="reth-repo" /> That design choice has a side effect the
project probably did not advertise as a feature: it makes the codebase enterable at 32 doors instead
of one. You can hold `crates/trie` in your head without holding the node in your head.

It has also stopped being a research project. Reth 2.0 shipped in April 2026, after a 1.0
"production-ready" release in June 2024, with an audit by Sigma Prime — the team that builds
Lighthouse — and an audit of revm, the EVM it uses.<Cite src="reth-readme" /> The practical
consequence for this module is that "the execution clients are Geth, Nethermind, Besu and Erigon" is
a stale sentence, and a curriculum still using it is describing 2022.

Where Reth is genuinely unusual is the contributing guide, which reads like it was written by someone
who has watched newcomers bounce off other projects. Four things in it are worth quoting because they
are policy, not sentiment:

- **"No contribution is too small and all contributions are valued."** Followed immediately by "do
  not let the document intimidate you".<Cite src="reth-contributing" />
- **"Any Reth community member is welcome to review any pull request."** Reviewing is explicitly not
  gated on being a maintainer — which means reviewing other people's changes is itself an available
  contribution, and one nobody competes for.<Cite src="reth-contributing" />
- **"Note that only *incremental* improvement is needed to land a PR."** The stated bar is better
  than the status quo, not perfect. Follow-up pull requests are the expected mechanism for the
  rest.<Cite src="reth-contributing" />
- **The local gate is one command:** `make pr` must pass before you open
  one.<Cite src="reth-contributing" />

There is also a rule that will save you a wasted afternoon, and it is the opposite of the advice
newcomers usually receive: **"At this time, we will not be accepting contributions that only fix
spelling or grammatical errors in documentation, code or elsewhere."**<Cite src="reth-contributing" />
"Fix a typo" is not a first contribution here. We will come back to what is, and why two separate
client teams felt the need to write that rule down.

The label scheme is the other half of the tractability story. Reth carries 83 labels in a structured
scheme: `A-` for area (`A-trie`, `A-devp2p`, `A-tx-pool`, `A-staged-sync`), `C-` for category
(`C-bug`, `C-docs`, `C-perf`, `C-test`), `D-` for difficulty, `E-` for the fork an item belongs to
(`E-osaka`, `E-Amsterdam`), `P-` for priority and `S-` for status.<Cite src="reth-labels" /> The
newcomer label is `D-good-first-issue` — **not** the GitHub default `good first issue`. That
difference is not cosmetic, and the next lesson shows what it does to anyone searching across
repositories by label name.

### One caution about reading a project's own README

Reth's repository description is "Modular, contributor-friendly and blazing-fast implementation of
the Ethereum protocol", and the README states the project is production-ready and actively
recommends professional node operators switch to it "for performance and cost
reasons".<Cite src="reth-repo" /><Cite src="reth-readme" /> Those are the project's claims about the
project. The modularity claim you can verify yourself in ten seconds by listing `crates/`. The
contributor-friendliness claim you can verify by reading the guide, which is what the previous section
did. The performance claim you cannot verify from the README at all — and to its credit, the README
does not ask you to, pointing instead at a third-party dashboard for comparison against other
clients.<Cite src="reth-readme" />

Keep those three in separate buckets. A README is a primary source for what a project *intends*, a
reliable source for what its structure *is*, and never a source for how it compares to its
competitors. This matters here because "which client is best" is the question newcomers actually ask,
and every client's README answers it in that client's favour.

## The three you skipped past, and why one of them deserves a look

The table at the top had five execution clients. Two got sections. The other three are where a
surprising number of tractable first contributions actually are.

**Nethermind** is written in C#, which is the least-represented mainstream language in this ecosystem
and therefore the one where an existing .NET engineer has the largest relative advantage. On
26 August 2026 the aggregator lists 14 open good-first-issues in Nethermind — the second-largest count
of any client repository it watches — although ten of those already have someone assigned to
them.<Cite src="gfi-ethereum-aggregator" /><Cite src="nethermind-repo" /> Read the titles and the
nature of the work becomes obvious: log levels, sync-progress reporting, config properties, IPC
connectivity to the consensus client. Operational surface, not consensus mathematics.

**Besu** is Java, and now lives at `besu-eth/besu` after leaving Hyperledger.<Cite src="besu-repo" />
**Erigon** is Go, at `erigontech/erigon`, and is the other Go option on the execution
layer.<Cite src="erigon-repo" /> Neither showed an open good-first-issue in the aggregator on
26 August 2026.<Cite src="gfi-ethereum-aggregator" /> Do not read that as "closed to contributors".
Read it as "this repository does not currently maintain a stocked list under the label that tool
searches for" — a claim about a label, which the next lesson takes apart properly.

<Aside kind="note">
**Erigon is where a Go engineer should look twice.** It sits in the same language as Geth without
Geth's twenty-two thousand forks, it appears repeatedly as a fellowship project target, and its work
in 2026 has been on exactly the kind of thing this module says protocol work actually is: parallel
execution, historical data serving, serialisation formats.<Cite src="erigon-repo" /> The absence of a
curated newcomer list is a reason to open the issue tracker directly and read it, not a reason to
skip the repository.
</Aside>

## Finding where a behaviour lives, in either codebase

Both entry points reward the same technique, and it is worth naming because it is the opposite of
what an experienced product engineer instinctively does. You do not read a client codebase top-down.
You start from a symptom and read outward. Here is the loop, with each step's purpose named rather
than just the step.

**Subgoal: turn the question into a string that appears in the source.** "How does the client decide
which transactions to keep in the pool?" is not searchable. `PriceBump`, `blobpool`, `underpriced`
and the exact log line your node printed are. Error messages and config flag names are the highest-
yield search terms in any client, because they are the strings that were written once and never
paraphrased.

**Subgoal: use the module boundary as a filter.** In Reth, the crate group is the boundary: transaction
admission is `crates/transaction-pool`, sync staging is `crates/stages`, the trie is
`crates/trie`.<Cite src="reth-repo" /> In Geth, the package prefix in every commit message tells you
the same thing — `eth, rpc:` is a change spanning two packages, and those package names map straight
to directories.<Cite src="geth-readme" /> The commit history of a directory is a curated reading list
someone else already wrote.

**Subgoal: find the test before you read the implementation.** A test names the behaviour, constructs
a minimal input, and asserts the expected output. It is the shortest correct description of what the
code is supposed to do, and unlike a comment it is verified to be current by CI. In Reth,
`cargo test -p reth-rpc mytest` runs a single named test in one crate without building the
workspace's entire test surface.<Cite src="reth-contributing" />

**Subgoal: change one line and watch what breaks.** The set of tests that fail when you break a
function is a precise, machine-generated map of everything that depends on it. This is the fastest
way to learn a subsystem's blast radius, and it costs one `git checkout .` to undo.

Nobody holds a whole client in their head. The people who maintain these codebases hold a subsystem
each and a shared mental model of the interfaces between them, which is exactly what the module
boundaries encode.

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson** does for the consensus layer what this one did for execution: two clients, two
languages, and two contributor processes that differ in ways which decide where your first pull
request goes — including one where basing a branch on the default branch is the wrong move.

**Immediately**, you have a repeatable evaluation. For any client, in under an hour: read the
contributing guide, list the labels, count the unassigned newcomer issues, and note which branch
pull requests target. Four artefacts, all public, all cheap, and together they predict your first
merge better than any ranking.

**And beyond Ethereum**, the transferable habit is the one this lesson opened with. When a reading
list hands you a URL, follow it and look at where you land. A 200 is not confirmation that the thing
you were told about still exists under the name you were told.
