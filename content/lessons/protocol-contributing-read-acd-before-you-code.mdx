Open the All Core Devs table in `ethereum/pm` and read the top three rows as they stood on
26 August 2026:

```text
24 Aug 2026   ACDT    93    issue #2195
20 Aug 2026   ACDC   185    issue #2187
17 Aug 2026   ACDT    92    issue #2186
```

Two things in there contradict what most curricula teach.<Cite src="ethereum-pm" />

The first is **ACDT**. Almost every description of Ethereum governance names two call series —
execution and consensus. There are three. ACDT is Testing, it runs weekly, and by call number 93 it
has been running long enough to have its own facilitators, its own agenda issues and its own
rhythm.<Cite src="ethereum-pm" />

The second is the numbering. ACDE reached 243, ACDC reached 185, ACDT reached 93.<Cite src="ethereum-pm" />
Those are not milestones. They are a weekly meeting that has happened, on schedule, for years, and
whose entire record — agenda, summary, transcript, recording — is public. If you want to know what
core development is, that table is a more honest answer than any explainer.

This lesson is about mining that record, because reading three months of it is the highest
information-per-hour activity available to someone deciding where to contribute, and it is the only
reliable way to avoid building something nobody asked for.

## Three call series, and what each decides

The three are not tiers of importance. They are different questions.

**ACDE and ACDC** — execution and consensus — handle higher-level planning and "what goes in the
fork" decisions.<Cite src="pm-eip-champion-guide" /> They alternate: on one week the focus is the
consensus layer, on the next the execution layer.<Cite src="ethereum-pm" /> On the August 2026 record
they land on Thursdays.<Cite src="ethereum-pm" />

**ACDT** — testing — handles implementation and testing, and the champion guide is precise about when
a topic belongs there rather than on ACDE or ACDC: "usually for current forks once something is
already CFI'd".<Cite src="pm-eip-champion-guide" /> On the same record it runs weekly on
Mondays.<Cite src="ethereum-pm" />

**Breakouts** handle everything that has active implementation work or open questions but cannot
responsibly fit in an ACD agenda.<Cite src="pm-eip-champion-guide" />

Who runs them is public and dated. On 26 August 2026 the execution calls were co-led by Ansgar
Dietrichs (from October 2025) and Nixo (from April 2026); consensus by Parithosh Jayanthi on an
interim basis (from May 2026); and testing jointly by the Ethereum Foundation's EthPandaOps and STEEL
teams.<Cite src="ethereum-pm" /> Past facilitators are listed too — Tim Beiko, Danny Ryan, Hudson
Jameson, Lane Rettig, George Hallam.<Cite src="ethereum-pm" /> A process that names its facilitators
and records when each took over is a process you can navigate; you know whose agenda issue to comment
on.

<Aside kind="note">
**Anyone can put an item on the agenda.** Agendas are GitHub issues in `ethereum/pm`, and the README
states that to add an item you simply comment on one of the agenda issues, subject to three
guidelines: the topic must be technical, must concern the protocol at a low level (applications and
ERCs are generally out of scope unless they bear on a protocol change), and must not be philosophical
— contentious non-technical questions belong on the Ethereum Magicians
forum.<Cite src="ethereum-pm" /> The champion guide adds one operational detail: agendas are
typically updated the day before the call.<Cite src="pm-eip-champion-guide" />

That is a lower barrier than most people assume, and a higher bar than most people meet. "Anyone may
comment" is not the same as "anything gets discussed".
</Aside>

## Reading the record without being misled by it

The record comes in four tiers, and they are not interchangeable. Each ACD row links to an agenda
issue, a summary, a discussion thread, a recording, and a full transcript in a separate
repository.<Cite src="ethereum-pm" />

The upgrade tracker that publishes many of those summaries is unusually honest about its own
limitations, and the warning is worth carrying. Its machine-readable guide states that the site is
built with a framework serving a real HTML shell per route while the page *bodies* render client-side
— so fetching a page URL gets you the title and navigation but not the content — and directs readers
to JSON indexes instead.<Cite src="forkcast-llms" /> The same document distinguishes edited call
summaries from raw transcripts, and says of the transcripts that they are "people thinking out loud
mid-sentence, so quoting transcripts as protocol fact is
unreliable".<Cite src="forkcast-llms" />

Internalise that hierarchy. A transcript tells you what someone said in the moment. A summary tells
you what the call concluded. A merged specification pull request tells you what the protocol now
says. They diverge, and the gap between them is where confidently wrong blog posts come from.

The same tracker also flags that its projected activation dates for upgrades that have not shipped
are "Forkcast's working *estimate*", "a planning assumption, never an announced date", and asks that
anyone quoting one says so.<Cite src="forkcast-llms" /> This is the correct posture, and it is the
one this module takes: **Glamsterdam has not shipped and there is no mainnet date to print.** Its two
headline EIPs are EIP-7732, enshrined proposer-builder separation, and EIP-7928, block-level access
lists.<Cite src="eip-7732" /><Cite src="eip-7928" />

## The stages an EIP passes through, and what each one means for you

Inclusion is not binary. It is a sequence of named stages defined in EIP-7723, and knowing them turns
the record from noise into a status board.<Cite src="pm-eip-champion-guide" /><Cite src="eip-7723" />

**Proposed for Inclusion (PFI).** Someone opened a pull request adding the EIP to the fork's meta-EIP
PFI list during the designated window. That is all it means. The champion's job at this point is to
get implementations started, surface concerns early, and drive down
uncertainty.<Cite src="pm-eip-champion-guide" />

**Considered for Inclusion (CFI).** The EIP was discussed on an ACD call during the discussion window
and rough consensus was reached; the facilitator then updates its status on the fork's meta
EIP.<Cite src="pm-eip-champion-guide" />

**Scheduled for Inclusion (SFI).** Rough consensus across client teams, and on the implementation and
testing path: devnets, then testnets, then mainnet. And a hard requirement worth remembering: for an
EIP to reach SFI it **must** have executable specifications.<Cite src="pm-eip-champion-guide" /><Cite src="eip-7723" />

That last clause connects this lesson to the previous one. The
[executable specification](/c/executable-spec) is not documentation produced after the fact; it is a
gate. Implementing an EIP in the Python execution specs, or the feature in
consensus-specs,<Cite src="consensus-specs" /> is on the critical path to it being scheduled at all
— which is why the champion guide tells EIP authors to attempt that implementation themselves and
says maintainers will step in to polish it.<Cite src="pm-eip-champion-guide" />

There is a fourth state nobody puts on the diagram: **Declined**. Forkcast's dated stage history
records EIP-7782, "Reduce Block Latency", as Declined for Glamsterdam with its last stage change on
14 August 2025.<Cite src="forkcast-stage-changes" /> The same dataset records EIP-7805, FOCIL, as
Scheduled — for Hegotá, not Glamsterdam, with its last stage change on 19 February
2026.<Cite src="forkcast-stage-changes" />

## Breakouts: sixteen doors, each with a name on it

The breakout list is the most actionable page in `ethereum/pm` and almost nobody reads it. It is
generated automatically from issues, counts a series as active if it met within the past three
months, and on its 24 August 2026 update it listed sixteen active
series.<Cite src="pm-active-breakouts" />

Among them, with their facilitators: FOCIL Breakout (jihoonsong), L1-zkEVM Breakout (jsign), P2P
Networking (kamilsa), PQ Interop (tcoratger), PQ Transaction Signatures (asanso), RPC Standards
(bomanaps), SSZ Engine API (RazorClient), Fast Confirmation Rule (mkalinin), Frame Transaction
Breakout (lightclient), Encrypt the Mempool (LoringHarkness), Stateless Implementers (gabrocheleau),
Glamsterdam Repricings (misilva73), All Wallet Devs (SamWilsn), EIP Editing Office Hour and EIPIP
Meeting (poojaranjan).<Cite src="pm-active-breakouts" />

Read that as a directory. Each row is a live workstream, a recurring call you can attend, and a named
human whose agenda issue you can comment on. If you want to know where the work is, this is a shorter
and more current answer than any roadmap diagram.

<Aside kind="warn">
**The inactive list is evidence, but of a narrower thing than it looks.** The same page lists series
that have not met in three months or are complete, and it includes Verkle Implementers, PeerDAS
Breakout, EOF Implementers and Portal Implementers.<Cite src="pm-active-breakouts" />

Be careful what you conclude. "This call series has not met since May" is a fact about meetings. It is
consistent with the work being finished — PeerDAS shipped — and consistent with it being parked.
Verkle's roadmap status is genuinely unsettled: protocol research has moved toward hash-based binary
trees and there is no single authoritative announcement resolving it. A dormant breakout series is one
piece of evidence, not a cancellation notice, and this module prints no date and no verdict for it.
</Aside>

## Showing up, and the four places surprises come from

The champion guide is unusually direct about attendance, and the norm it states applies to anyone
whose work is on an agenda, not only to EIP authors. If your item is scheduled, you are strongly
encouraged to attend the call. If you cannot, submit a written update beforehand — it can be added to
the agenda or posted in the relevant Discord channel — or send someone to represent you, and catch up
afterwards from the recording, transcript and summary.<Cite src="pm-eip-champion-guide" /> The reason
given is blunt: "decisions *can* be made without you".<Cite src="pm-eip-champion-guide" />

The same guide lists where to watch so that a decision does not blindside you: the Ethereum
Foundation blog's Checkpoint series in the research-and-development category, the `#allcoredevs`
channel in the Ethereum R&D Discord, the fork timeline on the upgrade tracker, and the `ethereum/pm`
agendas the day before each call.<Cite src="pm-eip-champion-guide" /> Discord is named as the primary
asynchronous venue, with per-layer testing channels — `#el-testing` for execution-layer help and
`#cl-testing` for consensus-layer help — pointed at directly when an implementation needs
review.<Cite src="pm-eip-champion-guide" /> The upgrade process document adds that a dedicated channel
is created for each EIP in an upgrade, to coordinate client developers, test engineers and security
researchers on that EIP.<Cite src="pm-protocol-upgrade-process" />

For a newcomer the practical reading of all that is narrower and more useful than it first appears.
You are not going to champion an EIP in your first year. But every one of those venues is readable
without permission, and reading them is how you find out that the thing you were about to build is
already half-built by someone with a Discord channel named after it.

## What the record tells you that a roadmap cannot

Here is the reading protocol, with each step's purpose named.

**Subgoal: find work that has a named owner and a gap.** ACD notes name people. An item discussed
with nobody attached is usually not an opening, however loudly it was debated. An item where someone
says they need help, or that a client team has not started, is.

**Subgoal: separate deferred from blocked.** Deferred means nobody is doing it — potentially yours.
Blocked means someone is waiting on someone else, and arriving with code does not unblock it.

**Subgoal: cross-check against the artefacts.** The [four-artefact reading](/c/reading-a-spec-change)
you already know applies: the EIP for intent, the specs pull request for the normative change, the
test vectors for observable behaviour, the client pull requests for reality. The ACD record adds a
fifth layer above all of them — *whether anyone wants it* — and that is the only one that tells you
whether the work is worth starting.

**Subgoal: measure the staleness rate.** Go back three months, extract every item, and check how many
were resolved before you finished reading. That number is a property of the process, and it calibrates
how fast anything you learn here decays.

## Devnets: where the process explicitly stops protecting you

The upgrade process document in `ethereum/pm` reads like a change-management policy, because it is
one. Its gates are specific:<Cite src="pm-protocol-upgrade-process" />

- Upgrades must not proceed until clients representing **90% of the active weight** pass all consensus
  and execution tests and are stable.
- There must be at least **14 days** between client releases being ready and the first testnet going
  live — reducible to 7 if that testnet is being shut down afterwards — so that internal security
  review, bug-bounty inclusion and any external review can happen.
- Where multiple testnet upgrades are scheduled, each must be at least **10 days apart**, ideally two
  weeks.
- The upgrade must have gone through **at least two testnets**.
- Mainnet must not upgrade **less than 30 days** after the final testnet is verified successful, to
  give downstream projects — layer 2s producing DAO proposals, among others — time to plan.
- **Mainnet should not have an upgrade date set until all testnets have been upgraded.**

That final bullet is why "no mainnet date" is a real state rather than evasion. The process forbids
setting one early.

And then, in the middle of all that rigour, one sentence about devnets:

> Devnets are expected to experience issues, are short lived, and a new one should be launched when
> relevant. Given their short lived nature, they are considered out of scope of this
> process.<Cite src="pm-protocol-upgrade-process" />

**That sentence is the invitation.** Devnets are the part of the pipeline where breaking things is the
purpose, where no process protects anyone, and where the value of a stranger running an unusual
configuration is highest — because the people who can fix a bug cannot also be the people running
every configuration that exposes it.

The skill ceiling is low and the skill floor is "can run a node and read logs". The output is a filed
issue a maintainer can act on: client versions on both layers, the devnet and its iteration, hardware,
the full log window around the failure, and a reproduction. The devnet branches in the execution specs
repository tell you which iterations are current — on 26 August 2026 those included
`devnets/glamsterdam/6` through `8`, several `devnets/bal/*` branches, `devnets/focil/0` and
`devnets/frames/0`.<Cite src="execution-specs-branches" /> Client teams label the resulting issues:
Lighthouse carries `fusaka-devnet-0` through `fusaka-devnet-3` and `peerdas-devnet-4` among its
labels, so you can read what previous devnets broke before joining the next
one.<Cite src="lighthouse-labels" />

The cross-client harness is the other half of this. `ethereum/hive` exists to run independent
implementations against each other, and its trophy list is a record of divergences it caught in
production clients.<Cite src="hive-repo" /> Devnets find what happens when implementations meet at
scale; hive finds it in CI. Both are chronically short of people.

You have three weekends and you want your first contribution to be something a core developer will be

Describe what you do with weekend one, and what you refuse to decide until you have done it.

<Answer>
**Refuse first.** I will not pick a workstream from a roadmap diagram, a conference talk or an
upgrade explainer. Those describe intent; the ACD record describes what is scheduled, contested, or
waiting on someone finding time — and the record carries a Declined state that the explainers do
not.<Cite src="forkcast-stage-changes" />

**Weekend one, in order.**

1. *Establish the shape of the record.* Read the `ethereum/pm` README: three series, not two — ACDE,
ACDC and ACDT — with ACDT covering implementation and testing for forks whose contents are already
decided.<Cite src="ethereum-pm" /><Cite src="pm-eip-champion-guide" />

2. *Read the process documents before the notes.* The champion guide gives the PFI → CFI → SFI
vocabulary the notes are written in, including that SFI requires executable
specifications.<Cite src="pm-eip-champion-guide" /><Cite src="eip-7723" /> Without that, half the
notes are unparseable.

3. *Read summaries, not transcripts.* The tracker's own guidance says the transcripts are people
thinking out loud mid-sentence and unreliable as protocol fact.<Cite src="forkcast-llms" /> Use them
only to find who said something, then confirm against the merged specification change.

4. *Extract with a fixed schema.* Call date, item, named person or team, linked issue or pull request,
and state — scheduled, contested, blocked, or deferred. An item with no named owner is usually not an
opening.

5. *Read the breakout list last, as the index.* Sixteen active series on 24 August 2026, each with a
facilitator and an agenda issue.<Cite src="pm-active-breakouts" /> If an item I extracted maps to an
active series, there is a recurring call about it and a person to ask.

**What weekend one is not for.** Writing code. The output is a shortlist of three workstreams with a
named contact each, plus a staleness count: how many extracted items were already resolved by the
time I finished reading. That number tells me how fresh my shortlist has to stay.

**And the thing I will have learned regardless.** The gates are explicit — 90% of active weight
passing tests, at least two testnets, no mainnet date until every testnet has upgraded — and devnets
are deliberately outside all of it, because they exist to
fail.<Cite src="pm-protocol-upgrade-process" /> That is where a stranger with a spare machine is worth
the most.
</Answer>

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson** turns to the programmes built on top of all this: the Ethereum Protocol
Fellowship, the open study group beneath it, and the two similarly-named websites that learners
confuse constantly. Everything in this lesson is a prerequisite for that one, because the fellowship
assumes you can already read the process you have just been reading.

**Immediately**, you have a weekly rhythm to attach to: three call series with public agendas you may
comment on, sixteen active breakouts with named facilitators, and a stage vocabulary that turns "is
this happening" into a question with a dated answer.

**And the transferable move** is the tier discipline. Transcript, summary, merged change, shipped
release: four different confidence levels wearing the same subject line. Any organisation whose
decisions are recorded has this structure, and quoting the wrong tier is how people end up defending
a decision that was reversed on the next call.
