Today [a slot](/c/slot-phases) has one arrival event that matters. A block shows up, or it does not, and everything
else in the twelve seconds is a reaction to that fact.

Every monitoring dashboard, every alert threshold, every latency budget and every mental model an
infrastructure engineer carries about Ethereum is keyed to that single event. After EIP-7732 there
are two arrival events, from two different senders, and two more deadlines besides. This lesson puts
numbers on all of them — from the configuration file, not from memory — and then works out what the
rearrangement is actually for.

## Read the deadlines from the config, and notice they are no longer seconds

The mainnet configuration expresses slot deadlines in **basis points of the slot duration** rather
than in seconds. Here is the relevant block, unedited:<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-gloas" />

```yaml
# 12000 milliseconds
SLOT_DURATION_MS: 12000
...
# 1667 basis points, ~17% of SLOT_DURATION_MS
PROPOSER_REORG_CUTOFF_BPS: 1667
# 3333 basis points, ~33% of SLOT_DURATION_MS
ATTESTATION_DUE_BPS: 3333
# 6667 basis points, ~67% of SLOT_DURATION_MS
AGGREGATE_DUE_BPS: 6667

# Gloas
# 2500 basis points, 25% of SLOT_DURATION_MS
ATTESTATION_DUE_BPS_GLOAS: 2500
# 5000 basis points, 50% of SLOT_DURATION_MS
AGGREGATE_DUE_BPS_GLOAS: 5000
...
# 5000 basis points, 50% of SLOT_DURATION_MS
PAYLOAD_DUE_BPS: 5000
# 7500 basis points, 75% of SLOT_DURATION_MS
PAYLOAD_ATTESTATION_DUE_BPS: 7500
```

The parameterisation change is worth a moment on its own. Deadlines used to be a count of intervals
per slot; they are now a fraction of a slot duration, which means the whole schedule scales
automatically if the slot duration ever moves. That is defensive design in a place you would expect
it: the proposal to shorten slots to six seconds, EIP-7782, was declined for this
upgrade.<Cite src="eip-7773" /><Cite src="eip-7782" /> The schedule is now expressed so that the
decision can be revisited without rewriting every deadline.

**Slot duration does not change in this upgrade.** Twelve seconds, before and
after.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-gloas" />

## The four phases, with the arithmetic done

Converting basis points of twelve seconds:

| Phase | Deadline | Today | Under Gloas |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Beacon block published | start of slot | t = 0 s | t = 0 s |
| Attestation due | `ATTESTATION_DUE_BPS` | 33.33% → 4 s | 25% → 3 s |
| Payload reveal due | `PAYLOAD_DUE_BPS` | — | 50% → 6 s |
| Aggregate due | `AGGREGATE_DUE_BPS` | 66.67% → 8 s | 50% → 6 s |
| Payload attestation due | `PAYLOAD_ATTESTATION_DUE_BPS` | — | 75% → 9 s |

All five rows come from the same configuration file.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-gloas" />

Read down the "Under Gloas" column and the slot's story is:

1. **t = 0.** The proposer publishes a beacon block containing a signed bid. No payload, no
   transactions, no blob commitments beyond the ones inside the
   bid.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />
2. **t = 3 s.** Attesters vote on that block. They have run the consensus state transition and
   nothing else.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-validator" />
3. **t = 6 s.** The winning builder must have revealed the payload envelope by now, or a PTC member
   watching its own inbox will record `payload_present = False`.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-validator" />
4. **t = 9 s.** PTC members must have broadcast their payload attestations.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-validator" />

Then the next slot begins, and its proposer builds on top of a payload it has had three seconds to
validate — which is where execution validity finally gets established.

### The attestation deadline got tighter, and that is not a mistake

The number that trips people is the second row. Today's attestation deadline is a third of the slot;
under Gloas it is a quarter. Attesters get **one second less**, not more.

That is coherent once you see what was removed from their job. Today, between the block arriving and
the attestation deadline, a validator must perform the consensus state transition, perform the
execution state transition, check blob data availability, and evaluate the new
head.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> Under the new arrangement it performs the consensus state transition and
evaluates the head. Execution and data-availability validation move out of that window entirely, to
be done between the builder's reveal and the next slot's attestation
deadline.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

Less time, much less work, and the bytes are smaller too: the block on the critical path no longer
carries the payload, so it propagates faster. The specification bounds the signed bid at
`MAX_SIGNED_EXECUTION_PAYLOAD_BID_SIZE`, 196,932 bytes — under 200 KiB for the object that must reach
every attester in three seconds, against a full block plus its blob commitments
today.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-preset-gloas" />

## How much wider is the validation window? Give the range, not a number

This is the claim you will see stated most confidently and it is the one that deserves the most care.

**What the EIP itself says.** Today, validators have until the attesting deadline — described as four
seconds on mainnet — to do consensus, execution, availability and head
evaluation.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> Under the new design, the split gives *the next proposer* six
seconds to validate the payload, computed as `SECONDS_PER_SLOT * 2 // INTERVALS_PER_SLOT`, and *every
other validator* nine seconds, computed as
`SECONDS_PER_SLOT * 3 // INTERVALS_PER_SLOT`.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

**What the configuration says.** The Gloas payload deadline is 50% of the slot and the payload
attestation deadline is 75%, which is where the six and the nine come
from.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-gloas" />

**What is genuinely unresolved.** Published descriptions of "how much wider" disagree, and they
disagree about the *baseline*, not the result. Some frame today's effective budget as roughly two
seconds — because the useful working time is what remains after propagation, not the whole interval
to the deadline — and some frame it as roughly four seconds, quoting the deadline itself. Depending on
which baseline is chosen, and whether the new figure quoted is the next proposer's six or every other
validator's nine, the same change gets described as "two to nine", "four to six", or "four to nine".
None of these is a lie and none of them is a fact you can cite. Sources examined for this curriculum
recorded both framings and did not average them.

<Aside kind="warn">
Do not publish a single pair of numbers for the widening. What is defensible: the two deadlines from
the configuration file, each with its unit and what it applies to, and the direction of the change.
What is not defensible: a headline like "the validation window triples", which requires picking a
baseline nobody agrees on and then dividing.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-gloas" /><Cite src="eip-7732" />
</Aside>

Also notice that the two figures apply to *different actors*. Six seconds is the next proposer's
budget; nine is everyone else's.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> A claim that omits which one it means is
underspecified even before the baseline problem is considered.

## Deferred execution, stated precisely

Strip the timings out and the mechanism is one sentence: **the execution payload's validity is
established against the following block rather than within the current one.**

The EIP spells out the consequence for how transactions are perceived. A transaction included in slot
N by the builder is not widely validated until the proposer of slot N+1 releases its beacon block on
top of block N, and the attesters of slot N+1 vote on that block as the
head.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> Confirmation, in the sense of "the network has checked this and agrees",
arrives a slot later than it does today.

That is a real cost and it should be named as one. What it buys is set out in the EIP's motivation as
five distinct things, and it is worth separating them because they are usually collapsed into
one:<Cite src="eip-7732" />

1. **A trust-free exchange between proposer and builder** — the subject of the previous lessons.
2. **Validators only do the consensus state transition in the critical window**, with execution and
   availability deferred to most of the remainder of the slot.
3. **Faster propagation on the critical path**, because the payload's size is no longer in the
   consensus block.
4. **Lower reorg risk for blob-carrying blocks**, because data-availability timelines lengthen
   naturally *and* because the builder may broadcast blob sidecars even before attestations for the
   beacon block have been released.
5. **Fewer missed attestations when a builder produces an invalid payload**, and stronger fork-choice
   weight properties in that case — today an invalid payload can cost honest attesters their votes.

Only the first of those is about MEV.

## The slot has phases because the state transition does

The phases are not a scheduling convention layered on top of an unchanged state machine. The state
transition function itself splits.

The Gloas beacon-chain specification says so in as many words: state transition is fundamentally
modified, broken into two parts — first importing a signed block, then importing an execution
payload.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" /> The EIP counts three transition functions in
total, because processing a `SignedBeaconBlock` changes only consensus-layer state, while processing a
`SignedExecutionPayloadEnvelope` changes **both** the execution-layer state and the consensus-layer
one — and the envelope therefore commits to the consensus-layer post-state
root.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

That last clause repays attention. The builder cannot assemble its envelope without knowing what the
consensus state will look like after its payload is applied, which means the builder is running the
consensus state transition too. Being a builder was already an infrastructure business; this makes it
formally a consensus-layer participant as well.

For a client implementer, the practical shape is that `process_block` no longer contains
`process_execution_payload`. In its place sits `process_execution_payload_bid`, which validates the
commitment and queues the payment, while `process_execution_payload` becomes a separate helper called
when the envelope arrives over the network.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> Several operations move with it:
deposit requests, withdrawal requests and consolidation requests are removed from `process_operations`
and deferred until the payload is processed.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

### Withdrawals become asynchronous, and that has a visible consequence

Here is the cleanest example of what "the payload might not arrive" does to a system that was built
assuming it always would.

Withdrawals move value from the consensus layer to the execution layer. Today both halves happen in
one block: the set of withdrawals is determined by the pre-state, the block carries them, and the
block is invalid if they do not match.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

Under EIP-7732 the halves separate. When the beacon block is processed, the withdrawals are deducted
from the beacon chain and the set just deducted is committed to the post-state. Any execution payload
built on that post-state is invalid unless it includes precisely that
list.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> Beacon state gains `payload_expected_withdrawals` to hold
it.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

Now make the slot empty. The deduction happened; the crediting did not. The specification's answer is
to stop: **the consensus layer does not process any more withdrawals until an execution payload has
fulfilled the outstanding ones.**<Cite src="eip-7732" />

Follow that to its operational conclusion. A run of empty slots stalls the withdrawal queue for
everyone — not just for the validators whose withdrawals were in flight. Withdrawal latency, which
today is a function of the sweep and the queue length, gains a new dependency on whether builders are
revealing payloads. The EIP discusses the alternative design, deferring all withdrawal processing to
payload validation, and notes it changes behaviour when several payloads are missing in a row, since
beacon-chain balances move in the meantime.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> Neither option makes the
interaction disappear.

## A slot where the payload is late

Trace one concrete failure through the schedule, because the abstract version hides which parties
notice and when.

**t = 0.** The proposer publishes a beacon block carrying a bid from builder B for 4 ETH. The block
is small and propagates quickly.

**t = 3 s.** Attesters vote for the block. They ran the consensus state transition; there was no
payload to execute and none was expected. The block is well
attested.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-validator" />

**t = 6 s.** B's envelope has not arrived — B's own upstream had a problem, or B decided not to send
it. Every PTC member checks its inbox, finds no envelope referencing this block root received before
the payload deadline, and sets `payload_present = False`.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-validator" />

**t = 6.4 s.** The envelope arrives. It is too late to change the attestations already forming; the
test was against the deadline, not against eventual arrival.

**t = 9 s.** PTC members broadcast attestations saying the payload was not present.

**Next slot.** The next proposer builds on the empty branch, the availability bit for the slot is
zero, and the slot has a beacon block and no execution
block.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

**End of epoch.** The pending payment for that slot cleared its attestation-weight quorum — the beacon
block was well attested, remember, and the quorum is about the block rather than the payload — so
builder B pays the proposer 4 ETH for a slot that produced no
transactions.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

Every party behaved according to the specification. The proposer earned its bid. The builder paid for
nothing. The users whose transactions were in that payload were not included and have to try again
next slot. That is the design working, not failing — and whether a builder would ever *choose* that
outcome is the entire subject of the next lesson.

## Why this is a scaling prerequisite

Put the constraint in one line: raising [the gas limit](/c/block-gas-limit) and
[the blob count](/c/peerdas-enables-more-blobs) means more bytes to move and more work to verify inside
a fixed twelve seconds, and the binding constraint is the attestation deadline.

Everything a block does has to fit before attesters vote, and attesters vote at a fixed fraction of a
fixed slot.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-gloas" /> There are three ways out of that box. Make
the slot longer — rejected, and the opposite was being proposed. Make blocks propagate faster —
worthwhile, and [bounded](/c/propagation-latency). Or take work off the critical path — which is what deferring execution does.

So the sequencing of the roadmap is not arbitrary. Enshrined PBS and block-level access lists are the
two headline items of the same upgrade, and together they are the "make blocks bigger safely"
package: one takes execution validation off the attestation deadline, the other makes execution
itself parallelisable.<Cite src="eip-7773" /><Cite src="etherworld-epbs-headliner" /> Reading either
of them as a standalone feature makes the upgrade look like a grab bag when it is a coherent
argument.

<Aside kind="note">
The specification carries a small piece of evidence for the ambition: the Gloas beacon chain spec
introduces a *gas limit schedule*, a list of epoch-and-gas-limit records that clients may use as a
recommended default, explicitly optional and introducing no new validity
rules.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" /> At the tag read for this lesson the table is
empty. An empty table with a defined schema is a mechanism waiting for numbers — which is a useful
signal about intent, and not a number you can quote.
</Aside>

### One duty moves house

A detail that matters more to node operators than its size suggests: **proposers no longer broadcast
`DataColumnSidecar` objects. That becomes a builder duty.**<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-validator" />

Think about what that does to a proposer's upstream bandwidth at t = 0. Today a proposer publishing a
blob-carrying block must push the block and its data columns into the network within the propagation
budget, from whatever connection it has. Under this design the proposer publishes a small beacon
block, and the entity with the data-centre connection publishes the large objects — and may start
before attestations for the beacon block have even been
released.<Cite src="eip-7732" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-builder" />

For a home staker proposing a full blob-carrying block, that is one of the more directly felt changes
in the whole upgrade, and it is nearly always omitted from summaries.

## The next proposer chooses which branch it extends

One more piece of the schedule, because it explains where the PTC's votes actually get spent.

When the next slot's proposer picks a head, it is not only picking a block. The previous slot's block
exists in fork choice in two forms — full and empty — and the proposer decides which one to build on.
The rule is `should_extend_payload`, and its structure is worth reading:<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-fork-choice" />

```python
def should_extend_payload(store: Store, root: Root) -> bool:
    assert store.blocks[root].slot + 1 == get_current_slot(store)
    if not is_payload_verified(store, root):
        return False
    proposer_root = store.proposer_boost_root
    payload_is_timely = payload_timeliness(store, root, timely=True)
    payload_data_is_available = payload_data_availability(store, root, available=True)
    return (
        (payload_is_timely and payload_data_is_available)
        or proposer_root == Root()
        or store.blocks[proposer_root].parent_root != root
        or is_parent_node_full(store, store.blocks[proposer_root])
    )
```

The first clause is the ordinary case: extend the payload when the PTC's view supports both timeliness
and availability. The remaining clauses are a default in the *other* direction — extend anyway, unless
the boosted proposer specifically built on this block and chose
empty.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-fork-choice" />

The bias is deliberate. Discarding a payload that did arrive is more destructive than keeping one that
arrived marginally late, so the rule leans toward keeping it and reserves the discard for the case
where the proposer with the boost has actively decided otherwise. And the first line of the body is
the same guard as before: a node that has not verified the payload locally will not extend it,
whatever the committee said.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-fork-choice" />

This is the sense in which fork choice stops choosing among blocks. It chooses among
`(block, payload_status)` pairs, and one of the tiebreakers between them is a committee vote about the
clock.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-fork-choice" />

<Aside kind="note">
One consequence for anyone reasoning about reorgs. `PROPOSER_REORG_CUTOFF_BPS` — the point after which
a proposer should not attempt to reorg a late block — remains 1667 basis points, about 17% of the
slot, and has no Gloas-specific override in the configuration at this
tag.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-gloas" /> The proposer's reorg decision window is therefore
unchanged in relative terms while everything after it moved. If you carry a mental timeline of the
slot, that is the one landmark that stayed put.
</Aside>

## Where the risk moved

Every change that buys time somewhere spends it somewhere else. Three places to keep an eye on.

**The payload reveal is now a race with its own deadline.** A builder whose envelope arrives at 6.1
seconds has, as far as the committee is concerned, not revealed. That is a hard edge, and it sits on
the builder's side of the network — which is the side with the best connectivity, which is part of
why it is tolerable.

**Confirmation semantics for users get one slot longer.** Anything in your product that says
"confirmed" based on inclusion in the current block is describing something weaker after this
change.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

**Two objects means two chances to fail.** Today a slot is lost if the block does not arrive. Now it
can be half-lost: block arrives, payload does not, chain continues with an empty slot. That outcome is
legal, recorded in state, and — as the next lesson works out — occasionally the builder's rational
choice.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

## What this unlocks

You can now describe a Gloas slot as a schedule rather than an event, with every deadline read from
the configuration file and expressed with its unit: block at 0, attestation at 25%, payload at 50%,
payload attestation at 75%, all of a 12,000-millisecond
slot.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-gloas" />

You can also say why the change exists without reaching for MEV: it moves execution validation off
the attestation critical path, which is the only lever available for larger blocks and more blobs that
does not lengthen the slot.<Cite src="eip-7732" /><Cite src="etherworld-epbs-headliner" />

And you can refuse, precisely, to quote a single figure for how much the validation window widened —
naming the two deadlines, the two actors they apply to, and the disagreement about the
baseline.<Cite src="ethresearch-epbs-threads" /><Cite src="ethereum-pm" />

Next: the thing this schedule makes possible. Between signing and revealing, the builder holds a
choice — and choices with deadlines and volatile underlying assets have a name in
finance.<Cite src="eip-7732" />
