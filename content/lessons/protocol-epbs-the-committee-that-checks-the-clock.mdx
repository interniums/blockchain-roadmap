Five hundred and twelve validators are picked for a slot, and the entire question put to them is
this:

```python
class PayloadAttestationData(Container):
    beacon_block_root: Root
    slot: Slot
    payload_present: Boolean
    blob_data_available: Boolean
```

Two booleans.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" /> Did the payload turn up, and was its
blob data there. Not "is it valid". Not "does it execute". Not "does the state root match". Did it
turn up.

This is the payload timeliness committee, and almost every wrong summary of EIP-7732 goes wrong right
here, by reading "the committee attests to the payload" as "the committee validates the payload". The
inversion matters because the committee's uselessness for validation is exactly what makes the design
work.

## What the committee is

Each slot, a subset of that slot's [attesters](/c/attestation) is selected as the PTC — a per-slot
[committee](/c/committee) in the protocol's usual sense of the word. The size is a preset:
`PTC_SIZE = 512`.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-preset-gloas" />

The selection concatenates every beacon committee for the slot in order, then draws 512 members from
that pool by balance-weighted selection, seeded from the slot number under
`DOMAIN_PTC_ATTESTER`:<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

```python
def compute_ptc(state: BeaconState, slot: Slot) -> PTC:
    """
    Get the payload timeliness committee, with possible duplicates, for the given ``slot``.
    """
    epoch = compute_epoch_at_slot(slot)
    seed = hash(get_seed(state, epoch, DOMAIN_PTC_ATTESTER) + uint_to_bytes(slot))
    indices: list[ValidatorIndex] = []
    committees_per_slot = get_committee_count_per_slot(state, epoch)
    for i in range(committees_per_slot):
        committee = get_beacon_committee(state, slot, CommitteeIndex(i))
        indices.extend(committee)
    return PTC(
        compute_balance_weighted_selection(
            state, indices, seed, size=PTC_SIZE, shuffle_indices=False
        )
    )
```

Two details in the docstring and the call deserve attention.

**"With possible duplicates."** Selection is balance-weighted, so a validator with a large effective
balance can occupy more than one of the 512 seats. The committee is 512 *seats*, not necessarily 512
distinct validators. Anyone computing committee statistics who assumes distinctness will be quietly
wrong.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

**The membership is precomputed and cached.** Beacon state carries a `ptc_window`, refreshed at epoch
boundaries, and `get_ptc` reads from it rather than recomputing.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />
On the validator side, `get_ptc_assignment` is called at the start of each epoch so a validator knows
its assigned slots in advance, the same rhythm as ordinary attestation
duties.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-validator" />

A member's obligation is a single `PayloadAttestationMessage` broadcast on its own global gossip
topic, before a deadline expressed in milliseconds into the
slot.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-p2p" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-validator" /> Those
messages are later aggregated into `PayloadAttestation` objects — an aggregation bitvector of
`PTC_SIZE` bits plus the shared data and signature — and up to
`MAX_PAYLOAD_ATTESTATIONS`, which is 4, ride in the *next* beacon
block.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-preset-gloas" />

## What the committee explicitly does not do

The EIP is unusually direct about this. PTC members do not need to validate the execution payload
before attesting to it. They perform basic checks — verifying the builder's signature and that the
correct block hash is included — and that is the extent of
it.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

Execution validity is established elsewhere and later: against the *following* block. The next
proposer builds on top of the payload and, in doing so, must have validated it; the attesters of the
next slot vote on that proposer's block.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> The chain still refuses invalid
payloads. It refuses them one slot later than it used to.

  belief="The payload timeliness committee validates the execution payload."
  reality="Its members attest only that the payload arrived within its window and that the blob data was available. Execution validity is checked against the next block."
  why="'Committee attests to the payload' reads as validation, and every other committee in the protocol votes on something's correctness."
  src="eip-7732"
/>

**This is the whole trick.** If the PTC had to validate, the payload's execution would be back on a
committee's critical path and nothing would have been gained. By restricting the committee's question
to something answerable by *looking at the clock and your own inbox*, the design gets a chain-wide
consensus answer to "did it arrive" at almost no computational cost — and answering "did it arrive"
is precisely what the payment and fork-choice rules need.

<Aside kind="note">
Note what kind of question "did it arrive on time" is. It is a question about each validator's local
view, and local views differ. That is why it needs a committee at all: no single node's opinion about
arrival is authoritative, and unlike execution validity, arrival is not something you can recompute
from first principles later. It has to be *witnessed*.
</Aside>

It is worth asking why a committee is needed rather than letting every node decide for itself. Every
node *does* decide for itself — that is the local-availability check in fork choice below. But two
things in the protocol need a single shared answer rather than a per-node one. The builder's payment
settles or does not settle in the state transition, which is the same for everyone by
definition.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" /> And fork choice has to converge, which it
cannot do if half the network considers a slot full and half considers it empty with no mechanism for
resolving the split. A sampled committee is the protocol's standard instrument for turning many local
observations into one number the state transition can read, and it is being used here for exactly
that.

What makes it cheap is the narrowness of the question. An attestation committee's vote requires each
member to have executed a block; a PTC vote requires each member to check a timestamp against its own
inbox. The design is buying consensus on a fact that costs nothing to observe, and deliberately not
buying consensus on the fact that costs everything.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

### One member's decision procedure, in full

The honest validator guide gives the whole algorithm for a PTC member, and it is short enough to read
as a whole. Written out:<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-validator" />

**Subgoal: decide whether you have anything to attest about.** If the validator has seen no beacon
block for its assigned slot, it does not submit a payload attestation at all — the guide notes it
would be ignored anyway. No block, no opinion.

**Subgoal: name what you are attesting about.** `data.beacon_block_root` is the hash tree root of the
block it did see; `data.slot` is the assigned slot.

**Subgoal: answer the timeliness question from your own inbox and your own clock.** If a previously
seen `SignedExecutionPayloadEnvelope` references that block root *and* it was seen before
`get_payload_due_ms()` milliseconds into the slot, set `payload_present` to `True`. Otherwise `False`.
That is the entire test: did this object cross my network interface before this millisecond.

**Subgoal: answer the availability question with the check you already run.** `blob_data_available` is
`is_data_available(data.beacon_block_root)` — the same data-availability routine the node already
performs, not a new one.

**Subgoal: sign the data, not the message.** The attester signs `PayloadAttestationData` only, not the
`validator_index` field of the message. That is what makes the messages aggregatable: 512 members
signing an identical payload produce signatures that combine.

Read that list and notice how little of it is judgement. Every step is either a lookup or a
comparison against a deadline. A PTC member cannot be wrong in an interesting way; it can only report
a different network view, which is the thing the committee exists to sample.

### Where the votes end up

The messages go out on a global gossip topic during the slot. The next slot's proposer aggregates
them and includes up to `MAX_PAYLOAD_ATTESTATIONS` — four — aggregate objects in its
block.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-validator" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-preset-gloas" />
Each aggregate is a `Bitvector[PTC_SIZE]` of which seats signed, the shared data, and one aggregate
signature.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

An observation, offered as an observation rather than as the specification's stated rationale: for a
fixed block root and slot, `PayloadAttestationData` has exactly two free bits, so the committee can
hold at most four distinct opinions. Four aggregates is therefore enough to carry every one of them.
The specification states the constant without explaining it, so treat the correspondence as a
consistency check on your reading, not as a sourced
justification.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-preset-gloas" />

## The committee cannot lie a payload into existence

There is a design detail in fork choice that decides how much power the PTC actually holds, and it
answers the obvious worry — that 512 validators voting "it arrived" could make everyone believe in a
payload nobody has.

```python
def payload_timeliness(store: Store, root: Root, timely: bool) -> bool:
    # The beacon block root must be known
    assert root in store.payload_timeliness_vote

    # If the payload is not locally available, the payload
    # is not considered available regardless of the PTC vote
    if not is_payload_verified(store, root):
        return not timely

    votes = store.payload_timeliness_vote[root]
    return sum(vote == timely for vote in votes) > PAYLOAD_TIMELY_THRESHOLD
```

The comment is doing the work.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-fork-choice" /> A node that does not
have the payload does not treat it as timely no matter how the committee voted. The PTC's votes
break ties *among nodes that already have the data*; they never substitute for having it.

The threshold is a simple majority of seats: `PAYLOAD_TIMELY_THRESHOLD = PTC_SIZE // 2`,
256.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-fork-choice" /> Worth flagging, because the EIP's rationale
discusses raising it to two thirds as a mitigation against a specific attack — so a description
written from the EIP's rationale and a description written from the merged fork-choice rules will
give you different numbers.<Cite src="eip-7732" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-fork-choice" />
The merged specification at the tag read for this lesson says 256.

### How hard is the committee to capture?

The EIP puts a figure on it: for an attacker controlling 35% of total stake, the expected time to
achieve majority control of the PTC is 205,000 years.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> Take the figure for what
it is — a statement about random sampling from a large validator set, under the stated stake share,
and nothing more. It says a randomly drawn 512-seat committee is a poor thing to try to capture by
luck. It says nothing about coordinated behaviour by a large staking entity that is *legitimately*
drawn into many seats over time.

And the specification is candid about a cheaper attack that does not require capturing the committee
at all. There is **no penalty for PTC equivocation or payload equivocation** — no slashing condition
for a builder that reveals a payload and simultaneously signals it withheld, nor for a PTC member that
signs both ways. A builder that controls a network partition, colluding with a single malicious PTC
member, can produce a split view in which part of the network concludes the payload was present and
part concludes it was withheld.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

The EIP names two possible mitigations — raising the timeliness threshold to two thirds, or adding
slashing conditions for equivocation — and states that it opted for simplicity of implementation
instead, on the grounds that the attack costs the builder something (it reveals a payload that may
not be included) and produces a split view rather than a
theft.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> Whether that is the right call is a live question, not a settled one,
and it is exactly the kind of thing that changes between devnets and
mainnet.<Cite src="ethresearch-epbs-threads" />

That last row pairs with something in the state transition worth noting explicitly.
`process_payload_attestation` checks three things — that the attestation is for the parent block, that
it is for the previous slot, and that the aggregate signature verifies — and then
returns.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" /> No balance is increased, no participation
flag is set, no penalty is applied.

So at this tag, the PTC duty carries **neither a reward for performing it nor a penalty for
skipping it**, and equivocating on it is not slashable. The incentive to participate is the ordinary
one — a validator that is also an attester has already done the work of watching for the payload —
plus whatever a client implementation chooses to do by default. Whether that stays true is precisely
the kind of thing that gets revisited between devnets and mainnet, and it is worth re-reading the
merged spec rather than assuming this
paragraph.<Cite src="ethresearch-epbs-threads" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

## A slot can be full, skipped, or empty

Now the consequence that reaches furthest outside the consensus layer.

The EIP defines three states a slot can be in:<Cite src="eip-7732" />

- **Full** — the beacon block and the execution payload were both revealed and included.
- **Skipped** — no beacon block, and therefore no payload.
- **Empty** — the beacon block was included; the committed payload was not.

The third one is new. Between the Merge and this fork, a valid beacon block implied an execution
payload, one for one, because the payload was inside it. After this fork a beacon block is valid
whether or not the thing it committed to ever showed up.

Beacon state records it explicitly:

```python
execution_payload_availability: Bitvector[SLOTS_PER_HISTORICAL_ROOT]
```

One bit per slot in the historical window.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" /> The chain
now carries, as first-class state, a record of which of its own slots have execution content.

Fork choice grows a matching notion. A node in the fork-choice tree carries a `payload_status` that is
one of `PAYLOAD_STATUS_EMPTY`, `PAYLOAD_STATUS_FULL` or
`PAYLOAD_STATUS_PENDING`.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-fork-choice" /> The same beacon block can be
tracked in two states — with its payload and without — and the head-selection rules choose between
them. Fork choice is no longer choosing among blocks; it is choosing among (block, payload-status)
pairs.

### Ordinary attesters vote on this too

The PTC is not the only source of payload-presence information. The `index` field of
`AttestationData`, unused since Electra, is repurposed: an attester sets `index = 0` when attesting to
the current block or to a past block whose payload is absent, and `index = 1` for a past block whose
payload is present.<Cite src="eip-7732" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-validator" /> Fork choice
reads that to decide which node a message supports.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-fork-choice" />

Two mechanisms, two jobs. The PTC gives a fast, same-slot signal used for timeliness and payment. The
ordinary attesters give the durable fork-choice weight for whether a past slot was full or empty. If
you have wondered why an EIP that "adds one committee" touches the attestation container as well, that
is why.

<Check type="explain" concept="payload-availability-bit">
You maintain a service that gives users a canonical block number for a given beacon slot — bridges,
receipt lookups and a block explorer all depend on it. Today the mapping is total: every slot with a
block has exactly one execution block. After this fork, describe what your schema and your API have
to change to, and name the failure you would ship if you changed only the parsing code.

<Answer>
**The mapping stops being total, and that is a schema change, not a parsing change.**

The relation between beacon slot and execution block becomes partial: a slot may be full, skipped, or
empty, and only full slots have an execution block.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> A column that was
`NOT NULL` is now nullable, and an API that returns a block number for a slot now needs a third
response distinct from both "here it is" and "no such slot".

Concretely, three things change:

1. **The return type.** "Slot exists, has no execution block" is a legitimate success, not an error
   and not a 404. Collapsing it into either is how downstream consumers get corrupted.
2. **The source of truth for presence.** You can read it from beacon state's
   `execution_payload_availability` bitvector rather than inferring it from whether a fetch
   succeeded — inference cannot distinguish "no payload exists" from "I could not get
   it".<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />
3. **The timing.** Payload presence is not decided at block arrival. Fork choice tracks the block as
   `PAYLOAD_STATUS_PENDING` until the votes come in, so a service answering the instant the block
   lands is answering before the answer exists.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-fork-choice" />

**The failure you ship by changing only the parser** is the quiet one. Your ingest reads the new
container, finds no payload for an empty slot, treats it as a fetch failure, and retries — or worse,
skips the slot and advances the cursor. Nothing throws. The gap appears weeks later, as a bridge that
cannot prove a receipt, or a balance history missing a day.

The general form: a breaking change that turns an impossible state into a legal one does not
announce itself at the type boundary. It announces itself in reconciliation, much later.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

You can now state the committee's job in one sentence without getting it wrong: 512 balance-weighted
seats drawn from the slot's attesters, answering two booleans about arrival and blob availability,
with no execution validity in scope and no authority over a node that does not hold the
data.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-fork-choice" />

You can also state the new chain state precisely — full, skipped, empty — and say where it is
recorded.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

What is still missing is the clock the committee is checking against. Everything above happens at
deadlines: the payload has one, the payload attestation has another, and the ordinary attestation
deadline moved. The next lesson puts numbers on all of them, works out how much validation budget the
design actually buys, and explains why the honest answer to "how much wider is the window" is a range
rather than a figure.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-gloas" />
