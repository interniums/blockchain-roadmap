A builder has won a slot. Its bid is inside a beacon block that is propagating right now, it owes the
proposer four ether whatever happens next, and it has until the halfway mark of the slot to decide
whether the block it built gets executed.

It does not have to decide anything about the payment. That is settled. The only decision left is
whether the world sees the payload.

Written as a payoff, with `V` for the bid already owed and `R` for what the builder realises if its
block executes:

- **Reveal:** pay `V`, receive `R`.
- **Withhold:** pay `V`, receive nothing, and the slot has no execution payload.

Reveal beats withhold whenever `R > 0`. The bid is sunk in both branches, so it drops out of the
comparison entirely. What the builder holds, between signing and the reveal deadline, is the right —
not the obligation — to have this specific block executed. That is an option, and this lesson is about
how much it is worth, why it exists, and why it is unusually hard to price out of the design.

## Where the option came from

It is worth being clear that this is not an oversight. It is the direct consequence of the decision
that made the relay unnecessary.

Recall the mechanics of today's arrangement. The builder submits a complete block to the relay
*before* the proposer chooses. The relay holds it. When the proposer signs the header, the relay
publishes the body.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2023-04" /> The builder is not consulted at
that point and cannot retract. **The relay's custody of the payload is exactly what denies the builder
an option.**

Under EIP-7732 nobody but the builder holds the payload. The builder broadcasts the envelope itself,
on its own gossip topic, when it chooses to.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-builder" /> Removing the
escrow agent removed the proposer's exposure — payment no longer depends on delivery — and in the same
motion handed the builder a choice it previously did not have.

## How long is the option, exactly

Options are priced partly by time to expiry, so get the window right rather than assuming it.

The reveal deadline is `PAYLOAD_DUE_BPS`, 5000 basis points — half of a 12,000-millisecond slot, so
six seconds after the slot begins.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-gloas" /> If the bid was made
for the current slot, that is roughly six seconds of optionality.

But a bid does not have to be for the current slot. The honest builder guide requires `bid.slot` to be
either the current slot or the next one.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-builder" /> A builder that
bids during slot N for slot N+1 has committed a price at some point during slot N and does not have to
reveal until halfway through slot N+1 — which stretches the window toward eighteen seconds, depending
on when in the previous slot it bid.

So the honest description is: **the option runs from the moment the bid is signed to the payload
deadline of the slot it targets, which is at most a little under eighteen seconds and at least about
six.** Anyone quoting a single duration has picked one of the two cases without saying so.

## What makes it worth anything

Six to eighteen seconds sounds like nothing. Two things make it non-trivial.

**The underlying is volatile and the position is leveraged.** A builder's block is not a passive
bundle of fee-paying transactions. It typically contains the builder's own or its searchers' positions
— arbitrage legs, liquidation calls, trades whose profitability depends on prices that were true when
the block was assembled. A price move against those positions does not merely reduce `R`; it can drive
`R` below zero, because executing a losing arbitrage is worse than not executing it. The option is
not on a small expected gain. It is on the downside tail.

**The strike is zero and the premium is already paid.** In ordinary option pricing you weigh the
premium against the expected payoff. Here the "premium" is the bid, and the bid is owed in both
branches. The builder is not choosing whether to buy an option; it already has one, and it exercises
whenever exercising is better than not. That removes the usual dampener on marginal exercise.

The EIP's own assessment is measured and worth quoting in outline rather than paraphrasing loosely.
Economically rational but malicious builders — malicious as defined by the honest builder guide — may
choose to withhold when withholding is profitable; this could result in missed slots and degradation
of user experience; and **preliminary simulation data suggests the number of these occurrences may be
noticeable.**<Cite src="eip-7732" />

<Aside kind="warn">
"May be noticeable", from preliminary simulations, is the strength of the available evidence. It is
not a measured mainnet rate, because there is no mainnet to measure — the upgrade has not
shipped.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> Treat anyone quoting a percentage of slots that will be empty under
ePBS as having invented it or having taken it from a simulation whose assumptions they should be
naming. The right form of the claim is directional: the incentive exists, and the people who modelled
it did not conclude it was negligible.
</Aside>

### A worked break-even, with the numbers labelled as illustrative

The arithmetic is small enough to do by hand, and doing it once fixes which quantities matter. Every
figure below is invented for the example; nothing here is a measurement.

**Subgoal: separate the parts of the block's value that can go negative from the parts that cannot.**
Say a builder's block is worth 5 ETH at assembly time, split into 1 ETH of priority fees from ordinary
transactions and 4 ETH of expected profit from two arbitrage legs it holds itself. Priority fees are
paid regardless of price movement: that part of `R` has a floor at zero. The arbitrage legs do not:
if the price moves through them, executing loses money.

**Subgoal: write the decision rule in terms of the movable part.** The builder reveals iff
`R > 0`, and `R = 1 + A` where `A` is the realised value of the arbitrage at reveal time. So the
builder withholds iff `A < −1`. The bid of, say, 4.5 ETH does not appear anywhere in that inequality,
because it is owed either way.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

**Subgoal: ask how far the underlying must move.** With 4 ETH of expected arbitrage profit, `A` has to
fall by 5 ETH — from +4 to below −1 — inside the reveal window for withholding to win. That is a large
move in six seconds and it will rarely happen. Now change the block: a builder whose block is 0.1 ETH
of fees and a single thin, highly leveraged position needs a far smaller move.

**Subgoal: state what the exercise rate depends on.** Not on the bid. Not on the slot's average value.
It depends on the *distribution of blocks whose realised value can cross zero within the window*, and
on how volatile the underlying is during that window. Both are empirical questions about which the
available evidence is a preliminary simulation.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

That last point is the reason to do the arithmetic at all. It tells you which measurement would
actually settle the argument — the fraction of blocks with negative-crossing realised value over a
six-to-eighteen second horizon — and it tells you that a debate conducted in terms of average block
value is measuring the wrong thing.<Cite src="ethresearch-epbs-threads" />

## Why it is hard to punish

The obvious fix is a penalty: charge a builder that fails to get its payload into the canonical chain.
The EIP records exactly that proposal — mitigations in the form of variable penalties for builders
that fail to include their payload envelopes in the canonical
chain.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> At the tag read for this lesson, no such penalty exists in the merged
state transition; there is no slashing condition and no balance decrease attached to a missing
payload.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

The difficulty is not implementation. It is that **withholding is sometimes correct**, and the
protocol cannot tell the two cases apart from the outside.

The honest builder guide says so explicitly: an honest builder that has seen a beacon block
referencing its bid, but where that block was not timely and is therefore not the head of the
builder's chain, **may choose to withhold** — it should act as if no block was
produced.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-builder" /> That is not a loophole; it is the correct
behaviour. Revealing a payload for a block that is not going to be canonical wastes bandwidth and can
leak the block's contents to a proposer who will not pay for them.

Now consider what a penalty would have to distinguish:

- Builder withheld because the block genuinely was not timely **in its own view**.
- Builder withheld because the block was timely but the payload had become unprofitable.

The difference is entirely inside the builder's local view of the network at a moment that has passed.
Nobody else can see it, and the builder's report of it is unfalsifiable. A penalty calibrated to catch
the second case will sometimes fire on the first, and the first is behaviour the specification
recommends.

This pairs with **builder withhold safety**, the fork-choice property that a builder is not charged
the bid's value when a beacon block carrying its commitment is withheld and revealed
late.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> That protection exists for good reasons — otherwise a malicious proposer
could sit on a block and bill a builder that never had a chance to deliver. It also means the
protocol has already committed to the position that "the block was not timely from where I stood" is
an acceptable defence.

## What withholding costs the builder anyway

The option is called free because there is no incremental fee for exercising it. That is not the same
as costless, and the honest analysis lists what a withholding builder actually gives up.

**The bid.** Already owed, so it does not enter the reveal-versus-withhold comparison — but it is
real money spent on a slot that produced nothing. A builder that withholds frequently is paying for
slots it does not use, and its bidding must price that in.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

**Its bidding capacity for the rest of the epoch.** Payments clear at epoch boundaries, and a
builder's available balance is reduced by everything pending. Burning a bid on a withheld slot
directly reduces what it can bid on subsequent slots in the same
epoch.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

**Whatever the block would have earned.** `R` is forgone. The builder only exercises when `R` is
negative or negligible, so this is small by construction — but the builder's estimate of `R` at
decision time can be wrong.

**Reputation with searchers and order flow.** Not a protocol cost, and not measurable here, but a
builder that regularly fails to land the blocks it wins is a builder searchers route around. This is
the same category of incentive that governed relay behaviour, which is to say: real, and not
something the state transition enforces.

<Answer>
It reintroduces the exact problem enshrinement was built to remove, and it does so on the proposer's
side.

**The proposer becomes exposed to the builder again.** Under conditional payment, a builder that
declines to reveal costs the proposer its entire slot revenue. The proposer signed first — that
ordering has not changed — and now receives second, conditionally, on an action the builder controls.
That is the pre-existing exposure that made escrow necessary, restored with no escrow agent present to
mitigate it.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

**It creates a griefing attack with no cost to the attacker.** A builder can now bid, win, and
withhold, denying a specific proposer its revenue for the price of the bandwidth it did not spend. The
current design at least charges the builder the bid for that
behaviour.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

**It does not even remove the option.** The builder still chooses whether to reveal; the choice's
payoff has changed from "pay `V`, receive nothing" to "pay nothing, receive nothing". The option got
*cheaper to exercise*, not harder — this proposal increases withholding rather than reducing it.

**And it collides with builder withhold safety.** A proposer could now withhold its own block,
reveal it late, and — under conditional payment with no compensating rule — leave the builder holding
a payload for a block nobody attested to. The current design handles this by not charging a builder
whose block was withheld and revealed late; conditional payment removes the thing being
charged and leaves the underlying manipulation intact.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

The general shape: unconditional payment is what removes the trusted third party, and any proposal
that reconditions the payment brings the third party back. The design space that remains is *penalties
for non-delivery layered on top of unconditional payment* — which is exactly what the EIP records as
the proposed mitigation, and exactly what runs into the problem that some non-delivery is
prescribed.<Cite src="eip-7732" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-builder" />
</Answer>
</Check>

**The proposer is unharmed.** It signed a bid and it is paid. Its slot produced no transactions, which
costs it nothing directly, since its revenue was the bid rather than the block's
fees.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

**The builder pays the bid** for a slot it did not use, having judged that the alternative was
worse.

**Users pay the visible cost.** Their transactions were in that payload and are not in the chain.
They wait a slot, in the ordinary case, and rejoin the next block's competition for inclusion. The
EIP names this directly: missed slots and degradation of user
experience.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

**Everyone pays a small chain-level cost.** A slot with no execution content is a slot with no
throughput. If exercise is rare, that is noise against the network's variance in block fullness. If
it is common, it is an effective reduction in capacity — which sits awkwardly beside the fact that
capacity is the reason the design exists at
all.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-gloas" />

Notice that the party bearing the cost is the party with no seat at the table. The proposer and the
builder are both compensated by the design's own logic; the user is not a participant in the trade and
absorbs the outcome. That asymmetry is worth naming when you evaluate whether the mitigation debate is
being conducted by the people with the most at stake.

## The honest summary

This is the cleanest example in the module of a pattern worth carrying beyond it: **a design removed
one trust assumption and created an economic one in its place.**

Before: the proposer trusted the relay to reveal, and the relay's custody denied the builder any
choice about revealing. After: nobody is trusted, the proposer is paid regardless, and the builder
holds a short-dated option on whether the slot has any content at
all.<Cite src="eip-7732" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-builder" />

That is a genuine improvement — an unquantified trust assumption traded for a quantifiable economic
one — and it is not free. Empty slots are a real user-facing cost, and the evidence on how many there
will be is a preliminary simulation rather than a measurement.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> The design
discussions treat it as open, and the mitigation space is still being
explored.<Cite src="ethresearch-epbs-threads" />

If you take one habit from this lesson: when a system claims to have removed a trusted party, ask what
the removed party's custody was *also* preventing. Escrow agents usually protect both sides, and
replacing one half of that protection is the normal way a new incentive
appears.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-fork-choice" />

The next lesson does the same interrogation at the level of the whole design, and reaches a
less comfortable answer about what enshrinement changes for the thing everyone assumes it fixes.
