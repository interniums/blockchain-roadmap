At 11:29pm UTC on 2 April 2023, a validator that had been registered seventeen days earlier
requested a block header from a relay for the slot it was about to propose.

It received one. The relay was holding a block built by a builder, and that block contained bundles
from several sandwich bots which had, hours earlier, taken the bait: a series of public-mempool swaps
with infinite slippage against deep pools, crafted to look like the most profitable sandwich targets
of the day.

The proposer then signed the header — after setting its state root and parent root to zero. Signed,
invalid, and returned to the relay.

The relay did what its code said. It verified that the proposer's signature over the header was
correct, attempted to publish the block, and released the body to the proposer. The publish failed:
the local beacon nodes rejected the block, because a block with a zeroed state root is not a block.
**The body was released anyway.**<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2023-04" />

The proposer now held every transaction in a block that would never exist, including the sandwich
bundles. It built its own block for the same slot, ordered so that the sandwiches came apart, and
proposed it. Roughly twenty million dollars moved.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2023-04" />

This lesson is about why that was possible, what class of thing the relay is, and which parts of the
failure are bugs that got patched versus properties that are still there.

## The escrow is a procedure, not a cryptographic guarantee

You already know [the shape](/c/relay-escrow): the proposer sees a commitment, signs it, and the relay
releases the body. It is worth being exact about *why* an intermediary is needed at all, because the enshrined
design is best understood as removing the need rather than replacing the party.

Two parties want to trade. The builder has a block worth a lot; the proposer has the exclusive right
to publish a block this slot. Neither can go first:

- If the builder sends the block first, the proposer can publish the block's contents as its own
  work and pay nothing. Everything valuable in the block is in the transactions, and transactions
  are copyable.
- If the proposer commits first without seeing anything, it has no assurance the thing it committed
  to is even a valid block, and it can be made to miss its slot.

A relay resolves this the way escrow resolves it everywhere: a third party takes custody, checks the
goods, and releases them only against a binding commitment from the buyer.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-docs" />

The essential property to notice is what is doing the work. **Nothing here is enforced by
cryptography or by the state transition function.** The signature over the header is real, and it
binds the proposer to that header. But "release the body only after the signature exists" is a
branch in a Go program running on a server owned by a company. It is a procedure. The security of the
whole arrangement is the security of that procedure, plus the honesty of whoever operates it, plus
the correctness of its implementation.

The 2023 incident is what it looks like when the procedure has one missing conditional. The escrow
agent checked *that the buyer signed* and did not check *that what the buyer signed was usable*.

## Why the theft was uncontested, and what that says about fork choice

There is a second half to the 2023 mechanism that is more interesting than the missing check, and it
is the part that generalises.

Normally, a proposer that obtains a block body and then publishes a *different* block for the same
slot has done something visible and costly. It has equivocated — two signed blocks for one slot —
and, more practically, its replacement block has to race the relay's block for attestations. The
relay published first and has a head start; the equivocating proposer is behind and loses the
fork-choice race in the ordinary case.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2023-04" />

That defence evaporated here for a reason nobody had priced in. The relay *could not* publish,
because the header the proposer signed was invalid. So there was no competing block. The attacker's
block was the only candidate for the slot and won by walkover.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2023-04" />

Sit with the structure of that. The safety property everyone relied on — "stealing the body doesn't
help you, because you'd have to beat the relay's block" — was conditional on the relay's block
existing, and the attacker controlled whether it existed. A defence that depends on your adversary
performing an action is not a defence.

<Aside kind="warn">
This is the reasoning pattern worth carrying out of the module, more than any specific container
name. When you read a claimed guarantee in a system like this, ask which party's action the
guarantee depends on. "The honest builder's payload will be canonical" and "the honest proposer will
be paid" are the two properties EIP-7732 claims to enforce, and the reason it is worth reading the
fork-choice rules carefully is exactly that each claim needs to survive an adversary who declines to
act.<Cite src="eip-7732" />
</Aside>

## The fix, and the second exposure it revealed

The patch was direct: relays were changed to require a successful publish before releasing the body,
so a signed-but-invalid header no longer buys you anything.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2023-04" />

Following up, the same teams found a related problem that had nothing to do with invalid headers. A
proposer could simply ask for the block *late* — far enough into its slot that the block could not
land — and the relay would hand over the body regardless. A proposer holding two consecutive slots
could use the contents of the first to build the second. Even a proposer with a single slot could
grief a builder into revealing its block for nothing, by deliberately missing the
slot.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2023-04" />

The mitigation was a cutoff: after some number of seconds into the proposer's slot, the relay stops
returning bodies. Relays deployed a two-second cutoff on the evening of 3 April 2023. Then honest
validators started missing slots, and the cutoff was raised to three seconds the following
morning.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2023-04" />

**Read that sequence carefully, because it is a small, complete example of the thing this module is
about.** A trust assumption was tightened. The tightening imposed a real cost on honest participants
— a validator whose networking is a little slow now loses its slot instead of merely losing its
bid — and the parameter had to be relaxed until the honest cost was tolerable. There was no setting
that removed the risk without also removing something from someone honest. The writeup says as much
in its own words: further investigation was still needed on whether the mitigation was worth its
cost.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2023-04" />

Hold that thought until the free-option lesson, where enshrined PBS faces a structurally identical
question and reaches for a structurally similar answer.

### The party that lost the money was not in the escrow

One detail of the 2023 incident deserves separating out, because it changes what the incident is
evidence *of*.

The escrow arrangement has two counterparties: the proposer and the builder. Neither of them lost
money. The proposer was the attacker. The builder — `builder0x69` in the writeup — had its block
leaked, which is a real harm, but the funds that moved belonged to somebody else
entirely.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2023-04" />

The money belonged to the sandwich bots, whose bundles were inside that block. They had been baited
into it deliberately: from 16 March 2023 the attacker registered a validator and waited for a
proposal slot, and shortly before it arrived, sent public-mempool swaps with infinite slippage
against pools with substantial liquidity — trades that look to a searcher like the most attractive
sandwich targets available, and that are unprofitable to sandwich if the sandwich comes
apart.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2023-04" />

So the sequence of harm ran: searcher submits bundle to builder, builder submits block to relay,
relay leaks block to proposer, proposer disassembles the bundle. The searcher had no relationship
with the relay and no way to evaluate its code. It was exposed to the correctness of an escrow it was
never a party to, two hops upstream.

That is the general property, and it survives every patch that has been applied. **In a pipeline of
voluntary bilateral arrangements, the risk each party carries is the union of everyone's
implementation quality, not their own counterparty's.** Enshrining the proposer-builder exchange
changes the terms of one hop in that chain. It does not, by itself, give a searcher any protocol
guarantee about what happens to a bundle it sends to a builder — a limitation worth remembering when
the module reaches what enshrinement does not fix.

## Three failure modes, stated at the right strength

The 2023 incident is the vivid one. It is not the whole argument, and overstating it is a way to get
the argument wrong. Name the modes separately and say precisely how strong each claim is.

### Correlated implementation failure

Relay implementations are few, shared and open source. The 2023 vulnerability existed in the
majority of relays — the `mev-boost-relay` implementation and `Dreamboat` are both named in the
writeup — which means one defect had market-wide reach.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2023-04" />

The November 2022 incident is the cleaner demonstration of the same property. A builder submitted
bids carrying an incorrect `timestamp` and `prev_randao`; beacon nodes rejected the resulting bids;
proposers across the network fell back to building locally, and MEV-Boost block share visibly dropped
for hours.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2022-11" /> No stake was lost and no slots were missed,
which is exactly the point: the failure was to the *market*, and the market is where nine in ten
blocks live.

**Strength of claim: strong, and evidenced twice.** A small number of shared implementations produces
correlated failure. That is a structural property, not an accusation about any operator.

<Aside kind="note">
Both incidents were survivable for one reason: local fallback worked. A proposer that could not get
a usable bid built its own block and proposed it on time. If you operate validators, that is the
capability worth verifying rather than assuming — an execution client kept in sync and capable of
producing a payload on demand, not merely kept running to serve the consensus client's engine calls.
The distinction only becomes visible on the day the market is unavailable, which is the worst day to
discover it.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2022-11" />
</Aside>

### Censorship chokepoint

A relay sees complete blocks before publication and decides whether to publish them. That makes it
the cheapest place in the entire pipeline to apply a rule about which transactions may appear. Any
filtering obligation that lands anywhere in this supply chain lands most naturally there.

**Strength of claim: strong on structure, weak on any particular number.** The structural argument is
solid and does not depend on measurement. The measurements — what fraction of blocks came from
relays applying a filter, in which month — move constantly and published figures disagree. If you
need a number, take it from a live dashboard over a stated window and quote the window with
it.<Cite src="relayscan-io" /><Cite src="mevboost-pics" />

### Compellability and discretion

A relay is an operating company. It can decide who may connect, it can blacklist a builder, and it
can be subject to legal process. The November 2022 writeup records a relay briefly blacklisting a
specific builder address while a fix was prepared, and unblocking it
afterwards.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2022-11" /> That was a defensive act during an active
incident and it was disclosed. It is also a demonstration that the capability is routine and
unilateral.

**Strength of claim: definitional rather than empirical.** The relay is a trusted third party in the
strict sense — a party whose misbehaviour is possible and whose good behaviour is assumed. Whether it
misbehaves is a separate question from whether it can.

## What the relay cannot do

Refusing to overclaim is part of getting this right. Several things a trusted third party might
plausibly be able to do, this one cannot.

**It cannot make an invalid block valid.** Everything the relay publishes is validated by every node
on the network in the usual way. The relay's discretion is over *which* valid blocks get published,
never over what counts as valid.

**It cannot take the proposer's stake.** There is no path from relay misbehaviour to a slashing
condition being met by an honest proposer. The proposer's downside is a lost bid, a lost slot's
rewards, or in the worst historical case a missed slot — not its principal.

**It cannot force a proposer to use it.** Local building is always available and clients implement
it as the fallback. The pressure to use a relay is economic.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-docs" />

**It cannot rewrite history.** Everything about this market operates on the block currently being
produced. Finality, and everything you know about it, is untouched.

**It cannot serve a block that misses the attestation deadline and expect it to stick.** The relay is
racing the same clock as everyone else: a block that arrives after most attesters have voted loses
the fork-choice race regardless of who published it, and the mainnet attestation deadline is a fixed
fraction of the slot, not a negotiable one.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-gloas" /> This is why
the late-request attack worked at all — it exploited the relay's willingness to *reveal* past the
point where publishing was still useful, not any ability to bend the deadline.

The accurate summary is narrow and still serious: the relay's power is over the *contents of the
next block and who learns them when*, plus the ability to stop participating. That is a small
surface, exercised roughly nine times in ten, at a chokepoint of two or three
organisations.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-docs" />

<Check type="explain" concept="relay-trust-assumption">
A proposal circulates: keep relays exactly as they are, but require every relay to publish a signed
attestation, after each slot, stating the header it served and the body it released. Any relay that
released a body for a block it did not publish would be provably caught. Does this remove the trust
assumption? Say what it fixes and what it does not.

<Answer>
It converts an unaccountable failure into an accountable one, and it does not remove the trust
assumption.

What it fixes: detection. After the fact, anyone could show that a relay released a body for a block
that never landed. In 2023 the detection problem was not the binding constraint — the attack was
reconstructed publicly within hours — but in the general case, evidence is worth
something.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2023-04" />

What it does not fix, in order of severity:

1. **The money has already moved.** The attestation is published after the slot. The bundles were
   already leaked and the block was already proposed. Detection is not prevention, and there is no
   mechanism here that returns anything.
2. **The relay still signs its own homework.** The attestation is a claim by the party under
   scrutiny. Catching a *dishonest* attestation requires an independent record of what the relay
   actually did, which is the thing you do not have.
3. **Censorship is untouched.** A relay that declines to serve certain blocks is not misbehaving
   under this scheme; it is exercising the discretion the scheme leaves it. You would be attesting
   faithfully to a filtered market.
4. **Correlated implementation failure is untouched.** The 2022 fallback event was a bug, not
   dishonesty. Attesting to it accurately would not have prevented it.

The general lesson: adding accountability to a trusted party changes the *cost* of misbehaviour, not
the *possibility*. Removing the trusted party changes the possibility. That is the difference between
a better relay and an enshrined design, and it is why the next lesson is about moving the exchange
into the state transition function rather than about improving the escrow
agent.<Cite src="eip-7732" />
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this leaves the design question

Two production incidents, one of them costing roughly twenty million dollars, both patched within
hours by the people who maintain the software. That last clause is the ambiguous part: a market that
can patch a live exploit in half a day is impressively responsive, and a system whose most
consequential component can be changed in half a day by a handful of people is not
trust-minimised.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2023-04" />

The argument for enshrinement is not that relay operators behaved badly. On the evidence they mostly
behaved well and quickly. It is that the property everyone wants — a fair exchange between proposer
and builder where neither can cheat the other — is the kind of property a consensus protocol knows
how to provide, and it is currently being provided by a service instead.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> The
design discussions that led to EIP-7732 return to that framing repeatedly: the goal is to make the
exchange safe by construction rather than by
operation.<Cite src="ethresearch-epbs-threads" /><Cite src="eip-7732" />

One more framing before we open the specification, because it decides how you read everything that
follows. The escrow exists to solve a *fair exchange* problem: two parties, neither able to go first.
The classical result is that fair exchange between two mutually distrusting parties is not achievable
without either a trusted third party or something that plays the role of one. A blockchain's state
transition function is unusually well suited to playing that role, because it is a thing all parties
already agree to be bound by. So the enshrined design does not invent a cleverer escrow. It notices
that the participants are already inside a system with a referee and moves the trade in front of
it.<Cite src="eip-7732" />
