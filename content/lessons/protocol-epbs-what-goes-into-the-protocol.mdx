Everything in this lesson is unshipped. Before a single container, fix the status precisely, because
the accuracy of every claim below depends on holding it.

EIP-7732 is a headline item of the Glamsterdam upgrade, which is the pairing of the consensus-layer
fork **Gloas** with the execution-layer fork **Amsterdam**.<Cite src="etherworld-epbs-headliner" /> In
the consensus specification, at the tag this lesson was read from, the fork's activation epoch is
`GLOAS_FORK_EPOCH: 18446744073709551615` — the far-future sentinel, meaning
unscheduled.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-gloas" /> In the upgrade's own meta EIP, the
activation table has three empty rows for Sepolia, Holešky and Mainnet, under a note saying they
will be filled in as activation times are decided by client teams.<Cite src="eip-7773" />

That is the whole status, from the two documents that would carry a date if one existed. Multi-client
devnets have run the slate and the upgrade is targeted for the second half of 2026; secondary
coverage circulating in 2026 offers specific mainnet dates that contradict each other, and none of
them appears in either primary artifact.<Cite src="ethereum-pm" /> **Print no date.** If you need
one, the place to look is the All Core Devs record, not an article.<Cite src="ethereum-pm" />

With that fixed: what actually changes.

## The one-sentence version, and why it is not enough

The beacon block stops carrying the execution payload and starts carrying a signed commitment to
one. The builder reveals the payload separately, later in the same slot.

Everything else — the committee, the availability bitvector, the four-phase slot, the free option —
falls out of that single structural move. But the one-sentence version hides the part that matters
to anyone who has written software against a beacon block, so we are going to read the containers.

## What leaves the beacon block

Here is the modified `BeaconBlockBody` as it stands in the merged Gloas specification, trimmed to
the lines that changed:<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

```python
class BeaconBlockBody(ProgressiveContainer(active_fields=[1] * 13)):
    randao_reveal: BLSSignature
    eth1_data: Eth1Data
    graffiti: Bytes32
    proposer_slashings: ProposerSlashings
    attester_slashings: AttesterSlashings
    attestations: Attestations
    deposits: Deposits
    voluntary_exits: VoluntaryExits
    sync_aggregate: SyncAggregate
    # [Modified in Gloas:EIP7732]
    # Removed `execution_payload`
    bls_to_execution_changes: BLSToExecutionChanges
    # [Modified in Gloas:EIP7732]
    # Removed `blob_kzg_commitments`
    # [Modified in Gloas:EIP7732]
    # Removed `execution_requests`
    # [New in Gloas:EIP7732]
    signed_execution_payload_bid: SignedExecutionPayloadBid
    # [New in Gloas:EIP7732]
    payload_attestations: PayloadAttestations
```

Three fields leave. Two arrive. The one that matters most is the first removal, and it is worth
stating in the bluntest available terms: **after this fork, a beacon block does not contain
transactions.**

Every tool that reaches into a beacon block for its execution content breaks. Explorers. Indexers.
Anything that walks the chain to build a database of transfers. MEV tooling that reconstructs what a
proposer published. Bridges and light clients that follow the beacon chain and expect an execution
payload header inside the block they are verifying. None of these are edge cases; this is the normal
path through the object.

<Aside kind="note">
Notice `ProgressiveContainer(active_fields=[1] * 13)` in the class definition. That is not part of
EIP-7732 — it is EIP-7688, forward-compatible consensus data structures, which is scheduled for the
same upgrade and rewrites how these containers are typed so that later field additions do not
invalidate existing Merkle proofs.<Cite src="eip-7773" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />
Two independent changes are landing on the same container in the same fork. If you are reading a diff
and wondering why fields you did not expect to change are annotated, that is why: the annotations
name which EIP touched each line, and there is more than one.
</Aside>

### What arrives in its place

```python
class ExecutionPayloadBid(ProgressiveContainer(active_fields=[1] * 12)):
    parent_block_hash: Hash32
    parent_block_root: Root
    block_hash: Hash32
    prev_randao: Bytes32
    fee_recipient: ExecutionAddress
    gas_limit: Uint64
    builder_index: BuilderIndex
    slot: Slot
    value: Gwei
    execution_payment: Gwei
    blob_kzg_commitments: BlobKZGCommitments
    execution_requests_root: Root
```

Wrapped in a signature, this is the [SSZ container](/c/ssz) `SignedExecutionPayloadBid` that now sits
in the beacon block.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" /> Read it field by field and the design
argument becomes legible.

**`block_hash` is the commitment.** One 32-byte value pins exactly which execution block the builder
has promised. The proposer is not committing to a set of transactions it has seen; it is committing
to a hash it has not opened. That is the same epistemic position as signing a blinded block today —
which is the point. The proposer's job did not get harder, it got specified.

**`builder_index` names the counterparty.** Not an address, not a public key: an index into a
registry in beacon state. The protocol knows who this is. That single field is the difference between
a trade and a market convention.

**`value` is the price, in gwei.** This is what the builder will pay the proposer. The next lesson is
about how that payment is enforced.

**`fee_recipient` is where the payment lands**, chosen by the proposer — the builder is instructed to
take it from the proposer's published preferences for that slot.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-builder" />

**`gas_limit` is negotiated in the bid.** The builder must satisfy a compatibility check against the
parent's gas limit and the proposer's stated target, so the proposer's gas-limit preference survives
the outsourcing rather than being whatever the builder felt like.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-builder" />

**`execution_payment` is the escape hatch, and it is the field most people miss.** Set to zero, the
payment is the trustless in-protocol one. Set to non-zero, it signals a *trusted* execution-layer
payment — a promise to be fulfilled elsewhere. The honest builder guide requires it to be zero for
any bid broadcast on the public gossip topic.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-builder" /> The EIP is
explicit that the protocol accommodates trusted payment arrangements alongside the trustless
one.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> Enshrinement does not outlaw private arrangements; it provides a default
that does not need them.

**And `blob_kzg_commitments` stayed here.** Which brings us to the thing worth learning more than any
individual field.

## The specification disagrees with itself, and you should be able to catch it

Directly above the modified `BeaconBlockBody` in the merged specification is this note:

> The removed fields (`execution_payload`, `blob_kzg_commitments`, and `execution_requests`) now
> exist in `ExecutionPayloadEnvelope`.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

Here is `ExecutionPayloadEnvelope`, from the same file:<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

```python
class ExecutionPayloadEnvelope(ProgressiveContainer(active_fields=[1] * 5)):
    payload: ExecutionPayload
    execution_requests: ExecutionRequests
    builder_index: BuilderIndex
    beacon_block_root: Root
    parent_beacon_block_root: Root
```

`execution_payload`: present, as `payload`. `execution_requests`: present. `blob_kzg_commitments`:
**not there**. It is in the bid, in the beacon block, which is where the container listing three
screens earlier plainly puts it.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

The note is stale relative to the containers. This is neither scandal nor sloppiness — it is what an
in-development specification looks like while a design is still moving, and the containers are
normative where the prose note is not. But it has a direct consequence for you: **a summary of this
EIP written by reading its prose will tell you blob commitments moved to the envelope, and a client
written by reading the containers will put them in the bid.** Every secondary article about EIP-7732,
including ones written carefully, inherits whichever of the two the author happened to read.

The design reason the commitments belong in the bid is worth working out, because it also explains
the envelope's shape. Data availability for blobs is checked by the payload timeliness committee at
reveal time, and the committee needs to know *which* commitments to check against — from the block,
which arrived first. The gossip validation rules for the payload envelope reach into
`block.body.signed_execution_payload_bid.message.blob_kzg_commitments` for exactly that
purpose.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-p2p" /> Commitments in the envelope would be a builder
attesting to its own homework.

<Aside kind="warn">
The same drift runs between the EIP text and the merged specification. The EIP's version of
`ExecutionPayloadEnvelope` carries `slot` and `state_root` fields; the merged version carries
`parent_beacon_block_root` and neither of those.<Cite src="eip-7732" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />
The EIP is the proposal; [`consensus-specs`](/c/consensus-specs-repo) is the thing clients implement. When they disagree, the
specification wins — and the specification moves, which is why every consensus-specs link in this
module is pinned to a release tag rather than to a branch. Paths under `dev` are rewritten between
one reader and the next.<Cite src="consensus-specs" />
</Aside>

### How the bid gets validated, and what is deliberately not checked

`process_execution_payload_bid` is the state-transition function that runs when a beacon block
arrives. Reading its assertions tells you what the protocol considers its business:

```python
# Verify that the builder is active
assert is_active_builder(state, builder_index)
# Verify that the builder is a payload builder
assert state.builders[builder_index].version == PAYLOAD_BUILDER_VERSION
# Verify that the builder has funds to cover the bid
assert can_builder_cover_bid(state, builder_index, amount)
# Verify that the bid signature is valid
assert verify_execution_payload_bid_signature(state, signed_bid)
...
# Verify that the bid is for the current slot
assert bid.slot == state.slot
# Verify that the bid is for the right parent block
assert bid.parent_block_hash == state.latest_block_hash
assert bid.parent_block_root == get_block_root_at_slot(state, Slot(state.slot - 1))
assert bid.prev_randao == get_randao_mix(state, get_current_epoch(state))
```

That is the whole shape: the builder exists, is the right kind of builder, can pay, signed this, and
aimed the bid at exactly this point in the chain.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />
There is also a bound on the number of blob commitments, checked against the blob parameters in force
for the current epoch — which is how the bid stays inside whatever blob capacity the network has
reached.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

Now notice what is absent. Nothing here executes anything. Nothing checks that the promised payload is
valid, or even that it exists. The proposer's block is processed, and accepted, on the strength of a
hash and a solvency check. **Execution validity is not this function's problem, and deferring it is
the point of the entire design** — the lesson on the four-phase slot works out what that buys.

## Two objects per slot

The structural change with the longest blast radius is not any field. It is arity.

Today, one slot produces one object that a node must receive: a `SignedBeaconBlock` containing
everything. After this change, a slot produces two objects that must be received, from two different
senders, on two different gossip topics, at two different deadlines:

Those bottom three are new global topics as well, and the first two are the ones on the critical
path.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-p2p" />

The relationship between the beacon block and the payload is now **commitment**, not
**containment**. A beacon block references a payload by hash the way a transaction references a
contract by address: the reference is valid whether or not the referent turns up.

Every consequence in the rest of this module descends from that sentence:

- Something has to vote on whether the payload turned up — that is the committee, two lessons from
  here.
- Beacon state has to record slots where it did not — that is the availability bitvector.
- Fork choice has to have an opinion about a block whose payload is missing — the specification gives
  fork-choice nodes a payload status, so the same block can be tracked as full or
  empty.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-fork-choice" />
- And the builder gets to decide, after committing, whether to reveal — the free option.

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="payload-bid">
An indexer runs against a beacon node. Its ingest loop, simplified, is:

```python
block = beacon_api.get_block(slot)
payload = block["body"]["execution_payload"]
for tx in payload["transactions"]:
    index(tx, block_number=payload["block_number"], slot=slot)
```

The team plans to handle the fork by changing the payload lookup to read the envelope instead, and
otherwise leaving the loop alone. Name every assumption in these four lines that the fork breaks —
not just the field access.

<Answer>
There are four, and only the first is the one the team spotted.

**1. The field is gone.** `body.execution_payload` does not exist after the fork; the body carries
`signed_execution_payload_bid` instead.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" /> This is the
crash, and it is the safe failure because it is loud.

**2. The payload is a separate network object, so the loop's timing is wrong.** The envelope arrives
later in the same slot on its own gossip topic and is fetched by its own route, not as a sub-object
of the block.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-p2p" /> An ingest loop that fetches a block and expects
its payload in the same response now has to wait, and has to decide how long to wait.

**3. The payload may never arrive at all.** This is the assumption that produces silent corruption
rather than a crash. A slot can end with a valid beacon block and no payload. Code shaped as "fetch
block, then fetch payload, then index" will, on those slots, either hang, retry forever, or — worst —
skip the slot silently and leave a gap nobody notices. The correct shape has an explicit third
outcome: block present, payload absent, legitimately.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

**4. Blob commitments are not where the migration guide said.** If the indexer also tracks blob
commitments and follows the specification's own prose note, it will look for them in the envelope and
find nothing. They are in the bid, inside the beacon
block.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

The general lesson: the breaking change that costs you a night is the one that raises an exception.
The breaking change that costs you a quarter is the one where a formerly impossible state became
legal and your code has no branch for it.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What the builder produces, in order

To close the loop on the containers, follow one builder through one slot as the honest builder guide
describes it.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-builder" />

**Subgoal: build a payload and pin it.** The builder asks its execution engine for a payload via
`engine_getPayloadV6`, then sets `bid.block_hash` to that payload's block hash. The commitment is now
fixed; nothing about the payload can change without invalidating the
bid.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-builder" />

**Subgoal: bind the bid to one point in history.** `parent_block_hash` is the payload's parent hash,
`parent_block_root` is the head of the consensus chain, and the two must be derived from the same
state. `prev_randao` must equal the randao mix of the parent's post-state, and `slot` must be the
current or the next slot.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-builder" /> A bid is not a general offer; it
is an offer valid at one place in the chain.

**Subgoal: honour the proposer's stated preferences.** The `fee_recipient` and the target gas limit
come from the `SignedProposerPreferences` message the proposer broadcast for that
slot.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-builder" /> That is the new gossip topic doing real work: the
proposer publishes what it wants in advance, and builders bid against it.

**Subgoal: state the price and prove you can pay it.** `value` is the promised payment, and the
builder must hold enough excess balance to cover this bid *and* every pending payment it already
owes.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-builder" /> Broadcast on the `execution_payload_bid` topic.

**Subgoal: if you win, honour the commitment.** Having seen a beacon block containing its bid, the
builder assembles the envelope — the payload with the same block hash, the execution requests, its
own index, and the block roots — signs it under `DOMAIN_BEACON_BUILDER`, and broadcasts it on the
`execution_payload` topic.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-builder" />

And then the sentence that the free-option lesson is built on, from the guide itself: an honest
builder that has seen a block referencing its bid, but where that block was not timely and is not the
head of its chain, **may choose to withhold the payload** — acting as if no block was
produced.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-builder" /> Withholding is not always misbehaviour. There
is a legitimate reason to do it, which is precisely what makes the illegitimate reason hard to
punish.

### Self-building is expressed inside the same machinery

A proposer that wants to build its own block does not take a different code path in the state
transition. It sets `builder_index` to `BUILDER_INDEX_SELF_BUILD`, which is `UINT64_MAX`. The state
transition then asserts two things: the bid's `value` must be zero, and the signature must be the
G2 point at infinity — the identity element, the canonical "no signature
here".<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

That is a small piece of design worth admiring. Local building becomes a special case of the bid
mechanism rather than a parallel path, so there is exactly one place in the specification where a
slot's execution content is committed to, and it is the same place whether the proposer sold the slot
or kept it.

## The design you may have read is not this design

EIP-7732 was created in June 2024 and has moved materially since.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> Two of the
changes invalidate whole paragraphs of material written in 2024 and 2025, and both are things a
confident reader will state wrongly:

**The container was renamed and re-scoped.** Early discussion describes a
`SignedExecutionPayloadHeader` — the proposer commits to a payload *header*. The merged design has
`SignedExecutionPayloadBid`, and the EIP is explicit that the old `ExecutionPayloadHeader` object was
changed and renamed to track only the minimum needed to commit to a builder's
payload.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> If your mental model has the proposer signing a header, update it: it
signs a bid, which is a smaller and differently shaped thing.

**The payment moved layers.** Earlier framings had the builder paying from an execution-layer account,
with the payment checkable as the block's first transaction. The current design has builders staked on
the beacon chain with a consensus-layer balance, and the EIP's rationale section walks through why:
both approaches need the funds provably available before the payload is revealed, and staking is how
you get that on the consensus layer without adding execution-layer consensus
logic.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> An article that says the builder pays with a transaction is describing
a design that lost.

There is also a variant still discussed rather than decided. Remove the `block_hash` commitment from
the bid and you get a **slot auction** instead of a block auction: the builder buys the slot and may
put any payload in it. The EIP lists the advantages — a transaction submitted in the first half of a
slot could make the next block, builders get a longer continuous window, and inclusion-list designs
compose more easily — and notes that a preliminary analysis suggests payload equivocation would not
weaken the finality gadget.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> It is written up as a compatible design, not as the
specification, and the argument between block auctions and slot auctions has run through the research
threads for years.<Cite src="ethresearch-epbs-threads" /> Treat anything you read on it as live
debate rather than settled fact.

## What this unlocks

You can now read the EIP and, more usefully, the merged specification, without being misled by either
one. Specifically you can:

- Say exactly which three fields leave `BeaconBlockBody` and which two
  arrive.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />
- Explain why blob commitments live in the bid and not the envelope, and recognise the stale note that
  says otherwise.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-p2p" />
- Describe the slot as producing two required network objects on two topics rather than
  one.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-p2p" />
- Distinguish the commitment relationship from the containment relationship, which is the sentence
  every remaining lesson in this module expands.

What you cannot yet say is how the money moves. The bid states a value; nothing so far explains what
makes the builder pay it, or what stops a builder from bidding a thousand ether it does not have. That
is the registry, and it is next.<Cite src="eip-7732" />
