Here is a claim that sounds obviously true and is false: enshrining proposer-builder separation
decentralises block building.

It is worth understanding why the claim is tempting. "Enshrined" sounds like the protocol has taken
the market in hand. The market's most cited problem is that a couple of firms build most blocks. The
change is named after the market. Put those together and the conclusion writes itself.

But nothing in EIP-7732 touches the reason building is concentrated. It changes **who holds the
payload during the trade**, and that is a different thing entirely.

## Why building concentrates, and whether the design addresses any of it

[Block building concentrates](/c/builder-centralization) because it is an economies-of-scale business. The three inputs compound:

**Order flow.** A builder's block can only be as good as the transactions it can see. Exclusive
arrangements with wallets, applications and RPC providers give a builder transactions its competitors
do not have. That advantage is commercial, it is self-reinforcing — more flow makes better blocks
makes more wins makes more flow worth buying — and it lives entirely outside the consensus layer.

**Simulation capacity.** Evaluating orderings costs compute, and the builder that can evaluate more
orderings in the same seconds produces more valuable blocks.

**Latency.** Every millisecond closer to the deadline is another millisecond of order flow included.

Now ask, for each: does EIP-7732 change it? Order flow — no. The EIP defines how a bid is committed
and paid, and says nothing about where transactions come from.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> Simulation — no.
Latency — no, and there is a case, examined below, that it gets slightly harder.

## About that concentration figure

You will want a number here, and the honest answer is that you have to go and get one yourself.

Published 2026 measurements put two builders somewhere between roughly three quarters and four fifths
of all mainnet blocks — but the sources naming those figures name **different pairs of firms**, so
they cannot both be describing the same present. Neither number is quotable as fact. What is not in
dispute is the shape: the top two dominate, the top handful is close to everything, and the driver is
order flow rather than a technical moat.

The same problem afflicts private order flow. One 2026 source reports a high share of usage of private
RPC endpoints; another reports top builders filling more than half of *their blocks' gas* from private
flow while others sit below half; an earlier baseline measured a much smaller share of
*transactions*. Those are three different metrics — share of transactions, share of gas, share of
value — and averaging them produces a number that measures nothing.

**The move that works:** read it live over a stated window from
[relayscan.io](https://www.relayscan.io/) or [mevboost.pics](https://mevboost.pics/), record the
window, and say which metric you took.<Cite src="relayscan-io" /><Cite src="mevboost-pics" /> A figure
with a date and a definition beats a rounder figure without one, every time.

<Aside kind="warn">
This is not pedantry about sourcing. A curriculum that prints "the top two builders produce 73% of
blocks" will be wrong within months and will have taught a reader to quote a number rather than
measure one. The durable skill is knowing *which* dashboard answers *which* question, and that skill
survives every change in the underlying figure.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-docs" />
</Aside>

## What genuinely does change

Refusing to overclaim cuts both ways. Several real things improve, and flattening the lesson into
"nothing changes" would be its own error.

**The gatekeeper disappears.** Today a builder that no major relay will connect has no route to
proposers at all. Under the enshrined design a builder registers in the protocol and broadcasts bids
on a public gossip topic that every proposer's client already
consumes.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-builder" /> Permission to *participate* stops being
something an intermediary grants. Whether that changes market shares is a separate question — the
firms with the order flow still win — but the barrier to being in the market at all is now a one-ether
deposit rather than a relationship.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-builder" />

**A compellable party leaves the critical path.** Relays are operating companies that can be subject
to legal process and can unilaterally decline to serve. Removing them removes the cheapest place in
the pipeline to apply a filter — as an *organisation*.

**The escrow failure mode goes away.** The class of incident where a defect in relay software leaks a
block's contents or strands a proposer cannot recur in the same form, because no third party has
custody of the payload.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2023-04" /><Cite src="eip-7732" />

**Proposers get a guarantee where they had a hope.** Payment is enforced by the state transition
rather than by the correctness of somebody's Go program.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

Those are not small. They are also, all four, about the *relay*.

## Where the censorship pressure goes instead

Follow the filtering question through rather than declaring victory on it.

Today, a filter applied at the relay affects every proposer connected to that relay. Remove the relay
and the filtering decision does not vanish; it moves one hop upstream, to the builder. A builder that
declines to include certain transactions produces blocks without them, and if that builder wins most
slots, those transactions are excluded from most blocks.

So the honest statement is that enshrinement **relocates** the chokepoint rather than eliminating it,
and it relocates it to a layer that is, by every contested measurement, at least as concentrated as
the relay layer. There is one genuine improvement in the relocation — a builder cannot be compelled
through a party it does not control, and proposers now see bids directly and can select among
them.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-builder" /> There is no improvement at all in the underlying
structure: a small number of parties still decide what goes into most blocks.

### The remedy that was supposed to accompany this

The censorship answer was never meant to be ePBS. It was meant to be fork-choice-enforced inclusion
lists, EIP-7805, in which a committee of validators names transactions a block must include and fork
choice refuses blocks that ignore them.<Cite src="eip-7805" /> That mechanism does not care who builds
the block — which is exactly what makes it the right shape for this problem.

**It was declined for Glamsterdam.** The upgrade's meta EIP lists EIP-7805 under "Declined for
Inclusion".<Cite src="eip-7773" /> The reasoning recorded in the process is that shipping enshrined
PBS and inclusion lists together would put too much untested interaction on mainnet at
once.<Cite src="ethereum-pm" /> It is targeted at the following upgrade; on the consensus-layer side
the specification repository names that fork Heze, and the pairing with the execution-layer codename
circulating in community coverage is inferred from ordering rather than confirmed by a primary
source.<Cite src="ethereum-pm" />

So the first version of enshrined PBS ships without an inclusion-list mechanism. That is not a
criticism of the sequencing — the argument for shipping one large change at a time is a good one — but
it does mean the censorship-resistance story for this upgrade is "deferred", not "addressed".

## Four more things it does not do

Each of these is attributed to ePBS somewhere in circulation, and none of them is in scope.

**It does not remove MEV.** Extractable value exists because a block producer controls inclusion and
ordering over shared mutable state. Enshrinement changes who is trusted during the sale of that
control; the control itself is unchanged, and so is everything downstream of it.

**It gives searchers no protocol guarantee.** A searcher's bundle still goes to a builder under a
purely commercial arrangement. Nothing in the state transition says a builder must include a bundle
it accepted, must not disassemble it, or must not run the idea itself. The party that lost roughly
twenty million dollars in the 2023 incident is the party whose position is least changed by this
upgrade.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2023-04" />

**It does not make blocks cheaper or faster for users.** The capacity argument is that ePBS is a
*prerequisite* for larger blocks — it moves execution validation off the attestation critical path so
that raising limits is safe.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> Prerequisite is not delivery. The limits are
raised by separate changes, and the upgrade's meta EIP carries a set of gas-cost and limit proposals
that are their own decisions.<Cite src="eip-7773" />

**It does not reduce the number of roles.** Searchers, builders, proposers: still three, plus
validators attesting and now a payload timeliness committee. One party leaves — the relay — and one
new duty appears.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-validator" /> The pipeline did not get simpler; it
got a referee.

## What would a fix for building concentration even look like?

Worth asking, because the answer explains why nobody has shipped one.

The concentration comes from private information — order flow one builder has and another does not —
and from returns to infrastructure scale. A protocol-level fix would have to attack one of those.

**Attacking order flow** means making transaction visibility uniform, which is a rule about what
happens *before* a transaction reaches the protocol. Nothing in a state transition function reaches
that far: the protocol sees a block, not the negotiations that produced it. Proposals in this space
tend to be about encrypted mempools or fair-ordering guarantees, which are large, contested designs of
their own.

**Attacking returns to scale** means making a large builder's block no more valuable than a small
one's, which is close to saying the auction should not be won by the highest bidder. That undoes the
mechanism proposer-builder separation exists to provide.

**Or route around it** — which is what inclusion lists do. They do not make building less
concentrated. They constrain what a concentrated builder is permitted to
omit.<Cite src="eip-7805" /> That is a strictly weaker goal and a far more tractable one, which is
exactly why it is the mechanism that exists in specification form.

Notice the pattern: the tractable interventions all target *outcomes* of concentration rather than
concentration itself. That is worth internalising before evaluating any future proposal that claims to
decentralise building — the first question is which of the two inputs it attacks, and the usual honest
answer is neither.<Cite src="ethresearch-epbs-threads" />

## An argument that the bar for builders goes up

Worth flagging as reasoning rather than as a sourced finding, because it is an inference from the
specification rather than a claim the specification makes.

Two of the builder's new obligations are not trivial. First, the payload envelope commits to the
consensus-layer post-state root, which means a builder must run the consensus state transition, not
only the execution one.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> Second, proposers no longer broadcast
`DataColumnSidecar` objects — that becomes a builder
duty.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-validator" />

Both shift work from proposers to builders. For proposers, especially home stakers publishing
blob-carrying blocks, that is a clear benefit. For builders, it raises the fixed infrastructure cost
of participating: consensus-layer state management and a connection capable of pushing data columns
into the network on a deadline.

Fixed costs that are trivial for a large operator and non-trivial for a small one are the standard
mechanism by which markets concentrate. Whether this effect is large enough to matter against
advantages that are already dominant is genuinely unknown, and nobody has measured it because there is
nothing yet to measure.<Cite src="ethresearch-epbs-threads" /> Hold it as a hypothesis worth checking
after the fork, not as a prediction.

A colleague reads the roadmap and concludes: "Once ePBS ships, we should expect builder market share

<Answer>
The premise is wrong about which advantage was removed.

**What was removed is relay intermediation.** Custody of the payload during the trade, and the
gatekeeping that came with it.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> Incumbent builders were not incumbents because
relays favoured them.

**What was not removed is why they win.** Order flow, simulation capacity and latency. A builder with
exclusive flow bids higher because its block is worth more; the proposer's client picks the highest
bid; the builder wins. Every step of that survives the fork
untouched.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-builder" />

**One thing genuinely gets easier, and it is not the thing that matters.** A new builder can now reach
proposers without a relay accepting it — registration is a deposit rather than a
relationship.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-builder" /> That lowers the barrier to *entering*, and
entering was not the binding constraint. Being able to bid and being able to win are different
problems.

**And one thing may get slightly harder.** Builders now run the consensus state transition and publish
data column sidecars, both fixed costs that favour scale — an inference from the specification, not a
measured effect.<Cite src="eip-7732" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-validator" />

**What I would actually predict:** roughly unchanged concentration, with relay share disappearing as a
metric because relays no longer exist. Anyone who reports "decentralisation improved" by pointing at
the disappearance of relay concentration is measuring the removal of a layer, not the spreading of a
market.

**How to settle it:** fix the metric now — share of blocks by builder over a rolling window from a
named dashboard — record it before the fork, and compare after. If it moves, we learn something. If
we only start measuring afterwards, we will not be able to tell.<Cite src="relayscan-io" /><Cite src="mevboost-pics" />
</Answer>
</Check>

## The summary worth memorising

Three clauses, in this order, and none of them can be dropped without misleading someone:

**Relay trust removed.** The escrow agent is gone and its guarantees are now state-transition rules.

**Builder concentration unchanged.** The economics that produce it are untouched, and the measured
share is contested enough that you should go and read it rather than quote
it.<Cite src="relayscan-io" />

**Censorship remedy deferred.** Fork-choice-enforced inclusion lists were declined for this upgrade
and targeted at the next.<Cite src="eip-7773" /><Cite src="eip-7805" />

That is a real, worthwhile, incomplete change — and being able to say all three parts is what
separates understanding the design from repeating its slogan.
