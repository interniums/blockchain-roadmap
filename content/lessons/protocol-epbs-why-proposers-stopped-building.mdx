A validator on a machine in someone's spare room gets a proposer duty. Three processes are running
on it: an execution client, a consensus client, and a third binary that is not part of Ethereum at
all.

At the top of the slot the consensus client does not ask the execution client for a block. It asks
the third binary. That binary has spent the previous seconds asking several remote services which of
them will pay the most for this slot, and it returns a winner — as a header. Not a block. A header,
plus a number in gwei. The validator signs the header without ever seeing a transaction inside it,
and the remote service publishes the body.

The operator did not configure anything unusual. This is the ordinary case. The interesting question
is not how the machinery works — you have already met [the four roles](/c/searcher-builder-relay-proposer)
and the flow through [MEV-Boost](/c/mev-boost) — but *why a system that had one job, "propose a block", ended up with the proposer not
choosing what is in it*, and what exactly was traded away to get there.

## The bargain has two sides and only one of them gets said out loud

The standard one-line defence of [proposer-builder separation](/c/pbs) is that it protects solo stakers. That
is true, and it is half a sentence.

Start from the counterfactual. Suppose the protocol required proposers to build their own blocks.
Not forbade outsourcing — simply made it impossible. What would a block be worth?

A block's value to its proposer is the sum of what the transactions in it pay. Priority fees are the
visible part. The larger and more variable part is [what you can capture by choosing the ordering](/c/mev):
arbitrage between venues that have drifted apart, liquidations that just became callable, the
sandwich around a large market order. Capturing that reliably needs four things that have nothing to
do with running a validator:

- **Searchers**, or the ability to be one — code that watches state and finds the opportunity before
  anyone else.
- **Simulation capacity** — the ability to execute thousands of candidate orderings against current
  state within the seconds you have, and price each one.
- **Exclusive order flow** — transactions that reach you and not your competitors, which in practice
  means commercial agreements with wallets, applications and RPC providers.
- **Latency engineering** — colocation, tuned networking, and a willingness to treat tens of
  milliseconds as an engineering budget.

None of those scale down. All of them have large fixed costs and near-zero marginal costs, which is
the textbook shape of a business that concentrates. If the protocol forced every proposer to do this
work itself, then the expected value of a validator would depend on the sophistication of its
operator, and validators run by unsophisticated operators would earn less. Sustained, that gap does
one thing: it moves stake to whoever can build best. Staking — the thing the protocol's security
budget is actually made of — concentrates.

So the protocol declines to force it, and lets the proposer sell the right to build instead. The
hobbyist and the professional now receive the same competitive bid for the same slot, because
neither of them is doing the building. Staking stays spread out.

**And building concentrates instead.** That is not a side effect anyone failed to anticipate. It is
the deliberate half of the bargain: take the centralising pressure off the role that secures the
chain, and let it land on a role that does not. Whether that landing zone is safe is the question
the rest of this module is about.

## What the protocol actually knows about any of this

Here is the part that should feel wrong the first time.

Open the consensus specification for the fork running on mainnet today and search it for the word
"relay". You will not find one, because there is no such object. Search for "builder" and you find
nothing in the state transition either. The `BeaconBlockBody` of every fork from the Merge until
Gloas carries an `execution_payload` field, full stop — a complete execution payload, sitting inside
the beacon block, signed by the proposer.<Cite src="consensus-specs" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" />

Everything in the opening scene happens in the gap between that specification and what operators
choose to run. The proposer's client supports building a block *blind*: it produces a
`SignedBlindedBeaconBlock` whose body carries only the hash tree root of a promised execution
payload, hands that to a third party, and that third party substitutes the full payload back in
before broadcasting.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> The API for the whole conversation — what a bid looks
like, how a header is requested, how a signed blinded block is exchanged for a body — lives in
`ethereum/builder-specs`, a separate repository from the consensus specs, describing a market rather
than a protocol.<Cite src="builder-specs" />

This distinction is worth holding precisely, because it is the thing EIP-7732 changes and almost
everything else about the design follows from it:

- **In protocol** means the state transition function enforces it. A block that violates it is
  invalid, every client agrees it is invalid, and no coordination is needed to make that true.
- **Out of protocol** means participants do it because it pays. Nothing in the rules requires it,
  nothing in the rules forbids stopping, and the guarantees are contractual and reputational rather
  than cryptographic.

MEV-Boost is entirely in the second category. It is middleware, it is optional, a validator can shut
it off between one slot and the next, and the chain notices nothing.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-docs" />

### One slot, in order, with the trust marked

Walk the ordinary case once, naming what each step buys rather than only what it does. A mainnet
slot is twelve seconds, and the consensus specification sets the attestation deadline at 3333 basis
points of the slot — a third of the way in, four seconds.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-gloas" />
Everything below has to fit before that mark.

**Subgoal: discover what the slot is worth, before committing to anything.** During the preceding
slot, builders assemble candidate blocks and submit them to relays with a promised payment. The
proposer's middleware polls the relays it is configured to trust and collects bids. Nothing is
signed. The proposer is shopping.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-docs" />

**Subgoal: commit to a payment without exposing the goods.** The winning bid is returned as a
header — a commitment to a payload, not the payload. The proposer signs a blinded block around that
commitment.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> The reason the body is withheld at this step is the builder's
protection, not the proposer's: a proposer shown a complete block could take its contents and
propose them as its own without paying.

**Subgoal: exchange the signature for the goods.** The proposer returns the signed blinded block to
the relay. The relay now holds a signature binding the proposer to this payload and releases the
body, publishing the assembled block to the network.<Cite src="builder-specs" />

**Subgoal: get paid.** The payment is a transaction inside the block the builder constructed, paying
the proposer's fee recipient. It only exists if the block lands.

Read that sequence for its ordering and the exposure becomes obvious. The proposer's signature is
irreversible at step three and the block's existence is not guaranteed until step four, and the
entire gap between them is covered by one party's software behaving as documented.

## Optional, and yet

Now put the two facts side by side. The software is optional. Roughly nine in ten mainnet blocks are
produced through it.<Cite src="flashbots-mev-boost-docs" />

A convention that voluntary and that near-universal is a strange object. It is not part of the
system by any formal definition, and it is load-bearing by every practical one. If it stopped
working tomorrow the chain would keep finalising — but the economics under every staking operator's
spreadsheet would change overnight, and the blocks would look different.

### The fallback is real, and you can see it in the data

There is a safety valve, and it has been exercised. If the proposer's client gets no usable bid, or
the bid it gets turns out to be unusable, it builds a block locally with its own execution client
and proposes that. Nothing is missed; the block is just worth less.

That path is not theoretical. In November 2022 a builder submitted bids with incorrect `timestamp`
and `prev_randao` values; beacon nodes rejected the resulting bids and proposers fell back to local
building. The visible signature was a measurable drop in MEV-Boost blocks over a four-hour window on
mainnet, and the writeup is explicit that proposers did not miss slots — they simply stopped selling
them.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2022-11" />

Hold onto that, because it establishes the shape of failure in today's system: **the market thins,
the chain continues**. Under the enshrined design the failure shape is different, and that difference
is the subject of the free-option lesson later in this module.

<Check type="explain" concept="pbs-problem">
A colleague proposes what sounds like a clean fix: forbid outsourcing. Make the consensus rules
require that the proposer of a block also constructed it, so that block building is spread across
the whole validator set by construction. Say what actually happens to decentralisation if that rule
ships.

<Answer>
Building would spread across the validator set. Staking would concentrate.

The rule does not change the fact that a maximally valuable block requires searching, simulation,
exclusive order flow and latency engineering. It only removes the ability to buy that capability
from someone who has it. So the value a validator earns per slot starts depending on the
sophistication of its operator, and the professional operator out-earns the home operator on every
proposal.

That gap does not stay a gap. Stake migrates toward whoever earns most on it, whether by operators
growing or by delegators moving, and within a few reward cycles the validator set is dominated by
the entities that were good at building. You have decentralised the role that does not secure the
chain and centralised the one that does — the exact inversion of what proposer-builder separation is
for.<Cite src="eip-7732" />

The honest form of the fix is not "forbid outsourcing". It is "make outsourcing safe", which is what
enshrining it attempts.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Reading the pipeline as a list of things that can be done to you

You know the four roles. The productive way to re-read them now is not as a flow diagram but as a
column of powers: at each hop, what can this party do to the party before it, and what stops them?

**Searcher to builder.** A searcher submits a bundle expecting it to be included atomically at a
price. The builder can decline it. The builder can also, in principle, take the idea in the bundle
and execute it itself. What stops that is competition for future flow and the searcher's ability to
move to another builder — commercial, not cryptographic.

**Builder to relay.** The builder hands over a complete block and a promised payment. The relay can
see the entire block, including every bundle inside it, before anyone else. What stops it from using
that is that relays are run by parties who have staked a reputation on not doing so.

**Relay to proposer.** The proposer signs a header and gets the body only afterwards. If the relay
never publishes, the proposer has signed a commitment to a block that does not exist and may miss
the slot. What stops that is, again, the relay's incentive not to be the relay that does that.

**Proposer to builder.** The proposer has the body eventually — and the malicious variant of this is
not hypothetical, as the next lesson shows in detail.

Notice the pattern. Every arrow in this pipeline is protected by incentives, reputation and code
that participants voluntarily run, and not one of them is protected by the state transition
function. That is a coherent way to build a market. It is a strange way to run the part of a
trust-minimised system through which nearly every block passes.

## Why this becomes a protocol problem rather than a market problem

A market with concentrated intermediaries is a normal thing in the world. Several arguments push
this particular one onto the protocol's desk.

**The commitment is one-sided.** The proposer signs first and receives second. Everything about that
ordering is a choice made by software the protocol does not specify, and it means the proposer's
exposure is set by an external party's implementation quality.

**The intermediary is a natural filter.** Any party that sees complete blocks before they are
published, and that publishes them, is the cheapest possible place to apply a rule about which
transactions may be in them. That is a structural fact about where the relay sits, independent of any
relay operator's intentions.

**The count is small.** Relays carrying meaningful volume number in the handful. A market with a
handful of intermediaries has correlated failure by construction — one bug in a shared
implementation reaches most of the market at once, which is precisely what the 2022 incident
demonstrated when a defect in one widely used relay implementation moved proposers across the
network onto local blocks.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2022-11" />

**And the protocol wants the time back.** This one is easy to miss and it is the reason the change is
being shipped now rather than eventually. Today a proposer's block contains the full execution
payload, which means the payload's bytes travel on the critical path between block publication and
the attestation deadline, and every attester must run both the consensus and the execution state
transition, plus a data-availability check, inside that window before it
votes.<Cite src="eip-7732" /> Every extra byte of block and every extra blob makes the window
tighter, and the window is a fixed fraction of a fixed slot
duration.<Cite src="consensus-specs-mainnet-config-gloas" /> Enshrining the separation is, among other
things, a way to take the payload off that path. That thread runs through this whole module and it
is the one most outside commentary gets backwards.

There is a fifth argument that is easy to state and hard to weigh, so state it carefully. A market
convention can be changed by the people who run it, faster than a protocol can be changed by the
people who specify it. That cuts both ways: it is why relays could patch a live exploit within hours
in 2023, and it is why the properties every proposer depends on can be renegotiated without a fork,
an All Core Devs call, or anyone outside the market noticing.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2023-04" />
Whether you consider that agility or fragility is a genuine judgement call, and it is worth deciding
what you think before the next lesson makes the case in one direction.

<Check type="recall" concept="mev-boost-today">
Someone hands you a beacon block from mainnet today and asks you to prove, from the block alone,
whether it was built by its proposer or bought from a builder. What can you actually establish, and
what would you need beyond the block?

<Answer>
From the block alone: almost nothing definitive.

The `BeaconBlockBody` of the current fork carries an `execution_payload` and a proposer
signature.<Cite src="consensus-specs-gloas-beacon-chain" /> There is no builder field, no relay
field, and no marker distinguishing a locally built payload from one that arrived through a relay,
because the state transition function does not model the distinction at
all.<Cite src="consensus-specs" />

What you can do is inference from outside the block. The `fee_recipient` of the execution payload
and the shape of the final transaction — a payment to a different address than the block's fee
recipient — are the usual heuristics. The reliable answer comes from the market's own records: relays
publish the bids they delivered, and dashboards aggregate them, so you match the block by slot and
block hash against relay data.<Cite src="relayscan-io" />

That is the whole lesson in one exercise. The most consequential fact about how a mainnet block came
to exist is not recorded in the block, and you have to ask a private company's API to find it
out.<Cite src="builder-specs" />
</Answer>
</Check>

## What you should be able to say now

Before moving on, make sure these are things you can state without hedging:

1. The centralisation being *avoided* by proposer-builder separation is in staking. The
   centralisation being *accepted* is in building. Both halves are deliberate.
2. Today's arrangement is a market convention: the consensus specification contains no relay and no
   builder, and the API describing them lives in a different repository from the protocol
   itself.<Cite src="builder-specs" /><Cite src="consensus-specs" />
3. The convention is optional and nearly universal, which makes it load-bearing infrastructure with
   no protocol guarantees behind it.
4. When it fails, today's failure mode is that the market thins and proposers build locally — the
   chain keeps running.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2022-11" />
5. Any share figure in this area is a measurement with a date. Read it live rather than quoting
   it.<Cite src="relayscan-io" />

The next lesson takes the third arrow in that column of powers — relay to proposer — and stops
treating it as an abstraction. There is a documented mainnet incident where the escrow assumption
broke exactly as written, roughly twenty million dollars moved as a result, and the fix was a patch
to software the protocol does not know exists.<Cite src="flashbots-relay-incident-2023-04" /> That
incident is the clearest single argument for why the escrow role should not be an off-protocol
service, and it is where we go next.
