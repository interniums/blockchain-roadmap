The previous lesson ended with a proposer building on its grandparent to steal a block. This one is
about a proposer building on its grandparent because the specification tells it to.

Same action. Same shape on the chain. Same orphaned block. The difference is eight conditions, and
learning them is worth more than the mechanism itself, because each one is a named answer to a
specific way the manoeuvre could be abused — which makes `get_proposer_head` the best short catalogue
of fork-choice attack surface anywhere in the spec.

Start with the case for the rule at all. A block that arrives after its slot's attestation deadline
gets few attestations, because attesters have already voted for its parent. It is a weak block sitting
at the head, and the next proposer has two options: extend it and inherit a chain whose tip nobody
supports, or build on the parent that the attesters actually voted for. The second is arguably what
the votes already said. The rule makes that reading explicit and gates it heavily.

## The eight conditions, and what each one forecloses

```python
def get_proposer_head(store: Store, head_root: Root, slot: Slot) -> Root:
    head_block = store.blocks[head_root]
    parent_root = head_block.parent_root
    parent_block = store.blocks[parent_root]

    head_late = is_head_late(store, head_root)
    shuffling_stable = is_shuffling_stable(slot)
    ffg_competitive = is_ffg_competitive(store, head_root, parent_root)
    finalization_ok = is_finalization_ok(store, slot)
    proposing_on_time = is_proposing_on_time(store)

    parent_slot_ok = parent_block.slot + 1 == head_block.slot
    current_time_ok = head_block.slot + 1 == slot
    single_slot_reorg = parent_slot_ok and current_time_ok

    assert store.proposer_boost_root != head_root  # ensure boost has worn off
    head_weak = is_head_weak(store, head_root)
    parent_strong = is_parent_strong(store, parent_root)

    if all([head_late, shuffling_stable, ffg_competitive, finalization_ok,
            proposing_on_time, single_slot_reorg, head_weak, parent_strong]):
        return parent_root
    else:
        return head_root
```

<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" />

Take them one at a time. Each is a door an attacker would otherwise walk through.

**`head_late` — the block missed its own deadline.** `is_head_late` is simply
`not store.block_timeliness[head_root]`,<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> and timeliness was recorded in
`on_block` as "arrived during its own slot and before the attestation
deadline".<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> Without this condition the rule would authorise reorging any
block at all, which is the ex-post attack with a specification behind it.

**`shuffling_stable` — not on an epoch boundary.** `slot % SLOTS_PER_EPOCH != 0`, with
`SLOTS_PER_EPOCH` = 32.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /><Cite src="fc-phase0-beacon-chain-v161" /> The
spec's stated reason is that the proposer shuffling could change at an epoch
boundary.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> Reorging across a boundary would let a proposer alter which
validators get to propose next, which is a far larger prize than one block.

**`ffg_competitive` — the switch does not cost justification.** The unrealized justification of the
head must equal that of the parent.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> Without it, an honest-looking reorg
could drop a block whose branch was about to justify a checkpoint, converting a head-level tidy-up
into a finality-level attack.

**`finalization_ok` — the chain is healthy.** `epochs_since_finalization` must be at most
`REORG_MAX_EPOCHS_SINCE_FINALIZATION` = 2.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /><Cite src="fc-config-mainnet-v161" />
When finality is already struggling, the rule turns itself off. Deliberate reorgs are a luxury
affordable only when the chain is otherwise settling.

**`proposing_on_time` — the reorging proposer is not itself late.** `time_into_slot_ms` must be at most
`get_proposer_reorg_cutoff_ms`, which is `PROPOSER_REORG_CUTOFF_BPS` = 1667 basis points of
`SLOT_DURATION_MS` = 12000 — roughly 17% of the slot, about two
seconds.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /><Cite src="fc-config-mainnet-v161" /> Note this is *tighter*
than the four-second attestation deadline.<Cite src="fc-phase0-validator-v161" /> A proposer that wants
to orphan someone else's late block must be conspicuously early with its own. A late block replacing a
late block would just move the problem.

**`single_slot_reorg` — exactly one block, no more.** Two equalities: the parent is one slot before the
head, and the head is one slot before now.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> The rule cannot be chained
into depth, and it cannot be applied retroactively to a block from several slots ago.

**`head_weak` — the block being dropped has almost no support.** `get_weight(head)` must be less than
`calculate_committee_fraction(justified_state, REORG_HEAD_WEIGHT_THRESHOLD)` with the threshold at
20.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /><Cite src="fc-config-mainnet-v161" /> Convert it: 20% of one slot's
committee, and one committee is 1/32 of total active stake, so the head must hold **less than 0.625% of
total stake**. That is a genuinely abandoned block, not a merely unlucky one.

**`parent_strong` — and the votes really did go where they appear to have gone.** `get_weight(parent)`
must exceed the same fraction computed with `REORG_PARENT_WEIGHT_THRESHOLD` =
160.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /><Cite src="fc-config-mainnet-v161" /> That is 160% of a committee —
more than one slot's entire attester weight — so **more than 5% of total stake**, which requires
attesters from multiple slots to have converged on the parent.

The spec's comment on that last one is the most instructive line in the function: it is there to check
that the missing votes are assigned to the parent **and not being hoarded**.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" />
Sit with that. A weak head is consistent with two stories: the block was late and the attesters
honestly voted for its parent, or an adversary withheld attestations it intends to release. The first
story implies a strong parent; the second does not, because the hoarded votes are not anywhere yet.
`parent_strong` is a test that distinguishes the two, and it is precisely a defence against being
tricked into performing the honest reorg on behalf of an ex-ante attacker.

<Aside kind="note">
The `assert store.proposer_boost_root != head_root` sits outside the `all([...])` list, with the
comment "ensure boost has worn off".<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> It guards against evaluating the
head's weakness while the head still carries its own 1.25%-of-total-stake
boost<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /><Cite src="fc-config-mainnet-v161" /> — which would make
`is_head_weak` compare against an inflated number. The spec also notes that the ordering of conditions
is a suggestion only and implementations are free to reorder from least to most expensive and return
early.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> So the *order* you read is pedagogical, not normative; the
*conjunction* is the rule.
</Aside>

## The word that changes how you should talk about this

Directly above the helpers, the spec says: **_Implementing these helpers is
optional_**.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" />

That single italicised line has consequences that outrun the mechanism.

**There is no network-wide behaviour to describe.** Whether a late block gets reorged depends on which
client the *next* proposer runs, which version it runs, and whether that client exposes the behaviour
behind a flag and whether the operator turned it on. Two proposers in consecutive slots can behave
differently and both be conformant.

**The thresholds you just read are the spec's configuration, not a guarantee about any node.** A client
is free to implement the rule with its own gating, or to implement part of it, or none. Reading
`REORG_HEAD_WEIGHT_THRESHOLD` = 20 out of `configs/mainnet.yaml`<Cite src="fc-config-mainnet-v161" />
tells you what the spec says; it does not tell you what the proposer in slot `n+1` will do.

**Therefore: do not teach or assume a rate.** How often late blocks are reorged in practice is an
empirical question about a heterogeneous deployed population, and it moves whenever a major client
changes a default. That measurement is exactly what this module's counting exercise asks you to make
against a beacon API rather than take from an article — including the base rate of *missed* slots,
which dominates.

**And there is precedent for the risk that optionality creates.** An uneven rollout of a fork-choice
change across clients is not hypothetical harm: it is one of the three named causes of the seven-block
mainnet reorg of 25 May 2022, where some validators applied proposer boost and some did not and the
votes split roughly in half for seven consecutive slots.<Cite src="monnot-reorg-post" /> A rule that is
optional by design is a rule whose adoption curve is part of its security analysis.

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="honest-reorg-of-late-blocks">
A monitoring service you maintain raises an alert whenever a proposer builds on a grandparent. The
alert text reads: "Possible ex-post reorg attack by proposer of slot N."

Over a week it fires eleven times. A junior engineer proposes suppressing it entirely, on the grounds
that it is always the honest late-block rule. Find the flaw in the alert as written, the flaw in the
proposed fix, and say what the check should actually compare.

<Answer>
**The flaw in the alert is that its condition does not discriminate.** Building on a grandparent is the
observable of both the attack and the specified honest rule — the rule's own return value is
`parent_root` where `parent_root` is the *head's* parent, so the new block's parent is the orphaned
block's parent.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> A detector whose signal is present in both the benign
and malicious case is not a detector; it is a counter with an accusatory label. And the label is the
worse half: it names a proposer as an attacker on evidence that is consistent with correct software.

**The flaw in the proposed fix is that it assumes the conclusion.** "It is always the honest rule" is
the claim the alert was supposed to test. Suppress it and you lose the only place in your stack where a
genuine ex-post reorg would have shown up at all — and an ex-post reorg needs no equivocation, no
withholding and no unusual network position, so nothing else in your monitoring will see it either.

**What the check should compare is the gating conditions, because they are the entire difference.**
For each grandparent-build, evaluate whether the orphaned block satisfied what the rule requires:

*Was the orphaned block late?* Timeliness is a per-node judgement recorded against the attestation
deadline in the receiving node's own store,<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> so record *your* node's
observation and label it as such. If your node saw it comfortably on time, that is the signal.

*Was the orphaned block weak?* The rule requires head weight below 20% of a committee — under 0.625%
of total active stake.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /><Cite src="fc-config-mainnet-v161" /> A block that
was orphaned while holding substantially more than that was not eligible.

*Was the parent strong?* The rule requires parent weight above 160% of a committee, more than 5% of
total stake.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /><Cite src="fc-config-mainnet-v161" /> This is the condition
that distinguishes "the votes went to the parent because the head was late" from "the votes are being
hoarded",<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> and it is the most informative of the three.

*Was it a single slot, off an epoch boundary, with finalization within two epochs, and was the new
block itself published inside the ~17% cutoff?*<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /><Cite src="fc-config-mainnet-v161" />
Any of these failing means the rule as specified would not have fired.

**Then fix the alert's wording, because it is making a claim it cannot support.** The honest text is
mechanical: "Slot N built on grandparent. Orphaned block: timely per this node, weight X% of stake.
Parent weight Y%. Conditions for the specified late-block reorg rule: not met — head was timely." That
sentence is reviewable. "Attack by proposer" is not, and it is one screenshot away from a public
accusation your data cannot back.

**One caveat to keep the report honest.** Even a full condition match does not prove benign intent. The
conditions are what an honest client checks; an adversary is free to wait for a genuinely late, genuinely
weak block and reorg it for exactly the reasons the rule permits, taking the MEV as a bonus. And because
implementing these helpers is optional,<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> a *failure* to reorg an eligible
block is equally uninformative — it may simply be a client that never implemented it. What the check
buys you is a clean separation between "consistent with the specified rule" and "not consistent with
it", and only the second is worth anyone's attention.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

- **Eight conditions as a threat-model checklist.** `get_proposer_head` is the densest enumeration of
  fork-choice abuse vectors in the spec, and each condition names one:<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" />
  epoch-boundary shuffling manipulation, justification loss, chained depth, hoarded votes, boost
  self-inflation. When you evaluate any proposed fork-choice change, walk this list and ask which
  condition the change would need.
- **Two numbers you can now compute for any validator set.** Head weakness is 0.625% of total stake and
  parent strength is 5%, derived from 20 and 160 percent of a committee where a committee is 1/32 of
  the set.<Cite src="fc-config-mainnet-v161" /><Cite src="fc-phase0-beacon-chain-v161" /> Both are
  measurable against a live beacon API.
- **"Optional" as a first-class property.** You now have a concrete case where the spec deliberately
  declines to mandate behaviour, and where the resulting heterogeneity is itself a documented source of
  a mainnet incident.<Cite src="monnot-reorg-post" /> Whenever a consensus rule is optional, adoption
  is part of its analysis.
- **Next: what would remove the need for all of this.** Every mechanism in this module so far exists
  because there is a gap between a block being proposed and it being settled. The next lesson is about
  designs that shrink that gap to almost nothing — and about how carefully their status has to be
  stated, because none of them is scheduled.
