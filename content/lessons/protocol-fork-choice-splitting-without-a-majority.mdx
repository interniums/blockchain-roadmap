Every attack so far has tried to make one branch heavier than another. This one tries to make sure
neither ever is.

Picture the outcome rather than the mechanism. Blocks keep being produced. Attestations keep being
cast. Nothing is slashed, nothing halts, no client logs an error. But the justified checkpoint stops
advancing, and it keeps not advancing — for an epoch, then two, then long enough that the inactivity
leak starts burning stake from validators who did nothing wrong. From the outside it looks like the
network has quietly lost the ability to make up its mind, because that is exactly what has happened.

The balancing attack does not need a majority. It does not need a third. Published analysis puts its
requirements at a very small stake fraction plus influence over who receives which message when — and
the reason it works is a property you already met in lesson two, stated in one line: for a validator
that votes twice, the vote that counts at your node is the one that reached you first.

## The attack, and why the goal is different

The clearest short statement is ethereum.org's. The attacker waits for its chance to propose, and when
it arrives it equivocates and proposes two blocks, sending one to half of the honest validator set and
the other to the other half. The equivocation would be detected by the fork-choice algorithm and the
proposer slashed and ejected — but the two blocks still exist, and about half the validator set
attests to each fork. Meanwhile the remaining malicious validators hold back their attestations. Then,
by selectively releasing the attestations favouring one fork or the other to just enough validators,
just as the fork choice executes, they tip the accumulated weight in favour of one side or the other.
This can continue indefinitely, with the attacking validators maintaining an even split. Since neither
fork can attract a two-thirds supermajority, the network does not
finalise.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

Three things in that description deserve to be separated out, because each one is a different kind of
claim.

**The objective is liveness, not safety.** Nothing conflicting is finalised, because nothing is
finalised at all. If you carry the safety-versus-liveness distinction from the fundamentals track, this
is a pure [liveness](/c/liveness-property) attack — and it inherits that property's most awkward
feature: no finite observation ever proves it is happening rather than that the network is having a
bad hour. There is no moment at which the attack becomes provable.

**The adversary spends votes, not stake.** Its validators are not slashed for withholding, and the
attestations it releases are singly signed and well-formed. The only slashable act in the whole
sequence is the initial proposer equivocation, and that is a one-off cost paid once to create the fork.

**The maintenance is where the real requirement lives.** Splitting the network once is easy. Keeping
two subtrees within a hair of each other, slot after slot, requires knowing what each half has seen and
delivering exactly enough weight to the right side at the right moment. That is the assumption that
made the attack look academic for years — and the assumption the literature has since attacked.

<Aside kind="note">
A related family aims at the same outcome by a different route. In a *bouncing* attack the withheld
votes are used to justify checkpoints that alternate between two forks, so that no source-target pair
on either chain can ever be finalised.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /> Balancing works on
the head rule; bouncing works on the finality gadget. Both end in a chain that runs and never settles,
which is why they are usually discussed together.
</Aside>

## Why "count one vote per validator" is not the fix

The obvious response to equivocation is to count only one of the conflicting messages. The spec does
that. It is not sufficient, and the reason is worth deriving rather than asserting.

Read the update rule from lesson two again, with equivocation in mind:

```python
    for i in non_equivocating_attesting_indices:
        if i not in store.latest_messages or target.epoch > store.latest_messages[i].epoch:
            store.latest_messages[i] = LatestMessage(epoch=target.epoch, root=beacon_block_root)
```

<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" />

**Subgoal 1 — establish which of two conflicting attestations a node keeps.** Replacement requires a
*strictly greater* target epoch. Two conflicting attestations from one validator in one epoch share a
target epoch, so the second does not replace the first. The node keeps whichever it accepted first.

**Subgoal 2 — establish what "first" is a property of.** Not of the messages: they are both signed,
both valid, both for the same epoch. It is a property of delivery. Whichever attestation reached this
node first is the one that counts at this node, and a different node may have received them in the
other order and be counting the other one.

**Subgoal 3 — identify who chooses that.** The adversary. It wrote both messages and it chose where to
send each one. "Count only one vote per validator" therefore does not neutralise the equivocation; it
delegates the choice of which vote counts to the party that equivocated, once per node.

**Subgoal 4 — check whether proposer boost coordinates the halves back together.** It does not, and
this is the sharpest version of the point. The boost goes to the first *timely* block a node sees:
`is_first_block` is true only while `store.proposer_boost_root` is still the zero
root.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> An equivocating proposer publishing two timely blocks therefore
gets the boost on block A at every node that saw A first, and on block B at every node that saw B
first. The mechanism designed to be a synchronisation bottleneck<Cite src="vbuterin-lmd-ghost-mitigation" />
is itself split in half, and each half is pushed further onto its own branch. The defence is applied
symmetrically to both sides of the fork it was supposed to resolve.

**Subgoal 5 — state the conclusion and its limit.** Counting one vote per validator bounds how much
weight an equivocation can *create*. It does nothing about which side that weight lands on, and that
is the quantity the attack manipulates. The bound is on magnitude; the attack is about direction.

## What the spec actually does about it

There is a real mechanism, it is stronger than the paraphrase above, and it has a precondition that
turns out to matter enormously.

```python
def on_attester_slashing(store: Store, attester_slashing: AttesterSlashing) -> None:
    attestation_1 = attester_slashing.attestation_1
    attestation_2 = attester_slashing.attestation_2
    assert is_slashable_attestation_data(attestation_1.data, attestation_2.data)
    state = store.block_states[store.justified_checkpoint.root]
    assert is_valid_indexed_attestation(state, attestation_1)
    assert is_valid_indexed_attestation(state, attestation_2)

    indices = set(attestation_1.attesting_indices).intersection(attestation_2.attesting_indices)
    for index in indices:
        store.equivocating_indices.add(index)
```

<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" />

Once an index is in `store.equivocating_indices`, `get_weight` skips it entirely and
`update_latest_messages` refuses to record anything further for it.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" />
This is stronger than counting one vote: **the validator's weight is removed from both branches, not
assigned to one.** An adversary that equivocates does not get to place its weight badly; it loses the
weight.

That is a genuinely good mechanism, and it is worth noticing three things about how it is wired.

**It runs off gossip, not off inclusion.** The spec's note says `on_attester_slashing` should be called
while syncing, and the handler's own docstring says it runs immediately upon receiving a new
`AttesterSlashing` from either within a block **or directly on the
wire**.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> The discount does not wait for a proposer to include the
evidence. Compare `validators[i].slashed`, which is a beacon-state flag and therefore only true on
branches where a slashing has actually been processed in a block.

**It is retained, not transient.** A client MUST maintain the equivocation set of `AttesterSlashing`s
from at least the latest finalized checkpoint.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" />

**It requires someone to construct the evidence.** An `AttesterSlashing` is a message containing *both*
conflicting attestations. Nobody can build it from one half. It exists only after some node has seen
both, packaged them, and gossiped the result.

That third point is the whole game. The defence is gated on evidence propagating — and the adversary
running a balancing attack is, by construction, an adversary exercising influence over propagation. It
is not required that it suppress the evidence forever; delaying it past the attestation deadline of the
slot in which it mattered is enough, because the vote for that slot has already been cast. The
mechanism engages, and engages late, repeatedly.

## What the literature actually established

Here is where careful reading matters, because the shape of the result is routinely reported wrongly in
both directions.

Neu, Tas and Tse present two attacks. The first exploits the GHOST paradigm through equivocating
blocks: proof of stake lets an adversary with a vanishing amount of stake produce an unlimited number
of equivocating blocks, and although most are orphaned, those uncle blocks still influence fork choice
under GHOST — which they describe as suggesting a fundamental conceptual incompatibility between proof
of stake and the GHOST paradigm.<Cite src="neu-tas-tse-two-attacks" /> Their own paper states that the
latest-message-driven aspect of current proof-of-stake Ethereum prevents a straightforward application
of that first attack.<Cite src="neu-tas-tse-two-attacks" /> Their second attack then shows how LMD
specifically can be exploited to obtain a new variant of the balancing attack that **overcomes a recent
protocol addition that was intended to mitigate balancing-type
attacks**.<Cite src="neu-tas-tse-two-attacks" />

Read that conclusion as the authors state it: proof-of-stake Ethereum without and with LMD is
vulnerable to the first and second attack respectively.<Cite src="neu-tas-tse-two-attacks" /> One
property blocks one attack and enables another. There is no version of the rule in that paper that is
safe from both.

Two further papers make the same point from the design side rather than the attack side.

The RLMD-GHOST paper states that despite attempts to mitigate the issue, the modified protocol still
does not achieve dynamic availability — that is, LMD-GHOST remains unable to fulfil its intended
function of providing dynamic availability for the protocol — which is why more secure dynamically
available protocols are needed.<Cite src="rlmd-ghost-paper" /> The Goldfish paper opens by calling
LMD-GHOST in its current form brittle, as evidenced by recent attacks and patching attempts, and
diagnoses the cause: attacks on LMD-GHOST exploit a lack of coordination among honest validators,
typically provided by a locking mechanism in classical BFT protocols — but locking requires votes from
a quorum of all participants and is not compatible with fluctuating
participation.<Cite src="goldfish-paper" />

**That diagnosis is the most useful sentence in this lesson.** The balancing attack is not a bug in a
constant. It is what happens when you remove the coordination primitive that classical consensus uses,
because you needed a protocol that tolerates validators coming and going. Proposer boost is an attempt
to reinstate a weak version of that coordination through weights. Lesson seven is about designs that
reinstate a stronger version through something other than weights.

## A worked correction: the bouncing mitigation you will read about is gone

This is a live example of why "read the spec at a tag" is not pedantry, and it is worth doing in full
because the stale version is on a page many readers will reach first.

ethereum.org states that to defend against bouncing attacks the fork-choice algorithm was updated so
that the latest justified checkpoint can only switch to that of an alternative chain during the first
third of the slots in each epoch, preventing the attacker from saving up votes to deploy
later.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

**Subgoal 1 — find the constant that rule corresponds to.** It is `SAFE_SLOTS_TO_UPDATE_JUSTIFIED`,
defined as `2**3` (= 8) slots, 96 seconds, and consulted by
`should_update_justified_checkpoint`.<Cite src="fc-spec-v1-1-10" />

**Subgoal 2 — check the arithmetic of the description.** Eight slots out of 32 is a quarter of an
epoch, not a third.<Cite src="fc-spec-v1-1-10" /><Cite src="fc-phase0-beacon-chain-v161" /> The prose
is approximate.

**Subgoal 3 — check whether the rule is still there.** It is not. `SAFE_SLOTS_TO_UPDATE_JUSTIFIED` and
`should_update_justified_checkpoint` are present at v1.1.10 and still present at
v1.2.0,<Cite src="fc-spec-v1-1-10" /><Cite src="fc-spec-v1-2-0" /> and neither the constant nor the
function appears anywhere in the current tagged fork-choice
spec.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" />

**Subgoal 4 — find what replaced it, so you know what to teach instead.** The store now tracks
`unrealized_justified_checkpoint`, `unrealized_finalized_checkpoint` and a per-block
`unrealized_justifications` map — the highest justified and finalized checkpoints *without regard to
whether on-chain realization has occurred*, since FFG processing only happens at epoch
boundaries.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> `compute_pulled_up_tip` computes these eagerly for each new
block, `get_voting_source` uses the pulled-up value for blocks from a prior epoch, and
`on_tick_per_slot` promotes the unrealized checkpoints at each epoch
boundary.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> A timing window has been replaced by a node computing, for
every block, what that block's branch *would* justify.

**Subgoal 5 — state the general rule this instance is an instance of.** Fork-choice mitigations are
replaced, not accumulated, and the replacement is usually a stronger computation rather than a wider
window. Any article describing a fork-choice defence by its timing is describing a design generation,
and you should check the tag before repeating it. The teaching point is not that ethereum.org is
wrong about the past — it is not — but that a page describing a shipped rule in the present tense can
outlive the rule.

<Check type="explain" concept="equivocation-handling">
You run a large staking operation. Finality has not advanced for four epochs. Blocks are being
produced, your validators are attesting normally, your clients report no errors, and your peers report
the same picture. Your incident channel is asking whether this is a balancing attack, and whether you
should do anything.

Say what you can determine, what you cannot, and what — if anything — is worth doing.

<Answer>
**Start with what cannot be determined, because the honest answer changes what the rest of the
response is for.** A stalled justified checkpoint is a liveness symptom, and liveness violations are
not observable from any finite observation — every prefix in which nothing has finalised is consistent
with finalisation resuming in the next slot. There is no measurement that distinguishes "an adversary
is balancing us" from "a large operator is offline and participation is under two thirds" from "a
network event is delaying aggregates". You will not resolve intent from your own telemetry, and a
postmortem that asserts one is asserting something it did not measure.

**Now the things that are genuinely diagnostic, in order of cheapness.**

*Participation rate.* If attesting participation has dropped below the two-thirds threshold, you have
an availability explanation that needs no adversary, and the protocol's own answer is the inactivity
leak. That is the first number to read because it is the most common cause by a wide margin.

*Whether two competing subtrees are actually present, and how close their weights are.* A balancing
attack has a signature: two branches held near parity across slots, rather than one branch clearly
ahead with insufficient participation behind it. Your beacon nodes know both subtree weights;
`get_weight` is a function of the store and nothing else.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> Near-parity
sustained over many slots is not proof, but its absence is close to a refutation.

*Whether your own nodes disagree with each other about the head.* Sustained head divergence between
machines you control, on different network paths, is the observable shadow of a split view — and a
split view maintained by selective delivery is exactly the adversary's method. Momentary divergence is
normal; four epochs of it is not.

*Whether attester slashings are propagating.* If a proposer or attester has equivocated, the
`AttesterSlashing` should be reaching you on gossip and populating `store.equivocating_indices`, which
removes that validator's weight from *both* branches.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> Seeing equivocation
evidence arrive tells you equivocation happened. Seeing none does not tell you it did not — the
evidence must be constructed by a node that saw both halves, and an adversary shaping delivery is
positioned to delay exactly that.

**What is worth doing, and what is not.**

*Do not change your validators' behaviour.* There is no configuration on your side that resolves a
split view, and every custom intervention risks producing the one thing that is genuinely catastrophic:
a slashable message. Your validators attesting honestly to their own client's head is the correct
behaviour in every branch of this diagnosis.

*Do check client and version diversity across your own fleet.* The seven-block mainnet reorg of May
2022 came from an uneven rollout of a fork-choice change across clients, plus a known incorrect
implementation of when the fork choice is run — a split view created by heterogeneous software rather
than by an adversary.<Cite src="monnot-reorg-post" /> If half your fleet is on a different fork-choice
generation from the other half, you may be a contributor rather than a victim.

*Do tell downstream consumers what has actually happened, in their vocabulary.* Anything in your
product that credits on finality is now stalled and should say so. Anything that credits on head or on
a depth is running normally and is exposed to a deeper reorg than usual, because the unfinalized tip is
now four epochs long instead of two.

*Do prepare for the leak.* If participation is the cause and it persists, the inactivity leak will
begin reducing balances of non-participating validators until the participating set regains a
supermajority. That is the protocol recovering, and it has a cost that belongs in your report.

**The one-line answer for the channel.** We cannot tell an attack from an outage, and no observation
will let us; what we can tell is whether participation is short and whether two branches are being held
level, and neither of those changes what our validators should do — which is exactly what they are
doing.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

- **A third attack objective, which most catalogues omit.** Ex-ante and ex-post attacks move the head.
  A balancing attack pins it. When you meet a new consensus attack, ask which of the three it is
  before asking how it works.
- **Arrival order as an adversarial input.** You have now seen it decide which of two conflicting
  attestations counts,<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> and which of two conflicting blocks receives
  proposer boost.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> Any mechanism whose output depends on delivery order
  is a mechanism an adversary with propagation influence can steer.
- **The diagnosis that motivates lesson seven.** LMD-GHOST is brittle because it lacks the coordination
  a locking mechanism provides, and locking was rejected because it is incompatible with fluctuating
  participation.<Cite src="goldfish-paper" /> Every replacement design in this module is an answer to
  that specific tension.
- **A habit worth keeping.** One widely read page describes a bouncing-attack defence that was removed
  from the spec several generations ago.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" /><Cite src="fork-choice-spec" />
  Before you teach or rely on a fork-choice rule, open `specs/phase0/fork-choice.md` at the tag your
  client targets and confirm the function still exists.
- **Next, the opposite case.** Every reorg so far has been an attack. The next lesson is about the
  reorg the specification tells honest proposers to perform, why it exists, and why it makes the
  attacks above harder to identify rather than easier.
