Two attacks, the same outcome, opposite halves of one instant.

Draw a vertical line at the moment an honest block is published. Everything the adversary does to the
left of that line is an ex-ante reorg: the trap is set before the victim exists, and the victim walks
into it. Everything the adversary does to the right of the line is an ex-post reorg: it looks at the
block, decides it wants the slot, and takes it.

That is the whole naming convention, and it is worth installing properly because the two attacks have
almost nothing else in common. They need different resources, they are defended by opposite settings
of the same constant, and only one of them requires the adversary to plan. A defence tuned for one
funds the other — which you saw as inequalities in the previous lesson and will now see as
timelines.

## Ex ante: build the trap first

The mechanism is a withholding attack, and the thing being withheld is not just a block.

The canonical description, in the shape ethereum.org sets out from the Neuder et al analysis: an
attacking validator creates and attests to a block `B` for slot `n+1` but does not propagate it. An
honest validator proposes block `C` for slot `n+2`. Almost simultaneously the attacker releases the
withheld `B` and its withheld attestations for it, *and* attests to `B` as head with its slot `n+2`
votes. When the next honest block `D` is released, the fork choice sees `D` building on `B` as heavier
than `D` building on `C` — and honest block `C` is gone from the canonical chain in a one-block ex-ante
reorg.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

Four properties of that sequence are worth separating, because each one is doing distinct work.

**The withheld object is a block *and* a set of attestations.** The block alone would carry no weight.
It is the saved votes, released with it, that make the branch heavy in the instant it becomes visible.
Withholding is how an adversary converts votes it was entitled to cast over several slots into a
single lump it can spend at a chosen moment.

**The release is timed against the attestation deadline, not against block publication.** What matters
is which branch is heavier in each attester's store when that attester decides — roughly a third of
the way into the slot, at `ATTESTATION_DUE_BPS` = 3333 basis points of the 12-second
slot.<Cite src="fc-phase0-validator-v161" /><Cite src="fc-config-mainnet-v161" /> An adversary
releasing a few hundred milliseconds before that instant catches attesters; releasing after it catches
nobody.

**Nothing in the sequence is slashable.** Publishing a block late is not an offence. Publishing your
own attestations late is not an offence. The adversary casts one vote per validator per epoch, all
well-formed, all signed once. There is no double vote and no surround vote, so there is nothing for
the [slashing conditions](/c/slashing-conditions) to catch. **The attack is executed entirely with
messages the protocol considers valid.**

**The reorg is of an honest block, not of the adversary's own.** The adversary does not need its
branch to be *better*; it needs the honest block's branch to be *lighter*, and it achieves that by
denying the honest block the slot `n+2` votes it would otherwise have collected.

### What it costs — a number that has fallen repeatedly

This is the part where the literature has moved a long way, and where secondhand summaries are usually
several years stale.

The original cost analysis considered a validator with 30% of the total
stake.<Cite src="fc-neuder-low-cost-attacks" /> That paper's framing is worth keeping because it names
the motive as well as the mechanism: in a malicious reorg an attacker withholds their blocks and
attestations before releasing them at an opportune time in order to force a reorganisation, which they
can take advantage of by double-spending or front-running
transactions.<Cite src="fc-neuder-low-cost-attacks" /> Reorgs are an MEV instrument, not only a
liveness nuisance.

ethereum.org's catalogue records the progression: an attacker with 34% of the stake has a very good
chance of succeeding; the attack was described at 30%; it was later shown viable with 2% of the total
stake, and then again for a single validator using balancing
techniques.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />

The strongest published version states its own requirements rather than a stake percentage. Combining
refinements of the earlier attacks yields a third attack allowing an adversary with a vanishingly small
fraction of stake **and no control over network message propagation** — assuming instead probabilistic
message propagation — to cause even long-range consensus chain reorganisations, which the paper notes
honest-but-rational or ideologically motivated validators could use to increase their profits or stall
the protocol.<Cite src="three-attacks-pos-ethereum" />

Read the italicised clause again. The usual dismissal of fork-choice attacks is that they require
implausible control over the network. That dismissal was answered in the literature: this variant
drops the network-control assumption and keeps the result.

<Aside kind="note">
Proposer boost is the defence against this specific direction and it is worth being exact about what
it does. The honest block at slot `n+2` enters the comparison with 1.25% of total stake behind
it — 40% of one slot's committee weight, which is itself total active stake divided by
32.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /><Cite src="fc-config-mainnet-v161" /><Cite src="fc-phase0-beacon-chain-v161" />
Against a single-slot ex-ante attack the requirement is `W_PB` greater than β; against an adversary
that controls `l` slots in a row and accumulates across them it becomes `W_PB` greater than
`l·β`.<Cite src="fc-proposer-boost-considerations" /> The defence is real and it is bounded, and the
bound is a function of how many consecutive slots the adversary happens to draw.
</Aside>

### The same attack, walked slot by slot with weights

The prose description hides where the margin actually comes from. Do it once with numbers, in the unit
from lesson two: fractions of total active stake. One slot's committee is 1/32, so
3.125%.<Cite src="fc-phase0-beacon-chain-v161" /> Let the adversary hold β = 5% of total stake, spread
uniformly, so it holds about 5% of every slot's committee.

**Subgoal 1 — establish what the adversary banks, and over how long.** It proposes at slot `n+1` and
does not publish. Its validators in slots `n` and `n+1` attest to its hidden block rather than to the
honest chain, and it does not publish those attestations either. Two slots of 5% of a 3.125% committee
is about 0.31% of total stake, sitting off-network.

**Subgoal 2 — establish what the honest branch loses by the withholding alone.** Slot `n+1` produced no
visible block, so slot `n+1`'s honest attesters vote for the block at slot `n` — the last head they can
see. That is not a loss for the honest branch; it is why the withholding is cheap. The adversary pays
nothing to skip a slot.

**Subgoal 3 — establish the honest block's position at the decisive instant.** The honest proposer at
slot `n+2` publishes on time. Its block carries proposer boost: 1.25% of total
stake.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /><Cite src="fc-config-mainnet-v161" /> Its own committee has not
voted yet — they are deciding right now. So at the attestation deadline the honest block's advantage
over its sibling is essentially the boost alone.

**Subgoal 4 — release, and compare the two subtrees in each attester's store.** The adversary publishes
the hidden block, its 0.31% of banked attestations, and its slot `n+2` validators' votes for the hidden
block. Both branches descend from block `n`, so the comparison happens at block `n`'s children. Honest
child: boost 1.25%, plus whatever slot `n+2` votes have already landed. Adversarial child: 0.31% banked
plus about 0.16% from its slot `n+2` validators. **At β = 5% the honest block wins, and comfortably.**
That is the boost doing its job, and it is why the simple version of this attack is not a live threat
at small stake.

**Subgoal 5 — find the crossing point, and notice what actually moves it.** The adversary's banked
total scales with how many consecutive slots it drew: `l` slots at β each. The condition is `W_PB`
greater than `l·β`.<Cite src="fc-proposer-boost-considerations" /> At `l = 2` the adversary needs
β above 20% of a committee's worth to break even — which is why the design analysis treats β = 0.2 as
the maximum adversary defensible at `l = 2`, and notes such an adversary gets the required run of two
slots once every 25 blocks.<Cite src="fc-proposer-boost-considerations" /> **The lever is not stake
alone; it is stake multiplied by the length of the run.** An adversary that cannot afford 20% of the
stake can wait for the shuffle to hand it three slots in a row and attack then, and the refinements in
the literature are precisely about lowering what each of those slots has to
contribute.<Cite src="three-attacks-pos-ethereum" />

## Ex post: take the block after seeing it

Now the mirror image, and the thing that makes it interesting is how little it requires.

The adversary is the proposer of slot `n+1`. Block `n` has been published by an honest proposer and
the adversary can see it. Instead of building on block `n`, it builds on block `n`'s *parent*. Its own
block is timely, so it receives the boost. If the boost plus whatever attestations the adversary has
withheld outweighs what block `n` has accumulated, honest attesters in slot `n+1` compute a head on the
adversary's branch and block `n` is orphaned.

Count what the adversary needed.

**No advance planning.** The decision is made after seeing the victim. There is nothing to withhold
across earlier slots — although withholding helps, it is not required.

**No equivocation.** One block, one parent choice. No conflicting signatures anywhere.

**No unusual network position.** The adversary publishes normally and on time. It *wants* its block to
propagate widely; that is how it collects the boost at every node.

**And no protocol violation.** Building on a grandparent is a legal parent choice. The block is
well-formed, the state transition is valid, and no rule has been broken. This is not a loophole being
squeezed — it is the fork choice being used as specified, by a proposer whose interests differ from the
network's.

The cost is where the previous lesson's arithmetic returns. The design document's worked case: a 10%
adversary proposing blocks `n` and `n+2` accumulates 10% from its slot `n` validators, 10% from its slot
`n+2` validators, and the boost — and at a boost of 70% that totals 90%, equal to the honest weight
voting for block `n+1`.<Cite src="fc-proposer-boost-considerations" /> Two thirds of the adversary's
weight in that calculation is the boost. **The defence against ex-ante attacks is the ammunition for
ex-post ones**, and the required inequality flips: against the sandwich you need `1 - β` greater than
`W_PB + l·β`, and against the pure ex-post case `W_PB` less than `1 -
β`.<Cite src="fc-proposer-boost-considerations" />

### Sandwich, and why the naming gets confusing

The ten-percent example is a *sandwich*: adversarial blocks on both sides of an honest one. It combines
both directions, which is why the document analyses it separately.

The design note is precise about where the boundary sits. The sandwich security condition
`1 - β` greater than `W_PB + l·β` holds only for `l` greater than one; for `l = 1` there is no ex-ante
component at all, and the sandwich is really just an ex-post reorg, which a minority adversary can
perform by abusing the boost.<Cite src="fc-proposer-boost-considerations" /> The extra β term drops out
because it would have come from the honest slot, and the adversary does not yet have a block for those
validators to vote for.<Cite src="fc-proposer-boost-considerations" />

Carry that distinction. "Sandwich reorg" in the fork-choice literature is a structural description —
adversarial blocks either side of the victim — and has nothing to do with the sandwich attacks you met
in [MEV](/c/mev-sandwich), which are about transaction ordering inside a block. Same word, different
layer, and the two get conflated constantly.

The last row is the one that turns this from theory into an operational problem. An ex-ante attack at
least leaves a fingerprint: a block that arrived late. An ex-post reorg leaves nothing. A proposer
building on a grandparent instead of a parent is doing something the specification explicitly
contemplates — as the next lesson but one shows, clients ship a rule that tells honest proposers to do
exactly that under stated conditions.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> **From the chain alone you cannot
tell an ex-post reorg from a client following its own reorg rule.** You can only compare against the
gating conditions and observe whether they held.

## Reading a reorg you did not cause

Three questions, in order, when a block you cared about leaves the canonical chain.

**First: was the orphaned block late?** Timeliness is a per-node judgement — `is_timely` compares
arrival against the attestation deadline in the receiving node's own
store<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> — so the honest answer is a distribution across observers, not a
fact. If your node saw it late, many others did too, and the most likely explanation is an ordinary
late proposal, not an attack. If your node saw it on time and it was orphaned anyway, that is the
interesting case.

**Second: who proposed the block that replaced it, and where did they build?** An ex-post reorg has a
signature that is suggestive without being conclusive: the next proposer built on the grandparent
while the parent was timely and well-attested. That combination is what the honest reorg rule is
gated *against*, so seeing it is a reason to look harder.

**Third: did the same entity propose both surrounding slots?** The sandwich requires adversarial slots
on both sides.<Cite src="fc-proposer-boost-considerations" /> Consecutive slots from one operator are
common by chance at any meaningful stake share, so this is a prior, not a finding.

<Aside kind="warn">
What you cannot conclude from any of this is intent, and stating it as if you could is how
postmortems become defamation. Every step of both attacks is executable by correct software behaving
correctly under an unlucky combination of latency and shuffling. The May 2022 seven-block reorg on
mainnet was caused by exactly such a combination — a late block, a partially rolled-out fork-choice
change, and a known implementation fault about *when* the fork choice is run — with no adversary
anywhere in the story.<Cite src="monnot-reorg-post" /> Write up mechanism and impact. Leave attribution
to people who have evidence you do not.
</Aside>

<Check type="predict" concept="ex-post-reorg">
You operate a bridge. Your L1 relayer credits a destination-chain transfer once the source deposit has
`k` blocks on top of it, and `k` is currently 8 — a number chosen years ago by a departed engineer.

A deposit lands in slot `n`. In slot `n+1` the block at `n` is orphaned; the new block at `n+1` builds
on block `n-1`. Your deposit transaction is included again two slots later, with a different
transaction index and a different block hash.

Your on-call engineer wants to raise `k` to 32. Say whether that helps, what it costs, and what the
correct fix is.

<Answer>
**Diagnose first: this is an ordinary single-block reorg, and `k` was never the wrong number for it.**
Eight blocks is already far more than a one-block reorg needs. What failed was not the depth but the
assumption underneath it — that a transaction, once seen at a depth, keeps its block hash and its
index. It was re-included, so the *transaction* survived while its coordinates did not. Any code that
keyed on `(blockHash, txIndex)` is now holding a key that no longer resolves.

**Raising `k` to 32 buys a specific thing and it is not the thing that broke.** Depth protects against
deeper reorgs. It does not make a transaction's coordinates stable, because a reorg at any depth
rewrites the coordinates of everything above it. And the deeper reorgs `k = 32` is aimed at are the ones
proposer boost is specifically bad at: an adversary controlling several consecutive slots needs only
`W_PB` less than `l·β` to succeed at depth `l`,<Cite src="fc-proposer-boost-considerations" /> and the
published refinements reach long-range reorgs with a vanishingly small stake fraction and no control
over propagation.<Cite src="three-attacks-pos-ethereum" /> Depth is a guess against an adversary whose
reach is not a function of your guess.

**What it costs is not zero and should be stated.** At 12 seconds a slot,<Cite src="fc-config-mainnet-v161" />
`k = 32` is 6.4 minutes of added latency on every transfer, for every user, forever — to defend against a
class of event that is genuinely possible and that your `k = 8` also failed to survive in a way depth
does not address. You are buying a worse product to not fix the bug.

**The correct fix has three parts, and only the third is about depth.**

*Key on the transaction, not on its location.* Track the deposit by transaction hash and by the
`(from, nonce)` pair, and treat block hash and index as mutable metadata. Then a re-inclusion is an
update, not a new event. This is the same discipline as
[reorg-safe indexing](/c/reorg-safe-indexing) and it is the actual defect here.

*Make the credit reversible, or make it wait for a guarantee that is not depth-based.* Depth is a
proxy for irreversibility and a poor one. The real guarantee is finality, which costs 64 to 95 slots
in practice — and if that is too slow for the product, that is a real constraint with a named research
answer, which is the last lesson in this module. Choose deliberately between "fast and reversible in
our ledger" and "slow and final"; do not land in between by picking an integer.

*Then, and only then, pick `k` — and write down the adversary it assumes.* If you keep a depth rule as
a cheap first gate, document what it defends against: a `k`-block reorg by an adversary that draws `k`
consecutive slots. That sentence is falsifiable and reviewable. "Eight because eight" is not.

**One thing the on-call engineer should also check.** Whether the orphaned block was *late*. If it was,
this may not have been an attack at all but the honest late-block reorg rule doing its
job<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /> — a deliberate, specified, expected event that your system must
survive as a matter of course. That distinction changes the incident's severity, and it does not
change the fix.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

- **A vocabulary that survives contact with a postmortem.** Ante and post are defined relative to the
  publication of the victim block, not to anything about severity, and the two need different
  resources from an adversary. When someone says "a reorg attack", the first question is which side of
  the line.
- **The tradeoff as lived experience rather than an inequality.** You have now seen the same 1.25% of
  stake acting as a shield in one timeline and a weapon in the
  other.<Cite src="fork-choice-spec" /><Cite src="fc-proposer-boost-considerations" /> That is what the
  module's reflection prompt is asking you to take a position on, and you now have the numbers to
  defend one.
- **Neither attack is slashable, and that is structural.** Both are executed with singly-signed,
  well-formed, on-time-or-late-but-legal messages. Punishment is not available as a defence, which is
  why every mitigation in this module is a fork-choice rule rather than a penalty.
- **Next: the attack that does not want to win.** Both attacks here try to make one branch heavier. The
  balancing attack tries to make neither branch heavier, needs far less stake, and requires
  equivocation — which introduces the one place where the spec does discount a validator's weight, and
  the reasons that discount is harder to apply than it
  looks.<Cite src="neu-tas-tse-two-attacks" /><Cite src="ethereum-org-pos-attack-defense" />
