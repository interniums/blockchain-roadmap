Before any of the designs in this lesson, one result. It is the reason they all exist, and it is
stated precisely enough to settle an argument.

The Goldfish paper proves that with proposer boost, LMD-GHOST is **not reorg resilient for β at or
above 1/4** — even at full participation and without subsampling — because the two required properties
are in conflict for such an adversary, *for any boost value*.<Cite src="goldfish-paper" /> The
derivation is two inequalities you have already met wearing different clothes. Reorg resilience against
the ex-ante direction holds only if the boost `W_p` exceeds `2β`, because otherwise the adversary
concludes an ex-ante reorg by revealing later votes that move all of its weight β from the proposer's
branch to a conflicting one and outweigh the boost. Resilience against the ex-post direction holds only
if `W_p + β` is less than `1 - β`, because otherwise an adversarial proposer uses the boost to conclude
an ex-post reorg. Both together require `3β` less than `W_p + β` less than `1 - β`, which is β less
than 1/4, achieved by setting `W_p` = 1/2.<Cite src="goldfish-paper" />

That is not "there is a tension". That is a proved ceiling on what tuning a constant can achieve, with
the optimal constant named. Every proposal below is an attempt to get above the ceiling by changing
something other than the constant.

## View merge: remove the disagreement instead of outvoting it

Proposer boost compensates for validators having different views. View merge tries to make their views
the same.

The mechanism, in the Goldfish paper's own terms, is called **message buffering**: each validator
buffers votes received from the network and carefully times the inclusion of those votes into its local
view, **with priority given to the votes relayed by the proposer**. The consequence is that in slots
with an honest proposer, all honest validators adopt the view of the proposer and vote in favour of the
proposed block — which is what gives the protocol reorg resilience, meaning honest proposals are
guaranteed to enter the ledgers output by honest validators.<Cite src="goldfish-paper" />

The name confusion is worth clearing up, because you will meet both words. The paper carries a footnote
recording that the message-buffering technique was publicised under the name **view-merge** by an author
of the work in a blog post dated October 2021, and that a similar technique had been used in the Highway
protocol in unpublished work in January 2021.<Cite src="goldfish-paper" /> The blog name is what
circulates in Ethereum research discussion; the paper name is what appears in the proofs. They are the
same mechanism.

Trace it against the balancing attack from lesson five, because the paper does exactly that. With
message buffering, honest validators do not immediately consider the adversary's late-released vote for
the competing block. They include that vote into their views only *after* voting for the honest block,
which prevents the adversary from swaying them.<Cite src="goldfish-paper" /> The attack depended on
delivering weight to each half at the moment its fork choice executed. Buffering makes that moment
insensitive to what arrives during it.

Two further properties of the proposal matter for judging it as an engineering proposition rather than
a paper.

**It replaces proposer boost rather than supplementing it.** The paper's own drop-in analysis says vote
expiry and message buffering would have to be introduced, with the latter replacing proposer
boost.<Cite src="goldfish-paper" /> There is no design in which both exist; they are two answers to the
same question.

**It leaves the surrounding machinery mostly alone, and says where it does not.** Proposer selection
need not be overhauled — Goldfish can operate with RANDAO<Cite src="goldfish-paper" /> — but the
interaction with Casper FFG would have to be modified to fit, and the paper notes the awkwardness
explicitly: FFG votes are cast together with LMD-GHOST votes on a schedule staggered through the epoch,
so it is not clear how to ensure that honest validators' views are consistent when casting FFG
votes.<Cite src="goldfish-paper" /> That unresolved interaction is a real reason the design has not
moved from paper to spec, and it is more informative than any adoption rumour.

## RLMD-GHOST: give votes an expiry date

The second proposal isolates a different property, and the attack that motivates it is worth walking
because it does not need equivocation, timing control, or stake.

The Goldfish paper's construction: take a validator set of size `n = 2m + 1`, split into `V1` and `V2`
of size `m` each, both honest, and `V3` of size one, adversarial. The adversary leads slot `t` and
broadcasts two conflicting proposals `B1` and `B2` such that `V1` sees only `B1` before voting and `V2`
only `B2`. `V1` votes for `B1`, and so does the adversary, so `B1` becomes canonical with `m + 1` votes.
The adversary then puts `V2` to sleep — they never wake again. `V1` keeps voting for descendants of
`B1`. The adversary casts nothing for a long time, and then, after more than any finite confirmation
time, votes for `B2`. Because the `m` latest votes of the sleeping `V2` are *still* for `B2`, it now has
`m + 1` votes and becomes canonical, reorging everything confirmed after slot `t`.<Cite src="goldfish-paper" />

The paper's conclusion is stronger than "here is another attack": without vote expiry, the votes of
honest asleep validators can be weaponised to execute an arbitrarily long reorg, which implies the
protocol is not dynamically available with *any* confirmation rule with finite confirmation
time.<Cite src="goldfish-paper" /> Latest-message-only means a validator's weight sits where it last
put it *forever*, including after that validator has permanently departed.

RLMD-GHOST is the parameterised fix. It introduces a vote expiry period η: only votes from the most
recent η slots are used, so the expiration period for slot `t` is the interval `[t − η, t)` and votes
broadcast before slot `t − η` are not considered.<Cite src="rlmd-ghost-paper" /> The parameter spans the
whole design space: at η = ∞ the rule reduces to LMD-GHOST, and at η = 1 it reduces to a variant of
Goldfish without subsampling of validators.<Cite src="rlmd-ghost-paper" />

What the parameter trades is stated plainly: considering votes from a longer period, η greater than one,
results in a protocol that is more tolerant of asynchrony, while shorter periods give better dynamic
availability.<Cite src="rlmd-ghost-paper" /> The paper's headline is that RLMD-GHOST is a synchronous
protocol that not only ensures dynamic availability but also maintains safety during bounded periods of
asynchrony — which it argues is appealing for practical systems where strict synchrony assumptions may
not always hold.<Cite src="rlmd-ghost-paper" />

**Notice what this is really for, because it is easy to file under "another fork-choice variant".** It
is a diagnostic instrument. By making the vote window a knob, it isolates precisely which property of
LMD-GHOST each attack is exploiting: attacks that need stale weight from departed validators are
attacks on η = ∞, and they vanish at finite η. That is why it belongs in this module even though nobody
is proposing to ship it — it tells you which sentence in the current rule is load-bearing for which
failure.

<Aside kind="note">
Both papers state the diagnosis in the same terms, which is a good sign they have found the right one.
Goldfish: attacks on LMD-GHOST exploit a lack of coordination among honest validators, typically
provided by a locking mechanism in classical BFT protocols — but locking requires votes from a quorum of
all participants and is not compatible with fluctuating
participation.<Cite src="goldfish-paper" /> RLMD-GHOST's abstract puts the consequence bluntly: despite
attempts to mitigate the balancing attack, the modified protocol still does not achieve dynamic
availability, so LMD-GHOST remains unable to fulfil its intended function of providing dynamic
availability for the protocol.<Cite src="rlmd-ghost-paper" /> Both are describing the same trade: the
protocol gave up BFT's coordination primitive in order to tolerate validators coming and going, and the
attacks are the invoice for that decision.
</Aside>

## Three-slot finality: shrink the arena instead of policing it

The first two proposals fix the head rule. This one asks why there is so much unfinalised chain for the
head rule to fight over.

Start with the motivation, in the paper's numbers. Gasper typically requires 64 to 95 slots to finalise
— so under ideal conditions, with a synchronous network and more than two thirds of validators behaving
correctly, proposers construct blocks on a non-finalised chain extending at least 64
blocks.<Cite src="3sf-paper" /> The consequences the paper names are the ones this module has been
demonstrating: a significant portion of the blockchain is exposed to reorganisations during changes in
network conditions, the delay heightens exposure to MEV exploitation, and the extended period forces
users to trade economic security against transaction speed.<Cite src="3sf-paper" />

The construction: a partially synchronous finality gadget, combined with two dynamically available
consensus protocols — synchronous protocols that ensure safety and liveness even with fluctuating
participation — producing a secure ebb-and-flow protocol that achieves finality within three slots after
a proposal.<Cite src="3sf-paper" /> The [ebb-and-flow](/c/ebb-and-flow) framing is the same one you met
in the fundamentals track: an available ledger and a finalised prefix, with different guarantees under
different network conditions.

If blocks finalise three slots after proposal rather than 64 to 95 slots after, the tree of unfinalised
blocks that fork choice operates over is a handful of blocks deep instead of a couple of epochs. Every
attack in lessons four through six needs room to work inside that tree. The attacks do not get defended
against; they get evicted for lack of space.

### The name is a correction, and it is the paper family's own correction

"Single slot finality" is a family name. The concrete leading design finalises in three slots, and the
ethresear.ch write-up exists largely to say so — its title is "3-Slot-Finality: SSF is not about
'Single' Slot".<Cite src="3sf-ethresearch" /> The paper itself claims finality within three slots after
a proposal, not one.<Cite src="3sf-paper" />

## Orbit: who votes, and how often

Fast finality has a cost that is not cryptographic: it needs a lot of validators voting a lot more
often. Orbit is the half of the plan that addresses that.

ethereum.org sets out the underlying arithmetic. The current design's capacity comes from spreading
attestations across an epoch: 32 slots × 64 committees × 256 validators per committee = 524,288
validators per epoch.<Cite src="ethereum-org-ssf-roadmap" /> Voting every validator every slot means
either a much larger per-slot load or a smaller voting set. The page's supercommittee framing takes the
second route and prices it: instead of requiring two thirds of total staked ether, an attacker would
need two thirds of the stake in the supercommittee, and preliminary community polls suggest 1 to 2
million ether is an acceptable cost of attack, implying roughly 65,536 to 97,152 validators per
supercommittee.<Cite src="ethereum-org-ssf-roadmap" />

Orbit is a specific design in that space, from a research post rather than from the roadmap page — and
ethereum.org does not use the name.<Cite src="ethereum-org-ssf-roadmap" /><Cite src="fc-orbit-ssf-ethresearch" />
Its stated goals are a set of properties held simultaneously: validator capping at some N active
validators at a time, with `N = 2^15 ≈ 32k` given as the example because that is the size of a committee
today; solo staking viability, meaning staking with 32 ETH remains possible with a yield not compared
unfavourably to delegated staking; high *eventual* economic security, with 20M ETH given as an example
threshold; fast finality, meaning some economic security available shortly after a proposal — the post
says think 10 to 30 seconds, not over 10 minutes; and an optimally secure consensus protocol, provably
resilient to roughly 1/2 adversaries under synchrony and 1/3 under partial
synchrony.<Cite src="fc-orbit-ssf-ethresearch" />

The mechanism is a change in *how* the active set is sampled. Rather than sampling by index, as
committees do today, validators are sampled by balance: with a stake threshold T, a validator with stake
S is selected into the active set with probability `min(S/T, 1)`, so a validator below the threshold is
selected proportionally to its stake and one at or above it is always in the
set.<Cite src="fc-orbit-ssf-ethresearch" /> The post's name for the shape is *stable core, rotating
periphery*, and its argument is that large validators contribute a lot of economic security while adding
the same load as any other index.<Cite src="fc-orbit-ssf-ethresearch" />

### The sentence in that post that ties this module together

The Orbit post explains why fast committee rotation — the obvious approach — is hard, and the
explanation is the mechanism of lesson four:

Much of the problems with the LMD-GHOST component of today's protocol come precisely from the
interaction of multiple committees; in short, an adversary can **accumulate weight across multiple
committees and use it to reorg honest blocks that have a single committee supporting
them**.<Cite src="fc-orbit-ssf-ethresearch" />

That is the ex-ante reorg, restated as a structural property of committee-based voting. The post is
explicit about the state of the art: there are optimally secure dynamically available consensus
protocols that allow committees, but all known ones suffer catastrophic failures under even short-lived
asynchrony, and it is not known whether this is a fundamental
limitation.<Cite src="fc-orbit-ssf-ethresearch" /> Orbit's answer is to avoid the problem rather than
solve it, by rotating slowly enough that the active committee effectively acts as a full validator
set.<Cite src="fc-orbit-ssf-ethresearch" />

## What is actually scheduled, and how to tell

The contrast is the most useful thing in this lesson, because it gives you a test you can run yourself.

**A change on a shipping track has spec text in a fork directory.** The Glamsterdam fork-choice changes
exist as `specs/gloas/fork-choice.md` in consensus-specs, and they are substantial: the fork-choice tree
gains a `payload_status` per node, `LatestMessage` is modified to track slot and payload presence
instead of epoch, the store gains `ptc_vote`, and a new `PAYLOAD_TIMELY_THRESHOLD` is defined as
`PTC_SIZE // 2` (= 256).<Cite src="fc-gloas-fork-choice-v161" /> That is what a fork-choice change with
a fork name attached looks like: a file, in a directory named after the fork, with constants in it.

**Glamsterdam's headline EIPs are EIP-7732 (enshrined proposer-builder separation, Standards Track:
Core, in peer review) and EIP-7928 (block-level access
lists).**<Cite src="eip-7732" /><Cite src="eip-7928" /> It has not shipped and there is no mainnet
date; timing claims in circulation contradict one another, and the working record is the ethereum/pm
all-core-devs notes rather than any article.<Cite src="ethereum-pm" />

**Two candidates were considered for Glamsterdam and declined, and both remain Draft.** EIP-7805
(FOCIL, fork-choice enforced inclusion lists) and EIP-7782 (Reduce Block Latency, cutting slot time from
12s to 6s) were both declined for Glamsterdam and deferred to a later
fork.<Cite src="eip-7805" /><Cite src="eip-7782" /><Cite src="ethereum-pm" /> Note that the second one
would have changed a number this module has used throughout: `SLOT_DURATION_MS` remains
12000.<Cite src="fc-config-mainnet-v161" />

**None of the four designs in this lesson has any of that.** No EIP number, no fork directory, no
configuration entry. The applicable test, in order: is there an EIP; is there a file under `specs/` in a
fork-named directory; are its constants in `configs/mainnet.yaml`; and has an all-core-devs call
scheduled it. Four proposals in this lesson score zero on all four questions, and that is a fact about
their status rather than about their quality.

## What this unlocks

- **One number worth remembering from this whole module.** With proposer boost, LMD-GHOST is not reorg
  resilient for β at or above 1/4 for any boost value, and the best available setting is `W_p` =
  1/2.<Cite src="goldfish-paper" /> The shipped value is
  40.<Cite src="fc-spec-v1-2-0" /><Cite src="fc-config-mainnet-v161" /> The gap between those two facts
  is the module's reflection prompt in numeric form.
- **A diagnosis, not a list of alternatives.** The attacks exist because LMD-GHOST lacks the
  coordination that BFT locking provides, and locking was rejected because it needs a quorum of all
  participants and does not tolerate fluctuating
  participation.<Cite src="goldfish-paper" /> Each proposal restores coordination differently:
  buffering restores it through the proposer's view, expiry through a window, 3SF by removing the arena.
- **A status test you can run on any claim.** EIP number, spec file under a fork directory, constants in
  `configs/mainnet.yaml`, an all-core-devs decision — in that
  order.<Cite src="fc-gloas-fork-choice-v161" /><Cite src="fc-config-mainnet-v161" /><Cite src="ethereum-pm" />
  It costs a minute and it is the difference between a roadmap and a rumour.
- **Next: the thing that is available now.** Every proposal here is years away, and the product problem
  they address — waiting minutes for a guarantee — is present today. The last lesson is about a rule
  that gives a much faster answer under an explicitly stated assumption, and about why stating the
  assumption is the whole value of it.
