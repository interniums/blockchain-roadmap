Open the blob store of a healthy mainnet node after a busy slot and look for a rollup batch.

There isn't one. There are four columns' worth of cells — 2,048 bytes each — and each of those cells
is 1/128th of a different rollup's extended batch. The node holds a sliver of Base's data, a sliver
of Arbitrum's, a sliver of every blob in that block, and a complete copy of none of
them.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> If a user turned up asking for the batch that
contains their withdrawal, this node cannot produce it from what it stores, and it is behaving
exactly as specified.

That is the design decision this lesson is about: **custody runs vertically.** It is the most
consequential choice in PeerDAS, it is the source of the module's best picture, and it is attached to
a claim that circulates in a stronger form than the specification supports.

## The matrix

Arrange the block's blobs as rows, each row being a blob plus its erasure extension. The result has
at most `MAX_BLOBS_PER_BLOCK` rows and a fixed `NUMBER_OF_COLUMNS`
columns.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> The spec's own helper types name the axes:

```python
class MatrixEntry(Container):
    cell: Cell
    kzg_proof: KZGProof
    column_index: ColumnIndex
    row_index: RowIndex
```

`RowIndex` is documented as the row identifier in the matrix of cells and `ColumnIndex` as the column
identifier; `compute_matrix(blobs)` walks blobs as rows and cell indices as
columns.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> A **column is one cell index taken across every
blob in the block.**

Fix the two dimensions in your head, because they behave completely differently:

- **Rows vary per block.** How many there are depends on how many blob transactions the proposer
  included, capped by the blob schedule — `MAX_BLOBS_PER_BLOCK` is 21 from epoch 419072 in the config
  read on 26 August 2026.<Cite src="consensus-specs-v161-mainnet-config" />
- **Columns never vary.** `NUMBER_OF_COLUMNS` is a **preset** constant defined as
  `uint64(CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB)` — 128, and preset constants are not what a blob-parameter-only fork
  changes.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-preset" /><Cite src="eip-7892" />

So the matrix grows *downward*. An empty block is a 0 × 128 matrix. A full one at the current cap is
21 × 128. There is no configuration in which it becomes 21 × 256.

The wire format says the same thing in its types. A `DataColumnSidecar` declares
`column: List[Cell, MAX_BLOB_COMMITMENTS_PER_BLOCK]` — a *list*, whose length is this block's blob
count, alongside `kzg_commitments` and `kzg_proofs` lists of the same
length.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> One sidecar is one column of the matrix, and it
gets taller as blocks get fuller.

## Why vertical, when horizontal is the obvious choice

Handing each node a few whole blobs would be simpler to explain and simpler to serve. Both the EIP
and the spec's FAQ address why that is not the design, and the reasons are worth having verbatim
because they are not the reasons people guess.

**Reconstruction is tractable over columns and awkward over rows.** EIP-7594's rationale says the
sampling scheme could be defined over rows, but then any reconstruction strategy would need to work
over "extension" blobs that do not a priori exist on the network; working over columns makes
reconstruction much more tractable because nodes can be assumed to have much more of the complete
data by default, for instance because most or all of the blobs are in the public
mempool.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

**Row sampling would put the extension on the critical path.** Sampling over rows requires *column*
extensions, which would have to be computed at block construction time; sampling over columns
requires *row* extensions, which can be done in advance, off the critical path, and whose proof
computation can be outsourced to the senders of blob
transactions.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> That is the same asymmetry the previous lesson traced through
the transaction wrapper, showing up as a scheduling argument.

**Rollups would not benefit from row custody anyway.** The das-core FAQ takes the strongest argument
for rows — that a rollup would naturally want its own blob — and dismantles it: a rollup does not
know a priori which row its blob will occupy in the block, so it would either need to listen to all
rows, which looks exactly like listening to every column, or fetch a particular row after seeing the
block, which is a request/response interaction rather than
gossip.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />

The FAQ also concedes what row custody would have bought — a more natural distribution to consumers,
and a distributed reconstruction story where holders of full rows compute extensions and seed missing
samples — and says the current design forgoes both for
simplicity.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> A specification that lists the advantages of
the road not taken is unusual, and it is a better guide than any summary of it.

## What it costs to want a whole blob

Vertical custody has a consequence that lands on products rather than on protocol: **getting one blob
is now a gather, not a fetch.**

Recovering row *i* needs 64 of that row's 128 cells.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> Cell
*j* of row *i* lives in column *j*, and no single node is obliged to hold more than its own custody
groups — four of them at the minimum.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> So assembling one
rollup's batch from a cold start means collecting 64 distinct columns, which at the minimum custody
setting is at least sixteen cooperating peers, and in practice means finding peers who hold more or
finding a supernode.

The request/response protocol is sized for that gather rather than for one-off lookups.
`MAX_REQUEST_DATA_COLUMN_SIDECARS` is `MAX_REQUEST_BLOCKS_DENEB * NUMBER_OF_COLUMNS` — 128 × 128 =
16,384 sidecars in a single request.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-v161-mainnet-config" />
And a responder is not permitted to give you a scattering: clients must include all data column
sidecars of each block from which they include any.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> The unit
of conversation is "all of these columns, for these blocks", not "this blob".

Three practical readings of that, in descending order of how often they bite.

**An indexer that reads blob data cannot get it from an ordinary node.** It needs either a supernode,
a node that reconstructs, or a provider that does one of those on its behalf. "Run a full node" is no
longer a sufficient answer to "where does our blob data come from".

**A rollup node reading its own batches has the easiest path and should still not assume it.** Its
data was in the public transaction mempool before the block, so the execution layer may still have
the blob whole — which is the escape hatch lesson seven is about — but that path is bounded by the
execution client's blob pool retention, not by the 4,096-epoch consensus
window.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" />

**The window and the gather compound.** Eighteen days is the period during which nodes must *serve*
their columns.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> Inside it, retrieval is a coordination problem;
outside it, retrieval is somebody's archive or nothing.

## The claim that needs trimming

Now the sentence you will meet everywhere: *because custody is per column, per-node bandwidth is
independent of how many blobs a block carries.*

The first half is right and the second half is not. Work it in bytes.

**Subgoal 1 — fix what a node actually moves per slot, since custody and sampling are different
quantities.** An honest node with the minimum settings custodies `CUSTODY_REQUIREMENT` = 4 custody
groups and downloads `sampling_size = max(SAMPLES_PER_SLOT, custody_group_count)` groups per slot,
which at the minimum is 8.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> With
`NUMBER_OF_CUSTODY_GROUPS` = `NUMBER_OF_COLUMNS` = 128, that is 4 columns kept and 8 columns
fetched.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-preset" />

**Subgoal 2 — price one column, because the price depends on the block.** One column is one cell per
blob at `BYTES_PER_CELL` = 2,048.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-preset" /> So a column costs 2,048
bytes per blob in the block, plus 96 bytes per blob of commitment and proof and a small fixed
header.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />

**Subgoal 3 — multiply out at three blob counts and read the pattern.**

Every column of that table is linear in the blob count. Nothing is constant except the *ratio*: the
node always fetches 8/128 of the extended matrix, which is 1/16 of it and therefore 1/8 of the
original blob data — the figure EIP-7594 states for current
parameters.<Cite src="eip-7594" /><Cite src="ethereum-org-peerdas" />

**Subgoal 4 — say what was actually bought.** A constant divisor. Before Fulu a node moved 100% of
the blob data; now it moves 12.5% of it. Multiply capacity by four and the node's traffic goes up by
four from a base one-eighth as large — so a node at a 48-blob cap moves less than a node at the old
6-blob cap did under whole-blob distribution. That is a real, large, structural win. It is not
independence, and calling it independence predicts that capacity can be raised without limit, which
is false.

### One sidecar plus local data reproduces all 128

The matrix picture pays off in a helper that is easy to miss.
`get_data_column_sidecars_from_column_sidecar(sidecar, cells_and_kzg_proofs)` takes **any single**
column sidecar and the cells and proofs for the blobs it commits to, and returns the full sequence of
sidecars — all 128 columns.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-validator" />

The reason it works is pure geometry. Every column sidecar for a block carries the same
`kzg_commitments`, the same inclusion proof and the same signed block
header;<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> only the `index` and the cells differ. So one
sidecar supplies the entire per-block context, and any source of the rows supplies the rest. The spec
names that source explicitly: this is for distributed blob publishing, reconstructing all sidecars
from any sidecar received on the wire, assuming all cells and proofs could be retrieved from the
local execution layer client.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-validator" />

Read what that means operationally. A node that sees *one* column of a block on gossip and has the
blobs in its own execution client's pool can rebuild and publish every other column — turning a
proposer's partial publication into a complete one without anyone reconstructing anything
cryptographically. It is the cheapest repair path in the system, and it exists because the block-level
context is replicated into every column rather than split across them.

## Where the ceiling actually is

If the divisor were the whole story, the limit would be a home node's link. It is not, because the
network needs a class of node that gets no divisor at all.

A **supernode** subscribes to every data column sidecar subnet, custodies every column and performs
reconstruction and cross-seeding.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> The p2p spec states the
requirement bluntly: in order to reconstruct missing data, there must be at least one supernode on
the network.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> Supernodes are not optional infrastructure that
would be nice to have.

Some are conscripted. A node connected to validators with a combined balance of at least 4,096 ETH
must be a supernode<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> — which is exactly
`NUMBER_OF_CUSTODY_GROUPS` × `BALANCE_PER_ADDITIONAL_CUSTODY_GROUP`, 128 groups at 32 ETH per
additional group, where the balance-scaled custody requirement hits its
cap.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-validator" /> The spec expects hundreds of them on mainnet, and
expects a node to be connected to several by chance.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" />

Now apply the arithmetic to that class. A supernode holds all 128 columns, which is the entire
extended matrix: **twice** the block's raw blob data, with no fraction in front of it. At 21 blobs
that is 5.25 MiB per slot; at 48 it would be 12 MiB per slot. Supernode cost scales linearly with
capacity and starts at 2×.

So the honest picture of PeerDAS's scaling headroom has two curves, not one. Ordinary nodes are cheap
and stay cheap for a long time. Reconstruction capacity is carried by nodes for whom the extension is
pure overhead, and how many of those exist is an empirical property of the network rather than
something the spec guarantees beyond "at least one".

### The lever that would move the divisor, and the seam prepared for it

EIP-7594 names the way out: the 1/8 can be decreased to 1/16 or 1/32 by reducing the size of samples,
which means increasing the number of columns.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> Smaller cells, more of them, and
a node's fixed handful of samples covers a smaller slice.

The spec already carries the seam that would absorb that change, and it is easy to walk past:

```python
def compute_columns_for_custody_group(custody_group: CustodyIndex) -> Sequence[ColumnIndex]:
    columns_per_group = NUMBER_OF_COLUMNS // NUMBER_OF_CUSTODY_GROUPS
    return [ColumnIndex(NUMBER_OF_CUSTODY_GROUPS * i + custody_group)
            for i in range(columns_per_group)]
```

Today `NUMBER_OF_COLUMNS` and `NUMBER_OF_CUSTODY_GROUPS` are both 128, so `columns_per_group` is 1
and the function is an identity dressed as a
loop.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-preset" /> It is
written as a loop because the two constants are separate quantities that are allowed to diverge: a
custody group is a *bundle of columns*, and the bundle currently contains one.

What that division buys is a way to add columns without changing how much anyone custodies. Double
the columns while holding the group count, and every group becomes two columns — same fraction of the
matrix, same sampling arithmetic, more granular pieces. Whether a future change would move the
divisor by holding the group count or by moving both is not something the current specification says,
and it would be a preset change either way rather than a blob-parameter fork. What is safe to carry
is the design intent: the indirection exists so the column count is not welded to the custody
accounting.

## What this unlocks

You have the picture the rest of the module annotates: rows are blobs, columns are fixed at 128, a
node owns vertical stripes and no complete row.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> You also
have the trimmed version of the scaling claim, which is the thing to carry into any capacity
discussion.

**Next: which stripes are yours.** The assignment is not random, not chosen and not handed out by a
coordinator — it falls out of your node id by repeated hashing, through an intermediate abstraction
called a custody group that most explanations skip. That indirection is what makes it possible to
find a peer who *must* have the column you are missing without asking anyone whether they have it.

**And keep the two-curve picture.** When someone asks how far blob capacity can go, the interesting
constraint is rarely the home node. It is the class of node that gets no divisor and is required to
exist.
