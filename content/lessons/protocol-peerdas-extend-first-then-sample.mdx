A rollup batcher builds a blob and, before it can even gossip the transaction, does 128 pieces of
elliptic-curve work it did not have to do a year ago. It produces 128 KZG proofs — one per cell of
the *extended* blob, including the half of the extension it is not going to
transmit.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> Then it sends 128 KiB of blob and 6 KiB of proofs, and the network
turns that into 128 independently checkable pieces without asking the batcher again.

Two design decisions are visible in that paragraph, and this lesson is about both. The extension is
what makes a random piece informative. The per-cell proof is what makes a random piece *checkable*.
Neither alone is enough, and the split of who computes versus who verifies is the reason the whole
thing is affordable.

## Extension first, because sampling without it is theatre

You have the argument from Scaling: sample the raw data and an adversary hides one piece; erasure-code
it first and the adversary must hide more than half. Here is the same idea as spec constants.

`FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_BLOB` is 4,096.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> `FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_EXT_BLOB` is 8,192 —
`2 * FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_BLOB`, set in the mainnet Fulu
preset.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-preset" /> EIP-7594 describes the construction in one
sentence: the blobs are extended using a one-dimensional erasure coding extension, each row
consisting of the blob data combined with its erasure code, subdivided into
cells.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

**One-dimensional** is doing work in that sentence. There is no second axis. Earlier sharding designs
extended in two dimensions — rows *and* columns — which buys a stronger reconstruction story at the
cost of a much more complicated commitment scheme and a construction step that has to happen while
the block is being built. PeerDAS extends each blob along its own single axis and stops. The
consequence the spec cares about is stated in its FAQ: in reconstruction, a node can reconstruct all
the blobs from 50% of the columns.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />

Note the shape of that guarantee: *any* half. Not a designated half, not the first half. This is what
distinguishes an erasure code from replication, and it is why the extension does not behave like a
backup copy.

## What the 4,096 numbers actually are — and a disagreement worth naming

Here is a question that looks pedantic and is not: *are a blob's 4,096 field elements the
coefficients of a polynomial, or its evaluations?* These are different polynomials. Answer it wrong
and every mental model of extension and reconstruction you build on top is wrong.

Two current sources say different things.

**ethereum.org's PeerDAS page** describes it as coefficient form: blob data are represented as a
polynomial whose coefficients encode the data, and the extension evaluates that polynomial at
additional points, doubling the number of evaluations; in reality, it says, this polynomial has
thousands of coefficients.<Cite src="ethereum-org-peerdas" />

**The consensus spec, at the last tag that carries it, says evaluation form.** In
`deneb/polynomial-commitments.md` at v1.6.1 the `Polynomial` type is documented as "a polynomial in
evaluation form"; the text states that all polynomials are always given in Lagrange form; and
`blob_to_kzg_commitment` is a linear combination against `KZG_SETUP_G1_LAGRANGE` under a bit-reversal
permutation.<Cite src="consensus-specs-deneb-polynomial-commitments" /> A commitment computed against
a Lagrange-basis setup is a commitment to evaluations.

**The shipped trusted setup does not settle it either way, and it is worth knowing why.** The
mainnet `trusted_setup_4096.json` carries three arrays: `g1_monomial` (4,096 points), `g1_lagrange`
(4,096 points) and `g2_monomial` (65 points).<Cite src="consensus-specs-kzg-trusted-setup" /> Both
bases ship. Reaching for the file to adjudicate gets you both answers.

### Where the two descriptions come from

The Fulu sampling spec shows how both sentences got written, because the pipeline uses both forms in
sequence:

```python
def compute_cells_and_kzg_proofs(blob):
    polynomial = blob_to_polynomial(blob)                    # evaluation form
    polynomial_coeff = polynomial_eval_to_coeff(polynomial)  # interpolate to coefficients
    return compute_cells_and_kzg_proofs_polynomialcoeff(polynomial_coeff)
```

`polynomial_eval_to_coeff` is documented as interpolating a polynomial given in evaluation form to a
polynomial in coefficient form, and `PolynomialCoeff` is a distinct type from
`Polynomial`.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-polynomial-commitments-sampling" /> So: the blob's
elements are evaluations; the extension pipeline interpolates them into coefficients and then
evaluates the resulting polynomial on further domains. Both objects are real. They are not the same
object, and "the blob's coefficients" names the wrong one.

### Why the distinction is not cosmetic

Shrink the blob to four field elements to see what is at stake. Call the data `d = [d₀, d₁, d₂, d₃]`.

*Read as coefficients*, the polynomial is `P(x) = d₀ + d₁x + d₂x² + d₃x³`, and recovering the data
from evaluations means interpolating and reading off coefficients.

*Read as evaluations*, the polynomial is the unique cubic with `P(ω⁰) = d₀`, `P(ω¹) = d₁`, `P(ω²) =
d₂`, `P(ω³) = d₃` on the fourth roots of unity. Its coefficient vector is the inverse DFT of `d` —
generally four completely different numbers.

These are two different polynomials over the same data, so they have two different KZG commitments,
and any proof computed against one is rejected against the other. There is no interpretation under
which the choice is a matter of exposition. It is also why the shipped setup carries both bases: a
commitment to evaluations is a linear combination against `KZG_SETUP_G1_LAGRANGE`, while the
extension machinery, which manipulates degrees and divides by a vanishing polynomial, wants the
monomial basis.<Cite src="consensus-specs-deneb-polynomial-commitments" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-kzg-trusted-setup" />

The practical test, if you are ever unsure which convention a codebase uses: look at what
`blob_to_kzg_commitment` combines against. Lagrange setup points means the blob holds evaluations.

`specs/fulu/polynomial-commitments-sampling.md` return 404 on the master branch as of 26 August 2026;
the `specs/` tree there now lists `phase0` through `heze` with no polynomial-commitments file in
either the `deneb` or `fulu` directory.<Cite src="consensus-specs-master-specs-tree" /> The Engine

`master/specs/fulu/polynomial-commitments-sampling.md#verify_cell_kzg_proof_batch` for the

So the honest statement is: **as of the last release-tagged consensus spec, a blob is evaluations in
Lagrange form; a widely-read foundation page says coefficients; and no file currently on the

cited.

## The cell: the smallest thing anyone can hand you

`FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_CELL` is 64, so `BYTES_PER_CELL` is 64 × 32 = 2,048 bytes, and
`CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB` is `FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_EXT_BLOB // FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_CELL` = 8192 / 64 =
**128**.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-preset" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-polynomial-commitments-sampling" />
That 128 is the same 128 as `NUMBER_OF_COLUMNS`, which is literally defined as
`uint64(CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB)`.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-preset" /> One cell index per column,
by construction rather than by coincidence.

A cell is not an arbitrary 2 KiB slice. The sampling spec gives it a type with a meaning: `Coset` is
the evaluation domain of a cell, and `CosetEvals` is a cell's evaluations over its
coset.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-polynomial-commitments-sampling" /> A cell is 64 evaluations of
the extended polynomial on one coset of the roots of unity. That structure is exactly what makes a
single opening proof for the whole cell possible — you are proving 64 evaluations at once because
they live on one algebraic object, not proving 64 unrelated points.

**Why 64 and not 8 or 512?** The spec presents it as a constant, not a derivation, and the honest
answer is a tradeoff rather than an optimum: bigger cells mean fewer proofs per blob and less proof
overhead per byte, smaller cells mean finer sampling granularity and more columns. EIP-7594 states
the direction of travel explicitly — a node currently downloads 1/8 of the total data, and this can
be decreased to 1/16 or 1/32 by reducing the size of samples, which is to say by increasing the
number of columns.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> Cell size and column count are the same dial viewed from
opposite ends.

### The arithmetic that will keep showing up

Carry these four numbers; the rest of the module is built on them.

- An extended blob is 128 cells × 2,048 bytes = 262,144 bytes = **256 KiB**, exactly twice the 128 KiB
  blob.<Cite src="eip-4844" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-preset" />
- One column of a block with *n* blobs is *n* cells = *n* × 2,048 bytes.
- At the config's `MAX_BLOBS_PER_BLOCK` of 21, one column carries 43,008 bytes of cell data — about
  42 KiB — and all 128 columns together are 5.25 MiB.
- A node taking 8 of those columns moves about 336 KiB of cell data per full slot, plus per-sidecar
  overhead: the commitment list and proof list are 48 bytes each per blob, so roughly 2 KiB more per
  column.<Cite src="consensus-specs-deneb-polynomial-commitments" />

The proof overhead is 48 bytes on a 2,048-byte cell — under 2.4%. That ratio is the reason the
per-cell proof scheme is viable at all, and it is worth holding next to the alternative in the next
section.

### Recovery is per row, and it is not free

The recovery function makes the threshold precise in a way the prose summary blurs.
`recover_cells_and_kzg_proofs(cell_indices, cells)` is documented as recovering all cells and proofs
for a blob given at least 50% of that blob's cells, and it asserts the bound directly:
`CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB // 2 <= len(cell_indices) <= CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB`, with the indices distinct,
sorted, in bounds, and every cell exactly `BYTES_PER_CELL`
long.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-polynomial-commitments-sampling" />

Read the signature carefully: it takes *one blob's* cells. The 50% threshold is a per-row condition —
64 of that row's 128 cells. What makes the block-level statement work is the geometry: because a
column is one cell index taken across every blob, holding 64 columns hands you 64 cells of *every*
row at once. One threshold, met simultaneously for all rows, which is why the spec can state it as
"50% of the columns" without qualification.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />

Two consequences worth carrying forward.

**Recovery regenerates the proofs, not just the data.** The last line of the function recomputes all
cells and proofs from the reconstructed coefficient
polynomial.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-polynomial-commitments-sampling" /> A node that
reconstructs pays the expensive side of the asymmetry — the side EIP-7594 deliberately pushed onto
transaction senders.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> That cost is the reason the reconstruction rule two
lessons from now tells nodes to stagger themselves rather than all reconstruct at once.

**The reference algorithm is not the one anybody runs.** `compute_cells_and_kzg_proofs` carries an
explicit warning in its docstring that it is an inefficient O(n²) algorithm and that a performant
implementation should use FK20, which runs in O(n log
n).<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-polynomial-commitments-sampling" /> The executable spec is a
definition of correctness, not a performance target — if you benchmark proving by running the pyspec,
you have measured the wrong thing by orders of magnitude.

## Every cell carries its own proof, and the sender pays for it

A sampled cell is worthless unless the sampler can tell it is genuine. Otherwise a peer answers every
request with 2 KiB of noise and your availability check passes on garbage.

The answer is a KZG opening proof per cell, verified against the blob's commitment. EIP-7594 puts the
purpose in one line: cell KZG proofs are used to prove that a KZG commitment opens to a cell at the
given index, which allows downloading only specific cells from a blob while still ensuring data
integrity with respect to the corresponding commitment.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> A node with one cell,
one proof and the commitment can decide the question alone — no block body, no peers, no quorum.

That is a stronger property than it sounds, and it removes an entire failure class other DA designs
have to handle. A producer who erasure-codes *incorrectly* would otherwise produce a block whose
pieces each look fine and which reconstructs to nothing; systems relying on plain Merkle commitments
over extended data need bad-encoding fraud proofs, honest reconstructors and a propagation window to
catch that. Here an incorrectly extended cell simply fails its proof on arrival. You saw the contrast
drawn in [checking without downloading](/c/erasure-coding-for-da); the mechanism responsible for it
is the object in your hand right now.

### Who computes, and why that changed

Computing cell proofs is expensive; verifying them is cheap. EIP-7594 draws the obvious conclusion
and moves the cost: because verification is much cheaper than computation, and the proof size is
negligible compared to cell size, blob transaction senders compute the proofs themselves and include
them in the transaction pool wrapper.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

The wrapper is now:

with `wrapper_version` a single byte set to 1 for the current version, and `cell_proofs` a list of
proofs for **all** cells of every blob, extension indices included — `CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB` proofs per
blob.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> The blobs themselves are sent unextended, at `CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB / 2`
cells each, because the receiver can recompute the extension cheaply and shipping them would be
redundant.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

Read the asymmetry there: **proofs for 128 cells, data for 64.** That is the sender doing the
expensive half of the work for a network that will do the cheap half many times over.

Validation on receipt is spelled out: equal counts of versioned hashes, blobs and commitments;
exactly `CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB * len(blobs)` cell proofs; the commitments hashing to the versioned
hashes; and the commitments matching the blobs and cell proofs, which requires computing the
extension cells and verifying every proof — batchable in one shot via
`verify_cell_kzg_proof_batch`.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> EIP-7594 also introduces a limit of 6 blobs per
transaction, enforced at submission, on receipt from the network, and during block production and
processing.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

The same shape appears on the Engine API side: `BlobsBundleV2`, returned by `engine_getPayloadV5`,
must contain exactly `CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB * len(blobs)` cell
proofs.<Cite src="engine-api-osaka" /> One rule, restated at each boundary the data crosses.

### The batch check, spelled out

The Osaka Engine API page writes out the batch verification a consensus client performs on a bundle,
and it is the clearest statement anywhere of how the flat proof list is meant to be
read:<Cite src="engine-api-osaka" />

- `commitments` is each blob's commitment repeated `CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB` times, once per cell.
- `cell_indices` is `list(range(CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB)) * len(blobs)` — the cell index cycles fastest.
- `cells` is every cell of every extended blob, obtained by running `compute_cells` locally on the
  unextended blobs the bundle actually carried.
- All four inputs therefore have the same length, `CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB * len(blobs)`, and one call to
  `verify_cell_kzg_proof_batch` decides the lot.

Two things fall out of that layout. First, the ordering convention is *blob-major, cell-minor*:
`cell_proofs[i * CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB + j]` is the proof for cell `j` of blob `i`, stated exactly that
way in EIP-7594.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> Getting the two loops the wrong way round produces a list of
the right length whose every entry is wrong, which fails verification with no clue about why.

Second, the receiver reconstructs the extension itself before checking anything — the bundle never
carried it.<Cite src="engine-api-osaka" /><Cite src="eip-7594" /> So the cost of admitting a blob
transaction includes an FFT per blob, on top of a batched pairing check. That is the "cheap" side of
the asymmetry, and it is still not nothing.

A rollup's batcher is being ported from the pre-Fulu blob wrapper. The new code does this:

1. Build the blob (4,096 field elements).
2. Compute the KZG commitment and its versioned hash.
3. Call `compute_cells_and_kzg_proofs(blob)` and keep the **first 64** proofs, "because the extension
   cells are derived data and the receiver can recompute them anyway — we already only ship 64 cells
   of blob".
4. Assemble `rlp([tx_payload_body, 1, blobs, commitments, cell_proofs])` and broadcast.

The transaction is rejected by every peer it reaches. Find the defect, explain why step 3's
reasoning fails specifically for proofs and not for data, and say what it would have cost to be
right.

<Answer>
**The defect is in step 3: `cell_proofs` must contain `CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB` proofs per blob — 128,
including the proofs for the extension indices — not 64.**<Cite src="eip-7594" /> The wrapper
validation rule is an exact count, so a 64-entry list fails before any cryptography runs.

**Why the symmetry the author assumed does not hold.**

*Data is recomputable from the blob. Proofs are not recomputable from the blob cheaply.* Extending 64
cells into 128 is an FFT the receiver already has to do; regenerating the 64 missing openings is the
expensive elliptic-curve work the entire wrapper change exists to avoid making receivers
do.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> The spec is explicit on both halves: proofs for all cells including the
extension, blobs sent unextended to avoid sending redundant data.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

*The receiver's check is over the full extended set.* Validation requires computing the extension
cells for all blobs and verifying all cell proofs, batched — so the proofs for cells 64 through 127
are not optional trailing data, they are inputs to the check that admits the transaction to the
pool.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

**The consequence downstream had the transaction somehow been accepted.** Columns 64 through 127 are
half the matrix. Every sidecar for those columns would be unprovable, so every node sampling them
would fail, and the reconstruction threshold of 50% of columns would sit exactly at the boundary of
what could ever be verified.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />

**The cost of being right.** 128 proofs at 48 bytes is 6,144 bytes per
blob<Cite src="consensus-specs-deneb-polynomial-commitments" /> against 128 KiB of blob — under 5% of
the wrapper, and it buys the receiver a batch verification instead of a proof generation. Halving it
saves 3 KiB on a transaction that carries at least 128 KiB per blob, which is the tell: an
optimisation whose upside is that small, against a spec that states an exact count, is not an
optimisation.

**One more thing to check in the same port.** EIP-7594 caps a blob transaction at 6 blobs, enforced
at submission, on receipt and during block production.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> A batcher that
previously packed more per transaction fails the same way, and the failure looks identical from the
outside: silently dropped by peers.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What the extension does not buy

Three limits, stated so you do not have to discover them in an incident review.

**It does not defend against a proposer who publishes nothing.** Erasure coding plus sampling detects
*partial* withholding. A block whose columns never appear at all is not a subtle attack and is not
detected by clever mathematics — it simply fails every node's availability check and never enters
fork choice.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> The extension exists for the case where the
adversary serves *most* of the data, which is the case naive schemes lose.

**It does not make any individual node's conclusion certain.** A passing check means the samples that
were drawn were answered. EIP-7594's own bound is over the probability of convincing a fraction of
the network, and it is explicit that the bound is trivial at the smallest
fractions.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> Lesson five takes this apart properly; the point here is that no
amount of extension converts a probabilistic check into a deterministic one.

**It does not extend the availability window by a second.** Extension changes how much of the data
you need in order to have all of it. It says nothing about how long anyone is required to keep any of
it, and the retention rule is a separate constant with a separate justification.

<Aside kind="note">
There is a fourth non-guarantee that trips teams building on top: the extension is per blob, so
recovering "the block's data" means recovering every row. A block with 21 blobs whose columns arrived
for 20 rows and not the twenty-first is not 95% available in any useful sense — the missing rollup
batch is missing. The matrix picture makes this easy to see and easy to forget, because the columns
are what move on the wire and the rows are what anyone actually wanted.
</Aside>

## What this unlocks

You can now say precisely what a sampler receives and why it is worth anything: 2,048 bytes of
evaluations on a coset, plus a 48-byte opening against a commitment the beacon block signed for, and
a verification that needs nothing else.<Cite src="eip-7594" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-polynomial-commitments-sampling" />

**Next: the geometry.** 128 cells per extended blob, arranged as one row per blob, is a matrix — and
the single most consequential fact about it is which way the slicing runs. The next lesson shows why
custody is vertical, what that means for a node that never holds a whole blob, and where the claim
that per-node bandwidth stops depending on blob count is right and where it quietly is not.

**Carry the basis question with you.** When you read anyone describing DAS extension, check whether
they say coefficients or evaluations, and check whether they cited a tag or a branch. It is a fast
test of whether the writer read the spec or read someone who read the spec.
