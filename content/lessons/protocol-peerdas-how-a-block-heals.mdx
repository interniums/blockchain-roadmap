A proposer publishes 70 of the 128 column sidecars for its block and stops. Not a crash — a choice.
Fifty-eight columns exist and are never sent.

Play the next two seconds forward under the pre-Fulu rules and it is a disaster: most nodes are
missing data, the block is unattestable, the slot is lost. Play it forward under PeerDAS and
something stranger happens. Nothing much, for a moment. Then a handful of machines that have never
communicated about it each notice, independently and at slightly different times, that they hold more
than half the matrix — and each rebuilds the other 58 columns and pushes them back out. Within the
slot, the withholding is undone by nodes the proposer never interacted with.

This lesson is that mechanism: the threshold, the deliberately randomised delay that makes it
tolerable, and the obligation to republish that turns a local recovery into a network property.

## The threshold, and who can possibly be at it

The rule is one sentence:

> If the node obtains 50%+ of all the columns, it SHOULD reconstruct the full data matrix via the
> `recover_matrix` helper.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />

`recover_matrix` walks the partial matrix row by row, collecting the cell indices and cells present
for each blob and calling `recover_cells_and_kzg_proofs` on
them.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> Fifty per cent of *columns* gives 64 cells of every
row simultaneously, which is exactly the per-row threshold that function
asserts.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-polynomial-commitments-sampling" />

Now ask who is ever in that position, because the answer reshapes the picture.

Sixty-four columns is **sixteen times** the minimum custody of 4 and eight times the validator floor
of 8.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-validator" /> An
ordinary full node cannot reconstruct, ever, from its own custody. By the balance rule — one group per
32 ETH of attached effective balance — a node needs 2,048 ETH attached to reach 64 groups, and 4,096
ETH to be a full supernode at 128.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-validator" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" />

So the healing property is carried by a specific, small, well-resourced class. The p2p spec says as
much without hedging: **in order to reconstruct missing data, there must be at least one supernode on
the network**, and a supernode is defined as one that subscribes to all column subnets, custodies all
sidecars, and performs reconstruction and
cross-seeding.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> The spec expects many — hundreds — and expects
a node to be connected to several by chance.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" />

## The instruction that looks like a bug

Immediately after the threshold, the spec says this:

> Nodes MAY delay this reconstruction allowing time for other columns to arrive over the network. If
> delaying reconstruction, nodes may use a random delay in order to desynchronize reconstruction
> among nodes, thus reducing overall CPU load.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />

A specification telling implementers to *wait, randomly, before doing the useful thing* is unusual
enough to be worth taking apart. Two independent reasons sit behind it.

**Reconstruction is expensive, and the expensive part is the proofs.** Recovery does not stop at
recovering data: `recover_cells_and_kzg_proofs` recomputes all cells **and all proofs** from the
reconstructed coefficient polynomial.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-polynomial-commitments-sampling" />
That is the elliptic-curve work EIP-7594 deliberately pushed onto transaction senders precisely
because it is the costly side of the asymmetry.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> And it is per row: a node
reconstructing a 21-blob block pays it twenty-one times. Every supernode doing that simultaneously,
every time a proposer publishes late, is a synchronised CPU spike across the most important nodes on
the network.

**The work is usually unnecessary by the time you would have finished it.** Columns keep arriving.
The delay is a bet that somebody else's copy of column 91 reaches you before you have finished
deriving your own — and it is a good bet, because every subscribed node forwards what it receives.
Waiting converts CPU into bandwidth you were going to spend anyway.

The distributed-systems idiom here is jitter, and the failure it prevents is the thundering herd:
*n* independent actors triggered by the same external event, acting at the same instant, on the same
resource. The novelty is only that the shared resource is aggregate network CPU rather than a
database.

### The number the spec does not give you

There is no delay constant. No `RECONSTRUCTION_DELAY_MS`, no distribution, no bound. The spec flags
the gap itself, as an inline note:

> There are timing considerations to analyze — at what point does a node consider samples missing and
> choose to reconstruct and cross-seed.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />

That is a shipped specification saying the timing policy is unresolved. It is left to clients, which
means it is a real source of behavioural divergence between implementations, and it sits against a
hard constraint from the other direction: whatever the delay is, the reconstructed columns are only
useful to other nodes' availability checks if they arrive before those nodes' attestation deadlines.
Delay too little and the network burns redundant CPU; delay too long and the repair lands after the
votes it was meant to enable.

A second inline note flags the other open area: there may be anti-DoS and quality-of-service
considerations around how to send samples and consider samples — whether each sample is an individual
message or they are sent in aggregate.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> Two open
engineering questions, both stated in the normative document rather than hidden in a discussion
thread. Read them as an invitation and a warning: this is where client behaviour will keep changing.

## Cross-seeding: the rule that makes it a network property

Reconstruction on its own repairs one node's view. The next paragraph is what repairs everyone's:

> Once the node obtains a column through reconstruction, the node MUST expose the new column as if it
> had received it over the network. If the node is subscribed to the subnet corresponding to the
> column, it MUST send the reconstructed `DataColumnSidecar` to its topic mesh neighbors. If instead
> the node is not subscribed to the corresponding subnet, it SHOULD still expose the availability of
> the `DataColumnSidecar` as part of the gossip emission process. After exposing the reconstructed
> `DataColumnSidecar` to the network, the node MAY delete the `DataColumnSidecar` if it is not part
> of the node's custody requirement.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />

Four obligations, in decreasing strength, and the gradient is the design.

**MUST expose, unconditionally.** Not "may share if convenient". A node that reconstructs and stays
quiet is out of spec.

**MUST gossip to mesh neighbours, if subscribed.** For a supernode that is every column, so a single
supernode reconstructing pushes all 58 missing columns into 58 different subnet meshes at once. From
there [gossipsub](/c/gossipsub) does what it does and the columns reach everyone subscribed to those
subnets.

**SHOULD advertise availability, if not subscribed.** The weaker fallback for a node that rebuilt a
column it does not follow — it does not push the bytes, but it makes itself findable as a source.

**MAY delete afterwards.** The reconstructing node is not conscripted into storing what it rebuilt.
It keeps its own custody groups and can drop the rest. Repair is a *transmission* obligation, not a
storage one.

The phrase "as if it had received it over the network" is doing precise work. A reconstructed sidecar
is not a special object with its own message type; it re-enters gossip as an ordinary
`DataColumnSidecar` and is subject to the same validation on arrival at every peer — correct subnet,
valid inclusion proof, valid KZG proofs, valid proposer
signature.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> Nobody has to trust the reconstructor, because the
proofs the reconstructor recomputed are checkable against the same commitments the proposer signed
for.

<Aside kind="note">
The same "behave as if received via gossip" discipline appears in the parallel path where a node
pulls blob data from its own execution client rather than reconstructing: clients must publish the
resulting `data_column_sidecar` on the topic **if and only if** they are subscribed to it, and must
update gossip-related data structures including the anti-equivocation
cache.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> Two different origins for the same bytes, one set of
rules for putting them on the wire. That uniformity is what keeps the gossip validation rules — like
the [IGNORE] on any sidecar that is not the first for a given slot, proposer and column
index<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> — meaningful regardless of where a sidecar came from.
</Aside>

### The "cross" in cross-seeding

The name is not decoration. A one-line note in the same spec section explains it:

> A node always maintains a matrix view of the rows and columns they are following, able to
> cross-reference and cross-seed in either direction.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />

**Either direction.** The matrix has two axes and repair can run along both.

*Columns to columns, via rows.* This is the path this lesson has been describing: gather 64 columns,
recover each row, emit the missing columns. The rows are the intermediate object.

*Rows to columns, without recovering anything.* A node that possesses whole blobs — rows — can
compute their cells and proofs directly and produce column sidecars from them. The validator spec
provides the helper:
`get_data_column_sidecars_from_column_sidecar(sidecar, cells_and_kzg_proofs)` takes any single column
sidecar for the block, plus cells and proofs for the blobs it commits to, and returns the full
sequence of all 128 sidecars — described explicitly as being for distributed blob publishing,
assuming all cells and proofs could be retrieved from the local execution layer
client.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-validator" />

The second path is cheaper and it is the one that runs most often, because blob-carrying transactions
sit in the execution layer's mempool *before* the block that includes them is proposed. A node that
sees one column of a block and has the blobs locally can reconstitute all 128 columns without any
Reed-Solomon recovery — it is not repairing an erasure, it is re-deriving from the original.

That is why the row direction matters even though nobody custodies rows. The rows exist, they arrive
by a completely different route, and the matrix view is what lets a node notice that it can supply a
column it never received.

## When healing does not happen

Two honest limits, both structural.

**If no connected node reaches the threshold, nothing is repaired.** Reconstruction requires 64
columns in one place. If a proposer publishes 70 columns but the nodes that received them all sit at
4 or 8 groups and none of them accumulates enough, no recovery occurs. Gossip forwarding makes this
unlikely — subscribed nodes propagate what they receive, so columns pool at the supernodes that
subscribe to everything — but "unlikely" here rests on the supernode population, which the spec
guarantees only as "at least one" and expects as "hundreds".<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" />

**Healing does not rescue the current slot for a node that already voted.** Repair is racing an
attestation deadline that does not wait. A block whose columns are republished at second six is
available — but the validators who had to decide at second four decided without them.

Neither limit is a flaw in the mechanism. They are the reason the module ends on timing rather than
on cryptography.

## Why partial withholding is not an attack

Put the threshold and the republication rule together and the adversary's option space collapses into
two cases with nothing useful in between.

**Case A — the adversary lets more than 63 columns out.** Somewhere on the network, a node reaches
64. It reconstructs, and it must republish everything it rebuilt into the relevant subnet
meshes.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> Within the slot the withheld columns are being
served from directions the adversary never chose and cannot revoke. The withholding is not merely
detected; it is undone.

**Case B — the adversary keeps the served set at 63 or fewer.** Now nothing is recoverable — but
nothing is recoverable *for the honest majority either*, and every node whose sample set is not
entirely contained in the served 63 columns fails its check. That is the case the probability
arithmetic covers, and it is the case where the block does not get the attestations it needs.

There is no strategy that serves enough columns to satisfy most nodes while keeping the data
unrecoverable, because "enough columns to satisfy most nodes" and "enough columns to reconstruct" are
nearly the same quantity, and cross-seeding closes the gap between them.

The subtler variant is worth naming because it is the one people propose: **serve different columns
to different peers**, so that no individual node ever accumulates 64 even though the union exceeds
63. It fails for a mundane reason rather than a clever one. Every node that receives a column on a
subnet it is subscribed to forwards it to its mesh neighbours — that is what gossipsub
is — so the union does not stay partitioned. Columns pool. Once the pool crosses 64 anywhere, Case A
applies.

A colleague objects to all three clauses. Work out what each one costs at scale, and write the policy

<Answer>
**Clause 1 — "immediately" is the expensive one.** Every supernode on the network crosses 64 at
roughly the same moment, because they are all fed by the same gossip. Reconstruction recomputes all
cells and all proofs, per row,<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-polynomial-commitments-sampling" /> so
"immediately" schedules a synchronised elliptic-curve burst across the network's highest-value nodes
at the same instant in the slot. The spec's counter-proposal is exactly this: delay to let other
columns arrive, and use a *random* delay to desynchronise and reduce overall CPU
load.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> Most of the work is also wasted — the columns you
were about to derive are usually inbound already.

**Clause 2 — "republish everything" is mostly right and needs one correction.** The obligation is to
expose reconstructed columns, and for subnets you are subscribed to that is a MUST to your topic mesh
neighbours.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> As a supernode you are subscribed to all of
them,<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> so "everything to every subnet" is correct *for this
node class*. The correction is that you should not republish columns that arrived normally and were
never reconstructed — the rule is scoped to columns obtained through reconstruction, and re-emitting
already-propagated sidecars is duplicate traffic that peers will [IGNORE] as
non-first anyway.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" />

**Clause 3 — "retain all of it" is correct here for a reason unrelated to reconstruction.** A
supernode custodies all 128 groups, so nothing it rebuilds is outside its custody and the MAY-delete
allowance never applies.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />
The clause would be wrong as a general policy: a node at 8 groups must expose and may then delete the
other 120.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />

**The policy to ship.**

*Trigger on the threshold, act after a jittered delay.* Arm a timer on crossing 64 columns, drawn
from a random distribution, and cancel any row that completes from the network before it fires.
Reconstruct only what is still missing at that point.

*Bound the delay by the deadline it serves.* Reconstructed columns help other nodes only if they
arrive before those nodes attest, and `ATTESTATION_DUE_BPS` is 3,333 basis points of a 12,000 ms slot
— about four seconds in.<Cite src="consensus-specs-v161-mainnet-config" /> A delay whose upper tail
lands after that produces correct data that arrives too late to be worth anything for the current
slot.

*Publish per column as each becomes available*, rather than batching the whole matrix, so the
earliest recovered columns start propagating while the rest are still being computed.

**The input the specification does not give you: the delay distribution, and the point at which a
node considers samples missing.** The spec raises this as an open timing consideration in an inline
note rather than specifying it.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> That makes it a client
policy, a live source of cross-client behavioural difference, and something to measure on a devnet
rather than to reason about from first principles. It is also the parameter most likely to change
underneath you — so put it in configuration, not in a constant.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

You have the repair loop: a threshold only well-resourced nodes can reach, a jittered delay that
keeps the network from convulsing, and a republication obligation that makes one node's recovery
everybody's.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> You also have the sharper reason partial
withholding fails, which does not need the probability argument at all.

**Next: the clock.** Everything in this lesson — the delay, the republication, the sampling requests
from the previous lesson — has to complete inside a slot, against an attestation deadline that does
not move. The next lesson puts numbers on that budget, walks the 128 subnets the traffic runs on, and
shows the escape hatch that lets a consensus client skip the network entirely and ask its own
execution client for the data.

**Carry the two open notes.** A shipped spec that says "there are timing considerations to analyze"
is telling you where the next year of client engineering will happen.
