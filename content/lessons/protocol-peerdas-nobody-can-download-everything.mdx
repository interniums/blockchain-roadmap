Sit with a stopwatch next to a home node and count what arrives.

A beacon block turns up. It is small — a few hundred kilobytes at most, and most of that is
attestations. Then the data that the block merely *commits* to starts arriving, and that is the part
with the interesting number attached. The mainnet blob schedule in `configs/mainnet.yaml` sets
`MAX_BLOBS_PER_BLOCK` to 21 from epoch 419072.<Cite src="consensus-specs-v161-mainnet-config" /> A
blob is 128 KiB.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Twenty-one of them is 2.625 MiB, and `SLOT_DURATION_MS` is
12,000, so a full block's worth of blob data arrives every twelve
seconds.<Cite src="consensus-specs-v161-mainnet-config" />

That is 18.5 GiB a day if every block were full, which no day is. It is also, and this is the part
that ends the conversation, the number *before* the protocol adds the redundancy that makes sampling
work at all. Under the pre-Fusaka rule — every node downloads every blob — raising the blob count is
raising that figure linearly, for every node on the network, permanently.

You met the consequence of that from the outside in Scaling: blob capacity is the binding constraint
on rollup cost, and it moves on a schedule of its own. This module is the inside. It is about the
mechanism that decoupled "how much data a block carries" from "how much data a node must fetch" —
and about the exact, smaller-than-advertised size of that decoupling.

## The unit everything is denominated in

Before any of the machinery, pin the primitive down precisely, because every constant later in the
module is a multiple or a divisor of it.

A blob is `FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_BLOB` = 4,096 field elements of 32 bytes each: 131,072 bytes, 128
KiB.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> It is not a byte array that happens to be that long. Each 32-byte word is
a canonical element of the BLS12-381 scalar field, which is why not every 32-byte string is a legal
blob and why encoding data into blobs costs you a few bits per word.

Three properties of that object matter for everything downstream.

**It travels beside the transaction, not inside it.** The transaction carries
[versioned hashes](/c/versioned-hash) — `0x01` followed by the last 31 bytes of the SHA-256 of the
blob's KZG commitment.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> The blob itself rides in a separate network wrapper.
The block body references commitments; it does not contain blob bytes.

**The EVM cannot read a byte of it.** `BLOBHASH` returns a versioned hash. The point-evaluation
precompile at `0x0A` takes a 192-byte input and confirms that a claimed evaluation of the committed
polynomial is correct.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Neither of those is a read. A contract can verify a
claim *about* blob contents that somebody else supplies; it cannot obtain the contents. That
restriction is not an oversight, it is the entire reason blob data can be priced in a separate market
from calldata — see [the lane the EVM cannot read](/c/blob-not-in-evm).

**It is discarded on a timer.** Nodes must serve data for
`MIN_EPOCHS_FOR_DATA_COLUMN_SIDECARS_REQUESTS` = 4096 epochs, roughly 18 days, and may prune
afterwards.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> Availability is a window, not an archive.

## The brute-force guarantee, and what it actually costs

The property a rollup needs from Ethereum is narrow and easy to state: *the batch was published*.
Not stored forever, not readable on-chain, not even read by anyone in particular — published, in the
sense that anyone who wanted it during the window could have got it. Everything a
[rollup](/c/rollup) promises rests on that. Without the batch, nobody reconstructs state, nobody
challenges a wrong claim, and nobody exits without the operator's cooperation.

The brute-force way to guarantee publication is to make every node download everything and refuse to
build on a block whose data did not arrive. It is a genuinely excellent guarantee. It is also
self-limiting in a way worth stating as arithmetic rather than as a principle.

**Subgoal 1 — express the cost as a per-node rate, because that is the quantity that gates
participation.** At `MAX_BLOBS_PER_BLOCK` = 21 and a 12,000 ms
slot,<Cite src="consensus-specs-v161-mainnet-config" /> full blocks mean 2.625 MiB per slot, which is
about 1.8 Mbit/s of sustained inbound just for blob payload, before protocol overhead, before
duplicate deliveries from a gossip mesh, and before everything else a node does.

**Subgoal 2 — add the redundancy, because the sampling design needs it and it is not free.** PeerDAS
extends each blob with a one-dimensional erasure code, doubling it:
`FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_EXT_BLOB` = 8,192 against `FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_BLOB` =
4,096.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-preset" /><Cite src="eip-4844" /> The network now carries 5.25
MiB per full slot, not 2.625.

**Subgoal 3 — multiply out the storage obligation, because that is what a node operator actually
budgets.** 4,096 epochs is 131,072 slots. A node that held the entire extended matrix for the full
retention window would be storing about 672 GiB, assuming every block full at 21
blobs.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-v161-mainnet-config" /> Real
blocks are not all full, so treat that as the ceiling the design has to survive rather than a
prediction.

**Subgoal 4 — read off why the number cannot simply be paid.** Every one of those figures scales with
`MAX_BLOBS_PER_BLOCK`, and that constant is now raised by
[blob-parameter-only forks](/c/bpo-fork) — a lightweight fork class defined by EIP-7892 for exactly
this purpose, changing target, max and the base-fee update fraction and nothing
else.<Cite src="eip-7892" /> A mechanism designed to raise capacity quickly, sitting on top of a
distribution rule where capacity multiplies straight into every node's bandwidth bill, is a
mechanism that runs out of room fast.

<Aside kind="note">
The blob schedule in the config read on 26 August 2026 has two entries: `MAX_BLOBS_PER_BLOCK` 15 from
epoch 412672 and 21 from epoch 419072.<Cite src="consensus-specs-v161-mainnet-config" /> Whether a
further BPO has been agreed and not yet merged, or shipped since, is not something the file can tell
you, and this project's own research register flags the current mainnet target/max as unverified.
Treat 21 as "what the checked-in config said on that date", never as "the current maximum".
</Aside>

## What sampling substitutes, stated without the sales pitch

Data availability sampling replaces "I downloaded it" with "I checked a small random part of it, and
so did thousands of others". EIP-7594's motivation puts it plainly: DAS scales data availability
beyond EIP-4844 by not requiring all nodes to download all data while still ensuring all of the data
has been made available.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

The honest version of the trade has two halves and both of them need saying.

**Individual nodes know strictly less than before.** A node that finishes its checks has not seen the
data. It has seen a fraction of it and inferred the rest. That inference is probabilistic, and no
amount of good engineering makes it not probabilistic. There is a real, computable chance that a
given node concludes "available" about data that is not recoverable.

**The network as a whole knows enough.** The security claim was never about one node. EIP-7594's
security analysis bounds the probability of convincing a *fraction* of nodes, and it is candid about
where that bound is worthless: for mainnet parameters at 10,000 nodes, the upper bound on attack
success at ε = 0.01 — a hundred nodes — is 1, and it only collapses from ε = 0.02
onward.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> Fooling a hundred nodes is not ruled out. Fooling five per cent of
them is bounded at 10⁻³⁰⁶·³⁴.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

You worked the arithmetic behind that in
[checking without downloading](/c/erasure-coding-for-da). What this module adds is everything between
the arithmetic and a node that actually does it: which columns a node holds and why those ones, how
it finds peers who hold the rest, what happens when half the data never shows up, and what "the
samples passed" is permitted to mean to fork choice.

### Three things "published" can mean, and only one of them is on offer

Say what your service needs before you decide whether the guarantee covers it. The word does at least
three jobs and they have different price tags.

**Reconstructable by somebody, during the slot.** Enough of the extended data reached enough honest
nodes that the block could be rebuilt from what the network holds. This is what data availability
sampling establishes, and it is the guarantee PeerDAS is engineered to deliver.

**Retrievable by you, now.** You can obtain the bytes on demand. PeerDAS does *not* promise this to
an arbitrary party: a node holds its own columns and serves requests for those, so getting a whole
blob means asking enough distinct peers to cover the columns you lack, within the window during
which they are still required to answer.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" />

**Retrievable by you in eighteen months.** Not on offer at all. The serving obligation ends after
4,096 epochs and nodes may prune.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> Anything longer is somebody
running an archive — the rollup itself, a third party, or you.

The failure mode this distinction prevents is common and expensive: a team hears "Ethereum guarantees

and discovers the guarantee expired eighteen days after the fact.

## What changed on the wire, and when

This is not a proposal. `FULU_FORK_EPOCH` is 411392 — 3 December 2025, 21:49:11
UTC.<Cite src="consensus-specs-v161-mainnet-config" /> Since that epoch the object a mainnet node
receives is not a blob.

The Fulu networking spec deprecates the `blob_sidecar_{subnet_id}` gossip topic outright and replaces
it with `data_column_sidecar_{subnet_id}`.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> The container that
travels on it looks like this:

```python
class DataColumnSidecar(Container):
    index: ColumnIndex
    column: List[Cell, MAX_BLOB_COMMITMENTS_PER_BLOCK]
    kzg_commitments: List[KZGCommitment, MAX_BLOB_COMMITMENTS_PER_BLOCK]
    kzg_proofs: List[KZGProof, MAX_BLOB_COMMITMENTS_PER_BLOCK]
    signed_block_header: SignedBeaconBlockHeader
    kzg_commitments_inclusion_proof: Vector[Bytes32, KZG_COMMITMENTS_INCLUSION_PROOF_DEPTH]
```

Three details in that declaration are worth reading now and will be unpacked later.

**`column` is a list whose length is the block's blob count, not a fixed-size vector.** One cell per
blob.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> That is the type-level statement of the coupling
this lesson refuses to let go of: more blobs, longer list, more bytes on the same subnet.

**Every sidecar carries the block's full commitment list plus a Merkle proof that the list is in the
block body.** `KZG_COMMITMENTS_INCLUSION_PROOF_DEPTH` is 4 on
mainnet.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-preset" /> A node receiving one column can bind it to a
signed block header without having the block body — which is what makes a column independently
useful.

**The proofs are per cell, not per blob.** EIP-7594 moved proof computation to the transaction
sender: the blob-transaction network wrapper now carries `cell_proofs`, `CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB` of them
per blob, plus a one-byte `wrapper_version` set to 1.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> Every rollup batcher and
every blob-submitting library had to change for that, and material predating it shows a tidy
one-to-one-to-one triple of blobs, commitments and proofs that no longer exists.

## The thing this module will keep insisting on

Here is the claim you will hear about PeerDAS, in roughly these words: *because nodes hold columns
rather than blobs, per-node bandwidth no longer depends on how many blobs a block carries.*

It is not true, and the shape of the untruth is the most useful thing in the module.

`NUMBER_OF_COLUMNS` is a **preset** constant, fixed at 128 — defined as
`uint64(CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB)`, itself `FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_EXT_BLOB // FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_CELL` =
8192 / 64.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-preset" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> It
does not move when the blob count moves. A column is one cell index taken across *every blob in the
block*, so adding blobs makes each column taller. More blobs means bigger columns, and a node holding
a fixed number of columns downloads more bytes.

What PeerDAS buys is a **constant divisor**. EIP-7594 names it: with current parameters a node
downloads 1/8 of the total data, and this can in future be decreased to 1/16 or even 1/32 by reducing
the sample size — which means *increasing the number of columns*.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> Read that
sentence twice. The stated next lever for scaling is raising the column count, and it is on the table
precisely because the current count is fixed and the coupling it leaves behind is real.

So the accurate sentence is: **PeerDAS divides each node's share of the data by a constant; it does
not sever the link between block capacity and node bandwidth.** Multiply blob capacity by eight and
an ordinary node's blob traffic goes up by eight, just from a base one-eighth as large. That is an
enormous win. It is not independence, and a design conversation that assumes independence will
mis-plan the next capacity increase.

<Check type="explain" concept="data-availability-problem">
A colleague proposes what sounds like a cheaper version of the same idea for an appchain: "Skip the
erasure coding and skip the columns. Just have every node download a random 1/16 of the blobs in each
block, whole. Same bandwidth as PeerDAS, way less machinery, and between 10,000 nodes every blob gets
fetched hundreds of times over, so nothing can go missing."

Say what this scheme does and does not guarantee, and identify the assumption doing the illegitimate
work.

<Answer>
**It guarantees redundant retrieval of whatever was served. It guarantees nothing about withholding,
which is the only attack the mechanism exists to stop.**

**The illegitimate assumption is that a missing piece is a random event.** The scheme's "hundreds of
times over" is an availability-of-*honest*-data argument: if all the blobs are being served, random
sampling will indeed fetch each one many times. Withholding is not a random event. It is a choice
made by the party that has the data, who can answer every request except the ones for blob 7 and be
caught by exactly the nodes assigned blob 7 — a computable minority — while every other node reports
a clean fetch.

**Whole-blob sampling makes the withheld quantity as small as the attacker likes.** With no
extension, one unserved blob out of 21 is one permanently missing batch. There is no threshold below
which withholding is harmless, so the detector has to catch a 1-in-21 event, and the nodes that do
catch it are outvoted by the ones that did not.

**What the extension changes is not detection sensitivity but the attacker's minimum move.** Once
each blob is extended so that any half of it reconstructs the whole, hiding anything at all requires
suppressing more than half the extended data — a target so large that a handful of random probes
finds it. The redundancy is not there to survive packet loss. It is there to make "hide a little" and
"hide a lot" the same act.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

**Two further things the proposal silently drops.**

*Verifiability of a fragment.* A node that receives one piece must be able to check it against the
block's commitment on its own. Whole-blob delivery gets this free from the versioned
hash;<Cite src="eip-4844" /> any scheme that hands out sub-blob pieces has to say what binds a piece
to the commitment, and PeerDAS's answer — a KZG proof per cell — is what the next lesson is about.

*Who reassembles.* Sampling proves reconstructability. If the appchain has no rule saying which nodes
rebuild the missing parts, when, and whether they must republish what they rebuilt, then a partial
withholding is never repaired and the block simply stays half-available. PeerDAS spends an entire
spec section on that.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />

**The one thing the colleague has right:** bandwidth-wise, 1/16 is 1/16. The costs are comparable.
That is what makes the comparison worth making — the machinery is not buying a smaller download, it
is buying a guarantee the cheap version does not have at any download size.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

You now have the two fixed points the rest of the module hangs from: a blob is 128 KiB of field
elements the EVM cannot read,<Cite src="eip-4844" /> and the network's aggregate assurance is
deliberately bought by making each node's individual knowledge weaker.

**Next comes the step that makes sampling mean anything.** The extension to 8,192 field
elements<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-preset" /> and the per-cell KZG proof are what turn "I asked
for a random piece and got one" from theatre into evidence. That lesson also settles a question this
one dodged: what, exactly, the 4,096 numbers in a blob *are* — and it will show you two current
descriptions that disagree, one of them on ethereum.org.<Cite src="ethereum-org-peerdas" />

**And keep the divisor claim in hand.** Every time a later lesson says a node holds "its share", ask
what the share is a share *of*, and whether that thing grew.
