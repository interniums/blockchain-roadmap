`ATTESTATION_DUE_BPS` is 3,333 basis points of `SLOT_DURATION_MS`, and `SLOT_DURATION_MS` is
12,000.<Cite src="consensus-specs-v161-mainnet-config" /> Four seconds. That is the entire budget.

Inside it, a validator must receive a beacon block, obtain eight columns' worth of data it may not
have been sent, verify every cell proof in them, and decide whether the block's data is available —
because that decision gates whether it attests. Sampling is not a download that finishes when it
finishes. It is a real-time deadline with a network on the other side of it, and everything about the
subnet layout, the peering strategy and the escape hatches exists because of that sentence.

This lesson is the timing budget, the 128 subnets the traffic runs on, the per-sidecar work that has
to fit, and the one mechanism that lets a consensus client skip the network entirely.

## A hundred and twenty-eight subnets, one per column

`DATA_COLUMN_SIDECAR_SUBNET_COUNT` is 128, and the mapping is the simplest possible
one:<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-v161-mainnet-config" />

```python
def compute_subnet_for_data_column_sidecar(column_index: ColumnIndex) -> SubnetID:
    return SubnetID(column_index % DATA_COLUMN_SIDECAR_SUBNET_COUNT)
```

Column *i* goes to subnet *i*. The `blob_sidecar_{subnet_id}` topic from Deneb is deprecated
outright; `data_column_sidecar_{subnet_id}` replaces
it.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> A node joins the gossipsub subnets for the custody groups
it custodies and no others — which is what keeps its inbound proportional to its custody rather than
to the block.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />

At the minimum that is four subnets out of 128. It is worth pausing on how aggressive that is: a
default full node is subscribed to about 3% of the topics carrying the data it is voting on being
available.

The obvious worry — 128 subnets is a lot of meshes to keep healthy — is addressed directly in the
spec's FAQ, and the answer relocates the problem:

> No, the number of subnets doesn't really matter. What matters to the network stability is the
> number of nodes and the churn rate in the network. If the number of the nodes is too low, it's
> likely to have a network partition when some nodes are down. For the churn rate, if the churn rate
> is high, we even need to have a higher number of nodes, since nodes are likely to be turned off
> more often.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />

Subnet count is not the stability variable. Population and churn are. Treat that as the thing to
monitor.

### What arrives, and the work it costs

A `DataColumnSidecar` landing on a subnet is not free to accept. Before forwarding it, a node runs a
validation list that includes: the sidecar is valid per `verify_data_column_sidecar`; it is on the
correct subnet; it is not from a future slot; its slot is beyond the latest finalized slot; the
proposer signature on the block header is valid; the parent has been seen and passes validation; the
sidecar's slot exceeds the parent's; the finalized checkpoint is an ancestor; the `kzg_commitments`
inclusion proof verifies; the column data verifies via
`verify_data_column_sidecar_kzg_proofs`; it is the first sidecar for that
`(slot, proposer_index, index)` tuple; and the proposer is the expected one for the
slot.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" />

That is thirteen conditions, three of which are cryptographic — the proposer signature, the inclusion
proof and the KZG proofs — and one of which, the KZG proof check, scales with the number of blobs in
the block, because a column carries one cell and one proof per
blob.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />

The spec offers one optimisation explicitly, and it is a good tell for where the cost sits: in the
inclusion-proof check, all sidecars of the same block verify against the same set of
`kzg_commitments`, so a client may cache the result keyed on the tuple of commitments, inclusion
proof and signed block header.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> Eight columns of one block
means one inclusion-proof verification, not eight.

### The proposer's side of the burst

Look at the same four seconds from the other end. A proposer does not publish one object; it publishes
128.

`get_data_column_sidecars` builds one sidecar per column index, each carrying the block's full
commitment list, the inclusion proof, the signed header, and one cell plus one proof per
blob.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-validator" /> The publishing rule is then: compute
`subnet_id = compute_subnet_for_data_column_sidecar(column_index)` for each, and after publishing all
columns to their respective subnets, peers may request the sidecar through sync
requests.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-validator" />

Price it. At 21 blobs a column carries 21 cells at `BYTES_PER_CELL` = 2,048 —
43,008 bytes — plus 21 commitments and 21 proofs at 48 bytes each and a small fixed
header.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-preset" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> Call it
about 44 KiB per sidecar. Times 128 sidecars: roughly **5.6 MiB that one machine has to push out**,
early enough in the slot that it can propagate across 128 separate meshes and reach attesters before
their deadline.

Notice the asymmetry this creates. A sampling node's inbound is ~352 KiB and its 1/8 divisor applies.
The proposer's outbound is the whole extended matrix and no divisor applies to it at all — the same
shape as the supernode problem, arriving once per slot at a different machine. This is why the
proposer publishing late is treated as a normal, expected event with dedicated mitigations rather
than as misbehaviour, and it is the load the distributed-publishing helpers exist to spread.

## Pulling what gossip did not deliver

If a node fails to get columns on the column subnets, it can use the request/response protocol to
query the missing columns from peers.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> Two protocols
matter, and their shapes tell you what they are for.

**`DataColumnSidecarsByRange`** takes a `start_slot`, a `count`, and a *list of column indices* —
"give me columns 31, 47 and 56 for these slots".<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> The request
is column-targeted by construction, which is exactly what the deterministic custody derivation makes
answerable: you already know which peers must have those columns.

**`MAX_REQUEST_DATA_COLUMN_SIDECARS`** is `MAX_REQUEST_BLOCKS_DENEB * NUMBER_OF_COLUMNS` = 128 × 128
= 16,384.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-v161-mainnet-config" />
Sized for backfill, not for a single slot's sampling.

Three rules on the serving side shape what you can rely on.

**Serving is an obligation with a window.** `data_column_serve_range` is
`[max(current_epoch − MIN_EPOCHS_FOR_DATA_COLUMN_SIDECARS_REQUESTS, FULU_FORK_EPOCH), current_epoch]`,
and clients must keep a record of sidecars seen in that range and support serving
it.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> Peers unable to reply within the range should return
`3: ResourceUnavailable`, and may be descored or disconnected at any
time.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" />

**Answers are per block, not per column.** Clients must include *all* data column sidecars of each
block from which they include any, sent in `(slot, column_index)`
order.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> You cannot ask for a scattering and get a scattering.

**A node advertises how far back it can go.** `Status` v2 gains
`earliest_available_slot`,<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> and a node that can serve blocks
but not all sidecars across the retention period should advertise the earliest slot from which it can
serve all sidecars.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> A node syncing from a recent weak
subjectivity checkpoint must backfill to `data_column_serve_range` to be fully compliant — and until
it does, peers may disconnect or temporarily ban it.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" />

## The clock

Now assemble the budget. Everything below is one slot on mainnet.

**t = 0.** Slot begins. The proposer builds, then publishes the beacon block and, separately, all 128
column sidecars to their subnets.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-validator" />

**t ≈ 0 to 4 s.** An attesting validator's window. `ATTESTATION_DUE_BPS` = 3,333 bps of a 12,000 ms
slot puts the deadline just under four seconds
in.<Cite src="consensus-specs-v161-mainnet-config" /> Inside it the node must: receive and validate
the block; receive its custodied columns on the subnets it subscribes to; **request** the
sampled-but-not-custodied columns from peers and receive answers; verify each sidecar; and evaluate
its availability check.

**The all-or-nothing rule turns every one of those into a critical path.** Sampling is successful only
if the node retrieves all selected columns.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> One slow peer,
for one column, and the check fails for the slot.

That is the whole reason this is an engineering problem rather than a mathematics problem. The
security argument assumes samples get answered; the network decides whether they do. Francesco
D'Amato's fork-choice notes on PeerDAS put the interaction between sampling and fork choice at the
centre for the same reason — the timing constraint is what shapes the
design.<Cite src="fradamt-das-fork-choice" />

<Aside kind="warn">
The four-second figure is the shipped one. The same config file also defines
`ATTESTATION_DUE_BPS_GLOAS` = 2,500 bps — 25% of the slot, three seconds — against a
`GLOAS_FORK_EPOCH` of `18446744073709551615`, the far-future
sentinel.<Cite src="consensus-specs-v161-mainnet-config" /> That is a constant defined for an
unshipped fork with no activation epoch set. Read it as evidence that a tighter attestation deadline
is *proposed*, not as a schedule: no date should be attached to it, and the value could change before
anything activates.
</Aside>

### Where the four seconds actually go

The budget is not four seconds of sampling. Order the events and the sampling window shrinks:

- The block has to be built and propagated before anything else starts. Whatever fraction of the slot
  that takes is subtracted first.
- Custodied columns arrive on gossip, which is a mesh propagation with its own hop count — not a
  point-to-point transfer from the proposer.
- Only then can a node tell which of its sampled columns are *missing*, because a request it did not
  need to make is the cheapest one.
- Requests to peers are round trips, and the peer must have the column, be online, and not be
  saturated.

The spec declines to specify the moment at which a node gives up on gossip and starts requesting: an
inline note in das-core names it as an open timing consideration — at what point does a node consider
samples missing.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> Same gap as the reconstruction delay,
same consequence: client policy, and a real source of behavioural divergence.

## The escape hatch: ask your own execution client

Here is the piece of the design that most improves the odds, and it works because of something you
already know about the mempool.

Blob-carrying transactions propagate to execution clients through the transaction pool *before* the
block that includes them is proposed. So a consensus client holding a beacon block but missing its
data does not have to wait on the consensus network at all — the bytes may already be sitting in the
execution client it is paired with, one local RPC away over the
[Engine API](/c/engine-api).

The p2p spec makes it a recommendation rather than an option:

> Honest nodes SHOULD query `engine_getBlobsV2` as soon as they receive a valid `beacon_block` or
> `data_column_sidecar` from gossip. If ALL blobs matching `kzg_commitments` are retrieved, they
> should convert the response to data columns, and import the
> result.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" />

And it names the purpose: implementers are encouraged to leverage this method to increase the
likelihood of incorporating and attesting to the last block when its proposer is not able to publish
data columns on time.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" />

### Two shapes, and why there are two

The Osaka fork introduced both:

- **`engine_getBlobsV2`** — all-or-nothing. If any requested blob is missing, or is an older-version
  blob carrying a blob proof rather than cell proofs, the response **must** be `null` — not a partial
  array.<Cite src="engine-api-osaka" /> It also returns `null` if the client is syncing or otherwise
  unable to serve pool data.
- **`engine_getBlobsV3`** — partial. Same request, but the client must return an array of the same
  length and order with `null` only at the positions of the missing
  blobs.<Cite src="engine-api-osaka" />

The reason both exist is the blob count. At a 21-blob cap, an all-or-nothing query fails if a single
one of the twenty-one is absent, discarding twenty perfectly good blobs the node could have used to
fill columns. V3 lets the consensus client take what is there and go to the network for the rest.

`engine_getBlobsV1` returns an unsupported-fork error after Osaka, and `engine_getPayloadV4` errors
for timestamps at or after Osaka activation.<Cite src="engine-api-osaka" /> The old shapes are gone,
not deprecated — the pre-Fulu bundle carried one proof per blob, and the new one carries
`CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB` cell proofs per blob, so there is no compatible reading of the old
response.<Cite src="engine-api-osaka" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-preset" />

### It also lets somebody else finish the proposer's job

The same local-blobs path powers a repair route that does not involve the proposer at all.

`get_data_column_sidecars_from_column_sidecar(sidecar, cells_and_kzg_proofs)` takes **any one** column
sidecar for a block plus the cells and proofs for the blobs it commits to, and returns the full
sequence of all 128 sidecars — described in the spec as being for distributed blob publishing,
reconstructing all sidecars from any sidecar received on the wire, assuming all cells and proofs
could be retrieved from the local execution layer client.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-validator" />

Chain the two facts together and a useful behaviour falls out. A node that sees one column on gossip
and holds the blobs in its own execution client can rebuild every other column and publish the ones
whose subnets it follows. No Reed-Solomon recovery, no 64-column threshold, no waiting: it is
re-deriving from the originals rather than repairing an erasure. That is why the p2p spec's
recommendation to query `engine_getBlobsV2` fires on receiving a valid `beacon_block` **or** a
`data_column_sidecar` — either one is enough context to
start.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" />

The consequence for a proposer running late is that it does not need to get all 128 columns out. It
needs to get the block and enough of a foothold out that nodes with the blobs in their pools can
finish the distribution for it.

### The rules for what you do with the answer

Blobs from your own execution client did not arrive over gossip, and the spec is careful about
re-entering them:

**Skip verification of the blobs themselves.** When clients use the local execution layer to retrieve
blobs, they should skip verification of those blobs.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> The
execution client already validated them on entry to its pool.

**Then behave exactly as if they had arrived by gossip.** When importing them as data columns, clients
must publish the corresponding `data_column_sidecar` on its topic **if and only if** they are
subscribed to it — whether that subscription comes from custody or from additional sampling — and
must update gossip rule data structures including the anti-equivocation
cache.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" />

The "if and only if" is doing real work. A node that published columns for subnets it does not follow
would inject sidecars into meshes it is not a member of, and a node that published nothing would
waste the repair opportunity. The rule threads between both.

## What to instrument

The timebound is only debuggable if you measure the right intervals, and the spec's structure tells
you which ones exist.

**Block arrival, separately from column arrival.** They travel on different topics through different
meshes.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> A node can have the block at 800 ms and still be
missing columns at 3,800 ms, and the two numbers have completely different causes.

**Per-column arrival, not aggregate.** The all-or-nothing rule means the metric that predicts a missed
attestation is the *last* column's arrival time, not the mean.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />
An average that looks healthy hides a tail that is failing you.

**Gossip-versus-request split.** For a minimum-custody node, four of eight sampled groups are not
subscribed, so they arrive by request.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> Tracking what
fraction of sampled columns arrived by subscription versus by request tells you directly whether
raising `custody_group_count` would convert round trips into pushes.

**`engine_getBlobs` hit rate and latency.** The spec recommends querying it on every valid block or
column sidecar,<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> so it fires constantly. Its hit rate is a
measure of your execution client's blob pool coverage, and its miss modes are distinguishable: `null`
for all-or-nothing under V2 versus positional nulls under
V3.<Cite src="engine-api-osaka" /> A collapse to zero hits after an upgrade is the signature of a
version mismatch rather than a network problem.

**Peer coverage of your own sample set.** Because custody is derivable, you can compute at any moment
how many of your current peers are obliged to hold each column you
sample.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> A column covered by one peer is a single point of
failure with a four-second deadline attached, and it is visible before it costs you anything.

None of these are exotic. They are the intervals the specification's own structure creates, and the
reason to enumerate them is that a node reporting "data unavailable" tells you nothing about which of
five different things went wrong.

A staking operator reports that since a recent client upgrade their validators are missing head votes
on roughly 3% of slots. Their monitoring shows: block arrival times unchanged, CPU well under
capacity, no disk pressure. The nodes run the minimum custody setting with no validators-driven
increase applied, and peer count is at the client's default floor. The operator asks whether they
should raise their custody group count to fix it.

Predict the mechanism behind the misses, say whether raising custody helps, and give the ordered list
of things to change.

<Answer>
**The likely mechanism is sampling failing to complete before the attestation deadline, not anything
about compute.**

A minimum-custody node samples 8 groups and custodies 4, so four of its eight columns are *not*
delivered by subscription — they must be requested from peers over request/response after the node
notices they have not arrived.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> Sampling is successful only
if it retrieves **all** selected columns,<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> and the whole
sequence must finish inside `ATTESTATION_DUE_BPS` = 3,333 bps of a 12,000 ms slot, just under four
seconds.<Cite src="consensus-specs-v161-mainnet-config" /> Idle CPU is consistent with this: the node
is waiting on the network, not computing.

**Does raising `custody_group_count` help? Yes, but not for the reason the operator thinks, and it is
not the first thing to try.**

Raising the count raises `sampling_size` one-for-one, since it is
`max(SAMPLES_PER_SLOT, custody_group_count)`.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> Above 8, the
sampled set *equals* the custodied set — so everything the node needs arrives by gossip subscription
rather than by request, and the round trips disappear. That is a genuine fix for this failure mode.
It is also permanent: the ratchet says a node should not later reduce its advertised
count,<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-validator" /> and the storage cost is linear in the number of
groups across the whole 4,096-epoch retention
window.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> Do not reach for it first.

**Ordered list.**

*1. Raise the peer count.* The failure is a missing answer, and EIP-7594 names peer redundancy as the
designed mitigation: a node generally has many peers, peer count can be cheaply increased, and if a
single peer fails another can serve the same sampling task.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> This is the
cheapest lever and it targets the actual cause.

*2. Check that column-targeted peering is working.* The point of deriving custody from node ids is
that a node can find peers who **must** hold a given column before dialling
them.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> A node whose peer set happens not to cover its four
sampled-not-custodied columns will fail systematically rather than randomly — which fits a stable 3%
rather than a jittery one.

*3. Confirm `engine_getBlobsV2`/`V3` is actually wired and answering.* Honest nodes should query it as
soon as a valid block or column sidecar arrives, and it exists precisely to raise the odds of
attesting when a proposer publishes data late.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /> If the upgrade
changed the Engine API version in use, a client falling back to a method that now returns an
unsupported-fork error<Cite src="engine-api-osaka" /> would remove this path silently — and "since a
recent client upgrade" is exactly the shape of that bug.

*4. Only then raise custody*, sized against the storage table, and accept that it is one-way.

**What not to conclude.** Three per cent of slots is not evidence of withholding. The all-or-nothing
success rule makes a slow peer and a hostile proposer produce the identical local
outcome,<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> so a node's failed check is never by itself
evidence about the proposer.
</Answer>

## What this unlocks

You have the real-time picture: 128 subnets, a subscription proportional to custody, a
request/response path for the rest, thirteen validation conditions per sidecar, and a four-second
deadline that all of it has to fit
inside.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-p2p" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-v161-mainnet-config" /> You
also have the escape hatch, and the honest statement of what it is: a latency mechanism bounded by
someone else's mempool retention.<Cite src="engine-api-osaka" />

**Next, and last: what the check is permitted to conclude.** Everything in this module has been
building toward one function in the fork-choice spec, and its content is narrower than the phrase
"data availability" suggests. The final lesson reads `is_data_available` as it is actually written,
says what fork choice does with a block that fails it, and closes on the eighteen-day boundary where
the guarantee stops.

**Carry the deadline.** When you meet a protocol claim that assumes a message was received, ask what
the budget was and who answers when the first peer does not.
