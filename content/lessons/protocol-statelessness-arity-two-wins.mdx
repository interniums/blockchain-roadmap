The instinct is unanimous and wrong. Ask an engineer whether a binary tree or a sixteen-way tree
gives smaller inclusion proofs and they will say the sixteen-way one, because it is shallower, and
shallower means less work.

Shallower does mean fewer levels. It also means every level you keep is fifteen times more
expensive, and that trade is not close.

This lesson is the arithmetic, the reason the original hexary choice was correct and then stopped
being, and the one question the current design has genuinely not answered: which hash function goes
underneath a state commitment securing very large amounts of value.

## The formula, and where it is minimised

Both live proposals print the same reasoning, in almost the same words. In an `N`-element tree where
each node has `k` children, the average branch is roughly:

  alt="The branch-size expression 32 times (k minus 1) times log N over log k, with 32 marked as the one constant, (k minus 1) marked as the sisters published per level and linear in k, and log N over log k marked as the depth, which is only logarithmic in k."

`32` is a hash. `(k-1)` is the sisters you must publish at each level so the verifier can recompute
the parent. `log(N) / log(k)` approximates the depth — EIP-7864 illustrates it with a tree of 625
nodes at arity 5, which has depth 4 because `625 = 5**4`.<Cite src="eip-7864" />

Evaluate it at `N = 2**24`:<Cite src="eip-7864" /><Cite src="eip-8297" />

| `k` | Sisters per level | Levels | Branch (chunks) | Branch (bytes) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **2** | 1 | 24 | 24 | **768** |
| 4 | 3 | 12 | 36 | 1,152 |
| 8 | 7 | 8 | 56 | 1,792 |
| 16 | 15 | 6 | 90 | **2,880** |

Sister count grows linearly in `k`. Depth shrinks only logarithmically. The product is minimised at
`k = 2`, for every `N`, and EIP-7864 says so directly: "For any `N`, the expression is minimized at
`k = 2`."<Cite src="eip-7864" /> The EIP adds the caveat that real branches run slightly longer than
this because the distribution is uneven, "but the pattern of `k=2` remains by far the
best".<Cite src="eip-7864" />

Twenty-four levels of one sister beats six levels of fifteen. That is the entire counterintuitive
result, and once you have written the product out there is nothing left to be surprised by.

<Aside kind="warn">
**768 and 2,880 are branch sizes, not witness sizes.** They describe one path through a
2^24-element tree.<Cite src="eip-7864" /> A block witness contains hundreds or thousands of paths
plus the values, and on the only real measurement available the proof component averaged about 12%
of a witness.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-verkle-replay" /> The two figures get quoted interchangeably —
"witnesses drop from 2,880 bytes to 768" — and that sentence is not true of anything. Keep the unit
attached: *branch, one path, 2^24 elements*.

Dedaub's write-up shows how to quote it correctly, and the care is worth copying: it names the
section it came from and states the condition, writing that "branch length for an equivalent-size
tree drops from ~2,880 bytes (k=16) to ~768 bytes (k=2)".<Cite src="dedaub-locality" /> Sixteen
words, three of which are the qualifier.
</Aside>

<Aside kind="stop">
**Three numbers in circulation that this module will not print as fact.** Secondary summaries of
EIP-7864 assert roughly 4× shorter branches, roughly 3× faster proving with BLAKE3, and "up to 100×"
with a Poseidon variant. None of the three appears in the EIP text, and this module found no
benchmark behind them. The EIP's own table gives 2,880 → 768, which is 3.75× on *branch length for
one path in a 2^24-element tree*, and says nothing at all about proving speed
multipliers.<Cite src="eip-7864" /> If you meet these numbers, ask for the benchmark.
</Aside>

### Count it on the tree that exists

The 2^24 table is a model. Run the same arithmetic against mainnet and the numbers get sharper.

**Subgoal: get today's real depth.** EIP-8297 reports that the account trie currently reaches a
maximum depth of about **12**.<Cite src="eip-8297" />

**Subgoal: price one path at that depth.** `15 * 32 * 12` = **5,760 bytes** for a single account
branch.<Cite src="eip-8297" /> That is one account. Not its storage — that is a second trie, hanging
off the leaf you just proved, with its own depth and its own sisters.

**Subgoal: get the binary depth against real state.** Grouping mainnet state into 256-value stems
gives about 1.23 billion stems, which puts a binary tree at roughly **31 levels**
(`log_2(1.23b)`).<Cite src="stateless-fyi-tree-shape" />

**Subgoal: price the same path.** `1 * 32 * 31` = **992 bytes** to reach a stem, plus the eight
levels inside the stem's 256-value group.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-tree-shape" />

**Subgoal: notice which term dominates.** Roughly 5,760 against roughly 1,250, on the real tree, for
the same logical item — and the entire difference is sisters. The binary tree is two and a half
times deeper and its branch is about a quarter the size.

<Aside kind="note">
**Depth is a real cost; it is just a different cost.** Thirty-one levels means thirty-one hash
computations to recompute a root, against twelve, and the Stateless Book acknowledges this directly:
EL clients "can employ engineering optimizations to deal with the high depth of Binary
Trees".<Cite src="stateless-fyi-tree-shape" /> The Verkle replay's own database measurements show
what a shallow tree buys on that axis — with the state fully converted, **80.6%** of leaves sat at
depth 4 and **19.3%** at depth 5, with a handful reaching
8.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-verkle-replay" /> That is the property being traded away, and trading it
is only reasonable because clients no longer fetch intermediate nodes from disk one at a time.
</Aside>

## Why hexary was right, and what changed

The current trie is sixteen-way for a reason, and the reason is not that anyone miscalculated proof
sizes.

EIP-3102 states it plainly, writing in 2020: hexary Merkle trees are shallower than binary ones,
which means less hashing — and more importantly, fewer disk accesses. "Over the course of the 5
years of Ethereum's existence, it has become apparent that disk accesses are a greater bottleneck
than hashing."<Cite src="eip-3102" /> The Stateless Book gives the same account: the original goal
was to reduce disk lookups, with larger state proofs as the accepted
downside.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-trees" />

Then the premise changed. EIP-3102, in the same paragraph, notes that clients were "moving away from
a storage model in which all internal nodes are stored, in favor of a flat (key, value) storage
model first used by turbo-geth, in which the intermediate nodes are recalculated only when
needed."<Cite src="eip-3102" /> Once intermediate nodes are not individually fetched from disk, tree
depth stops driving disk access, and the entire justification for paying fifteen sisters per level
evaporates.

Notice the shape of that, because it is the same shape as the Verkle reversal one lesson ago. The
design was not wrong. The property of the environment it was selected against — disk lookups
dominate — stopped holding, and nobody revisits a settled decision until something forces it.

<Aside kind="note">
**The rest of EIP-3102's complaint reads like a preview of 2026.** It objects to the MPT's
"uncanny optimizations for size, that have a limited effect — at the cost of prohibitive
complexity", giving as an example the nesting of children whose RLP is under 32 bytes, which it
estimates saves about 1 MB of disk against the ~300 GB a fast sync needed at the time. Its verdict
on those optimisations: "a significant source of errors, and therefore a consensus-breaking
risk."<Cite src="eip-3102" /> Both current proposals drop RLP from the state tree entirely, and
EIP-8297 removes extension nodes as a distinct node type as
well.<Cite src="eip-7864" /><Cite src="eip-8297" />
</Aside>

## What a hash-only tree actually looks like

Arity two plus a hash function is a short specification, which is the point. EIP-7864's version has
four node types and four merkelization rules:<Cite src="eip-7864" />

```text
InternalNode   left_hash, right_hash
StemNode       stem, left_hash, right_hash
LeafNode       32-byte value, or empty
EmptyNode      an empty subtree

hash([0x00] * 64)   = [0x00] * 32
internal_node_hash  = hash(left_hash || right_hash)
stem_node_hash      = hash(stem || 0x00 || hash(left_hash || right_hash))
leaf_node_hash      = hash(value)
empty_node_hash     = [0x00] * 32
```

`H` is "the selected cryptographic hash function", and the input is only ever 32 or 64
bytes.<Cite src="eip-7864" /> Compare that with Verkle's Bandersnatch modulus, 256-point Pedersen
basis, scalar-field encoding of 32-byte values across two field elements, and batched IPA
openings.<Cite src="eip-6800" /> One of these can be reimplemented from the spec in an afternoon.

### It is deliberately not a sparse Merkle tree

The obvious hash-based design is a sparse Merkle tree: fix the depth at 248 bits, treat every absent
key as a known-empty subtree, and you get uniform proofs and trivially simple rules. EIP-7864's
author calls that "the ideal design for a spec... due to its simplicity" and then rejects
it.<Cite src="eip7864-magicians" />

The reason is hashing volume. A full-depth tree means 248 hashes per path, and EIP-7864 says
avoiding that "helps reduce the hashing load in proving systems, which is currently a throughput
bottleneck on commodity hardware."<Cite src="eip-7864" /> So the design compresses — but only just
enough. EIP-7864 puts the minimum number of internal nodes needed to separate stems that share a
prefix, with no extension nodes in paths.<Cite src="eip-7864" /> EIP-8297 goes further and folds a
shared run of bits into a `BranchNode`'s explicit `prefix` field, so a long chain of single-child
nodes collapses to one node.<Cite src="eip-8297" />

That compression is not cosmetic. EIP-8297 explains why it needs it: storage buckets "manufacture
long shared runs", because every one of an account's overflow storage groups shares the same
`key_hash(address)`, up to 256 bits.<Cite src="eip-8297" /> Without folding, that would be a
256-node chain with one occupied child at every step.

### The grinding attack the compression also defeats

Here is where the hash-only design earns its Security Considerations section, and where you can see
two drafts at different levels of maturity.

An attacker who can choose storage slots — trivially, in their own contract, or through mapping keys
in anybody's — can grind for keys whose digests share leading bits, deepening the tree on the paths
they control and inflating everyone's proofs. EIP-8297 works the arithmetic: grinding for a shared
`k`-bit prefix buys a `k`-node chain for about `2**(k/2)` work if there is no compression. With
compression, that run folds into one branch prefix of about `k/8` bytes, and `d` genuinely extra
nodes cost about `2**d` work — exponential in the damage rather than square-root.<Cite src="eip-8297" />
Binding the group-spreading digest to the address as well as the index confines the attack to the
attacker's own storage bucket, so a slot set ground for one contract is random in every
other.<Cite src="eip-8297" />

EIP-8297 also makes node hashing injective on purpose: every preimage begins with a one-byte tag
distinguishing leaves from branches, and a branch's prefix carries an explicit bit count, so no leaf
and branch preimage can coincide and no two prefixes of different length pack to the same
bytes.<Cite src="eip-8297" /> That is the [second-preimage discipline](/c/merkle-second-preimage) you
already know, applied at the node-encoding layer.

EIP-7864's Security Considerations section, in full, reads: "Needs discussion."<Cite src="eip-7864" />
Two documents, one lineage, fourteen months apart. Status lines are not the only thing worth reading.

### The group inside the stem is a Merkle tree too

One detail is easy to skim past and it is the hinge of the whole redesign.

Both proposals group 256 related values under a shared stem. Verkle committed to those 256 with a
polynomial vector commitment. The binary tree commits to them with **a full Merkle tree of eight
levels** — the Stateless Book's phrasing is that it uses "a full Merkle tree as a form of vector
commitment".<Cite src="stateless-fyi-binary-tree" /> EIP-7864's merkelization code makes it literal:
hash all 256 values, then pair-wise fold the level until one hash remains, then bind that to the
stem.<Cite src="eip-7864" />

So the grouping survived the primitive change unaltered, and the cost of opening a group went from
"independent of width" to "eight hashes". Eight is small. That is the whole reason 256 was allowed
to stay a reasonable group size after the commitment underneath it changed — and the Stateless Book
notes the number is now free to move, since 256 was chosen "for historical reasons rooted in Verkle
Trees proving efficiency" and binary trees have "more flexibility in choosing a different
size".<Cite src="stateless-fyi-data-encoding" />

Watch that parameter. It is the kind of constant that looks settled because it has been printed
identically in four specifications, and is in fact inherited rather than derived. The June 2026
abstract for EIP-8297 described 32-byte keys; the specification as it reads today uses
variable-length keys with `MAX_KEY_LENGTH` at **8,192 bytes**, a bound derived from a two-byte bit
count in the branch-prefix encoding.<Cite src="eip8297-magicians" /><Cite src="eip-8297" /> Constants
in a Draft are proposals, not facts.

## The hash function is not decided, and that is the live question

Everything above works with *any* secure hash. Which one goes in is explicitly open, and EIP-7864
front-loads the warning into its abstract: the current implementation uses BLAKE3 "to reduce
friction for EL clients experimenting with this EIP, but the final decision remains TBD... **Do not
assume BLAKE3 is a final decision.**"<Cite src="eip-7864" /> EIP-8297 carries the same
note.<Cite src="eip-8297" />

The Stateless Book puts numbers on the gap in words rather than digits, and the words are stark.
Proving performance on target hardware for conventional hash functions such as Keccak or BLAKE3 is
"still an order of magnitude slower than required for L1 blocks" — so choosing one means waiting for
provers to improve, or requiring more powerful hardware from block builders. With an arithmetic hash
such as Poseidon2, current proving performance on target hardware is "more than enough for L1 block
needs".<Cite src="stateless-fyi-binary-tree" />

And the objection, from the same page: Poseidon2 "is still not considered safe for use in L1", with
the Ethereum Foundation cryptography team having launched a public initiative to assess it more
formally.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-binary-tree" /> The book then offers the honest optimistic case and
immediately marks it as insufficient: several ZK L2s have used Poseidon for years with billions of
dollars publicly at stake, and while that is not a formal bounty, anyone able to break it could
already have attacked those networks — "This doesn't prove Poseidon is safe, but it gives an
optimistic perspective while the formal assessment is done."<Cite src="stateless-fyi-binary-tree" />

<Aside kind="warn">
**Poseidon's status on L1 is contested and this module will not resolve it.** Both EIPs, read on
**26 August 2026**, still list Poseidon2 as a live candidate with EF security analysis
ongoing.<Cite src="eip-7864" /><Cite src="eip-8297" /> Secondary reporting collected by this project
says the opposite — that Ethereum L1 has moved away from Poseidon toward SHA/BLAKE-family hashes for
post-quantum reasons — and no first-party decision record for that was located. Two plausible
readings: the reporting is ahead of the specs, or it is wrong. Both EIPs also note that choosing
Poseidon2 would require *additional* specification for field selection and for encoding 32-byte
values into field elements, which is itself an undecided sub-question rather than a
detail.<Cite src="eip-7864" /> Do not teach either answer as settled.
</Aside>

### Why the choice can be deferred at all

It is fair to ask how a specification can leave its central primitive open and still be worth
implementing.

The answer is a property of the design rather than a concession. Because every merkelization rule
takes an input of exactly 32 or 64 bytes and returns 32, the hash appears in the specification at
exactly one place, behind one signature. EIP-7864 says so: the function "can be switched with
minimal impact on implementations as this EIP progresses toward
acceptance."<Cite src="eip-7864" /> The Stateless Book agrees, describing the construction as
working with "any secure hash function".<Cite src="stateless-fyi-binary-tree" />

Verkle could not do this. Its commitment was not a swappable component — the curve determined the
field, the field determined how a 32-byte value had to be split into two elements, and that split
determined the shape of every stem node.<Cite src="eip-6800" /> Cryptography that reaches up into
the data layout cannot be deferred, and cannot be replaced later without redesigning what sits on
top of it.

So the open hash question is not an unfinished specification. It is a specification that put its
riskiest dependency behind an interface and can therefore ship the rest while the risk is being
assessed. That is an ordinary engineering discipline, and it is unusual to see it applied to a
consensus-critical cryptographic primitive.

### What is actually being decided

Strip the candidate names and the decision has a clean shape, and it is not primarily technical.

An arithmetic hash makes the state tree cheap to prove, which lets block builders run smaller
hardware, which is a decentralisation argument — and, as the Stateless Book puts it, keeps proving
the hash function from getting in the way of raising the gas limit.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-binary-tree" />
A conventional hash makes the state tree expensive to prove and rests on decades of public
cryptanalysis.

So: **how much novel cryptography are you willing to put underneath the state commitment of a
network securing very large amounts of value, in exchange for how much proving throughput.** That is
a question about risk tolerance, and the Foundation's own zkEVM work supplies the cautionary case —
its December 2025 write-up reports that foundational STARK conjectures were mathematically disproven
and that systems advertised at 100 bits of security might actually have had
80.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" /> Novel cryptography has been wrong recently, in this
exact neighbourhood, and the response was to reset the security bar rather than to ship.

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson** takes the other half of the redesign: one key space instead of a tree of trees,
and the stem-and-suffix layout that makes 256 related values share a single branch opening. That is
where the tree change stops being a client concern and starts showing up in your storage layout.

**Lesson seven** returns to the hash question from the other side. If blocks are going to be proved
in real time, the merkelization hash is one of the largest single terms in the proving cost — which
is why a decision that looks like a cryptography detail is entangled with the whole roadmap.

**In your own designs**, the transferable move is the conditional. Every "X is better than Y" in
this lesson is true only against a stated primitive and a stated bottleneck: hexary was right while
disk lookups dominated, Verkle was right while gossip was the constraint, binary is right if proving
is. Write the condition down beside the conclusion, and the conclusion becomes re-checkable when the
condition changes rather than becoming folklore.
