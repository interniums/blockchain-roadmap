Two ways to let somebody verify a block while holding no state.

**Send them the state the block touched, with proofs.** They execute it themselves and check the
resulting root. That is statelessness, and the previous six lessons were about making the bundle
small enough to send.

**Send them a proof that the block was executed correctly.** They check the proof. They need neither
the state nor a large witness, because they never execute anything.

These are usually presented as rivals, with a winner implied by whoever is presenting. The sources
do not support that framing, and this lesson is about what they do support: where the two genuinely
compete, where one is a prerequisite for the other, and what happens to the third option — deleting
state outright — which has been proposed for longer than either and scheduled never.

## They are not alternatives at the layer people compare them

Start with the awkward fact for the rivalry framing.

EIP-7864's *motivation* — the first paragraph of the proposal that replaced Verkle — is not about
witnesses. It reads: "Ethereum's long-term goal is to allow blocks to be proved with validity proof
so that chain verification is as simple and fast as possible. One of the most challenging parts of
achieving that goal is proving the state of the tree, which is required for EVM
execution."<Cite src="eip-7864" /> EIP-8297 opens the same way.<Cite src="eip-8297" />

It then lists what makes the current trie hostile to proving: RLP node encoding, Keccak as the
hashing function, being a "tree of trees", and not including account code in the
state.<Cite src="eip-7864" /> Every one of those is also a witness-size problem, which is why one
tree change serves both routes.

The EIP-7864 author says it outright in the discussion thread, answering a question about sync
strategies: "changing the state tree is a stepping stone into SNARKifing the L1, which means that
most nodes won't have to sync the entire state (that's one of the main goals, after
all)."<Cite src="eip7864-magicians" />

And the Stateless Book's chapter on binary-tree proof construction gives the two options for the
same tree in the same paragraph: build an ordinary Merkle proof, which is simpler than an MPT proof
and smaller because of the arity; or, for an L1 block's worst-case pre-state where a raw tree proof
"is bigger than desired", create a SNARK/STARK proof that is a Merkle-proof verifier inside a
circuit — more work to generate, smaller to send.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-binary-tree" />

So the honest structure is: **the state commitment has to change either way**, and the choice of a
hash-based binary tree over a curve-based wide one is precisely a bet on the proving
route.<Cite src="eip-7864" /> Read the last four lessons again with that in mind and the
"rivals" story dissolves. Arity two, no RLP, chunked code, an unsettled hash function — all of it
reads as preparation for proving, with better raw witnesses as a side effect.

## Where they do compete

The competition is real, and it is about the **marginal verifier**: what a given participant must
have in order to check a block.

**Witness route.** Every verifier receives the state slice and re-executes. Costs: bandwidth inside
a slot, and an EVM plus a tree implementation on every verifier.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-stateless-clients" />
Risks: witness size in the worst case, and the delivery deadline.

**Proving route.** One party proves; every verifier checks a proof. Costs: proving latency and
hardware for that party, and a dependency on a proof system's soundness. Risks: novel cryptography,
and a liveness dependency on provers.

The Stateless Book's own framing of what statelessness buys makes the comparison sharper, because it
puts throughput first: removing the requirement to hold state raises the gas limit, since the IO
bottleneck is "the principal hindrance" to raising it, and it lets a node join the network from an
execution-layer block rather than from a sync.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-intro" /> The proving route
gets the throughput benefit too — it decouples verification time from the gas limit entirely — but
not the sync benefit, since a prover still needs the state.

The tradeoff is legible: the witness route distributes a modest cost across everybody, the proving
route concentrates a large cost on somebody. Which is preferable depends on whether the concentrated
cost is affordable and whether the concentration is acceptable — and note that weak statelessness
already concedes a concentration of its own, since builders hold full state and everyone else does
not.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-stateless-clients" />

<Aside kind="warn">
**Do not compare their numbers.** The proving side's published costs and latencies use mismatched
units and scopes: figures quoted per proof and per transaction are not comparable; per-transaction
figures divide a block's proving cost by however many transactions were in it; and latency claims
span workloads from one L1 block to a five-thousand-transaction L2 batch, on clusters from a desktop
to sixteen GPUs, across different years. The scaling track works this through in detail. The rule
here is the same one: **publish no proving figure without its unit, its workload, its hardware and
its date**, and prefer a source that attaches all four — the Ethereum Foundation's Ethproofs board
publishes cost and latency per proven block with the cluster
named.<Cite src="ethproofs" /><Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" />
</Aside>

The Foundation's own bar has moved in a direction worth noting, because it tells you what the risk
actually is. Its first real-time-proving post set an envelope around latency and hardware; its
December 2025 follow-up reported the performance definition met and then **reset the bar around
security**, after foundational STARK conjectures were mathematically disproven and systems
advertised at 100 bits of security turned out possibly to have had
80.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" /><Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" />

That is the shape of the proving route's risk. Not "will it be fast enough" — it got fast — but "do
we know what it proves". Which is the same question the hash choice in lesson four turns on, and it
is not a coincidence: an arithmetic hash under the state tree and a novel proof system over the
execution are the same bet placed twice.

## State expiry: the third option, and why it never happens

There is an option neither route addresses. Instead of avoiding the state or proving over it,
**stop counting it**.

State expiry makes state nobody has touched for a period inactive. Using it again requires a
resurrection proof. Unlike statelessness, which relocates the cost, expiry attacks state growth
directly.

The version with a number is EIP-7736, and it is Stagnant. Its
mechanism:<Cite src="eip-7736" />

```text
EPOCH_LENGTH         15,778,800 seconds (6 months)
NUM_ACTIVE_EPOCHS    2
INITIAL_EPOCH_COUNTER 0
RESURRECT_TX_TYPE    TBD

read/write allowed only if:  current_epoch < last_epoch + NUM_ACTIVE_EPOCHS
                             otherwise revert
```

Each group of values carries a `last_epoch`, updated on write. When an epoch rolls over, groups
whose `last_epoch` fell out of the active window can be deleted, keeping only a **keepsake** — the
stem value and the commitment — so siblings can still be inserted and the data resurrected. A new
transaction type carries the deleted values back with a proof.<Cite src="eip-7736" />

Its own motivation explains why it is shaped so modestly. Previous attempts at state expiry "have
been stalled by the quickly-increasing complexity, require heavy change in the structure of ethereum
(address space extension, oil, multiple trees, …)", and this one deliberately removes only leaf
nodes, leaving the rest of the tree intact, to avoid "methods that would be detrimental to the user
and developer experience."<Cite src="eip-7736" />

Read that as an admission. The reason expiry keeps not shipping is not that nobody can design it. It
is that the consequences land in the places users notice: an address that quietly stops working, a
wallet that must construct and pay for a resurrection proof before an ordinary transaction, and
address-format changes to carry the information needed to find what was expired.

### And a measured reason it might not even pay

Here is the finding that most changes how you should hold this.

The mainnet tree-shape analysis groups state into the 256-value groups both proposed trees use, and
counts how full those groups are. Storage-slot groups have an **average and median of one non-zero
value**; account-header groups average four with a median of two. And
**871,033,634** storage-slot groups hold exactly one non-zero value — about **92%** of storage-slot
groups and about **71% of all groups** on the network.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-tree-shape" />

The analysis draws the conclusion itself: for leaf-level expiry, expiring data in such groups
"wouldn't reclaim much space since we're only deleting 32 bytes (single non-zero value) but keep the
corresponding stems, which aren't collapsed — this is more significant in a Binary Tree scenario
than a Verkle Tree one because of the arity."<Cite src="stateless-fyi-tree-shape" /> It then flags
that this needs detailed investigation, because it depends on how many such groups expire in a
typical epoch.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-tree-shape" />

So the sharpest available critique of leaf-level expiry is not about user experience at all. It is
that on the actual distribution of mainnet state — dominated by Solidity mappings scattering one
word per group — you delete 32 bytes and keep the structure that costs the most. And the design that
replaced Verkle makes that *worse*, because a deeper tree means more retained nodes per surviving
stem.

<Aside kind="note">
**"Parked" rather than "abandoned" is the accurate word, and the sources support it.** EIP-7736 is
Stagnant.<Cite src="eip-7736" /> EIP-7864's rationale keeps a place for it: expiry strategies "such
as EIP-7736 could still be applied, requiring a change in the design", and it sketches two ways to
carry the extra field.<Cite src="eip-7864" /> EIP-8297 goes further, arguing that per-account and
per-bucket expiry is "a natural operation on the zone topology" — record the hash of an account's
storage bucket and prune below it — while stating that "the mechanism itself is left to a separate
EIP".<Cite src="eip-8297" /> The Stateless Book lists three complementary models under active
research: leaf-based with resurrection "nearly complete in Geth", epoch-based with a new tree per
epoch and a resurrection bitmap, and a "1 year of active state" model — plus a separate
compression-based approach that moves cold data to flat files behind
pointers.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-active-projects" /> Live research, no numbered successor, no fork.
</Aside>

### The options nobody puts on the slide

Between "send a witness", "send a proof" and "delete it", there is a middle band of proposals that
are less ambitious and correspondingly more likely to happen. The Stateless Book's active-projects
page lists them, and they are worth knowing because they change the shape of the problem the
headline proposals are trying to solve.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-active-projects" />

**Partial statefulness.** A node syncs a *segment* of the state rather than all of it, formalised
under the name OOPSIE — Opt-in Ownership of Partial State of Interest,
Exclusively.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-active-projects" /> Note what this does to the binary framing:
a node that holds the slice it cares about is neither stateful nor stateless. And EIP-8297's zoned
key space is explicitly designed to support it, since a zone or an account's storage bucket is a
key-space region that can be synced or pruned on its own.<Cite src="eip-8297" />

**Temporary contract storage.** Semi-persistent storage managed by a system contract that clears on
a fixed cadence of about six months, to move ephemeral data out of permanent
state.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-active-projects" /> This is expiry with the hard part removed: no
resurrection, no address-format change, because nothing that expires was ever promised to be
permanent. Opt-in at write time rather than imposed at read time.

**Compression-based expiry.** Move cold data out of the active database into flat files behind
pointers, and remove the corresponding internal trie nodes to improve
IO.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-active-projects" /> A client-side change wearing the word expiry, with
none of the consensus consequences.

**And BloatNet**, which is not a fix but the measuring instrument — a dedicated network for
stress-testing under state growth, used to produce multi-client benchmarks and validate the gas
repricing proposals.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-active-projects" /> The 650 GB threshold from the first
lesson comes from there, and it is the number that turns every proposal above from an argument into
a deadline.

The pattern across all four is the same trade: **give up the property that requires a consensus
change, keep most of the benefit.** That is why they are moving and the headline designs are not.

## What is actually moving

Set the three routes against what has a fork attached, because that is the only ranking that is not
opinion.

**Nothing in this module has a fork.** EIP-7864, EIP-8297 and EIP-8347 are Draft. EIP-7736, EIP-7612
and EIP-6800 are Stagnant. EIP-4762 has been Draft since
2022.<Cite src="eip-7864" /><Cite src="eip-8297" /><Cite src="eip-8347" /><Cite src="eip-7736" />

**History is the one that got solved.** EIP-4444, which bounds how long execution clients must serve
historical data, is the oldest item in this neighbourhood and the only one whose direction is
broadly settled.<Cite src="eip-4444" /> It is worth asking why: history is append-only and
sequential, nothing needs it to execute the next block, and dropping it changes no commitment and
breaks no address. Every property that made it tractable is a property state does not have.

**Repricing is moving.** EIP-8037 and EIP-8038 are in Review, and they attack state growth without
changing the tree, the address format or anything a user sees. EIP-8037 introduces a cost-per-state
-byte variable targeting an average growth rate at a reference gas limit; EIP-8038 raises access and
write costs against benchmarks run on a March 2026-sized
snapshot.<Cite src="eip-8037" /><Cite src="eip-8038" /> Repricing is unglamorous, needs no
migration, and is the route with the shortest path to mainnet.

**Declaring access is moving fastest.** EIP-7928 is in Review and is one of the two headline items
of the Glamsterdam upgrade, alongside EIP-7732.<Cite src="eip-7928" /><Cite src="eipsinsight-upgrades" />
It has a block declare every account and storage location it touches with post-execution values,
enabling parallel disk reads and parallel validation.<Cite src="eip-7928" />

<Aside kind="warn">
**Glamsterdam has not shipped and this module prints no activation date for it.** The upgrade
following Fusaka — which went live on 3 December 2025 and raised the gas limit to 60M — was still in
its testnet phase when this lesson was written, with devnets having run through
2026.<Cite src="eipsinsight-upgrades" /> Trackers and secondary coverage give **mutually
inconsistent** mainnet targets, so none is printed here. The upgrade after it, Hegotá, is in
scoping, anchored by FOCIL (EIP-7805) — which was proposed for Glamsterdam and declined, on the
reasoning that combining it with ePBS in one upgrade would put too much untested interaction on
mainnet at once.<Cite src="eipsinsight-upgrades" /> A numbered, specified, widely-taught EIP can be
cut from an upgrade late. That is the same lesson as Verkle's, on a shorter timescale.
</Aside>

And notice the coupling that has already formed. EIP-8347's catch-up mechanism replays EIP-7928
access lists to bring a converted snapshot forward to the chain tip, on the stated reasoning that
those lists are "already produced for other reasons and already exchanged between
peers".<Cite src="eip-8347" /> The state-tree migration — for a tree with no fork, using a migration
with no fork — is designed against a mechanism that has not activated either. Dependencies form
between unshipped things, and when one of them is cut, the graph moves.

<Answer>
**The premise is backwards, and the EIPs say so in their first paragraph.**

Both live state-tree proposals *open* by naming validity proofs as the long-term goal and then
identify the current trie as one of the hardest parts of reaching it — RLP encoding, Keccak, a tree
of trees, and code held only as a hash so no slice of it can be
proved.<Cite src="eip-7864" /><Cite src="eip-8297" /> The tree change is not a competing programme;
it is a dependency of the one he has read about. The author's own phrasing in the discussion thread
is that changing the tree is "a stepping stone into SNARKifing the
L1".<Cite src="eip7864-magicians" />

**And the tree choice was made *for* the proving route**, which is the part that makes this concrete.
Verkle produced smaller single proofs but curve-based commitments are expensive to verify inside a
circuit; the successor is hash-only and its two named advantages are post-quantum security and
in-circuit friendliness.<Cite src="eip-7864" /> Even the open hash question is a proving question:
conventional hashes are described as roughly an order of magnitude too slow in-circuit for L1
blocks, and an arithmetic hash as sufficient but not yet considered safe for
L1.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-binary-tree" /> Stop tracking the tree and you stop tracking the largest
single input to proving cost.

**What I would track instead of "is proving real yet".**

1. *Fork assignment, not status.* Draft and Review mean an idea; a meta-EIP for a named upgrade
   means a date. Right now nothing in the state-tree lineage has one, while EIP-7928 is a
   Glamsterdam headliner.<Cite src="eip-7928" /><Cite src="eipsinsight-upgrades" />
2. *The `Fork` section of whichever tree EIP is current.* That is where EIP-8297 names its migration,
   and where the migration design changed from an in-consensus overlay to an offline
   conversion.<Cite src="eip-8297" /><Cite src="eip-8347" />
3. *The hash decision.* It moves in-circuit proving cost by about an order of magnitude and
   determines how much unreviewed cryptography sits under the state
   root.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-binary-tree" />
4. *Proving figures only with their scope.* Cost per proof and cost per transaction are not the same
   quantity, and latency claims span workloads and hardware generations. Take them from a board that
   publishes cost, latency and cluster per proven block.<Cite src="ethproofs" />
5. *Security milestones, not performance ones.* The Foundation met its performance definition and
   then reset the bar around provable security after STARK conjectures were
   disproven.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" /> That is the term most likely to move the
   date.

**And what to tell him plainly:** neither route is scheduled, both need the same tree change, and the
work that is actually shipping in this neighbourhood is gas repricing and access-list declaration —
neither of which is what either headline promised.

## What this unlocks

**This module ends where the roadmap actually is**, which is not at a decision. Two routes to a
verifier holding nothing, one shared prerequisite, three tree designs and two migration designs in
six years, and the things with fork assignments are the modest ones: bound history, reprice state,
declare what you touch.

**For your own reading**, the durable artifacts are the ones this module cited hardest: EIPs at a
number, their status lines, their rationale sections, and their discussion threads' first posts. The
explainers move slowest and were correct when written. The practice exercises for this module ask
you to measure a real proof and to write the correction sheet for a course that teaches Verkle,
which is the same skill twice: check the artifact, date the check.

**And the reflection prompt** asks what process change would have surfaced this cancellation earlier.
This lesson's honest contribution to that answer is the list of things that actually changed, in the
order a reader could have seen them: a status line moving to Stagnant, a successor's rationale
arguing against the incumbent by name, one sentence edited into the top of a forum thread, and a
`Fork` section naming a different EIP. Each is free to check. None of them sends a notification. What
that process costs you every week it finds nothing is the part of the prompt worth being honest
about — and the fact that this module found two more reversals while being written is the argument
for paying it.
