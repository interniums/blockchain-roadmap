Open the EIP that specifies Verkle trees for Ethereum state. Read the status line above the title.

**Stagnant.**<Cite src="eip-6800" />

Now open the EIP that specifies how the state would transition to a Verkle tree. **Stagnant.**<Cite src="eip-7612" />
The one specifying state expiry on that tree. **Stagnant.**<Cite src="eip-7736" />

That is the whole cancellation, visible in three status lines, on pages that have not otherwise
changed. There was no announcement post. There is no "Verkle has been dropped" blog entry from the
Ethereum Foundation that this module could locate, and none of the sources here claims one exists.
What exists is a successor proposal whose rationale explains, in its own words, why it does not use
elliptic curves — and a body of still-circulating course material, written between 2022 and 2024 and
correct when written, teaching Verkle as Ethereum's next state tree.

This lesson does three things. It teaches the idea that made Verkle the obvious answer, because that
idea is genuinely beautiful and you need it to understand why arity two later wins. It states the
reasons for the reversal with the exact provenance each one has. And it is careful about the shape of
the claim, because "a design was cancelled and nobody announced it" is a claim that gets overstated
in both directions.

## The idea: a commitment that does not charge you for width

Start from why widening a hash-based tree makes proofs worse.

In a Merkle node with `k` children, the node's hash is a function of all `k` child hashes. To prove
one child, you publish the other `k-1` so the verifier can recompute the parent. Widen the node and
you shorten the tree — fewer levels — but every level you keep now costs `k-1` hashes instead of
one. EIP-7864 states the tradeoff as a formula: the average branch in an `N`-element tree with `k`
children per node is roughly `32 * (k-1) * log(N) / log(k)` bytes.<Cite src="eip-7864" /> Sibling
count is linear in `k`; depth reduction is only logarithmic. Width loses.

A **vector commitment** breaks that link. It commits to a whole list of values such that any single
position can be opened with a proof whose size does not depend on how many entries the list has.
Given one, everything inverts: widening a node costs nothing per level, so you want the widest node
you can build, and the tree becomes very shallow.

You have already met a vector commitment. [KZG](/c/kzg-commitment) commits to a polynomial and opens
it at a point with a proof that is one group element regardless of the polynomial's degree — which
is exactly what makes a blob's 128 KiB provable with 48 bytes. Verkle applies the same move to a
tree node: interpolate the children as a polynomial, commit, and open at the position you need.

<Aside kind="note">
**Why "Verkle".** The name is a portmanteau of vector commitment and Merkle tree, and the Stateless
Book traces the construction to a paper by John Kuszmaul.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-verkle-tree" />
The book also records that the idea superseded *older binary tree approaches* — which is worth
holding onto, because the direction of travel later reverses exactly along that
line.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-verkle-tree" />
</Aside>

## What EIP-6800 actually specified

Concretely, so the later contrast has something to bite on.

The tree is **arity 256**: `VERKLE_NODE_WIDTH` is the length of the Pedersen basis, which is
256.<Cite src="eip-6800" /> The curve is **Bandersnatch**, with its modulus written out in the spec
alongside a Pedersen basis of 256 points.<Cite src="eip-6800" />

Keys are 32 bytes, split into a **31-byte stem** and a **1-byte suffix**, so 256 related values sit
under one stem. Because a group element's scalar field is smaller than 256 bits, each 32-byte value
has to be split across two field elements — which is why a stem's 256 values are committed as **two
256-entry vectors, C1 and C2**, covering the first and last 128 values, and why the encoding needs a
rule to distinguish a written zero from an empty position (`2**128` is added to the lower half of a
present value).<Cite src="eip-6800" /><Cite src="stateless-fyi-verkle-tree" />

Proving one value means opening C1 at two positions (the value's low and high halves), then the
extension node at three positions, then each internal node up to the root — with all of those
openings batched into a single short proof.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-verkle-tree" />

And the payoff EIP-6800 claims for all of that: a witness accessing an account in today's hexary
trie is "in the average case, close to 3 kB", and Verkle "reduce[s] witness sizes to ~200 bytes per
account in the average case".<Cite src="eip-6800" />

Fifteen times smaller, on the metric the whole programme was blocked on. That is why Verkle was the
plan for four years, and anyone who called it obvious was right on the evidence available.

EIP-3102 proposed a binary trie in 2020 with an explicit stateless motivation — "Binary tries make
for smaller (~4x) proofs than hexary tries, making it the design of choice for a stateless-friendly
Ethereum" — and merged the account and storage tries for the same
reason.<Cite src="eip-3102" /> The Stateless Book records what happened next: "Then, SNARKs were
still in the early stages, and soon after, Verkle Trees became a more promising approach, so this
direction was abandoned."<Cite src="stateless-fyi-binary-tree" />

So the sequence is binary, then Verkle, then binary again, and the book names the two things that
changed to swing it back: quantum-computing progress raising a concern about elliptic curves, and
the rapid improvement of SNARK/STARK proving throughput for
hashes.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-binary-tree" /> The design did not fail. **The environment it was
selected in moved**, twice, and the selection followed.

That is also the honest answer to "was four years wasted". EIP-3102's binary trie was the right call
against 2020's proving systems and the wrong call against 2020's expectations of them; Verkle was
the right call against 2023's; and the current direction is a bet on 2026's. Each was a bet on the
state of a fast-moving neighbouring field, made by people who said so.

## The reversal, with each reason's provenance attached

The successor proposal, EIP-7864, is a hash-based binary tree. Its rationale contains a section
titled "SNARK friendliness and Post-Quantum security", and that section is where the reversal is
argued. Here is what it says, and how much weight each part carries.

**Reason one: elliptic curves are not post-quantum.** The rationale states that expert estimations
consider quantum computers could become real in the 2030s, that NIST suggests stopping the use of
ECC by 2030, and that "Other alternatives, such as Verkle Trees, introduce a new cryptography stack
to the protocol, which relies on elliptic curves that aren't post-quantum secure." Its own design,
depending only on hash functions, "is still safe in this new
paradigm".<Cite src="eip-7864" /> EIP-8297 repeats the argument almost
verbatim.<Cite src="eip-8297" />

**Reason two: proving systems caught up, removing Verkle's advantage.** The same section: "there has
been impressive progress in proving systems, which indicates that we could be close to achieving the
required performance for creating pre-state & post-state proofs fast enough. This last point is
crucial since **the main advantage of Verkle Trees was having proofs that are small and fast to
generate**."<Cite src="eip-7864" />

**Reason three: adopting Verkle guarantees a second migration.** The rationale's closing line on the
subject is that the hash-based proposal "will probably be the final state tree used in the protocol,
compared with Verkle Trees, which would eventually need to be replaced by a post-quantum secure
alternative."<Cite src="eip-7864" /> The Stateless Book says it more bluntly: "if Verkle Trees are
deployed, at least one extra state tree conversion is
guaranteed to happen."<Cite src="stateless-fyi-binary-tree" />

**Reason four, which is not what you would guess: SNARK-hostility.** Polynomial commitments are
expensive to verify *inside* a proof system. If the long-term direction is proving blocks, the state
commitment has to be cheap in-circuit, and a curve-based one is not.<Cite src="eip-7864" /> This is
the reason most often garbled in secondary coverage, because it sounds like a performance complaint
and is not: Verkle proofs are small and fast to make *outside* a circuit. The problem is what they
cost when a prover has to verify them as part of proving a block.

<Aside kind="warn">
**The shape of this claim, stated exactly.** These four reasons are consistent across EIP-7864's
rationale, EIP-8297's rationale, and the Stateless Book — three current, first-party
documents.<Cite src="eip-7864" /><Cite src="eip-8297" /><Cite src="stateless-fyi-binary-tree" />
What this module did **not** find is a single authoritative statement announcing the cancellation
and giving its reasons. So the correct form of the claim is: *the successor's own rationale argues
against curve-based commitments on these grounds, and the Verkle-lineage EIPs are Stagnant.* That is
strong evidence and it is not a press release. Teach it in that shape; anyone who tells you they
read the announcement should be asked for the link.
</Aside>

### On the metric it was chosen for, Verkle still wins

It would be comfortable to conclude that the successor is better in every dimension. It is not, and
saying so is the difference between teaching a decision and teaching a slogan.

Take the numbers from the same mainnet analysis. Grouping current state into 256-value stems gives
**1,234,691,757 stems** — about 1.23 billion — split into 23.40% account-header stems, 76.42%
storage-slot stems and 0.18% code-chunk stems.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-tree-shape" /> From that, the
depths follow directly: a Verkle tree at arity 256 would be about **4 levels deep**
(`log_256(1.23b)`); a binary tree about **31**.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-tree-shape" />

Now price one small, realistic proof — a single account's nonce and balance, which is what a
stateless mempool or an inclusion-list mechanism needs:<Cite src="stateless-fyi-tree-shape" />

The EOA case is smaller than the contract case because the eight levels inside a stem's value group
compress better when the siblings are known-empty hashes.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-tree-shape" />

Read the comparison honestly: **for a single small opening, the binary tree's proof is larger than
Verkle's**, by something like 40% to 70%. The analysis says as much and adds the qualifier that
matters — for mainnet blocks each proof type "will shine differently in different dimensions",
because a binary tree proof for a whole block would be SNARKified rather than
shipped raw.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-tree-shape" />

That is the actual trade. Verkle is better at the thing witnesses were originally about: sending a
small proof over a network. The binary tree is better at being *proved inside a circuit*, which is a
different requirement that only became the dominant one when real-time block proving started looking
achievable. Choosing the binary tree is choosing to optimise for the proving route rather than the
gossip route.

## Verkle was measurably slower, and that is not why

Here is where most retellings go wrong, so it is worth planting a flag.

There *is* a measured performance penalty. The Stateless Consensus team replayed 200k mainnet blocks
twice, once with Pedersen key hashing and once with SHA-256. By the end, the SHA-256 run was **75,072
blocks ahead** — which the analysis puts at "a loss of 3 hours per day", or SHA-256 being **~12%
faster**.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-verkle-replay" />

The analysis's own conclusion from that number is instructive: "Coupled to the fact that Pedersen
hashes aren't quantum resistant, it seems like a good idea to reconsider using Pedersen hashes to
compute trees."<Cite src="stateless-fyi-verkle-replay" /> Read the coupling. Twelve percent is a
real cost and, on its own, an argument for optimisation rather than abandonment. It becomes decisive
only in combination with the security property.

### The headline number and the measured one, from the same team

One more piece of honesty is owed here, because it changes how much the cancellation cost.

EIP-6800's claim is ~200 bytes per account against ~3 kB today: **fifteen times
smaller**.<Cite src="eip-6800" /> That number is what everybody quoted, and it is what made Verkle
look like the answer.

Then the same working group measured whole witnesses on a Verkle-converted replay of mainnet and got
a **median of 380 KB and a maximum of 928 KB** per block, with the Verkle proof averaging **12%** of
those bytes and raw pre-state values averaging **78%**.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-verkle-replay" />

Both are that group's own figures and neither is wrong. They measure different things: one is the
proof component for one account, the other is the whole object a verifier must receive. Divide 928 KB
by a 12-second slot and you get roughly 620 kilobits per second of additional sustained gossip in
the worst measured block — genuinely deliverable, which is the point, and not remotely "200 bytes".

Two consequences follow, and they pull in opposite directions.

**The cancellation cost less than the headline suggests.** If the commitment is 12% of the witness,
replacing a very good commitment with a merely adequate one moves the total by single-digit
percentages, not by an order of magnitude. The binary tree is not throwing away a 15× win at the
level anybody experiences.

**And the original framing was slightly wrong all along.** Statelessness was widely explained as
blocked on proof size. Measured, it is mostly blocked on *value* size plus one catastrophic worst
case — unchunked code — and the worst case is what both replacement designs actually remove. That
reframing is what makes the arity argument in the next lesson feel like engineering rather than
capitulation.

## What survived, which is most of it

The reversal is often described as starting over. It was not. Read EIP-7864 next to EIP-6800 and
most of the design is identical, because most of the design was never about the commitment.

Both use a **31-byte stem plus a 1-byte suffix**, grouping 256 related values under one
stem.<Cite src="eip-6800" /><Cite src="eip-7864" /> Both **merge the account and storage tries** into
one key space. Both **drop RLP**. Both put **chunked code in the tree** with the same 31-bytes-plus-a
-PUSHDATA-count chunker. Both **pack version, code size, nonce and balance into a single basic-data
leaf** so an account read needs one branch opening rather than three or
four.<Cite src="eip-7864" /> EIP-7864's own author says so in the discussion thread: it "pulls other
ideas similar to EIP-6800 which are agnostic to Verkle".<Cite src="eip7864-magicians" /> The
Stateless Book agrees, describing the group size of 256 as "entirely arbitrary, but for historical
reasons rooted in Verkle Trees proving efficiency" — and notes that a binary tree is under no
obligation to keep it.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-data-encoding" />

The one thing that changed is what commits a group of 256 values. Verkle used a polynomial vector
commitment. The binary tree, in the Stateless Book's phrasing, uses "a full Merkle tree as a form of
vector commitment" — the 256 values are an eight-level Merkle tree hanging off the
stem.<Cite src="stateless-fyi-binary-tree" />

That sentence is the whole reversal in one line. Same shape, same grouping, same key derivation
strategy, same code chunking. A different primitive underneath, chosen for what it does not depend
on rather than for what it does.

<Aside kind="note">
**The gas EIP is the clearest fossil.** EIP-4762 specifies the gas schedule for a witness-carrying
world, and it is a Draft that EIP-7864 names as its access-events reference.<Cite src="eip-7864" />
Its own motivation says it targets "the fork coming right before the verkle tree fork", and the
Stateless Book's chapter on it says flatly: "this EIP is currently designed for the Verkle Trees
proposal."<Cite src="eip-4762" /><Cite src="stateless-fyi-gas-costs" /> A live document, still
referenced by the current design, still describing the design that was replaced. Read the body, not
the citation.
</Aside>

## Reading a cancellation that nobody announced

The generalisable skill here is not about Verkle. It is about how a decision like this is actually
observable, since it was not observable as an announcement.

**Status lines move before prose does.** EIP-6800, EIP-7612 and EIP-7736 all read Stagnant; EIP-7864
and EIP-8297 read Draft.<Cite src="eip-6800" /><Cite src="eip-7612" /><Cite src="eip-7736" /><Cite src="eip-7864" /><Cite src="eip-8297" />
That is a five-second check and it is the highest-signal one available.

**Rationale sections argue against the alternative by name.** A successor proposal has to justify
itself, so it says what is wrong with the incumbent in its own words. That is a better source than
any summary of the debate, and it is a primary one.

**Discussion threads get edited at the top.** The EIP-7864 Magicians thread's first post now opens
with a parenthetical: "Please note EIP-8297 has a new iteration of the BT design!"<Cite src="eip7864-magicians" />
One sentence, inserted into a two-year-old post, is the only in-band signal that the current
proposal has itself been superseded. Follow it and you find the same pattern one level down: the
abstract posted to the EIP-8297 thread in June 2026 describes 32-byte keys and a zone identifier in
the high four bits, while the EIP text read today specifies variable-length keys and a full zone
byte.<Cite src="eip8297-magicians" /><Cite src="eip-8297" /> Two months, one design, two
descriptions. Cite what you read, on the date you read it.

**Cross-references outlive the things they reference.** EIP-7864 names EIP-7748 as its migration
path, and EIP-7748 is titled "State conversion to Verkle Tree" and `Requires` the Stagnant
EIP-7612.<Cite src="eip-7864" /><Cite src="eip-7748" /><Cite src="eip-7612" /> The pointer was never
updated. Lesson six is about what replaced it.

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson** takes the counterintuitive half seriously. Without a vector commitment, the
formula that made width attractive runs the other way, and the minimum is at arity two — a deeper
tree with smaller proofs. That lesson also opens the question the binary design has genuinely not
settled: which hash function goes underneath.

**Lesson six** is where the cancellation pattern repeats at a smaller scale. The migration plan for
the new tree has itself been replaced, by a proposal published two months ago, and the current tree
EIP has a successor of its own.

**In your own architecture reviews**, the transferable observation is about what a reversal actually
costs. Verkle's grouping, key derivation, code chunking and leaf packing all survived intact; the
part that was discarded was one primitive. Designs that isolate their cryptographic dependency into
a replaceable layer survive their dependencies being wrong. EIP-7864 says this explicitly about its
own open question: the hash function "can be switched with minimal impact on
implementations".<Cite src="eip-7864" />

**And when you next write "X is coming"**, put the artifact you checked and the date beside it. Half
the material this lesson corrects is not wrong; it is undated.

<Aside kind="note">
**Research done against the cancelled design mostly still counts.** In 2021 the Ethereum Foundation
commissioned Dedaub to measure what Vitalik Buterin's Verkle gas-metering proposal would do to real
contracts. Dedaub's 2026 write-up of that work describes the proposal it measured as "the direct
precursor of EIP-7864" and applies its findings to the binary tree
directly.<Cite src="dedaub-locality" /> The measurement targeted a locality-and-chunking cost model,
and that model outlived the commitment scheme it was designed for — which is exactly the separation
this lesson has been drawing. Lesson five is built on those results.
</Aside>
