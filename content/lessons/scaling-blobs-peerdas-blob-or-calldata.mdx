A chain flipped its batcher to blobs the week Dencun shipped, watched the L1 bill fall by more than an
order of magnitude, and closed the ticket. That was the right decision and it has been the right
decision on almost every day since.

"Almost every day" is doing the work. The setting is `blobs`, hard-coded, and it has never been
re-evaluated because there has never been a day where anyone noticed it should be. The chain also
posts every five hours and its blobs go out about a fifth full, which — as this lesson will derive —
moves the point at which that setting becomes wrong by a factor of five, in the direction nobody
checked.

Optimism's own account of the Fusaka upgrade frames the DA-type question as a live one rather than a
migration that finished: the batcher can post as calldata or as blobs, and both are fully secured by
L1.<Cite src="optimism-fusaka-is-live" /><Cite src="op-docs-configure-batcher" /> Two markets, two
paths, and a decision that has to be made again for every batch.

## Price both paths in the same unit

The comparison is only tractable if you price per **byte actually posted**, not per transaction. Do
each side separately.

### The calldata side: 40 gas per byte, and the floor always binds

You know [calldata is priced at the edge](/c/calldata-priced-at-the-edge) rather than by execution.
What changes the batcher's arithmetic is EIP-7623, which added a floor.

Let `tokens_in_calldata = zero_bytes_in_calldata + nonzero_bytes_in_calldata * 4`. Then:

```python
tx.gasUsed = 21000 + max(
    STANDARD_TOKEN_COST * tokens_in_calldata + execution_gas_used + ...,
    TOTAL_COST_FLOOR_PER_TOKEN * tokens_in_calldata,
)
```

with `STANDARD_TOKEN_COST` = 4 and `TOTAL_COST_FLOOR_PER_TOKEN` = 10.<Cite src="eip-7623" /> The EIP
describes this as raising calldata from 4/16 to 10/40 gas per byte for transactions that do not spend
enough on EVM operations relative to calldata.<Cite src="eip-7623" />

Now notice which branch of that `max` a batcher lands in, and why it is not a coin flip. A batcher
transaction on the OP Stack sends data to an address with no code, so `execution_gas_used` is
essentially zero.<Cite src="op-specs-derivation" /> Drop that term and the comparison is
`4 * tokens` against `10 * tokens`. **The floor branch wins unconditionally.** EIP-7623's motivation
names rollups posting data as the growth it was written to address, and a batcher transaction is the
purest example of the shape it targets.<Cite src="eip-7623" />

So for a batcher the calldata price is not "4 or 16 depending on the byte". It is **10 gas per zero
byte and 40 gas per non-zero byte, always**. And compressed output is close to uniformly random, so
zero bytes are rare: call it **40 gas per byte** and be within a percent.

### The blob side: about one blob gas per byte

Each blob costs `GAS_PER_BLOB` = 2<sup>17</sup> = 131,072 blob gas regardless of how much of it you
fill, and holds about 129,200 usable bytes.<Cite src="eip-4844" /><Cite src="op-docs-tune-batcher-costs" />
So a **full** blob charges 131,072 ÷ 129,200 ≈ **1.014 blob gas per byte**.

That figure is confirmable against a live chain rather than only derived: OP Mainnet's
`blobbasefeeScalar` is 1,014,213, which in the L1 fee formula's fixed-point scaling is 1.014213 blob
gas per compressed byte — the same number to five digits.<Cite src="op-docs-fee-parameters" />

## The crossover, and the thing that actually moves it

**Subgoal 1 — set the two per-byte prices equal.** Blobs cost `1.014 × blobBaseFee` per byte; calldata
costs `40 × baseFee` per byte. Equality is at
`blobBaseFee = 40 × baseFee ÷ 1.014 = 39.4 × baseFee`.<Cite src="eip-7623" /><Cite src="eip-4844" />

**Blobs win while the blob base fee is under about forty times the execution base fee.** That is a
strikingly wide margin, and it is why the hard-coded setting has been right for years.

**Subgoal 2 — introduce the term everyone omits.** You buy blobs whole. If a blob is fraction `f`
full, its per-byte price is `1.014 × blobBaseFee ÷ f`, and the crossover becomes:

`blobBaseFee < 39.4 × f × baseFee`

**The fill ratio scales the crossover linearly.** A chain running full blobs is safe until the blob
market is forty times hotter than the execution market. A chain running blobs a fifth full loses that
protection at eight times. At 5% full, at twice.

**Subgoal 3 — sanity-check the bottom of the range against the reserve price.** If EIP-7918's reserve
is in effect, the blob base fee cannot sit below `BLOB_BASE_COST / GAS_PER_BLOB` of the execution base
fee — exactly one sixteenth, or 0.0625 ×.<Cite src="eip-7918" /> Compare that to the full-blob
crossover at 39.4 ×: the reserve sits about six hundred times below it. Solving `39.4 × f = 0.0625`
gives `f = 0.0016` — **a blob would have to be 0.16% full, about 205 bytes, before the reserve price
alone made calldata cheaper.** So the reserve does not change the posting decision for any real chain.
Worth doing once, because "a floor tied to execution gas" reads like it should.

**Subgoal 4 — add the per-transaction constants to both sides.** Both paths pay the 21,000-gas
transaction floor at the execution base fee. Both pay a priority fee. Neither is per-byte, so both
argue for fewer, larger transactions — and both are the reason a chain producing 20 KB an hour is told
to accumulate rather than post promptly.

**Subgoal 5 — check the ceilings, because one of them moved.** The blob path caps at **6 blobs per
transaction**, a limit EIP-7594 requires clients to enforce at submission, on receipt, and during
block production and processing<Cite src="eip-7594" /> — roughly 775 KB of usable payload. The
calldata path now has a ceiling too, and it is newer: EIP-7825 caps a single transaction at
**16,777,216 gas**.<Cite src="eip-7825" /> At the 40-gas floor price, `(16,777,216 − 21,000) ÷ 40`
works out to about **419,000 bytes** — so a calldata transaction cannot carry more than roughly 419 KB
of compressed batch data at all, regardless of price. A fallback plan that assumes calldata can absorb
whatever a blob transaction was carrying is wrong by a factor of nearly two on the top end.

<Aside kind="note">
Run the whole thing once on a real quantity to feel the magnitudes. 520 KB of compressed channel data
is five blobs, one transaction. As calldata at the floor it is 40 × 520,000 = 20.8 million gas — which
exceeds the 16,777,216-gas transaction cap, so it is two transactions before you have priced
anything.<Cite src="eip-7825" /><Cite src="eip-7623" /> At an execution base fee of 5 gwei that is
about 0.104 ETH; the same data in five blobs at a blob base fee of 1 gwei is about 0.00076 ETH.
The blob path is not marginally cheaper here, it is over a hundred times cheaper — which is the
regime the industry has spent most of its blob-era history in, and precisely why the exceptions get
missed.
</Aside>

## Switching is not free, and both major stacks say so in code

Knowing the crossover is not the same as flipping at it. Two costs make naive switching worse than a
slightly wrong static choice.

**The mempool cost.** Blob transaction replacement is expensive: operator documentation states that
current transaction pool implementations require a **doubling** of fees to replace a blob transaction,
which is why the recommended multi-blob configuration raises `OP_BATCHER_RESUBMISSION_TIMEOUT` to
240 seconds to allow more time for inclusion before bumping.<Cite src="op-docs-configure-batcher" />
The same documentation warns that during blob congestion, running with high blob counts can backfire —
inclusion gets harder and the fee bump follows.<Cite src="op-docs-configure-batcher" /> Arbitrum
handles the same problem as a range: `min-blob-tx-tip-cap-gwei` and `max-blob-tx-tip-cap-gwei`, both
defaulting to 1 gwei, with the guidance that setting the minimum below the maximum lets the data
poster start low and escalate through replace-by-fee.<Cite src="arbitrum-batch-poster-fee-tuning" />

**The accounting cost.** On the OP Stack the L1 data fee charged to your users is computed from
`basefeeScalar` and `blobBaseFeeScalar` on the SystemConfig, and the documentation is explicit that a
DA-type switch also changes the correct scalar values, so both must move in the same maintenance
window — blob-appropriate scalars under calldata, or the reverse, misprice every user transaction
until corrected.<Cite src="op-docs-tune-batcher-costs" /><Cite src="op-docs-post-blobs" /> A batcher
that flips DA type per batch and a fee formula that assumes one of them is a revenue bug that compounds
silently.

So both stacks build in hysteresis rather than a bare comparison.

**Optimism** offers three values for `--data-availability-type`: `calldata` (the flag's default),
`blobs`, and `auto`, where `auto` estimates the current cost per byte of both markets and picks the
cheaper.<Cite src="op-docs-tune-batcher-costs" /><Cite src="op-docs-configure-batcher" /> Its
guidance is a decision table, not a formula: `blobs` as the sensible default at any throughput,
`auto` to have spikes handled automatically, and `calldata` only to pin the type during a sustained
spike, treated as the exception.<Cite src="op-docs-tune-batcher-costs" />

**Arbitrum** enables blobs with `post-4844-blobs`, will fall back to calldata when blob prices are
high unless `ignore-blob-price` is set to force blobs, and applies an explicit damper: after a non-blob
transaction is posted, **the next sixteen transactions also use calldata**, specifically to prevent
frequent switching.<Cite src="arbitrum-batch-poster-blobs" />

That sixteen is the detail worth stealing. Somebody measured that oscillating between paths cost more
than being briefly on the wrong one, and encoded the answer as a fixed dwell time. If you build this
decision yourself, build the dwell time with it.

## The other half of the same decision: how often

Posting path and posting cadence are usually treated as separate settings. They are one decision,
because cadence sets the fill ratio and the fill ratio sets the crossover.

The trade in one sentence, from the specification's own glossary: submitting in smaller, more frequent
steps means less delay in confirmation of L2 block inputs, lets verifiers establish safety sooner, and
reduces time to L1 data finality; submitting in larger, less frequent steps aggregates more data and
reduces the fixed overhead of batch submission, "especially for lower throughput chains that do not
fill data-transactions (typically 128 KB of calldata, or 800 KB of blobdata) as
quickly."<Cite src="op-specs-glossary" />

Every term in that sentence has an owner outside infrastructure.

**[Hard finality](/c/hard-finality) is a product property.** Arbitrum's documentation states that
transactions reach parent-chain-equivalent finality once the data has been posted in a
batch.<Cite src="arbitrum-chain-finality" /> Posting less often means users spend longer on soft
confirmations, and the gap is measured in the units your users care about.

**Reorg exposure scales with the gap.** Arbitrum's batch poster guidance makes the point directly:
timely batch posting minimises the window in which an L1 reorg can affect unposted
data.<Cite src="arbitrum-batch-poster-config" />

**Downstream integrations sit on the safe head.** Exchanges and third-party bridges commonly wait for
transactions to be marked safe before processing deposits and withdrawals, so a five-hour channel
duration is a five-hour worst-case deposit delay for those partners.<Cite src="op-docs-configure-batcher" />

**And the cheapest setting is explicitly the most latent.** The recommended
`OP_BATCHER_MAX_CHANNEL_DURATION` of 1,500 L1 blocks yields the cheapest fees and lets the safe head
stall for up to five hours.<Cite src="op-docs-configure-batcher" /> Arbitrum's `max-delay` defaults to
one hour with guidance to run five to fifteen minutes on high-throughput
chains.<Cite src="arbitrum-batch-poster-config" />

The conclusion the concept statement lands on: **this parameter belongs to whoever owns the product's
risk, not only to whoever owns the infrastructure bill.** An infrastructure team optimising it alone
will optimise the one term it can measure.

<Aside kind="warn">
There is a third lever most operators do not know is already on: batcher-driven sequencer throttling,
which is **enabled by default**. When the batcher's backlog of un-posted data grows past a threshold,
it instructs the sequencer's execution client via the `miner_setMaxDASize` RPC to limit DA-heavy
transactions and blocks, with default backlog thresholds of 3,200,000 and 12,800,000
bytes.<Cite src="op-docs-configure-batcher" /><Cite src="op-docs-tune-batcher-costs" /> Turning it off
means accepting unbounded backlog growth during spikes and posting that backlog at whatever L1 charges
later.<Cite src="op-docs-tune-batcher-costs" /> If `throttle_intensity` is routinely non-zero, your
steady-state throughput exceeds your posting budget and the fix is upstream of the thresholds.
</Aside>

crediting deposits off the safe head, the five-hour duration was already costing more than the blob
space was.<Cite src="op-docs-configure-batcher" />

consecutive calldata submissions after any switch away from blobs, precisely so a batcher does not
oscillate across a threshold it is sitting on.<Cite src="arbitrum-batch-poster-blobs" />

## What this unlocks

**Next**, the parameters that set every threshold in this lesson. Target, max and the update fraction
now move on a schedule of their own, which means the crossover you compute today is computed against a
capacity number with an expiry date — and the correct response is to read it from your client rather
than from a document.

**Later in this track**, alternative data availability. Everything here priced two paths onto
Ethereum; a third path takes the data off Ethereum entirely, and the comparison stops being arithmetic
and becomes a trust argument.

**And carry the two-term model.** Cost per byte on each path, times the fill ratio, plus per-transaction
fixed costs, against ceilings on both sides. Four terms, all readable from public data, and they will
survive every constant in this lesson being replaced.
