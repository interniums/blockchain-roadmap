Open a batcher transaction on an L1 explorer and the first thing you notice is how little is there.
The `to` address is an ordinary account with no code. The input data is `0x`. The transaction did
nothing — no logs, no state changes, no internal calls — and it cost a few tens of thousands of gas
plus a line item the explorer labels "blob fee".

Somewhere in that transaction is an hour of another chain's history. None of it is in the part you
can see.

You already know the protocol side of this from the [blob](/c/blob) module: type-3 transactions,
[KZG commitments](/c/kzg-commitment), [versioned hashes](/c/versioned-hash), sidecars that the EVM
cannot touch. This lesson is the rollup's half — how a stream of L2 blocks becomes the bytes inside
that blob, why the destination address is not a contract, and what the absence of a contract means
for who is allowed to be wrong.

## From L2 blocks to a payload, in four nested containers

The derivation specification defines four levels, and confusing any two of them produces a wrong cost
model.

**A batch** is the list of transactions for one L2 block, plus the parent hash, the epoch number and
hash of its L1 origin, and a timestamp.<Cite src="op-specs-derivation" /> One batch, one L2 block.

**A channel** is a sequence of batches compressed together — grouped, the specification says, "simply
to obtain a better compression rate."<Cite src="op-specs-derivation" /> A channel is the compression
unit, and it is the reason the cost of your transaction depends on what everybody else's transaction
looked like.

**A frame** is a chunk of a channel. Its encoding is
`channel_id ++ frame_number ++ frame_data_length ++ frame_data ++ is_last`, with `channel_id` a
`bytes16`, `frame_number` a `uint16`, `frame_data_length` a `uint32` capped at 1,000,000 bytes, and
`is_last` a single byte — **23 bytes of fixed overhead** per frame, deliberately fixed-size so that
packing frames of varying content length does not create a circular dependency with the target total
length.<Cite src="op-specs-derivation" />

**A batcher transaction** is `version_byte ++ rollup_payload`, where version `0` means one or more
concatenated frames and version `1` means an alt-DA commitment.<Cite src="op-specs-derivation" /> A
single transaction may carry frames from more than one channel, which lets the batcher pack the small
final frame of one channel beside frames of the next.<Cite src="op-specs-derivation" />

Then, and only then, that payload is placed in blobs.

The critical thing to hold: **the frame boundary and the blob boundary are different boundaries.**
Frames exist because a channel may be larger than a transaction. Blobs exist because the L1 sells data
in fixed lots. A channel can span many transactions; a transaction can carry many frames; and the
mapping from frames to blobs is a packing decision, not a structural one. On the OP Stack it is a
single setting: `OP_BATCHER_TARGET_NUM_FRAMES=6` targets six frames, which is six blobs per blob
transaction.<Cite src="op-docs-configure-batcher" />

## How much fits, and why the answer is not 131,072

A blob is `FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_BLOB` = 4,096 field elements of `BYTES_PER_FIELD_ELEMENT` = 32 bytes
each, so 131,072 bytes of raw structure.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> That is not the usable capacity, and
the reason is arithmetic rather than convention.

Each 32-byte word is not an arbitrary 256-bit string. It is an element of the field modulo
`BLS_MODULUS`, which EIP-4844 gives as
52435875175126190479447740508185965837690552500527637822603658699938581184513 — a prime slightly
below 2<sup>255</sup>.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Any 32-byte value at or above that modulus is not a
valid field element, so an encoder cannot simply pour bytes in. It must guarantee every word lands
below the modulus, and the cheap way to guarantee that is to leave part of each word unused.

Operator documentation quotes the practical figure as **about 130 KB of usable capacity per blob,
which must be bought whole whether or not you fill it**.<Cite src="op-docs-tune-batcher-costs" /> You
can cross-check that against a live chain's own fee configuration: OP Mainnet's `blobbasefeeScalar` is
1,014,213, and the L1 fee formula divides the scaled result by 10<sup>12</sup> against a size term
scaled by 10<sup>6</sup> — so the chain is charging roughly 1.014 blob gas per compressed byte, which
back-solves to 131,072 ÷ 1.014 ≈ **129,200 usable bytes**.<Cite src="op-docs-fee-parameters" /><Cite src="eip-4844" />
Two independent routes to the same number is the level of confidence a capacity constant deserves
before you build a cost model on it.

The OP glossary states the transaction-level consequence directly: a data transaction is typically
about 128 KB of calldata, or **800 KB of blobdata**.<Cite src="op-specs-glossary" /> Six blobs.

<Aside kind="warn">
That six is a protocol limit, not a tuning choice. EIP-7594 introduces a limit of **6 blobs per
transaction**, which clients enforce at submission, on receipt from the network, and during block
production and processing.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> A batcher sized against the per-*block* maximum
rather than the per-*transaction* limit builds transactions that are rejected before they are ever
priced.
</Aside>

## The destination is not a contract, and that is the whole design

Here is where the rollup side diverges most sharply from what a Solidity engineer expects.

There is no L1 contract that receives a batch, validates it and stores it. A batcher transaction is
authenticated by two address comparisons: its `to` must equal the batch inbox address, and its `from`
must equal the batch-sender address recorded in the system configuration as of the L1 block the data
is read from.<Cite src="op-specs-derivation" /> No EVM code executes. The specification is explicit
that any data can be submitted at any time, and only data matching the derivation rules is valid from
a verifier's perspective.<Cite src="op-specs-batcher" />

Follow the consequences.

**Malformed batches are not rejected — they are ignored.** Unknown transaction version bytes make the
batcher transaction invalid; if any one frame in a transaction fails to parse, every frame in that
transaction is rejected; unknown batch versions and malformed contents make a batch
invalid.<Cite src="op-specs-derivation" /> All of that happens in nodes reading L1 afterwards, not in
a revert. Your batcher can post garbage successfully and pay for it in full.

**Cost is almost entirely data, not execution.** A blob transaction that does nothing pays the
21,000-gas floor plus its data, which is precisely why this shape is affordable.

**"Posted to L1" is a claim about bytes existing, not about anyone having checked them.** Verification
is a separate activity performed by whoever cares, using data they fetch themselves.

Not every stack makes the same choice. Arbitrum in Rollup mode has the sequencer submit the batch
directly to the **sequencer inbox contract** on the parent chain, as calldata or blobs; in AnyTrust
mode it submits a Data Availability Certificate instead, with the data held by a
committee.<Cite src="arbitrum-data-availability" /> An inbox contract can enforce things an inbox
address cannot. When you evaluate a chain you have not seen before, "what does the L1 endpoint
actually execute" is a real question with different answers.

## What a contract can do with data it cannot read

The EVM's access to a blob is exactly two operations, and both work on the commitment rather than the
content.

`BLOBHASH`, opcode `0x49` at 3 gas, returns the versioned hash at a given index in the current
transaction's `blob_versioned_hashes`.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> That hash is
`VERSIONED_HASH_VERSION_KZG ++ sha256(commitment)[1:]` — version byte `0x01` followed by the last 31
bytes of the SHA-256 of the KZG commitment.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

The point-evaluation precompile at address `0x0A`, costing `POINT_EVALUATION_PRECOMPILE_GAS` = 50,000,
verifies that a claimed value is the evaluation of the committed polynomial at a claimed
point.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> That is a proof *about* the data, supplied by whoever wants to convince
the contract, at a single point of their choosing or of the contract's.

So a dispute over an L2 state transition never happens by reading the blob on-chain. It happens by
someone asserting what a specific position in the data contains and being forced to prove that
assertion against the commitment the chain already has. The commitment is the anchor; the bytes live
off-chain, with everyone who bothered to keep them.

And they are kept for a while and then not: blob sidecars are retained under a minimum-serving
obligation of 4,096 epochs, described by the specification as around eighteen
days.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> The rollup-side consequence, which the [pruning](/c/blob-pruning) lesson
set up and this one cashes: a node syncing your chain from genesis cannot get old batches from the
beacon network at all. Someone has to have archived them, and if that someone is only you, your chain
is one backup away from being unreconstructable.

## Worked example: sizing one hour of a chain

Your chain produces, after span-batch encoding and Brotli, 520 KB of compressed channel data per hour.
You want to know what the batcher will actually emit. Work it in the order that keeps the four
containers separate.

**Subgoal 1 — convert bytes to blobs, using usable capacity rather than raw.** 520,000 ÷ 129,200 ≈
4.02, so five blobs, the last one 1% full.<Cite src="op-docs-tune-batcher-costs" /> Using 131,072 as
the divisor gives 3.97 and the wrong answer — four blobs — which is how off-by-one capacity errors
become "the channel did not fit" incidents.

**Subgoal 2 — convert blobs to transactions, using the protocol limit.** Five blobs is under the
six-per-transaction limit, so one transaction carries the hour.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> At 700 KB per
hour it would be six blobs and still one transaction; at 800 KB it becomes two, and the second one
pays its own 21,000-gas floor and its own priority fee.

**Subgoal 3 — add the frame overhead, and check it is negligible.** Five frames at 23 bytes fixed
overhead is 115 bytes against 520,000.<Cite src="op-specs-derivation" /> It is negligible *here*. On a
chain posting 20 KB per hour it would not be, and neither would the 21,000-gas floor — which is the
real reason low-throughput chains are told to accumulate for hours before posting.

**Subgoal 4 — price the quantisation, because it is the line people forget.** You are buying five
blobs' worth of blob gas — 5 × 2<sup>17</sup> = 655,360 blob gas — regardless of the fact that the
fifth is nearly empty.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> The 129,200th byte and the 519,999th byte cost the same
as the first byte of a fresh blob: nothing extra. The 129,201st byte costs an entire blob.

**Subgoal 5 — state what you have and have not computed.** You have the *quantity*: five blobs, one
transaction, per hour. You do not have a cost, because blob gas has a price that moves independently
of everything in this calculation. Keeping the two apart is what makes the next lesson tractable —
quantity is a property of your chain, price is a property of the market, and mixing them is why most
published DA cost figures cannot be reproduced.

<Aside kind="note">
Verify the outcome rather than trusting the arithmetic. The op-batcher exports a `blob_used_bytes`
histogram, and the documented health check is that it should sit near blob capacity; persistently
part-empty blobs mean the channel duration or frame count is oversized for your
throughput.<Cite src="op-docs-tune-batcher-costs" /> Arbitrum's equivalent is coarser but immediate:
the batch poster logs a `numBlobs` field per submission, where `0` means a calldata transaction was
posted instead.<Cite src="arbitrum-batch-poster-blobs" />
</Aside>

<Check type="predict" concept="blob-transaction">
A team is building an L1 contract that pays a bounty when someone publishes a specific dataset as a
blob. Their design: the publisher sends a type-3 transaction carrying the blob and calling
`claimBounty()`; the contract reads the blob with `BLOBHASH`, hashes the contents, compares against
the expected hash, and pays.

Say what breaks, and what the closest workable design is.

<Answer>
**`BLOBHASH` does not return the contents.** It returns the versioned hash at an index — the byte
`0x01` followed by the last 31 bytes of `sha256` of the KZG commitment.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> The
contract can learn *that* a blob is attached and *which* commitment it has. It can never see a byte of
it, so "hash the contents and compare" has no implementation.

**The comparison they want is possible, but only against a commitment.** If the expected dataset is
fixed and known in advance, compute its KZG commitment off-chain once, derive the versioned hash the
same way the protocol does, and store that 32-byte value in the contract. Then `claimBounty()` is a
comparison of `BLOBHASH(0)` against the stored constant — one opcode at 3 gas, no precompile
needed.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> This is exact, cheap, and it is the design most people are reaching
for when they say "verify what was posted".

**If only part of the data is fixed, use the precompile instead of the whole-blob comparison.** The
point-evaluation precompile at `0x0A`, at 50,000 gas, verifies that a claimed value is the committed
polynomial's evaluation at a claimed point.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> The claimant supplies the point,
the value and the proof; the contract supplies the versioned hash from `BLOBHASH` and chooses the
point. That turns "prove you published the right thing" into a spot check the contract can afford.

**Three things they must design around regardless.**

*Retention.* The commitment is permanent and the data is not — the serving obligation is 4,096 epochs,
around eighteen days.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> A bounty whose whole purpose is that the public can
later fetch the dataset needs someone contractually obliged to keep it, because the protocol will not.

*Cost model.* One blob is bought whole at 2<sup>17</sup> blob gas whether the dataset is 4 KB or
129 KB.<Cite src="eip-4844" /><Cite src="op-docs-tune-batcher-costs" /> A bounty priced per byte will
be wrong for small payloads by however much of the blob was left empty.

*Failure semantics.* The blob fee is charged on the transaction regardless of what the call does. If
`claimBounty()` reverts, the publisher has still paid to publish. Design the check so a failed claim
is cheap to retry rather than expensive to repeat.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**Next**, the price of everything you just counted. Blob gas is metered by its own accumulator and
priced by its own curve, and the arithmetic of that curve explains both why data posting has felt free
for long stretches and why the moments it stops being free arrive faster than operators expect.

**Later in this track**, the same containers reappear from the other direction. Forced inclusion is a
path that bypasses the batcher entirely and lands transactions through L1; understanding what a
batcher transaction *is* is what makes it obvious why forced inclusion has to be a different
mechanism rather than a flag on this one.

**And carry the boundary discipline.** Batch, channel, frame, transaction, blob. Whenever you read a
per-transaction data cost, ask which of the five the author divided by. Most of the time they will not
know they had a choice.
