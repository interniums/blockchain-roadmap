A finance lead asks a reasonable question: "L1 gas is at 45 gwei today, three times last week. What
did that do to our posting bill?"

Nothing. It did nothing. The bill is set by a different number, on a different curve, driven by
different demand, and this week that number is 1 wei.

Six weeks later the same question gets asked on a day when L1 gas is at 8 gwei and the answer is that
posting costs have gone up sixty-fold since Tuesday.

Both answers are correct and neither is intuitive, because English gave two unrelated prices the same
name. This lesson makes the second price derivable — not "the blob base fee follows an EIP-1559-like
curve", which you already know, but the specific arithmetic that tells you how fast it can rise, how
fast it falls, why it spends so much time pinned at its floor, and what a mechanism designed to unpin
it actually does.

## Two accumulators in one header

The execution fee market and the blob fee market share a block header and nothing else.

Each blob consumes `GAS_PER_BLOB` = 2<sup>17</sup> = 131,072 blob gas, so a transaction's blob gas is
that constant times the number of versioned hashes it carries.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> The header
carries `blob_gas_used` for the block and `excess_blob_gas`, a running total of blob gas consumed above
target, and the price per unit of blob gas is a function of that accumulator
alone.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

Since EIP-7892 the update reads its parameters from the blob schedule rather than from constants
compiled into clients, and `TARGET_BLOB_GAS_PER_BLOCK` was removed entirely in favour of
`GAS_PER_BLOB * blob_schedule.target`:<Cite src="eip-7892" /><Cite src="eip-7840" />

```python
def calc_excess_blob_gas(parent: Header, blob_schedule: BlobSchedule) -> int:
    target_blob_gas = GAS_PER_BLOB * blob_schedule.target
    if parent.excess_blob_gas + parent.blob_gas_used < target_blob_gas:
        return 0
    return parent.excess_blob_gas + parent.blob_gas_used - target_blob_gas

def get_base_fee_per_blob_gas(header: Header, blob_schedule: BlobSchedule) -> int:
    return fake_exponential(
        MIN_BASE_FEE_PER_BLOB_GAS,
        header.excess_blob_gas,
        blob_schedule.base_fee_update_fraction,
    )
```

Three things are worth naming before the arithmetic, because each one is a place people assume the
execution market's behaviour transfers and it does not.

**The accumulator is cumulative, not per-block.** `excess_blob_gas` carries forward. A block that is
exactly at target changes nothing; a block above target adds the overshoot to a total that persists;
a block below target subtracts from it, floored at zero. The price is a function of history, not of
the last block.

**`fake_exponential` is a Taylor series in integers**, approximating
`factor * e ** (numerator / denominator)` by accumulating terms until they round to
zero.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> No floating point, no lookup table, and a strictly increasing function
of the accumulator.

**Nothing in either function mentions execution gas.** Blob demand does not enter the execution base
fee update and execution demand does not enter this one. That independence is the design, and it is
why the two markets routinely move in opposite directions.<Cite src="blocknative-blob-gas" />

## Deriving how fast the price can move

The useful quantities are not in any specification as numbers. They fall out in five steps, and once
you have done it once you can redo it for whatever the schedule says next year.

**Subgoal 1 — express one block's contribution to the accumulator.** A block carrying `n` blobs adds
`n * GAS_PER_BLOB` and subtracts `target * GAS_PER_BLOB`. So the net change is
`(n − target) * GAS_PER_BLOB`, positive above target and negative below.<Cite src="eip-7892" />

**Subgoal 2 — convert an accumulator change into a price change.** The fee is
`exp(excess / fraction)` in shape, so multiplying rather than adding: a change of Δ in the accumulator
multiplies the fee by `e^(Δ / fraction)`.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> This is the step that turns an
additive counter into a multiplicative price, and it is why blob fees move in ratios.

**Subgoal 3 — name the two extremes.** The largest single-block increase comes from a maximally full
block: `Δ = (max − target) * GAS_PER_BLOB`. The largest decrease comes from an empty block:
`Δ = −target * GAS_PER_BLOB`. These are not symmetric unless `max = 2 × target`.

**Subgoal 4 — substitute the verified schedule.** EIP-8135 records the full history in one table:
Cancun target 3, max 6, update fraction 3,338,477; Prague 6 / 9 / 5,007,716; BPO1 10 / 15 / 8,346,193;
BPO2 14 / 21 / 11,684,671.<Cite src="eip-8135" /> Take BPO2's row and compute.

Downward, an empty block: `14 × 131,072 / 11,684,671 = 0.15704`, so the fee multiplies by
`e^(−0.15704) = 0.8547` — **down 14.5% per block**, a halving every 4.4 blocks.

Upward, a maximally full block: `(21 − 14) × 131,072 / 11,684,671 = 0.07852`, so the fee multiplies by
`e^(0.07852) = 1.0817` — **up 8.2% per block**, a doubling every 8.8 blocks.

**Subgoal 5 — check the derivation against the one number the specification does state.** EIP-4844
says its `BLOB_BASE_FEE_UPDATE_FRACTION` of 3,338,477 was chosen to target a maximum change rate of
`e^(TARGET_BLOB_GAS_PER_BLOCK / BLOB_BASE_FEE_UPDATE_FRACTION) ≈ 1.125` per
block.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Run the same arithmetic on Cancun's row: `3 × 131,072 / 3,338,477 =
0.11778`, and `e^0.11778 = 1.1250`. The derivation reproduces the published constant exactly, which is
how you know it is sound before applying it to rows the specification never commented on.

### Two properties that fall straight out

**The responsiveness has been held constant since Pectra, deliberately.** Divide each fraction by its
target: Cancun gives 1,112,826 while Prague, BPO1 and BPO2 all give 834,619. The ratio
`target × GAS_PER_BLOB / fraction` is therefore identical — 0.15704 — for all three post-Cancun
rows.<Cite src="eip-8135" /> Every capacity increase scaled the update fraction in exact proportion,
so raising capacity has never changed how quickly the price reacts. That is a design decision hiding
in a table of large numbers, and it means the dynamics you learn now survive the next fork even though
the constants will not.

**The market is asymmetric, and by a documented rule.** Since Pectra the max has been 1.5 times the
target — 9/6, 15/10, 21/14 — so the up-exponent is exactly half the down-exponent.<Cite src="eip-8135" />
The blob base fee **falls at twice the exponential rate at which it can climb.** A spike decays faster
than it built. Under Cancun's 3/6 the two were equal; the asymmetry is a post-Pectra property that
most material predates.

### The next block's price is already known

One more property falls out of the same functions, and it is the most useful one to a batcher.

`get_base_fee_per_blob_gas` takes only the header's `excess_blob_gas` and the schedule's update
fraction, and `calc_excess_blob_gas` derives the next block's accumulator from the parent's
`excess_blob_gas` and `blob_gas_used`.<Cite src="eip-4844" /><Cite src="eip-7892" /> Both inputs are
in a block you can already see. So **the blob base fee that will apply to the next block is exactly
computable the moment the current block lands** — not estimated, not sampled from a fee history
endpoint, computed.

That is a stronger guarantee than the execution market gives you in practice, where a tip has to be
guessed against a competitive mempool. On the blob side there is no tip to guess, so a batcher that
implements these two functions locally knows its next submission's data cost to the wei before it
signs anything. Anything that presents blob pricing as a forecast is doing arithmetic you could have
done exactly.

## Capacity is the max; price is the target

Two parameters, two jobs, and swapping them produces confident wrong predictions.

**The max caps how many blobs a block may carry.** It is a supply ceiling. Raising it alone lets more
data through per block and, holding demand fixed, lowers the price by making it easier to stay under
target.

**The target is what the fee mechanism steers towards.** It appears in `calc_excess_blob_gas` and
nowhere else.<Cite src="eip-7892" /> Raising the target alone lowers the equilibrium price without
adding a byte of capacity, because more usage now counts as "not enough".

The BPO forks moved both together: BPO1 raised 6/9 to 10/15 at epoch 412,672 on 9 December 2025, and
BPO2 raised it again to 14/21 at epoch 419,072 on 7 January 2026.<Cite src="fusaka-mainnet-announcement" /><Cite src="eip-8134" /><Cite src="eip-8135" />
Which is why "blob capacity went up" and "blob prices went down" are two consequences of one change
rather than one causing the other.

## The floor, and what one wei actually buys

`MIN_BASE_FEE_PER_BLOB_GAS` is **1 wei**.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Whenever sustained demand sits below
target, the accumulator floors at zero, `fake_exponential` returns its factor, and the price sits
there.

Give that a magnitude, because "effectively free" is doing a lot of work in most descriptions.

One blob at the floor is 131,072 blob gas × 1 wei = 131,072 wei, or 1.31 × 10<sup>−13</sup>
ETH.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> A maximally packed six-blob batcher transaction is 786,432 wei — under a
picoether. At any exchange rate anyone has ever quoted, the data component of that transaction rounds
to zero against its 21,000-gas execution floor. The batcher's cost in this regime is entirely the
execution side of a transaction that executes nothing.

This has a corollary that will save you from a whole genre of headline. **Any ratio computed against a
1 wei baseline is meaningless.** A rise from 1 wei to 15 million wei is a fifteen-million-fold
increase and also a rise to 0.015 gwei, which for six blobs is about 1.2 × 10<sup>−5</sup> ETH. This
curriculum's research sweep found exactly such a multiple in circulation, unverified, and it is not
repeated here for two reasons: it was not confirmable, and even if it were, the ratio would be
arithmetically unremarkable and rhetorically misleading.

<Aside kind="stop">
Neither "blobs are scarce and rollups fight over them" nor "blob space is free" describes the system.
Each describes a **regime**, and the mechanism moves between them: EIP-7918's motivation is written
around a fee that repeatedly settles at 1 wei and then has to climb again when demand picks
up.<Cite src="eip-7918" /> People generalise from whichever regime was current when they learned.
Build the batcher for both, and treat any claim about current utilisation as needing live data rather
than a citation. Peer-reviewed measurement of the post-4844 blob fee market
exists;<Cite src="ieee-4844-impact" /> its specific findings were not read for this curriculum and are
therefore not summarised here.
</Aside>

## Why the floor is a problem for the mechanism, not just a discount

A price pinned at its minimum is not merely cheap. It is a broken control loop, and EIP-7918 exists
because of it.

The argument in the EIP is worth following because it is a clean piece of mechanism design. A dynamic
pricing auction works when the price it sets is the price the consumer feels. But a rollup's real cost
of using blob space includes execution costs for the blob-carrying transaction, proof verification and
priority fees. When those dominate, the blob base fee stops being a meaningful component, and the EIP
puts a number on how badly: under such circumstances "a change in the blob base fee of 10% may shift
the total cost of utilizing blobspace by just 0.0000001%."<Cite src="eip-7918" /> Demand becomes
inelastic to the very quantity the mechanism controls, so the loop keeps lowering the price until it
hits 1 wei and stays there.<Cite src="eip-7918" />

The recovery is then slow and ugly. The EIP states that when demand picks up, "over an hour of
near-full blocks is required to restore a market-clearing equilibrium fee, with the mechanism
intermittently resorting to a first-price auction, considered a worse UX by blob
consumers."<Cite src="eip-7918" />

**You can derive that hour.** From 1 wei to 1 gwei is a factor of 10<sup>9</sup>, so
`ln(10⁹) = 20.72` in exponent terms. At BPO2's maximum upward exponent of 0.07852 per block, that is
20.72 / 0.07852 ≈ **264 blocks**, and at twelve seconds a slot, **about 53 minutes of maximally full
blocks**.<Cite src="eip-8135" /><Cite src="eip-4844" /> Real blocks are not all maximally full, so the
observed figure is longer — which is exactly the "over an hour" the EIP asserts. The same arithmetic
run downward gives 20.72 / 0.15704 ≈ 132 blocks, **about 26 minutes**, so the descent back to the floor
takes half as long as the climb.

That asymmetry is the operational headline of this lesson. **You get roughly an hour of warning while
the price climbs, and half an hour of relief once it stops.** A batcher that reacts to blob-fee spikes
on a five-minute polling loop is reacting inside the ramp, not after it.

### The mechanism itself

EIP-7918 assigns blobs a reserve price denominated in execution gas, with a single constant
`BLOB_BASE_COST` = 2<sup>13</sup> = 8,192, and an `if`-clause added to
`calc_excess_blob_gas`.<Cite src="eip-7918" /> When
`BLOB_BASE_COST * base_fee_per_gas > GAS_PER_BLOB * base_fee_per_blob_gas`, the function does not
subtract `target_blob_gas`; instead the accumulator grows by
`blob_gas_used * (max − target) // max`.<Cite src="eip-7918" />

Read the ratio it fixes: the reserve blob base fee is `BLOB_BASE_COST * base_fee_per_gas /
GAS_PER_BLOB`, so the ratio of reserve blob base fee to execution base fee is
`BLOB_BASE_COST / GAS_PER_BLOB` — **exactly 1/16** with the proposed constant.<Cite src="eip-7918" />

The cleanest way to hold it: **at the reserve, one blob costs the same as 8,192 units of execution
gas.** At an execution base fee of 10 gwei that is 0.00008192 ETH per blob, and the reserve blob base
fee is 0.625 gwei per blob gas. For calibration against something you measured yourself in the gas
track, 8,192 gas is a little under four cold `SLOAD`s at 2,100 gas each.<Cite src="measured-forge-1-7-1" />
A blob at the reserve price costs about what four cold storage reads cost. That is a floor with a
meaningful shape, rather than a floor at the smallest representable number.

The EIP's second justification is worth carrying because it is about your infrastructure, not about
auctions: nodes must batch-verify cell KZG proofs for every blob entering a mempool, and the EIP
estimates that verification at roughly fifteen times the compute of the single point evaluation a
contract pays 50,000 gas for.<Cite src="eip-7918" /> A price of 1 wei charges nothing for real work
somebody's hardware is doing.

<Answer>
**How long you have: about 44 minutes of maximally full blocks.**

Work it the same way as the lesson. The rise from 1 wei to 0.1 gwei = 10<sup>8</sup> wei is a factor of

`(21 − 14) × 131,072 / 11,684,671 = 0.07852`.<Cite src="eip-8135" /><Cite src="eip-4844" /> So
18.42 / 0.07852 ≈ 235 blocks, and 235 × 12 s ≈ **47 minutes** — call it three quarters of an hour, and

operationally: the ramp is slow enough to react to and fast enough that a daily review will miss it
entirely.

**What happens at your cap: your transactions stop being includable, and you start burning the
sequencing window.** `max_fee_per_blob_gas` is a cap on the blob base fee you will accept, and a blob
transaction whose cap is below the current base fee simply is not included.<Cite src="eip-4844" />
Two aggravating details. There is no priority component on the blob side, so you cannot outbid anyone
with a tip on blob gas — you compete on the execution tip or you wait.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> And
replacing a pending blob transaction is expensive: operator documentation notes that current
transaction pool implementations require a **doubling** of fees for blob transaction replacements,
which is why the recommended multi-blob configuration also raises the resubmission timeout to give
inclusion more time before bumping.<Cite src="op-docs-configure-batcher" /> Arbitrum's batch poster
exposes the same problem as a tip-cap range with RBF escalation between a minimum and a
maximum.<Cite src="arbitrum-batch-poster-fee-tuning" />

So the failure is not "we paid more". It is "we paid nothing and posted nothing", which is the
[batcher stall](/c/batcher) from the first lesson, arriving through the fee market instead of through
an empty account — and the twelve-hour clock is running the whole time.

**How long the pain lasts: about half as long as it took to arrive.** Once demand falls back below
target, empty-relative-to-target blocks decay the accumulator at exponent 0.15704 per block, so the
same 18.42 unwinds in 18.42 / 0.15704 ≈ 117 blocks ≈ **23 minutes**.<Cite src="eip-8135" /> The
asymmetry is structural: since Pectra the max has been 1.5 times the target, so the fee can only climb
at half the rate at which it falls.<Cite src="eip-8135" />

**What to change.** Replace the fixed gwei cap with one expressed relative to the current blob base
fee, and cap total spend per interval rather than price per unit — a fixed price cap converts a cost
problem into a liveness problem, which is a strictly worse trade for a batcher. Then alert on the
*derivative*: two consecutive blocks of blob base fee rising is a signal that arrives forty minutes
before the number itself becomes alarming, and forty minutes is enough to decide deliberately whether
to keep posting, post less often, or switch posting path entirely — the subject of the next lesson.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**Next**, the decision this arithmetic feeds. With two independent prices, neither posting path is
always cheaper, and the crossover point turns out to depend as much on how full your blobs are as on
either market.

**Two lessons out**, the parameters themselves. Target, max and the update fraction all live on a
schedule that now moves on its own cadence, which is why this lesson derived the dynamics rather than
printing a table of outcomes.

**And carry the method, not the multipliers.** Take the target, the max and the update fraction from
whatever schedule is live; multiply by `GAS_PER_BLOB`; divide by the fraction; exponentiate. Two lines
of arithmetic reproduce every rate in this lesson and will keep reproducing them after every constant
here is stale.
