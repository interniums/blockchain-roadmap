A wallet shows you two routes off an OP Stack chain. One says seven days, no fee. The other says two
minutes, 0.08%. They render as adjacent rows in the same dropdown, in the same font, with the same
green tick.

The first row is the rollup. The second row is a company.

That is the entire content of this lesson, but stated that baldly it is useless, because "a company"
is not a risk you can reason about. What you need is the number: **how many parties, acting alone or
in collusion of size k, can mint an unbacked token or move the escrow?** For the native bridge you
worked that out last lesson — it is the rollup's own answer, whatever that is, and nothing more.
Every design below changes k, and the honest way to compare them is to say by how much and in
exchange for what.

## Why anything other than the native bridge exists

Two reasons, and only two. Conduit's write-up for rollup operators names both as the cons of the
native bridge: withdrawals wait out the seven-day challenge period, and the native bridge connects
your rollup to Ethereum and to nothing else — it cannot reach other L2s or
L3s.<Cite src="conduit-native-vs-third-party" />

The second reason is more fundamental than it looks, and it is worth being precise about, because it
is the one that cannot be engineered away.

**Subgoal: identify what the native bridge is actually exploiting.** The rollup and its L1 share a
root of trust. The L2 derives its state from L1 data; the L1 adjudicates the L2's state through the
proof system. There is a contract on L1 that is *entitled* to have an opinion about L2 state, because
verifying that opinion is its job.

**Subgoal: remove that shared root and see what remains.** Take two unrelated chains — an L1 and a
different L1, or two rollups that settle to different places. Neither derives from the other. Neither
adjudicates the other. There is no contract on either side with any entitlement to an opinion about
the other's state.

**Subgoal: state the consequence exactly.** A message between them must therefore be believed on some
other basis. Either the destination chain verifies the source chain's consensus itself — which means
running a light client of the source chain inside a smart contract, with everything that costs — or
somebody the destination chain has been configured to trust says the message is genuine. There is no
third option that is not one of those two wearing a hat.

ethereum.org's classification is built on exactly this axis. Its central security question is "who
verifies the system", and its answer is that bridges secured by external validators are typically
less secure than bridges secured locally or natively by the underlying blockchain's own
validators.<Cite src="ethereum-org-bridges" /> Its top-level split is trusted versus trustless, where
trusted means externally verified — a federation with a multisig, a multi-party computation system,
an oracle network — and trustless means the bridge relies on the chains it connects and their
validators, adding no new trust assumptions.<Cite src="ethereum-org-bridges" />

Note what that framing does *not* say. It does not say external verification is bad. It says it is
the thing you are buying, and it is priced in connectivity: externally verified bridges "offer great
connectivity and enable fully generalized message passing" and "tend to perform well with speed and
cost-effectiveness", at the cost of security.<Cite src="ethereum-org-bridges" /> Light-client designs
buy security at the cost of connectivity, and liquidity networks buy speed and security at the cost
of not being able to pass messages at all.<Cite src="ethereum-org-bridges" /> Three corners, pick two.

## Lock and mint: the wrapper is a claim, not the asset

The oldest pattern. ethereum.org lists it first among the three ways to move an asset across chains,
alongside burn-and-mint and atomic swaps: lock the asset on the source chain, mint a representation
on the destination.<Cite src="ethereum-org-bridges" />

The mechanism is two lines long. The interesting part is the invariant it is supposed to maintain,
and what the invariant depends on.

**Subgoal: write the invariant.** For each asset and each bridge deployment, *the wrapped supply
outstanding on the destination chain must not exceed the amount escrowed on the source chain.* Two
numbers on two chains, one relationship. It is checkable from public data on both sides, by anyone,
at any time.

**Subgoal: identify who can break it.** Only the minting authority. Nothing a user does can create
wrapped supply; only the authority that the destination contract accepts messages from can. Which
means the invariant does not rest on the escrow contract being correct — it rests on the *message
verification* on the destination side being correct, because that is the only door through which
supply enters.

**Subgoal: check the prediction against the record.** In the Wormhole incident of February 2022, the
attacker bypassed the verification step, generated a message specifying that 120,000 wETH be minted,
and minted it by calling `complete_wrapped` — 120,000 wETH on Solana with no matching
collateral.<Cite src="certik-bridge-exploits-2022" /> The escrow on Ethereum was untouched throughout.
Nothing was stolen from it; something was *created* on the other side. In the Nomad incident of
August 2022 the same class of outcome produced a total value locked that fell from around $190
million to roughly $12,000.<Cite src="certik-bridge-exploits-2022" />

**Subgoal: draw the conclusion that generalises.** A wrapped token is a claim on one specific
deployment's escrow, enforced by one specific deployment's message verification. It is not the asset.
Two wrapped tokens for the same underlying, minted by two different bridges, are two different
instruments that happen to share a ticker in your wallet's UI.

<Misconception
  belief="Wrapped tokens from a bridge are the same asset as the original."
  reality="They are a claim on that specific bridge's lock, released by that specific bridge's verification. If the verification is bypassed the wrapper becomes unbacked supply while the original asset sits untouched on the source chain."
  why="Wallets, price feeds and block explorers display them under the same ticker and the same price, because the price is quoted against the peg, and the peg holds right up until it does not."
  src="wormhole-postmortem"
/>

The practical consequence for anything you build: **the question "do I accept this token" is really
the question "do I accept this bridge's verifier set".** If your protocol lists a wrapped asset as
collateral, you have taken a dependency on a verification configuration maintained by people you have
never met, and its failure arrives as your bad debt rather than as their loss. That is not a
hypothetical; it is why the exploit-archaeology track treats unbacked supply as a *collateral* event
rather than a bridge event.

### Burn-and-mint, and why the choice is about who holds the issuance right

ethereum.org lists burn-and-mint beside lock-and-mint as a distinct
mechanism.<Cite src="ethereum-org-bridges" /> The difference is small in code and large in
governance.

Lock-and-mint escrows the real asset and creates a *new* token on the destination. The escrow exists,
so the wrapper's backing is visible on chain, and the bridge holds custody. Burn-and-mint destroys
supply on the source and creates supply on the destination of what is meant to be the *same* token,
so there is no escrow to inspect and no custody — but the destination contract must hold the token's
actual mint authority. The invariant shifts from "escrow ≥ wrapped supply" to "total supply across
all chains is conserved", and the second one is much harder to check from any single chain.

Which you can use is not an engineering decision. It depends on whether the token issuer will hand
the bridge a mint right, which is why burn-and-mint arrangements are typically the issuer's own and
lock-and-mint wrappers are typically everyone else's. When a chain has several wrapped versions of
one asset with different tickers and different depths, that is what you are looking at: several
independent escrows, each with its own verifier configuration, none fungible with the others except
through a market that assumes all of them are fine.

<Misconception
  belief="A bridge aggregator removes the risk of picking the wrong bridge."
  reality="Aggregators inherit the strengths of the bridges they route through, and the risks too — ethereum.org notes they are exposed to the same smart contract and technology risk, with more contracts rather than fewer."
  why="Aggregation genuinely solves price and liquidity fragmentation, so it is assumed to solve the security question as well. It solves the routing problem and leaves the trust question exactly where it was, now one layer further from you."

/>

<Aside kind="warn">
Aggregate loss figures for bridges are quoted with wildly different scopes and should not be
combined. One audit firm's 2022 review counts five cross-chain bridge attacks totalling roughly $1.32
billion, about 57% of all Web3 losses that year.<Cite src="certik-bridge-exploits-2022" /> A vendor
education page, updated later, cites more than $2.8 billion hacked from cross-chain bridges,
representing almost 40% of all value hacked in Web3, attributing the figure to a public
dashboard.<Cite src="chainlink-bridge-vulns" /> Different years, different denominators, different
methodologies. Sources disagree; quote a scope with every figure or quote neither.
</Aside>

## Liquidity bridges: you are buying the wait, not removing it

Now the design that dominates the "two minutes" row in the wallet dropdown, and the one most often
misdescribed.

Conduit's description of how third-party bridges achieve instant withdrawals is exact and worth
holding onto: they maintain liquidity pools for supported assets on both chains, and fund the user's
address on the second chain with funds equivalent to the withdrawal *before the challenge period is
done* — taking on the risk of transactions being invalidated during that
period.<Cite src="conduit-native-vs-third-party" />

Nothing was made faster. Somebody was paid to sit through the seven days on your behalf.

That reframing immediately gives you three quantities you can actually reason about.

**Subgoal: derive the fee floor from capital cost.** The operator's capital is committed from the
moment they pay you until their own native withdrawal finalises — the full challenge window, seven
days on OP Stack and Arbitrum mainnets.<Cite src="conduit-native-vs-third-party" /> If the operator's
opportunity cost of capital is r per year, the pure carry on capital C is:

Those are floors, not prices — r is a modelling choice, not a measurement, and the operator must also
cover gas on three transactions, the risk premium, and a margin. But the shape is informative: a
seven-day carry at plausible rates lands in the tens of basis points, which is the same order as the
fees these products actually quote. **A liquidity bridge charging far less than its carry is either
subsidising you, or is not actually holding the position it appears to hold.** Both are worth
knowing.

**Subgoal: derive the capacity constraint.** A unit of the operator's capital serves one withdrawal
and is then locked for the window before it can serve another. So to support a steady flow of V per
day with a window of W days, the pool must be at least:

```text
C ≥ V · W        W = 7  →  C ≥ 7V
```

Supporting $10M per day of instant withdrawals requires roughly $70M of standing capital on the
destination side. This is why liquidity bridges have deep pools or they have queues, and it is why
"instant" quietly becomes "pending" during a rush. It is also the reason the *shorter* the challenge
window, the cheaper this service gets — the two variables in the constraint are the two things the
rollup controls.

**Subgoal: name what the user is now exposed to.** Not a minting authority — there is no wrapper
here. The assets on both sides are real, and the underlying chains verify their own transfers, which
is why ethereum.org classifies liquidity networks as *locally verified* and rates them well on
security and speed.<Cite src="ethereum-org-bridges" /> What you are exposed to is the operator's
solvency and honesty during the seconds between your L2 transaction and their L1 payment, plus
whatever contract holds the pool.

And what you gave up: liquidity networks primarily transfer assets and generally do not support
cross-chain message passing at all — that is the explicit trade-off in the
classification.<Cite src="ethereum-org-bridges" /> A liquidity bridge cannot carry a contract call.
If your product needs to move a *message*, this whole family is unavailable to you and you are back
among the externally verified designs.

### A third thing that is neither: fast withdrawal inside the rollup

Worth separating out, because it gets lumped in with liquidity bridges and is a different animal.
Some rollups shorten the wait at the *chain* level rather than selling around it. Conduit notes
Arbitrum's fast withdrawals for some Orbit chains, where a committee of validators processes
transactions and withdrawals can be offered in as little as fifteen minutes provided the committee's
validation is unanimous; and OP Stack chains adopting validity proofs for fast finality through OP
Succinct.<Cite src="conduit-native-vs-third-party" />

These are not the same as a liquidity bridge, and the difference is exactly the k question. A
validating committee is a new trust assumption *added to the chain itself* — every user of the chain
now depends on it, whether or not they are bridging. A validity proof adds no committee at all. A
liquidity bridge adds an operator that only its own users depend on. Same headline ("faster
withdrawals"), three completely different blast radii.

<Aside kind="note">
Configuration for specific chains — whether a given Orbit chain runs a fast-withdrawal committee,
what its threshold is, whether a given OP Stack chain has adopted validity proofs — changes on each
chain's own schedule. Read the chain's own documentation and its L2BEAT risk rows at the time you
need the answer. Nothing in this lesson should be treated as a current statement about any named
chain.
</Aside>

## The ladder, stated as trust rather than as marketing

Put the module's four families in one order, by what each asks you to add to the rollup's own
assumptions.

Two observations about that ordering, both of which will be tested in the next three lessons.

**Going down the ladder buys capability, not speed.** The liquidity bridge is already fast. What the
lower rungs buy is the ability to carry arbitrary messages and to reach chains with no shared
settlement layer. If all you need is "move this asset from here to there", you may never need to
descend past rung two — and a great deal of bridge risk is carried by products that descended anyway
because the integration was easier.

**The bottom rung is where the money was lost.** That is not a coincidence and it is not about code
quality. It is where a single verification decision, made by a small set of parties, releases the
value of everything that ever crossed. Lesson five is that sentence with numbers attached.

<Check type="explain" concept="third-party-bridge">
Four bridges are offered for the same transfer: 500,000 USDC from an OP Stack rollup to Ethereum. For
each, state (a) the smallest set of parties who could cause you to lose the funds, (b) whether the
seven-day window still elapses somewhere, and (c) what capability you gained.

1. The chain's native bridge.
2. A liquidity bridge that pays you on Ethereum in two minutes from a pool it controls.
3. A lock-and-mint bridge that mints wrapped USDC on Ethereum against an escrow on the rollup.
4. The same liquidity bridge, but the destination is a different L1 with no shared settlement layer.

<Answer>
**1 — native.** (a) Whoever can upgrade the rollup's L1 contracts; there is no separate bridge
operator to add. That set is the rollup's [upgrade authority](/c/upgrade-key-risk), which at
[Stage 0](/c/l2beat-stage-0) is a multisig — so the answer is "the same people who could already take
everything you hold on this chain". (b) Yes, for you, three transactions and seven
days.<Cite src="op-docs-bridging-messaging" /> (c) Nothing new; this is the baseline.

**2 — liquidity bridge.** (a) The operator, plus whoever controls the pool contract. Note this is a
*solvency and honesty* exposure over roughly two minutes, not a custody exposure over seven days —
the funds you receive are real USDC on Ethereum the moment they land. (b) Yes, but for the operator,
which is what the fee pays for.<Cite src="conduit-native-vs-third-party" /> (c) Time. That is all,
and it is a real thing to buy.

**3 — lock-and-mint.** (a) Whoever the destination contract accepts mint messages from — the verifier
set — plus whoever can change that set. This is a strictly larger and less legible group than in
case 2, and the failure mode differs in kind: you do not lose your USDC, you end up holding a token
that is no longer backed, which may still trade at par for hours. (b) Not necessarily; the mint can
be immediate. The window was *replaced*, not waited out. (c) Nothing here, which is the point — for a
rollup-to-L1 transfer of an asset that exists on both sides, lock-and-mint is capability you did not
need and risk you did not have to take.

**4 — liquidity bridge to an unrelated L1.** (a) The operator and the pool, as in case 2 — *if* the
design really is locally verified end to end. Check that, because the moment a route has no shared
settlement layer, the operator's own repayment leg usually runs over an externally verified message
path, and that path's verifier set is now in your dependency chain even though the front end never
mentioned it. (b) There is no seven-day window on this route at all; there is no challenge period
between two unrelated L1s to wait out. (c) Reach — a destination the native bridge structurally
cannot serve.<Cite src="conduit-native-vs-third-party" />

**The pattern.** Only case 1 and case 2 have short, nameable answers to (a). The moment the route
leaves the rollup's settlement relationship, the honest answer to (a) becomes "read the deployed
configuration", which is precisely the reading that nobody does and that lesson five is built out of.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**In lesson three**, one family tries to remove the verifier set entirely by proving source-chain
state instead of attesting to it — the storage-proof design. You will find it does not remove trust
so much as relocate it, and the relocation is honest and worth understanding. The most candid public
write-up of such a design says so directly, listing among its risks that the storage-proof provider
can go offline, and shipping a fallback onto the rollup's native messaging system for exactly that
case.<Cite src="aligned-storage-proof-bridge" />

**In lesson four**, the liquidity bridge grows up into the intent model, where the operator becomes a
solver, the pool becomes the solver's own balance sheet, and the capacity constraint you derived
above turns into a documented attack surface.

**In your own architecture decisions.** The ladder is a checklist. Write down what your product moves
— assets only, or messages. Write down where — inside one settlement relationship, or across
unrelated chains. Those two answers determine which rungs are even available to you, and most teams
descend further than their answers require because a bridge with more features looked like a bridge
with fewer integrations.
