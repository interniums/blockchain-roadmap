You have already written this contract. In the state track you verified a Merkle-Patricia proof
against a state root and pulled a storage slot's value out of it; `eth_getProof` hands you the
account proof and the storage proofs, and the whole structure is checked against the state root in a
block header.<Cite src="eip-1186" />

Point that contract at a different chain and you have a storage-proof bridge. That is the entire
idea, and it is genuinely a reduction in trust: instead of "the committee says a deposit happened on
chain A", the destination contract gets "here is a proof that slot *s* of account *a* held value *v*
under state root *R*", and it checks the proof itself. No committee has to be honest for the *state*
claim to hold.

Then you go to deploy it and discover the question nobody's diagram shows. Where does *R* come from?

## Separate the inputs into two piles

Do this mechanically, because the answer falls straight out.

**Subgoal: enumerate what the verifier consumes.** A storage proof verification takes an account
address, a storage key, a claimed value, an account proof, a storage proof, and a state root — the
shape `eth_getProof` returns.<Cite src="eip-1186" />

**Subgoal: sort those by who supplies them and whether they are self-checking.** The address, key,
value and both proof arrays are all supplied by whoever is calling your contract, and all of them are
self-checking: hash the leaf, walk the path, compare against the root. A wrong value produces a wrong
hash and the verification fails. An attacker can supply anything they like here and gain nothing.

**Subgoal: notice the one that is different.** The state root is not self-checking. It is the axiom.
Everything else is checked *against* it, and nothing checks it. Whatever else the contract believes,
it believes *R*.

**Subgoal: state the reduction.** The security of a storage-proof bridge is therefore not the security
of Merkle-Patricia proofs, which is the security of keccak and is not in question. It is entirely the
security of the answer to one question: **how did this chain come to believe a state root belonging
to another chain?** The proof machinery moved the question. It did not answer it.

This is the header verification problem, and it is the reason the family is called *trust-minimised*
rather than trustless by anyone being careful.

## Three ways to acquire a foreign root, and what each costs

There are only three families, and each one relocates the trust somewhere specific.

**An on-chain light client.** The destination chain runs the source chain's consensus verification
inside a contract: track the validator set, verify signatures on headers, follow the fork-choice
rule, accept roots that pass. This adds no new trusted party — the assumption is the source chain's
own consensus, which is what you wanted. ethereum.org places light-client designs among the
trustless generalized-message-passing bridges precisely on those grounds, and names the price in the
same sentence: their strength in security comes at the cost of
connectivity.<Cite src="ethereum-org-bridges" /> Not every consensus is cheap to verify in a
contract, so the set of chains you can connect this way is constrained by their consensus design, not
by your engineering budget. And the verification runs forever, at gas, whether or not anyone is
bridging.

**A validity proof of consensus.** Rather than executing the source chain's consensus checks on
chain, prove off-chain that a header satisfies them and verify the succinct proof instead. This
compresses the recurring cost and widens the set of connectable chains, and it replaces the light
client's gas bill with proving infrastructure and its own trusted setup and circuit-correctness
assumptions. A design write-up in this family lists exactly that among the user's risks — a bug in
the circuits of the validity-proof verification, mitigated by using several independent proof systems
rather than one.<Cite src="aligned-storage-proof-bridge" />

**An external attester set signs headers.** A committee watches the source chain and signs the
headers it sees; the destination contract accepts a root that carries a threshold of signatures. This
works everywhere, costs almost nothing, and **puts the trusted party back exactly where the storage
proof removed it**. You now have a validator set with a threshold k, which is the design lesson two
called externally verified, and which lesson five's exploit record is made of. A storage proof on top
of a committee-supplied root is not a trust reduction; it is a trust *rearrangement* that makes the
committee harder to see.

That third option deserves its own warning because it is genuinely common and it reads as rigorous.
A system can be described accurately as "verifies Merkle proofs on chain" and still have a
five-of-nine multisig deciding which roots exist.

## The direction that is nearly free, and why the shipping designs use it

Here is the observation that makes this family practical, and it comes straight out of lesson one's
asymmetry.

A rollup's L2 *already has an authenticated view of L1 headers*. On the OP Stack the `L1Block`
predeploy exposes information about the latest known L1 block, and it is written by the protocol
itself through a system deposit transaction — not by a relayer, not by a
committee.<Cite src="op-docs-smart-contracts" /> The same page notes a `BeaconBlockRoot` predeploy
exposing L1 beacon block roots, following EIP-4788.<Cite src="op-docs-smart-contracts" />

So on a rollup, in the L1-to-L2 direction, the header verification problem is *already solved by the
chain*, for free, as a consequence of the rollup being a rollup. The root arrives through the same
derivation pipeline that carries deposits.

Read the first two rows together with lesson one and something clicks. The native withdrawal is
already a storage proof — `OptimismPortal.proveWithdrawalTransaction` verifies a Merkle proof that
the message hash is set in `L2ToL1MessagePasser.sentMessages`, against an output root claimed by a
dispute game.<Cite src="op-docs-withdrawal-flow" /> Scroll's is the same idea against a dedicated
Withdraw Trie.<Cite src="scroll-cross-domain-messaging" /> **The rollup's own bridge is the
storage-proof bridge whose root problem is solved by the settlement contract.** Everything a
third-party storage-proof design does is an attempt to get a root sooner, or from a chain that has no
settlement relationship with you.

### A design that faces this honestly

The clearest published example inverts the direction to exploit exactly the free row above.

**Subgoal: follow the money, not the message.** The user locks the tokens to be bridged into an
escrow contract on the L2, naming a destination address on Ethereum. A market maker watching the
escrow calls a `PaymentRegistry` contract on Ethereum, which pays the user on
Ethereum.<Cite src="aligned-storage-proof-bridge" /> The user is done, in under thirty seconds by the
authors' account — as fast as the market maker can see the deposit and
pay.<Cite src="aligned-storage-proof-bridge" />

**Subgoal: notice which claim needs proving, and on which chain.** The unproven claim is "the market
maker paid the user on Ethereum". It has to be proven *to the L2 escrow*, because that is where the
market maker's repayment sits. A storage proof of the Ethereum `PaymentRegistry` contract's state is
generated and verified by the L2 escrow, which then releases the user's original
tokens to the market maker.<Cite src="aligned-storage-proof-bridge" />

**Subgoal: name why that direction was chosen.** The proof travels L1 to L2 — the direction in which
a rollup already has authenticated L1 headers.<Cite src="op-docs-smart-contracts" /> The design never
has to solve the general header problem at all. It arranged the protocol so that the only root it
needs is one its chain is given.

**Subgoal: read the fallback, because it is the honest part.** If the storage-proof providers are
unavailable, the market maker can instead prove fulfilment through the rollup's native messaging
system — which the authors note carries the same trust assumptions as the L2s used in the
transfer.<Cite src="aligned-storage-proof-bridge" /> A liveness dependency on a proving service,
degrading to the slow path rather than to a trusted one. That is what a well-specified fallback looks
like: slower, never weaker.

The authors are equally direct about what the design does *not* do. Storage proofs alone do not
fundamentally change a traditional bridge's design — they enable a safer coordination mechanism,
with the user's capital locked first so the market maker has a guarantee of
repayment.<Cite src="aligned-storage-proof-bridge" /> The risks do not disappear; they are
transferred to the market maker, who additionally carries chain-reorganisation risk and the fact of
being repaid on the L2 rather than on Ethereum.<Cite src="aligned-storage-proof-bridge" /> And there
is a real limitation: users can only bridge tokens present on both the origin and destination
chains.<Cite src="aligned-storage-proof-bridge" />

<Aside kind="note">
The timing asymmetry in that design is the same one from lesson two, measured. The user is finished
in under thirty seconds; the market maker waits for the storage proof, normally five to fifteen
minutes, and must then rebalance over the native bridge and wait out its
finality.<Cite src="aligned-storage-proof-bridge" /> Someone always waits. The design question is who,
and whether they were paid enough to.
</Aside>

## Four things a storage proof does not prove

Worth listing explicitly, because each has produced a real design error.

**It does not prove the header is canonical.** A valid proof against a header that gets reorged out
is a valid proof of a state that no longer exists. The design above lists reorganisation risk
against the market maker for exactly this reason.<Cite src="aligned-storage-proof-bridge" /> Any
consumer of foreign state needs a confirmation policy on top of the proof, the same way your indexer
does — this is [reorg](/c/reorg) handling wearing a cryptographic hat.

**It does not prove freshness.** A proof is a statement about a block. Nothing in it says the block
is recent. A verifier that does not bind the proof to a bounded window of recent roots will happily
accept a correct proof of a state from last year, which is a replay with extra steps.

**It does not prove absence.** Proving a slot holds a value is easy; proving no slot anywhere holds
some value is not the same operation and generally is not available. Designs that need "this has not
been claimed" must express it as a positive claim about a specific slot — which is precisely what a
`sentMessages`-style mapping is for.<Cite src="op-docs-withdrawal-flow" />

**It does not prove intent.** The slot's value is a fact about a contract's storage, not about what
the contract meant. If the source contract's own accounting can be manipulated, the storage proof
faithfully proves the manipulated number. Storage proofs move the trust boundary to the source
contract's correctness — which is now inside your trust boundary, and is code your team did not
write.

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="header-verification-problem">
A team shows you a cross-chain bridge between two unrelated L1s. The pitch: "No multisig, no
validators. The destination contract verifies a Merkle-Patricia storage proof that the deposit was
recorded on the source chain. It is cryptographically secured."

You get to ask three questions before the audit. Which three, and what would each answer tell you?

<Answer>
**Question 1: where does the destination contract get the source chain's state root, and who can
change that mechanism?** This is the whole review. If the answer is "a set of signers submits
headers", the multisig they said they did not have is right there, and the storage proof only
constrains *what* those signers can be used to claim, not *whether* they can claim it. If the answer
is an on-chain light client, the bridge really is trust-minimised and your review moves to the client
implementation and to the connectivity constraint that comes with
it.<Cite src="ethereum-org-bridges" /> If the answer is a validity proof of consensus, your review
moves to the circuit and to how many independent proof systems back it — the mitigation the honest
designs use is several implementations rather than one.<Cite src="aligned-storage-proof-bridge" />
The second half of the question matters as much as the first: a light client that an admin key can
replace with a signature check is a signature check.

**Question 2: how many confirmations behind the head does it accept a root, and what happens on a
reorg of the source chain?** A valid proof against an orphaned header is a valid proof of a state
that never finalised. There must be an explicit policy, and it must be a *contract* policy, not an
operational habit of whoever submits roots.<Cite src="aligned-storage-proof-bridge" />

**Question 3: what binds this proof to this claim, once — nonce, replay set, and freshness window?**
A storage proof is a statement about a block, and blocks stay in history. Ask which slot encodes
"this deposit, not yet claimed", and confirm the destination marks it consumed atomically with the
release. The native design's answer is a per-message hash recorded in a mapping and a nonce inside
the hashed fields to keep two identical withdrawals distinct.<Cite src="op-docs-withdrawal-flow" />

**What you have deliberately not asked about:** the Merkle proof verification itself. It is the part
they will want to walk you through and the part least likely to be wrong — keccak and trie traversal
are well-trodden. Spend the review on the root, the reorg policy and the replay set. That allocation
of attention *is* the lesson.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**In lesson four**, the design above turns out to be an intent bridge wearing a proof: the user
signs for an outcome, a market maker fronts capital, and a settlement path reimburses them. The
proof is the settlement mechanism. Lesson four takes the settlement gap seriously and finds that it
is where the current research on this family is concentrated.

**In lesson five**, the four-question review discipline you just used becomes the general method.
Every headline bridge failure is a missing check on an input that was never self-checking — a signer
set, a root, an initialisation value. The storage proof narrows what can go wrong; it does not change
the shape of what does.

**In anything you build that consumes foreign state.** The two-pile exercise generalises past
bridges. For any external input, sort it into self-checking and axiomatic, and then spend your review
entirely on the axioms. Oracles, [Merkle](/c/merkle-patricia-trie) airdrops, cross-domain
authorisation, signature-gated mints: in each case there is one value the whole construction rests on
and a great deal of machinery that checks itself. Find the value nobody checks.
