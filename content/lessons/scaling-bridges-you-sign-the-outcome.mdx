Take lesson two's liquidity bridge and remove two things. Remove the pool: pay the user out of the
operator's own balance sheet instead of a shared contract. Remove the operator's monopoly: let anyone
with capital on the destination chain compete to be the one who pays. What is left is a market, and
the user's transaction no longer names a bridge at all. It names a result.

That is an intent, and it is now the shape most user-facing cross-chain transfers take. The canonical
bridge did not go away — it moved underneath, where it settles between professionals instead of
between you and a wrapper. So the honest question changed too. Not "who can mint an unbacked token",
but "who is out of pocket, for how long, and what happens to everyone else when they run out".

## An order is an offer, and the solver is the counterparty

The standardisation effort defines the object precisely, and the definition is worth taking
literally: an order is an offer of payment in exchange for the fulfilment of a set of requirements,
and intent protocols let users express desired outcomes as orders, with specialised actors called
solvers executing the required actions and receiving compensation.<Cite src="erc-7683" />

That is a contract between two parties with a *time gap in the middle*. The user's signature is the
offer, the solver's fill is the performance, and the payment comes afterwards from a settlement
process that must first become convinced the fill happened — which, between two chains with no shared
settlement relationship, is exactly lessons two and three.

**Nothing here verifies anything faster than before.** It rearranges who waits. In lock-and-mint the
user waits for a mint they should not trust. In a liquidity bridge the operator waits out the
challenge window. In an intent bridge the solver waits out the settlement path, and the user is
decoupled from it entirely.

## Follow the capital, and the whole risk model falls out

Four subgoals, and the third produces the number that explains why this design won.

**Subgoal: identify who is out of pocket, and between which two events.** The academic treatment
states it in one line: solvers front liquidity to fulfil intents immediately, while reimbursement
occurs only after an asynchronous and variable settlement
delay.<Cite src="arxiv-intent-liquidity-exhaustion" /> The solver is out of pocket from paying the
user until their reimbursement is final and spendable. Call that the **settlement gap**.

**Subgoal: measure the gap rather than assume it.** The same study measured it across three live
protocols over June to November 2025, covering 3.5 million intents moving $9.24
billion.<Cite src="arxiv-intent-liquidity-exhaustion" /> Median solver refund time came out at
roughly 989 seconds for deBridge and 1281 seconds for Mayan Swift, while Across is listed at 7200
seconds on the theoretical basis of a two-hour fraud-proof
window.<Cite src="arxiv-intent-liquidity-exhaustion" /> One study, one window; any of the three could
have re-parameterised since. The order of magnitude is the point: **minutes, not days.**

**Subgoal: recompute lesson two's capacity constraint with that number.** You derived there that an
operator fronting V of value per day against a window of W must hold roughly `C ≥ V · W`. The
challenge window on OP Stack and Arbitrum mainnets is seven days,<Cite src="conduit-native-vs-third-party" />
which is 604,800 seconds. Against a settlement gap near 1,000 seconds:

That arithmetic is mine, on their measurements — and it is the commercial engine of the category. An
intent solver turns capital over hundreds of times more often than a liquidity bridge behind a
seven-day window, so it can quote a far thinner fee on the same volume and still clear its cost of
capital. **Intents did not make settlement faster. They made the same capital serve six hundred times
as many transfers.**

**Subgoal: name what that efficiency spent.** Capital that turns over every sixteen minutes is
capital that is nearly always deployed. A pool sized for seven days has enormous headroom by
construction; a solver balance sheet sized for a thousand seconds has almost none. The design
converted a custody risk into a **capacity** risk — and capacity risks fail not by theft but by
everyone's transfer failing at once.

## The attack is not a hack, and it is cheap

That capacity risk has been studied directly, and the results are the most useful thing published
about this family.

**The mechanism.** An attacker submits carefully timed, large-value intents designed to temporarily
drain solver liquidity; honest solvers cannot participate while their capital is committed;
subsequent legitimate user requests fail for want of a filler; and the attacker either captures the
high-margin intents inside that window or simply suppresses availability — the paper's rational and
byzantine variants.<Cite src="arxiv-intent-liquidity-exhaustion" />

Notice what is absent. No signature forged, no contract broken, no key stolen. The attacker uses the
protocol exactly as designed, and the cost is essentially the protocol fee on the intents they submit
plus gas.<Cite src="arxiv-intent-liquidity-exhaustion" />

**The economics.** Against deBridge under the study's baseline strategy, 210 historical attack
instances yielded a mean net profit of $286.14, with 80.5% of attacks
profitable.<Cite src="arxiv-intent-liquidity-exhaustion" /> A targeted variant cut costs sharply —
the maximum fell 90.41%, from $1,507.32 to $144.56 — and against Mayan Swift it dropped the minimum
from $175 to $7 and the maximum from $247 to
$25.<Cite src="arxiv-intent-liquidity-exhaustion" /> Measured damage on the other side reached solver
profit losses of up to $978 roughly every sixteen
minutes.<Cite src="arxiv-intent-liquidity-exhaustion" />

Two-figure attack costs. This is not a nation-state problem.

**The parameter that decides it reads backwards.** The three protocols the study characterised:

The naive read picks Across as the weak one: it pays solvers almost nothing, so surely nobody shows
up. The study finds the opposite. Across remains robust in all tested configurations due to low
solver margins and very high liquidity, while protocols with higher solver profitability, such as
deBridge, are vulnerable.<Cite src="arxiv-intent-liquidity-exhaustion" />

The reason is arithmetic. The attacker's induction cost is a fraction of the liquidity they must tie
up, paid at the protocol's own fee rate — so fat solver margins quote the attacker a *discount on the
protocol's own denial of service*, and deep liquidity quotes them a much larger bill. **What looks
like a worse deal for solvers is what makes the protocol expensive to attack.**

The authors' mitigations follow the same logic and are best read as design levers: rate-limit
fulfilment against available solver liquidity, shorten refund times through partial or conditional
refunds, automate rebalancing, price congestion dynamically, and increase solver diversity and total
liquidity.<Cite src="arxiv-intent-liquidity-exhaustion" /> Each shrinks the window or raises the
capital the attacker must beat.

## ERC-7683: read the ERC, not the explainers

This is the part where most published material describes a document that no longer exists.

**Status first.** ERC-7683, "Cross Chain Intents", is a Standards Track ERC created 2024-04-11 with
status **Draft**, requiring EIP-7930 for interoperable addresses.<Cite src="erc-7683" /> Anything
calling it ratified or final is wrong, and the adoption percentages circulating for it trace to a
single vendor source family, so they are not repeated here.

**What it standardises is narrower than almost anyone says.** The ERC states outright that it does
not require protocols to use a common escrow, settlement contract, or fill
function.<Cite src="erc-7683" /> It standardises solver order *consumption* and nothing else. A
protocol publishes orders as opaque byte payloads and deploys a **resolver** contract; a solver calls
that resolver off-chain via `eth_call` to turn the payload into a common
representation.<Cite src="erc-7683" />

```solidity
struct Assumption { string name; bytes data; }

interface IResolver {
    struct ResolvedOrder {
        bytes[] steps;
        bytes[] variables;
        bytes[] payments;
        Assumption[] assumptions;
    }
    function resolve(bytes calldata payload) external view returns (ResolvedOrder memory);
}
```
<Cite src="erc-7683" />

Four fields, each a piece of the risk model you just derived.

- **steps** are calls — target, 4-byte selector, arguments, and attributes such as `SpendsERC20`,
  `SpendsGas`, `RevertPolicy` and `TimingBounds`.<Cite src="erc-7683" /> What to execute, and what it
  costs the solver.
- **variables** are values the solver chooses or must observe — `PaymentRecipient`, `PaymentChain`,
  `StepCaller`, `ExecutionOutput`, plus `Query` and `QueryEvents` roles resolved through `eth_call`
  and `eth_getLogs`.<Cite src="erc-7683" /> This is what makes a solver *programmable* rather than
  protocol-specific.
- **payments** carry an `estimatedDelaySeconds` field on the ERC-20
  payment.<Cite src="erc-7683" /> **The settlement gap is a named parameter in the standard** — the
  design is not pretending it away, it is asking the protocol to quote it.
- **assumptions** are conditions the resolver cannot verify, surfaced by name so the solver validates
  them before filling.<Cite src="erc-7683" /> An explicit "here is what I am not checking" field.

**Why resolution is off-chain but the resolver is on-chain.** Off-chain resolution removes the
gas-cost constraint, letting orders be semantically rich rather than squeezed into
calldata.<Cite src="erc-7683" /> On-chain publication exists because resolvers are trust points:
solver operators whitelist resolver addresses, and once vetted, a resolver's instructions are trusted
on the strength of audits, bounties and liveness analysis.<Cite src="erc-7683" />

That is this module's question in a new costume. **The trust decision here is "whose resolver did my
solver whitelist"** — a set, maintained by parties you did not choose, in which a compromised
resolver emits instructions a correctly implemented solver will follow.

### The superseded draft, and why the ERC threw it away

Earlier drafts did standardise the order lifecycle: `OnchainCrossChainOrder` and
`GaslessCrossChainOrder`, an on-chain feed via `IOriginSettler.open` and `openFor`, an
implementation-agnostic `ResolvedCrossChainOrder`, and a solver fill via
`IDestinationSettler.fill`.<Cite src="erc-7683" /> Any explainer with a common order struct in it is
describing that document. The ERC's own rationale gives four reasons it was
dropped:<Cite src="erc-7683" />

1. The structs carried implementation-specific fields, so orders received only superficial
   standardisation — solvers still needed protocol-specific support.
2. `maxSpent` and `minReceived` were only lower bounds, so non-tight bounds made profitable orders
   look unprofitable and go unexecuted; some protocols could supply only `UINT256_MAX` for
   `maxSpent`.
3. It assumed escrow-per-order as a prerequisite for fulfilment, which is wrong for fill-first
   protocols using resource locks.
4. Large calldata structs and full resolved-order emission in `Open` events imposed gas overhead,
   making compliance less attractive than a bespoke interface.

### The security section is the reachback to the rest of the module

The ERC's own analysis of solver exposure is the settlement gap generalised: a solver is exposed from
the moment it commits capital, an approval or a transaction until the expected payment finalises and
becomes spendable, and security analysis must cover the complete execution and settlement window —
including adversarial changes to protocol state, chain state, token behaviour, oracle values,
permissions, or **message-delivery paths** that could render valid instructions
unsafe.<Cite src="erc-7683" />

That last item is the whole module in three words. The message-delivery path under an intent
protocol's settlement leg is a bridge with a verifier set, and lesson five is what happens to it.

## Where the last two lessons meet

Lesson three's storage-proof design was an intent bridge the entire time: a user locks tokens in an
L2 escrow, a market maker pays them on Ethereum within thirty seconds, and a storage proof of that
payment releases the locked tokens to the market maker five to fifteen minutes
later.<Cite src="aligned-storage-proof-bridge" /> User side, an intent; solver side, a settlement gap
closed by a proof instead of by off-chain accounting, so the counterparty in the window is a verifier
rather than an attester. The authors say plainly that storage proofs alone do not fundamentally
change a traditional bridge's design; they enable a safer coordination
mechanism.<Cite src="aligned-storage-proof-bridge" /> Coordination is the right word. Every design in
this module pairs a fast leg with a slow one. What varies is who stands in the gap.

heavy-tailed — medians between roughly $74 and $200, 90th percentiles from about $223 to about $1,200

predicts.<Cite src="arxiv-intent-liquidity-exhaustion" /> **Your loss is timing, not principal — and
if your treasury process has a deadline, timing is principal.**

**(c) There is no single number, and saying so precisely is the deliverable.** Give three:

1. **Principal at risk in a fill** — ideally zero, since your funds sit in escrow or a resource lock
   until the fill is proven and the solver fronts the capital. Confirm which side holds your assets
   during the window; that is a per-protocol fact the ERC deliberately does not
   standardise.<Cite src="erc-7683" />
2. **Time at risk** — the refund or expiry period, over which your transfer can neither complete nor
   be redeployed. That is what an attack costs you, and the standard gives the protocol a field to
   quote a related figure: `estimatedDelaySeconds`.<Cite src="erc-7683" />
3. **Tail risk on the settlement leg** — the message-delivery path underneath, which the ERC's
   security section names as a risk it does not cover.<Cite src="erc-7683" /> Over a native rollup
   bridge your tail is the rollup's;<Cite src="op-docs-withdrawal-flow" /> over an externally verified
   bridge it is that verifier set, which is lesson five.

**The pattern.** The intent model removed a custody risk you could name and replaced it with an
availability risk you have to measure. Usually a good trade — and not one the moment your product
treats a cross-chain transfer as a step that always completes.

## Where this pays off

**In lesson five**, the settlement leg stops being an abstraction. Every intent protocol's
reimbursement eventually rides a message-delivery path, and the four canonical failures are all
failures of that path's verification. The ERC names adversarial changes to message-delivery paths as
an unaddressed risk;<Cite src="erc-7683" /> lesson five is the record of what it has cost.

**In any product that quotes a cross-chain transfer.** Three numbers to demand before integrating:
the settlement gap, who is capitalised across it, and what happens to your user when that capital is
committed elsewhere. None appears on a marketing page; all three are measurable.

**In how you read security research.** The protective parameter here looked like a defect. Thin
solver margins and deep idle liquidity read as inefficiency on a pitch deck and as resilience in an
attack model. When a competitive market optimises a metric, check which direction the attacker also
benefits from before calling it progress.
