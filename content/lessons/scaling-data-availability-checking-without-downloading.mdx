Put yourself on the wrong side of this for a moment. You have produced a block. You want the network
to accept it while one particular transaction stays hidden — say, the one that would let somebody
prove you took their money.

The network's defence is that nodes will sample: each asks for a few random pieces of your block and
refuses to attest if any request goes unanswered. You choose which pieces you answer. Work out
whether you win.

The block is cut into 128 pieces and the transaction you want hidden sits in exactly one of them. A
node picks 8 distinct pieces at random. The chance all 8 miss your one hidden piece is
(128 − 8) / 128 = **0.9375**. You survive that audit 93.75% of the time, and the single piece you
withheld is enough to stop anyone reconstructing the block.

Push the node's effort up hard — 30 samples, nearly a quarter of the block — and you still survive
(128 − 30) / 128 = **76.6%** of audits. Nearly four times the cost, and you are still winning.

**Sampling raw data does not work.** Not "works less well than you'd hope" — does not work. Anything
built on it is theatre.

This lesson is about the one change that turns that arithmetic completely around, what the resulting
guarantee actually says, and three places where the argument is routinely stated more strongly than
either specification supports.

## The change: make hiding a little the same as hiding a lot

Erasure-code the data before cutting it into pieces. Reed-Solomon extension takes the original
symbols and produces a longer sequence with the property that any sufficiently large subset
reconstructs the original — the redundancy is spread across every piece rather than duplicated into
some of them.

Ethereum applies this per blob. EIP-7594 extends the EIP-4844 blob using a one-dimensional erasure
coding extension: each row is the blob data combined with its erasure code, subdivided into cells,
which are the smallest units that can be authenticated against that blob's KZG
commitment.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> The constants make the rate explicit:
`FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_EXT_BLOB` is `2 * FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_BLOB`, and `CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB` is that
divided by `FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_CELL` = 64, giving 128 cells per extended
blob.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> A blob of 4,096 field
elements<Cite src="eip-4844" /> becomes 8,192, in 128 cells, of which any 64 suffice.

The consequence is stated directly in the spec: in reconstruction, a node can reconstruct all the
blobs from 50% of the columns.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> A node obtaining 50% or
more of all columns SHOULD reconstruct the full data matrix via
`recover_matrix`.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />

Now redo the attacker's arithmetic, because the target has changed shape entirely.

**Subgoal 1 — establish what the withholder must now achieve, since "hide one transaction" is no
longer a thing that exists.** Every cell of the extended blob is a function of the whole blob. There
is no cell that contains "that transaction" and no cell that does not. To keep any part of the
original unrecoverable, the withholder must prevent the network from assembling *any* 64 of the 128
columns — so at most 63 columns may be served, and at least 65 must be refused to
everyone.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />

**Subgoal 2 — compute the per-sample odds, because that is the quantity the attacker cannot
improve.** With at most 63 of 128 columns answerable, a single uniformly random request has at most a
63/128 ≈ 0.492 chance of landing on one the withholder will serve. Better than a coin flip for the
sampler, on every single draw, and no choice the attacker makes moves it — serving more columns
starts making the block recoverable.

**Subgoal 3 — compound it across a node's sample set, which is where the asymmetry appears.** Eight
distinct columns, all of them landing inside a 63-column answerable set, has probability
C(63, 8) / C(128, 8) ≈ 0.0027 — about **1 in 370**. Sixteen samples take it to roughly 1 in 250,000.
Thirty samples, the same effort that bought a 76.6% attacker success rate against unencoded data,
now leaves about 1 chance in 1.8 × 10¹¹.

**Subgoal 4 — read off the design implication rather than the number.** The attacker's success went
from 93.75% to 0.27% at *identical* sampler cost. Nothing about the node's work changed. The
redundancy changed what withholding has to look like, and a withholding large enough to matter is a
withholding large enough to be caught.

That is the entire idea. [Erasure coding](/c/erasure-extension) is not a storage optimisation bolted
onto [sampling](/c/data-availability-sampling) — it is the thing that makes sampling mean anything.

## Three places the argument is weaker than it sounds

The clean (1/2)ᵏ story is the one that circulates. Here is where it overstates.

### It is a bound on fooling one node, not on fooling the network

The computation above answers "will this node be deceived". That is not the security question. The
security question is whether an attacker can convince *enough* of the network while keeping the data
unrecoverable, and that is a harder combinatorial problem because the attacker chooses which nodes to
satisfy.

EIP-7594 states the real bound. With n sampling nodes, m total possible samples and k samples per
node, it bounds the probability of convincing a fraction ε of nodes that withheld data is available
as a product of three terms: the number of ways to choose the nε nodes to be tricked, the number of
ways to choose a maximally large sample subset to serve without allowing reconstruction, and the
probability that all chosen nodes' queries fall inside that subset.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

The table the EIP publishes for mainnet parameters at 10,000 nodes is the honest part, and it is
worth reading rather than summarising. At ε = 0.01 — one percent, a hundred nodes — the upper bound
on attack success is **1**.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> Not negligible. Unbounded by this analysis. At
ε = 0.02 it drops to 10⁻²⁰·⁰⁴, and by ε = 0.05 to 10⁻³⁰⁶·³⁴, which is how the EIP concludes PeerDAS is
secure against withholding.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

So the precise claim is: *briefly fooling a small minority of nodes is not ruled out; fooling a
meaningful fraction is astronomically unlikely.* That is a strong result and it is not the same
sentence as "no node can be fooled". It also explains a design choice you already know — a node's
availability decision is [made against its own samples](/c/is-data-available) and feeds its own vote,
so the protocol only ever needed the fraction-level guarantee.

### It assumes the encoding is honest, and the two networks buy that differently

Every step above assumed the extension is a correct Reed-Solomon extension of the real data. A
producer who encodes incorrectly has a block where the pieces look fine individually and reconstruct
to nothing, which defeats sampling completely.

Celestia's answer is fraud proofs, and its FAQ is explicit about the assumption chain that requires:
blocks are erasure coded, malicious encoders could extend data incorrectly, bridge nodes need the
full block to produce fraud proofs of bad encoding, and if only light nodes receive data and bridge
nodes cannot reconstruct the block, the network could miss invalid
encoding.<Cite src="celestia-da-faq" /> Their stated DAS security assumptions follow from that:
enough light nodes must sample for the given block size that an honest bridge node can reconstruct
from their collected shares, light nodes must connect to at least one honest bridge node to receive
those fraud proofs, and eclipse attacks can break that
assumption.<Cite src="celestia-da-faq" />

**Ethereum does not inherit any of that, and the reason is structural rather than a matter of
degree.** A [cell carries a KZG proof](/c/cell-kzg-proof) verifying it against the blob's commitment,
so a cell is the smallest unit that can be authenticated with its blob's KZG
commitment.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> An incorrectly extended cell fails that check on arrival. There is
no bad-encoding fraud proof in PeerDAS because there is no bad encoding to detect: the commitment
scheme rules it out pointwise, so no honest-bridge-node assumption, no fraud-proof propagation
window, and no eclipse-attack exposure on that path.

Carry that as a general habit. Two systems described identically as "erasure coding plus sampling"
can have entirely different trust dependencies, because the mechanism binding pieces to the original
differs. Avail is a third point on the axis: L2BEAT describes it as combining erasure coding, KZG
commitments and DAS, and records in the same risk row that its block reconstruction protocol is still
under development.<Cite src="l2beat-avail-vector" /> Same ingredients, different maturity in the part
that actually recovers the data.

### It proves reconstructability, not possession — and reconstruction has to happen

Sampling says the data *could* be assembled. Something still has to assemble it. In PeerDAS that is
the 50%-of-columns rule, and the spec makes reconstruction deliberately unsynchronised: nodes MAY
delay it to let other columns arrive, and MAY use a random delay to desynchronise across nodes and
reduce overall CPU load.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> Once a node reconstructs a
column it MUST expose it as though it had received it over the
network<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> — and that re-seeding is what turns a scattered
sampling result back into reachable data.

The whole thing also runs against a clock. An honest node downloads
`sampling_size = max(SAMPLES_PER_SLOT, custody_group_count)` custody groups per slot, with
`SAMPLES_PER_SLOT` = 8, and sampling counts as successful only if it retrieves *all* selected
columns.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> All of them, inside a slot, in time to attest —
which is why [the timing bound](/c/das-timebound) belongs to the security argument rather than to
implementation.

<Check type="predict" concept="erasure-coding-for-da">
A team is designing DAS for their own chain and proposes: split each block into 128 pieces, commit to
each with a Merkle root, have light nodes sample 30 pieces at random, and treat 30 successes as proof
of availability. No erasure coding — "the Merkle proofs already stop anyone serving fake data, and 30
out of 128 is a quarter of the block".

Predict what an adversary does, and state the minimum change that fixes it.

<Answer>
**The adversary withholds one piece and wins most audits.** A sampler drawing 30 distinct pieces from
128 misses any specific piece with probability (128 − 30) / 128 = 76.6%. So roughly three of every
four light nodes see 30 clean responses and attest, while nobody can reconstruct the block, because
without erasure coding one missing piece is one missing piece.

**The Merkle roots do not help, and identifying why is the whole finding.** Commitments constrain
*what* is served, not *whether* it is served. Every piece the adversary does return verifies
perfectly. The attack is not forgery, it is silence, and an inclusion proof has nothing to say about
a request that went unanswered.

**Raising the sample count is not a fix.** It is linear pressure on an attacker whose cost is
constant. Sixty samples — half the block, at which point light-node sampling has no point left —
still leaves (128 − 60) / 128 = 53% attacker success.

**The minimum change: erasure-code first, then sample.** Extend each block to twice its length so any
half reconstructs the original.<Cite src="eip-7594" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /> The
adversary can now only withhold something meaningful by refusing more than half the pieces, which
makes every individual sample better than a coin flip for the sampler. At 8 samples that is about 1
in 370; at their proposed 30 it is about 1 in 1.8 × 10¹¹. Same node cost, and the security parameter
becomes the sample count instead of the block size.

**Three things they must specify next, none optional.**

*How a piece is bound to the original.* If it is a plain Merkle root over the extended data, a
producer can extend incorrectly and every piece still verifies — and then the design needs
reconstruction-plus-fraud-proofs, which brings an honest-bridge-node assumption and eclipse-attack
exposure with it.<Cite src="celestia-da-faq" /> Per-piece proofs against a polynomial commitment
avoid the entire class, because an incorrect extension fails on arrival.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

*Who reconstructs, and when.* Sampling establishes the data could be assembled, not that it was. The
rule needs a threshold, a delay policy, and an obligation to re-expose what was
recovered.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />

*What fraction of the network must be convinced.* Their scheme's security is not "P(one node
fooled)". The bound that matters is combinatorial in the node count, the sample space and the target
fraction — and even a correct design leaves the smallest fractions unprotected, which is why
mainnet's published bound is 1 at ε = 0.01 and collapses only from ε = 0.02
onward.<Cite src="eip-7594" />
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

You now have the mechanism that makes a DA guarantee affordable, and the vocabulary to say what it
does and does not establish. Two payoffs.

**Comparing DA layers on their sampling story becomes possible.** Not "does it have DAS" — all of
them do — but what binds a piece to the original, whether bad encoding needs a fraud proof, who
reconstructs, and whether that reconstruction protocol has
shipped.<Cite src="celestia-da-101" /><Cite src="l2beat-avail-vector" /> Those four questions
separate designs the marketing describes identically, and they are the substance behind the risk-row
labels the next lesson uses.

**The withholding attack becomes legible as what this defends against.** Lesson five is a chain that
never signed up for the defence at all — data goes to a committee, sampling does not exist, and the
failure this lesson's arithmetic rules out is simply available as a business decision.
