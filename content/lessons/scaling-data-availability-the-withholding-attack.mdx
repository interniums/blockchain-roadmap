Nothing goes wrong. That is the shape of this failure and the reason it is hard to argue about.

A validity-proving chain publishes its data off-chain. On Tuesday its operator stops answering the
data endpoint. The proving pipeline keeps running. State updates keep arriving on L1, each carrying a
valid proof, each verifying. A monitoring dashboard watching the L1 contracts sees a chain in perfect
health: proofs accepted, roots advancing, no reverts, no anomalies, no alerts.

Meanwhile nobody outside the operator can say what any account's balance is.

Then the operator stops proposing too — or does not, it makes little difference. Users go to the
escape hatch that was built for exactly this moment. The contracts work. Every function behaves as
written. And the users cannot use them, because the first step of the exit requires a piece of data
that the operator is under no obligation to hand over, and has not.

You know [rollup versus validium](/c/rollup-vs-validium) and you know an [escape hatch](/c/escape-hatch)
is meant to be the guarantee of last resort. This lesson works out precisely where the exit breaks,
why no contract can be written to detect the breakage, and what that implies for how you weigh a
chain's two guarantees against each other.

## Why a withdrawal needs the data

Start from what withdrawing actually is, because the constraint falls out of the mechanism rather
than from anything about DA.

An L1 contract holds the deposits and knows one 32-byte commitment to the L2's state. To pay you, it
must be convinced that a particular leaf — your account, with your balance — is in the tree under
that root. You supply the leaf and the sibling path, it hashes upward, and it compares. This is the
[Merkle inclusion proof](/c/merkle-inclusion-proof) you built by hand in the fundamentals track, and
the L1 side of every rollup withdrawal is that comparison.

StarkEx documents the escape path in a validium in four steps, and step one is the whole lesson: the
user obtains a Merkle path of the position to be evicted with respect to the frozen position tree
root — and, in the documentation's own words, once the application is frozen such data should be made
public or obtainable from an application API, **depending on the application's data availability
approach**.<Cite src="starkex-escape-frozen-contract" /> With the path in hand the user calls
`verifyEscape`, which registers a fact keyed on the position and the root; then `escape`, which
credits the position balance to an on-chain pending withdrawals account; then the ordinary withdrawal
function moves the funds.<Cite src="starkex-escape-frozen-contract" />

Three of those four steps are pure contract calls that cannot fail for want of information. Step one
is not a contract call at all. It is a request to whoever has the data.

**So the exit is data-gated, and the gate is upstream of every line of Solidity anyone can audit.**

<Aside kind="note">
The rest of the machinery is unusually well built, which is why this is a good example rather than a
strawman. A user first sends a forced request on-chain; if the operator does not serve it within a
predefined grace period the user calls `freezeRequest`, which any user may call, and the exchange
becomes frozen and can accept no further state
updates.<Cite src="starkex-forced-operations" /><Cite src="starkex-freezing-the-operator" /> The
forced-withdrawal entry point is deliberately expensive — StarkEx notes `fullWithdrawalRequest` costs
more than 1M gas specifically to prevent a flood of requests being used as a denial-of-service
attack.<Cite src="starkex-forced-operations" /> Anti-censorship, anti-DoS, permissionless freeze.
Every part of it correct, and all of it downstream of a data request.
</Aside>

### Why you cannot pre-compute your way out

The obvious defence is to keep your own proof. Ask for your Merkle path every day while things are
calm, store it, and produce it if the chain freezes.

Work out whether that survives contact with the mechanism.

**Subgoal 1 — establish what the proof is bound to.** A Merkle path is not a property of your leaf.
It is the sequence of sibling hashes on the route from your leaf to one specific root. It authenticates
your leaf *against that root* and against no other.

**Subgoal 2 — establish how often the root changes, because that is the expiry rate of your stored
copy.** Every batch that touches any account produces a new root. Not your account — *any* account.
On a chain with users, that is constantly.

**Subgoal 3 — check whether your old path is still usable.** Only if none of your siblings changed,
which requires that nothing in your subtree neighbourhood moved. On a busy tree this is close to
never, and you cannot know which siblings you share without the data you are trying to substitute
for.

**Subgoal 4 — check which root the escape runs against, because that decides whether staleness is
fatal.** The escape is verified with respect to the *frozen* root, and a frozen exchange accepts no
further state updates<Cite src="starkex-freezing-the-operator" /> — so the target is the last root
the operator committed before the freeze. Your stored path matches it only if the chain happened to
stop at exactly the state you last downloaded.

**Subgoal 5 — state the conclusion honestly.** Self-archiving your own path is a lottery ticket on the
freeze happening at a particular instant. What *does* work is archiving enough of the chain's data to
recompute the tree at any root — which is to say, being your own DA layer. That is available to a
sophisticated institution and to essentially no retail user, and it is only available at all while
the operator is still serving.

## Why nothing can detect it

This is the property that makes withholding a different category of event rather than a worse one.

Consider what an L1 contract could be asked to check. It can verify a proof — it does, and the proof
is valid. It can check signatures — they are present. It can run a timeout — but a timeout on *what*?
The operator produced valid state updates on schedule. Nothing was late, nothing was malformed,
nothing violated a rule.

**No misbehaviour occurred in the sense any on-chain mechanism can express.** A fraud proof answers
"this state root does not follow from this data". Here the root does follow from the data — that is
what the validity proof established. The disputed claim is "the data was published", and that claim
is not about the contents of any transaction. It is about the absence of an event on a network the L1
cannot observe.

Every row but the last has an on-chain representation. The last one is a non-event, and contracts
cannot be written against non-events on networks they cannot see. This is exactly why L2BEAT sorts
chains on where the data goes rather than on proof type: withholding is not a stronger version of a
proof failure, it is a failure with no L1 vocabulary at all.<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" />

And it is why the same failure cannot happen on a rollup. On Ethereum, publication is a validity
condition of the L1 block itself — full nodes download the data, a block is valid only if all data is
available and passes the availability check, so the canonical chain cannot contain unavailable data
commitments.<Cite src="l2beat-ethereum-da" /> A rollup operator who wants to withhold has to persuade
Ethereum's validators to accept a block they will refuse. There is no equivalent move.

## The weaker link, argued rather than asserted

"A chain's security is the minimum of its proof system and its DA layer" is a slogan. Derive it, so
you can tell when it applies.

**Subgoal 1 — write down what each guarantee is necessary for.** Validity is necessary for "no
illegitimate state change". Availability is necessary for "I can determine my state and exit". A
product needs both; a user who has only one of them has no usable position.

**Subgoal 2 — ask what an attacker must defeat.** Not both. Breaking validity produces a wrong state
you cannot appeal. Breaking availability produces a correct state you cannot reach. Either one, alone,
ends with the user unable to act on their funds.

**Subgoal 3 — combine.** The attacker's cost is the *cheaper* of the two attacks, so the defender's
security is the *weaker* of the two guarantees. There is no configuration in which a strong proof
system compensates for a weak DA arrangement, because they are not substitutes — they are conjuncts.

**Subgoal 4 — extend to the third axis, because two is still an under-count.** A valid proof system
sitting under an upgrade key that can replace the verifier is bounded by that key, which is what
L2BEAT's stage framework measures and why chains with working validity proofs can still sit at the
lowest stage.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" /> Three conjuncts, one minimum.

**Subgoal 5 — turn it into a reading habit.** When a chain markets one guarantee loudly, that is the
prompt to go and check the other two. Marketing volume correlates with strength on the axis being
marketed and tells you nothing about the rest — and the omission is where the information is.

## What actually reduces the risk

Four moves, in order of how much they change the failure rather than how good they sound.

**A DA bridge that can force publication changes the category.** The OP Stack's alt-DA mode is the
clearest example: a permissionless challenge contract on L1 lets any party challenge an input
commitment, which forces providers to submit the input data on-chain to prove it is available within
the resolve window, and if the challenge goes unresolved chain derivation must be reset, omitting the
data and causing a reorg.<Cite src="op-specs-alt-da" /> That converts withholding from a non-event
into an on-chain event with a deadline and a consequence. The bond is sized so challenging costs at
least as much as resolving, and the challenger's reward is deliberately not net positive, so this is
a mechanism for altruistic fishermen and for users willing to pay for on-chain
availability<Cite src="op-specs-alt-da" /> — a real defence with a named funder, not a free one.

**Committee composition, if it is a committee.** Threshold, member count, and genuine independence:
distinct organisations, distinct key custody, distinct infrastructure. StarkEx is explicit that a
state update is accepted only if a quorum of DAC members signs, and equally explicit that using a DAC
requires trust in the committee members.<Cite src="starkex-data-availability" /> The parameter that
matters is the number of *independent* failure domains, not the number of names.

**Per-position choice, where the design offers it.** Volition lets a user choose on-chain or
off-chain data per asset, and StarkEx implements it as two vault ranges with transfers between
them.<Cite src="starkex-data-availability" /><Cite src="l2beat-glossary" /> A product can put
operational float in the cheap range and user principal in the expensive one, which is a real
engineering answer rather than a disclosure.

**Honest disclosure, which is not a mitigation but is the thing you owe users.** One sentence naming
who would have to misbehave, and what happens if they do. If the sentence is hard to write, the
difficulty is the finding.

<Aside kind="warn">
The move that does **not** work is monitoring the L1 contracts. The whole point of this failure is
that L1 looks healthy throughout. If you operate on a chain with off-chain DA, your availability
monitor has to be an independent client that fetches the data and reconstructs state on its own
schedule — and it must alert on *fetch failure*, which every well-behaved system treats as a
transient error to retry. Retrying quietly is exactly the wrong behaviour here.
</Aside>

<Check type="explain" concept="validium-withholding-risk">
Your company custodies user funds on a validium. Its DAC has seven members and a five-signature
threshold; the chain's contracts include a forced-withdrawal path, a permissionless freeze, and an
escape function verified against the frozen state root. Legal asks you to write the risk disclosure.

A colleague argues no disclosure is needed: "Five of seven independent members would have to collude,
and even then the escape hatch is permissionless — anyone can freeze the chain and exit."

Say what is wrong with that argument, and what the disclosure has to say.

<Answer>
**The collusion threshold is the wrong number, because the attack does not require collusion.** Five
signatures are needed to get a state update *accepted*. Nothing about that threshold obliges anyone to
*serve* the data afterwards. A DAC member signs that it knows the balances determining the new
state<Cite src="starkex-data-availability" />; serving those balances to a stranger on request is a
separate act with no on-chain enforcement. The failure needs unavailability, not a forged attestation,
and unavailability can arrive through indifference, an outage, a legal instruction, or an operator
that simply stops.

**The escape hatch does not close the gap, and tracing it shows exactly where it stops.** Forced
request, grace period, permissionless `freezeRequest`, exchange frozen and accepting no further state
updates<Cite src="starkex-forced-operations" /><Cite src="starkex-freezing-the-operator" /> — all of
that works with no cooperation from anyone. Then the escape requires the user to obtain a Merkle path
for their position with respect to the frozen root, and the documentation says outright that where
such data comes from depends on the application's data availability
approach.<Cite src="starkex-escape-frozen-contract" /> If the DA approach is "the operator's API",
and the API is the thing that stopped, the permissionless part of the mechanism has nothing to
operate on. `verifyEscape` will reject anything that is not a valid path, and a valid path cannot be
manufactured.

**And users cannot self-insure.** Storing your own Merkle path in advance does not help: the path
authenticates a leaf against one specific root, and the root changes whenever any account changes.
The escape runs against the frozen root, so a stored path is only useful if the chain froze at the
exact state you last downloaded.<Cite src="starkex-freezing-the-operator" /> Reconstructing the tree
instead means holding the chain's whole history, which is being your own DA layer.

**Why nothing will alert you.** There is no L1 event. Proofs verify, roots advance, contracts behave
correctly, and no protocol rule is broken — which is precisely why L2BEAT declines to call such a
chain a rollup and files withholding as a distinct category of
risk.<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" /><Cite src="l2beat-da-risk" /> Contrast a chain posting to blobs,
where publication is a validity condition of the L1 block itself and the canonical chain therefore
cannot carry unavailable commitments.<Cite src="l2beat-ethereum-da" />

**What the disclosure must contain.** Three sentences, in user language.

*Where the data lives, and who is obliged to serve it.* "The information needed to compute your
balance is held by a committee of seven named parties rather than published on Ethereum."

*The consequence, stated as the user's outcome.* "If those parties stop making that information
available, your funds cannot be withdrawn — including through the emergency exit — even though every
transaction remains provably correct and the contracts continue to function."

*What the company will do about it.* Whatever is actually true. Running your own archiving client
that fetches and reconstructs state independently and alerts on fetch failure is the one control that
materially changes the outcome, because it converts an invisible failure into a paged incident while
the data is still obtainable.

**Two follow-ups for the protocol team, not the disclosure.** Is there a DA bridge, and can it force
publication — a challenge mechanism turns withholding from a non-event into a resolvable on-chain
event with a deadline.<Cite src="op-specs-alt-da" /> And is there a volition mode, so user principal
can sit in the on-chain range while operational balances take the cheap
path.<Cite src="starkex-data-availability" />

**The one-line answer to the colleague.** The threshold protects against a lie. Nothing here protects
against a silence, and silence is the attack.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

This is the end of the module, so the payoff is a way of reading rather than a next mechanism.

- **Two sentences per chain, never one.** What is proven, and by whom. What is published, and
  enforced by whom. Then the third: who can upgrade the contracts underneath both.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />
- **The freeze question as the shortest useful test.** "Who would have to misbehave for a user to be
  unable to withdraw?" On a rollup the answer is contorted. On a validium it is one sentence, and the
  answer names people rather than protocol rules.
- **The absence-shaped risk, generalised.** This lesson's real subject is a failure that produces no
  event. You have met the pattern before in [oracles as a trust boundary](/c/oracle-manipulation) and
  in liveness failures that no finite observation ever proves. The habit transfers: when a system's
  safety rests on someone *doing* something rather than on someone *not* breaking a rule, ask what
  observable signal exists — and if there is none, build the observation yourself or accept the
  assumption explicitly.
- **The reflection prompt** asks you to write the rollup-versus-validium disclosure and test it on a
  non-technical reader. The test is whether they can say who would have to misbehave for their funds
  to become unreachable. If they cannot, the paragraph is describing proofs.

One last thing to check on any chain you actually depend on: open its DA row, find whether a bridge
exists, and if the answer is none, note that Ethereum has no proof of data availability for that
project at all.<Cite src="l2beat-celestia-no-bridge" /> That is not a criticism of a design. It is a
fact about what your L1 contracts can and cannot know, and it belongs in your risk register in those
words.
