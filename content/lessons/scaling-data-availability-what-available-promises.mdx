An L1 contract accepts a 32-byte word. The transaction succeeds, the event fires, your indexer
records a new state root for chain X at L1 block N. Nothing reverted.

Now answer two questions about the chain that word describes.

**Could you compute that root yourself?** Not "would you bother" — could you, in principle, from
public data, starting from the previous root and applying the transactions in between?

**Is that root the right one?** Not "did the contract accept it" — is it what an honest execution of
those transactions actually produces?

These are different questions with different answers, secured by different machinery, and they fail
in ways that look nothing alike. The first is data availability. The second is validity. The habit
this module exists to break is the one that files both under "rollup security" and then reasons about
the total as though it were a single dial.

You already know what a [rollup](/c/rollup) is and what a [state root](/c/state-root) commits to.
This lesson makes the first question precise — what "available" promises, at what moment, enforced by
whom — and then shows exactly how far the answer to it travels toward the second. The distance is
zero. That is the whole point.

## What the word actually names

L2BEAT's glossary states it in one sentence worth reading slowly: data availability is the property
of a rollup's data being reachable by any node retrieving the data that were rolled up and executed
to reach the proposed state.<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" /> Three load-bearing words in there.

**"Any node."** Not the operator, not a partner, not a subscriber to an API. The guarantee is about
an arbitrary party with no relationship to the chain. L2BEAT's phrasing elsewhere in the same entry
sharpens it: a rollup must choose a DA layer that guarantees any node can retrieve this data
permissionlessly under any circumstance.<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" />

**"The data that were rolled up."** Not a summary, not the state, not a proof. The inputs — enough of
them that an outsider can redo the computation. Celestia's docs frame the same property as a question
about publication: has this block's data been published, and can it be
downloaded?<Cite src="celestia-da-101" />

**"To reach the proposed state."** The availability guarantee is scoped to a specific commitment. It
attaches to the batch behind the root at L1 block N, not to the chain in general.

Two things follow immediately, and both are usually missed.

**Availability is temporal.** It is a claim about a moment — the moment the commitment was made. What
happens to those bytes afterwards is a different property with a different security argument, and the
[next lesson](/c/da-is-not-storage) is about the gap between them. Celestia's own FAQ separates them
outright: data availability is about new blocks being publishable and verifiable, while data storage
concerns keeping and retrieving historical data under a 1-of-N honesty
assumption.<Cite src="celestia-da-faq" />

**Availability is not enforced by the rollup.** This is the part that reorganises how you read a risk
page. The rollup's contracts do not check that data was published; they cannot, because they cannot
see it. Whatever consensus set is responsible for the data is what enforces the guarantee. On
Ethereum that is the validator set, and L2BEAT describes the mechanism precisely: full nodes download
the full data for both execution-layer transactions and consensus-layer blobs, and for a block to be
considered valid all data must be available and pass the `is_data_available()` check, so from the
rollup's perspective Ethereum's canonical chain cannot contain unavailable data
commitments.<Cite src="l2beat-ethereum-da" />

That is why L2BEAT labels Ethereum's DA bridge "Enshrined" — unlike a non-enshrined bridge, it places
no honesty assumption on an external committee providing availability
attestations.<Cite src="l2beat-ethereum-da" /> There is no bridge because there is nothing to bridge
from. The chain that settles your rollup is the same chain that saw the data.

<Aside kind="note">
You met [`is_data_available`](/c/is-data-available) in the protocol track as a fork-choice
gate — a node refuses to consider a block head until its own custodied columns and samples come back.
Read it again with this lesson's framing: that function is the entire enforcement mechanism for
Ethereum's DA guarantee, and it runs inside every node independently. Nobody attests availability to
anybody. Each node decides for itself and votes accordingly.<Cite src="l2beat-ethereum-da" />
</Aside>

## The two guarantees, drawn apart

Here is the framing to carry out of this module. Two independent axes, four quadrants, and every one
of them is occupied by real systems.

| | **State proven** | **State not proven** |
|---|---|---|
| **Data available** | A rollup with a live proof system | Data published, roots asserted by a whitelisted proposer |
| **Data unavailable** | A validium: valid proof, withheld data | An optimium with a silent committee |

Read across the top row first. Both cells publish everything. They differ in whether anything checks
the result. L2BEAT's stage framework exists precisely because the second cell is common: a chain can
post complete transaction data to Ethereum and still have its state roots accepted on the strength of
a permissioned proposer and an upgrade key, which is what the stage classification measures rather
than the DA choice.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />

Now read down the left column. Both cells have a working proof system. They differ in whether anyone
outside the operator can see the inputs. StarkEx's documentation makes the contrast explicit inside a
single product: in ZK-Rollup mode the Cairo program ensures all data required to recover the balances
Merkle tree is published on-chain, and the stated advantage is that all the data needed to reclaim a
user's funds on an escape event is present and visible on the
blockchain.<Cite src="starkex-data-availability" /> In validium mode the same proofs run over
balances that are not published on-chain at all, and the stated disadvantage is one line long: using
a committee requires trust in the committee members.<Cite src="starkex-data-availability" />

Same proof system. Same contracts. Different quadrant.

### Why the confusion is structural rather than careless

Availability is a *precondition* for one particular way of checking validity, and that adjacency is
what fuses them in people's heads.

For an [optimistic rollup](/c/optimistic-rollup), the connection is real and tight. The challenge
mechanism only works if a challenger can reconstruct the disputed execution, which requires the
inputs. EIP-4844 says so directly in its rationale: rollups need data to be available once, long
enough to ensure honest actors can construct the rollup state, but not
forever.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Withhold the data and there is no challenger, so the proof system
degrades to an assertion.

For a [validity rollup](/c/validity-rollup), the connection is looser and this is where intuition
breaks. The proof verifies without the data. The verifier contract takes a proof and public inputs
and returns true or false; it does not need the batch. So a validity chain can hold its state
provably correct while nobody outside the operator can say what that state *is*. Correct and
unknowable at the same time is not a contradiction. It is a product configuration, and it has a name,
and the last lesson in this module is about what it does to your users.

The general statement: availability buys you the ability to check, to reconstruct, and to exit.
Proofs buy you the assurance that a specific transition was legal. Neither implies the other, and a
chain's operational security is the [weaker of the two](/c/security-is-the-weaker-link) rather than
the louder of the two.

## Worked example: what an accepted state root entitles you to conclude

Go back to the opening scene. A contract at L1 block N accepted root R for chain X. Derive what you
know, one step at a time, naming what each step is for.

**Subgoal 1 — establish what the contract actually verified, because that bounds everything
downstream.** Read the accepting function. If it verifies a proof, you know that *given* some
committed inputs, R follows from the previous root by the rules the circuit encodes. If it merely
records R subject to a later challenge, you know only that a proposer asserted it and a window is
open. If it checks a signature threshold, you know that some set of keys signed. Three very different
starting points, and the transaction receipt looks identical in all three.

**Subgoal 2 — establish where the committed inputs live, because that is the availability question
and nothing in the receipt answers it.** The contract references the inputs by commitment: a
versioned hash for a [blob](/c/blob), a keccak hash of a payload, or a
DA-layer-specific commitment. The OP Stack's alt-DA specification shows the shape — commitments are
encoded as a type byte followed by the commitment bytes, where type 0 is `keccak256(tx_payload)` and
type 1 is a DA-service commitment carrying its own DA-layer byte.<Cite src="op-specs-alt-da" /> The
commitment is present on L1 in every case. The *data* it commits to may or may not be.

**Subgoal 3 — establish who enforced publication, because that names the trust assumption you have
just inherited.** If the commitment is a blob versioned hash, publication was enforced by Ethereum's
own validators through the availability check every node runs
independently.<Cite src="l2beat-ethereum-da" /> If it is an external DA layer's commitment,
publication was enforced by that network's validator set or committee, and L2BEAT's DA risk framework
scores exactly that: economic security and fraud detection for the layer, plus committee security,
upgradeability and relayer failure for the bridge that reports it to
Ethereum.<Cite src="l2beat-da-risk" /> If there is no bridge at all, Ethereum has no proof of data
availability for the project whatsoever.<Cite src="l2beat-celestia-no-bridge" />

**Subgoal 4 — combine, and refuse to combine further than the evidence allows.** With a proof and
blob data you can say: this transition is legal and I can reconstruct it. With a proof and no
published data you can say: this transition is legal, and I cannot tell you what state it produced.
With published data and no live proof system you can say: I can check it myself if I choose to, and
nothing has. Those are three different sentences and they are the honest outputs of this analysis.

**Subgoal 5 — convert each into the user-facing failure it implies.** Legal-but-unknowable means a
user cannot construct a withdrawal. Checkable-but-unchecked means a bad root can be finalised if
nobody watches and the window closes. Neither of those is "less secure by some percentage". They are
specific, nameable operational failures with different first responders.

<Aside kind="warn">
Subgoal 1 is the one people skip. "The L1 contract accepted it" gets treated as a single kind of
event, when the accepting function is doing one of at least three completely different jobs. Note in
particular that a signature-threshold check — the DAC pattern you meet in lesson four — produces the
same successful receipt as a proof verification does.<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" />
</Aside>

## Where the analogy to L1 stops

It is tempting to reason from L1 experience: on Ethereum, if a block is in the canonical chain, its
data is there and its transitions are valid, so why separate them at all?

Because on L1 those two properties are produced by the *same* act. A validator that attests to a block
has executed it and has checked its data availability; the two conditions gate the same
vote.<Cite src="l2beat-ethereum-da" /> There is no configuration in which one holds and the other
does not, so no language distinguishing them was ever needed.

A rollup breaks that coupling deliberately. Execution moves off-chain, and the two guarantees are
re-established by two mechanisms chosen independently: a proof system, and a DA arrangement. Because
they are chosen independently they can be strong and weak in any combination — and because the
vocabulary is inherited from L1, they get discussed as though they still travel together. They do
not, and the question that sorts them turns out to be
[a single question about where the data goes](/c/rollup-vs-validium).

A chain's documentation says: "Every state root is accompanied by a zero-knowledge validity proof
verified on Ethereum. No fraud proofs, no challenge window, no trust in the operator." The verifier
contract is deployed, the proofs verify, and the L1 transactions are there to read.

A prospective integrator asks whether their users' funds could become inaccessible. Say what you
still need to know, and why the proof does not answer the question.

<Answer>
**The proof answers the second question from the top of this lesson and says nothing about the
first.** It establishes that each state root follows from its predecessor under the circuit's rules.
It does not establish that anyone other than the operator can determine what that state contains.

**What to ask, in order.**

*Where do the committed inputs go?* The verifier takes a proof and public inputs; it does not take
the batch. So the batch is committed by hash somewhere and stored somewhere, and the documentation
quoted says nothing about which. Under L2BEAT's definition, if that data is stored somewhere other
than a permissionless L1, the project is not a rollup but a validium or an
optimium.<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" />

*Who enforced publication?* If the answer is blobs, publication was enforced by Ethereum's own
validators, each of which independently refuses a block whose data it cannot
sample.<Cite src="l2beat-ethereum-da" /> If the answer is a committee or an external network, the
enforcing party is that committee or network, and L2BEAT scores it on economic security and fraud
detection separately from the bridge that reports it to
Ethereum.<Cite src="l2beat-da-risk" /><Cite src="l2beat-da-summary" />

*Is there a bridge at all?* Without a DA bridge, Ethereum has no proof of data availability for the
project — the L1 contracts accept the operator's word that publication
happened.<Cite src="l2beat-celestia-no-bridge" />

**Why the proof cannot cover this.** A withdrawal is a claim about a leaf in the chain's state, and
proving that claim on L1 requires the leaf and its path. StarkEx's own escape procedure begins with
the user obtaining a Merkle path with respect to the frozen state root, and notes that where such
data comes from depends on the application's data availability
approach.<Cite src="starkex-escape-frozen-contract" /> A validity proof over the transition does not
hand you that path. Nothing about the proof being valid produces the bytes you need.

**The answer to give the integrator.** "Funds cannot be moved illegitimately" and "funds cannot become
inaccessible" are different guarantees. The proof addresses the first. The second depends on the DA
arrangement, which the quoted paragraph does not mention — and the omission is the finding, because
a chain publishing to blobs generally says so, since it is the stronger claim.

**One more thing worth checking even after DA is settled.** A verified proof constrains execution and
not governance: [a valid proof system sits underneath an upgrade key](/c/proofs-do-not-imply-trustless),
and L2BEAT's stage framework is where that second axis gets scored.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />
Three axes, three questions, no averaging.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

You now have the axis. The rest of the module walks it.

- **Availability is not storage.** The next lesson takes the temporal claim seriously: Ethereum
  commits to serving blob data for a bounded window chosen to outlast proof and challenge deadlines,
  not to archive it<Cite src="eip-4844" />, and what happens after that window has a materially
  weaker security argument.
- **Sampling is what makes the guarantee affordable.** Lesson three works the probability that makes
  a light node's handful of requests into a real statement about a whole block, and is careful about
  which version of that argument is the one the specification actually makes.
- **One question sorts every chain.** Lesson four turns "where does the data go" into L2BEAT's
  taxonomy and into the concrete machinery — committees, bridges, and the OP Stack's alt-DA
  challenge contract.<Cite src="op-specs-alt-da" />
- **Then the failure itself.** Lesson five is the case in the bottom-left quadrant of that table: a
  valid proof over data nobody has, where every contract behaves exactly as designed and no user can
  leave.

The habit to start now, before any of that: whenever you read a chain's page, write down two
sentences rather than one. What is proven, and by whom. What is published, and enforced by whom. If
the second sentence is hard to write, you have found something.
