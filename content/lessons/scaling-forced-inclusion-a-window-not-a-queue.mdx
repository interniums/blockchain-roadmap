Someone trained on the previous two lessons opens an OP Stack chain's L1 contracts during an incident
and looks for the forcing function. It is not there. There is no `forceInclusion`, no delayed-message
cursor, no permissionless call that advances anything.

The reflex is to conclude the chain has no escape hatch. The opposite is true: it has one that nobody
has to invoke, because the obligation lives in the derivation rules rather than in a contract. Making
that concrete is worth doing carefully, because "you do not have to call anything" is a stronger
guarantee than "anyone may call this" and it is delivered by a completely different kind of argument.

## Epochs, origins, and a window measured in L1 blocks

Three definitions from the specification, and the mechanism falls out of them.

Every L1 block following chain inception maps to a **sequencing epoch** containing at least one L2
block. Each L2 block belongs to exactly one epoch, and the L1 block it maps to is its **L1
origin**.<Cite src="op-specs-derivation" /> To derive the L2 blocks of epoch `E` you need the L1
blocks in the range `[E, E + SWS)` — the **sequencing window**, of size `SWS` — plus the batcher
transactions inside that range, the deposits made in the L1 origin, and the L1 attributes of the
origin.<Cite src="op-specs-derivation" />

Note what "deposits made in the L1 origin" means. Your forced transaction does not join a queue. It
becomes an input to exactly one epoch: the one whose origin is the L1 block your deposit landed in.
User-deposited transactions appear only in the first L2 block of that epoch, and their content comes
from the `TransactionDeposited` events emitted by the deposit contract on
L1.<Cite src="op-specs-deposits" />

So there is nothing to advance. Your transaction is already, permanently, part of the input to one
specific epoch. The only question is whether that epoch ever gets derived.

## Why it must get derived

Three rules, in the order they bind.

**Rule one — late batches are dropped.** Among the batch-validity rules the specification lists
`batch.epoch_num + sequence_window_size < inclusion_block_number -> drop`, glossed as: the batch must
be included timely.<Cite src="op-specs-derivation" /> A sequencer batch that shows up after the window
has closed is not merely late. It is invalid, permanently, for every honest node.

**Rule two — the gap must be filled.** If no batch can be accepted, and the stage has finished
buffering everything readable from the L1 block at height `epoch.number + sequence_window_size`, and
the next epoch is available, then an **empty batch** is derived — parent hash from the safe head,
timestamp at the next slot, and no sequencer transactions at
all.<Cite src="op-specs-derivation" /> Deposited transactions are added by the next stage of the
pipeline, not by the batch.<Cite src="op-specs-derivation" />

**Rule three — every epoch gets a block.** The specification requires that each epoch contain at least
one L2 block, stating the reason directly: so that all relevant information from L1, deposits
included, is represented on L2 even if the epoch has no sequencer
batches.<Cite src="op-specs-derivation" />

Put them together and the guarantee is structural. Past the window, a node that wants to follow the
canonical chain has no choice: it must produce a block for that epoch, that block contains no
sequencer transactions because none arrived in time, and it contains your deposit because deposits
come from the origin. OP Stack's own guide states the outcome in the same terms — after the window
expires, nodes begin generating blocks deterministically, incorporating only the force-included
transactions.<Cite src="op-docs-forced-transaction" />

## Read the window from the chain's own config

The figure everyone quotes is twelve hours. It is a chain parameter, and the Superchain Registry is
where a Superchain chain declares it.<Cite src="superchain-registry-repo" /> OP Mainnet's entry reads:

```toml
block_time = 2
seq_window_size = 3600
max_sequencer_drift = 600
```

**Subgoal: convert the window into wall-clock time.** `seq_window_size` counts L1 blocks, and Ethereum
produces one block per 12-second slot.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos" />

```text
3,600 L1 blocks × 12 s = 43,200 s = 12 hours
```

That is where the headline number comes from, and it comes from a chain's configuration file rather
than from a doc.<Cite src="superchain-registry-op-mainnet" />

**Subgoal: notice which unit it is in, because it changes what varies.** The registry entries for Ink
and Unichain also carry `seq_window_size = 3600`, but their `block_time` is 1 second rather than
2.<Cite src="superchain-registry-repo" /> A one-second chain produces twelve L2 blocks per epoch where
a two-second chain produces six, so the *number of L2 blocks* you wait through differs by a factor of
two while the *wall-clock wait* is identical. A window denominated in L1 blocks is invariant to L2
block time by construction. Any chain that quoted its window in L2 blocks would have a window that
changed length whenever it tuned its block time.

**Subgoal: refuse to resolve a parameter the sources disagree about.** `max_sequencer_drift` is the
bound on how far ahead of L1 the sequencer may run.<Cite src="op-specs-derivation" /> The registry
records 600 seconds for OP Mainnet.<Cite src="superchain-registry-op-mainnet" /> The specification's
parameter table gives a Bedrock default of 600 and a latest default of 1800, changed at Fjord, with
the note that it went from a chain parameter to a constant at that
upgrade.<Cite src="op-specs-derivation" /> The forced-transaction guide quotes a max time drift of 30
minutes, which is 1800.<Cite src="op-docs-forced-transaction" /> Three first-party sources, two
values, and the most likely explanation — a chain-config field left in place after the value became a
constant — is an inference, not a citation. Take the operational lesson instead: when a config file
and a specification disagree about a parameter, the node implementation is the tiebreaker, and neither
document is.

## What the wait actually looks like

The window is the worst case, not the expectation. OP Stack's guide gives the shape by
duration.<Cite src="op-docs-forced-transaction" />

| Sequencer downtime | What happens to your forced deposit |
|---|---|
| Under 30 min | Included within the usual max time drift once the sequencer resumes |
| 30 min to 12 h | Included when the sequencer resumes, or when the window expires — the L2 state is uncertain until one of those |
| Over 12 h | Nodes generate deposit-only blocks deterministically; regular L2 transactions are deferred |

Two details in there repay attention.

**The window is rolling.** It is a rolling 12-hour delay from when an L1 block is first known, and the
guide names the design intent: give the sequencer a grace period to come back without causing L2
reorganisations.<Cite src="op-docs-forced-transaction" /> That is the same reorg purchase the previous
lesson found on the Arbitrum side, bought with a different instrument.

**The recovery has a signature.** A sequencer returning after more than 30 minutes but less than 12
hours enters a catch-up phase: it produces deposit-only blocks while advancing the L1 origin with
every block, switching the normal 6:1 ratio of L2 blocks to L1 origins to
1:1 until the origin is back inside the drift window.<Cite src="op-docs-forced-transaction" /> If your
indexer assumes six L2 blocks per L1 origin, a real outage recovery is when that assumption breaks.
The 6:1 figure is itself derivable — 12-second L1 slots over 2-second L2 blocks — and the
specification says so, adding that the sequencer will produce larger epochs to maintain liveness
across skipped L1 slots.<Cite src="op-specs-derivation" /><Cite src="ethereum-org-pos" />

## The trap in the entry point

Submission is one call: `depositTransaction(address _to, uint256 _value, uint64 _gasLimit, bool _isCreation, bytes memory _data)`
on the portal, which emits `TransactionDeposited` carrying the packed opaque data the rollup node
derives from.<Cite src="optimism-portal2-source" /> Two constraints on it are worth knowing before you
need them, and one behaviour has cost people their funds.

**The gas limit has a floor that grows with your calldata.** `minimumGasLimit(uint64 _byteCount)`
returns `_byteCount * 40 + 21000`, and a deposit below that reverts.<Cite src="optimism-portal2-source" />
A 1,000-byte forced call therefore needs at least 61,000 gas declared. Calldata is separately capped
at 120,000 bytes, chosen so the transaction fits the 128 KB peer-to-peer
policy.<Cite src="optimism-portal2-source" />

**A contract that deposits does not arrive as itself.** If the caller is a contract, the `from`
address is transformed by adding `0x1111000000000000000000000000000000001111`, unchecked, as a
`uint160`, so it may overflow.<Cite src="op-specs-deposits" /> The portal applies this whenever the
sender is not an externally owned account.<Cite src="optimism-portal2-source" />

**Subgoal: make the arithmetic concrete, because the consequence is not.** Take an L1 contract at
`0x0000000000000000000000000000000000001234`. Its alias is that value plus the constant:

```text
  0x0000000000000000000000000000000000001234
+ 0x1111000000000000000000000000000000001111
= 0x1111000000000000000000000000000000002345
```

**Subgoal: state what that does to a runbook.** If your treasury on L2 is controlled by a multisig and
your incident plan is "the multisig on L1 forces a withdrawal through the portal", the forced
transaction executes on L2 from `0x1111…2345`, an address that holds nothing and is authorised for
nothing. The call will be included — inclusion is guaranteed — and it will do nothing you wanted.

The specification gives the reason for aliasing and it is a good one: it prevents attacks in which an
L1 contract shares an address with an L2 contract that has different code, and EOAs are exempt because
they are guaranteed to have the same absence of code on both
chains.<Cite src="op-specs-deposits" /> The portal's own comment adds the practical warning that
contracts sending value directly will lose it to aliasing.<Cite src="optimism-portal2-source" /> None
of which helps you at 03:00 if you have not tested the path.

<Check type="explain" concept="sequencing-window">
Your product holds user balances on an OP Stack chain. The sequencer has been unreachable for two
hours. A colleague proposes: "Submit everyone's exit through the portal now, then call the L1 function
that forces inclusion once twelve hours have elapsed."

What is wrong with the second half of that sentence, and what should the runbook say instead?

<Answer>
**There is no such function.** Nothing on L1 forces inclusion on an OP Stack chain, because inclusion
is not an action. Once `depositTransaction` returns, the deposit is an input to the epoch whose origin
is that L1 block, and honest nodes must produce a block for that epoch containing
it.<Cite src="op-specs-deposits" /><Cite src="op-specs-derivation" /> Waiting for a call to make is
waiting for something that will never become available.

**Nor is twelve hours the wait to plan for.** The sequencing window is the bound on how long the chain
can stay uncertain, not the latency of a deposit. If the sequencer returns at hour three, the deposits
are picked up in its catch-up phase; only if it stays down past the window do nodes begin producing
deposit-only blocks deterministically.<Cite src="op-docs-forced-transaction" />

**What the runbook should say.** Two things, neither of which involves an L1 call after submission.
First, the actual submission step, with the portal address, the argument list, and the gas floor of
`byteCount × 40 + 21000` computed for the calldata you are sending.<Cite src="optimism-portal2-source" />
Second, a check on the sender: if the step is executed by a multisig rather than an EOA, the
transaction arrives on L2 from the aliased address and must be authorised for that
address.<Cite src="op-specs-deposits" /> Then a monitoring step — watch for the L2 block whose origin
is your L1 block — rather than an action step.

**A useful sanity check for any runbook of this kind.** If a step says "call X to force inclusion",
ask which stack you are on. On the Arbitrum shape that step exists and is
`forceInclusion`.<Cite src="nitro-contracts-sequencer-inbox" /> On the OP Stack shape it does not exist
and its absence is the stronger guarantee, because a guarantee nobody has to exercise cannot fail
because nobody exercised it.
</Answer>
</Check>

That last row is the honest comparison. A call that anyone may make is only as good as the population
of people who will make it, and the previous lesson found no one is paid to. A rule that nodes cannot
violate has no such dependency — which is exactly why L2BEAT rates both Base and Arbitrum One as
*self sequence*, the strongest of its three sequencer-failure
categories.<Cite src="l2beat-base-project" /> Different mechanisms, same rating, because the rating
asks what a user can complete alone.

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson** widens the frame and it is where the module's argument turns. Everything you have
learned so far — queue, call, window, derivation — unblocks exactly one of four chokepoints between a
user and their money on L1. The other three have their own operators and their own failures, and one
of them freezes withdrawals no matter how well inclusion works.

**Immediately useful.** The specification is written so that a rollup node is a deterministic function
of L1, which means the escape hatch is only as strong as your ability to run one.<Cite src="op-specs-protocol-overview" />
If the answer to "who on our team can sync a rollup node from L1 without the sequencer's RPC" is
nobody, the guarantee in this lesson belongs to someone else.
