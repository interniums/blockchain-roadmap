Suppose everything in this module works. The queue accepts your message, the deadline elapses, the
cursor advances, a proposer posts a root, the game resolves your way, and the airgap passes. Four
chokepoints, all open, every step driven by you.

Two things can still take your money, and neither is a chokepoint. The first is a clock: an upgrade
that lands before you finish. The second is a crowd: an L1 that is full of everyone else doing what
you are doing. This lesson is about those two, and it is the lesson that turns the module's machinery
into a verdict.

## An exit window is a subtraction

L2BEAT's definition is the load-bearing one and it is worth reading as a formula rather than as a
phrase. The exit window is the amount of time users have to exit a system before an unwanted upgrade,
and it **takes into account upgrade delays, forced transaction delays and other time
factors**.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />

So it is not the upgrade delay. It is the upgrade delay minus what it costs you to leave.

```text
exit window  =  time until the upgrade executes  −  time your exit takes
```

Both terms are chain configuration, and the second one is everything the previous four lessons
measured. That is why this module put the mechanisms first: without them the subtrahend is a guess.

The thresholds attached to it are precise. For Stage 1, a rollup needs an exit window of at least
**7 days** if upgrades are initiated by a permissioned actor less decentralised than a Security
Council. For Stage 2 it needs at least **30 days** in all cases outside on-chain provable
bugs.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />

### The subtraction, performed in public

Arbitrum One's risk row shows the working, which is unusual and useful. On 26 August 2026 the regular
upgrade path reads **10 days**, with the note that non-emergency upgrades are initiated on L2 and go
through an 8-day delay on L2 and a 3-day delay on L1; since there is a 1-day delay to force a
transaction — forcing its inclusion in the following state update — users have 10 days to
exit.<Cite src="l2beat-arbitrum-project" />

Now hold that against the same page's state-validation row, because the two numbers sit a few
hundred pixels apart and do not obviously agree. Arbitrum One is recorded with a **6 day 8 hour
challenge period plus a 2 day execution delay**.<Cite src="l2beat-arbitrum-project" /> Base is
recorded with a **5 day challenge period** and no execution
delay.<Cite src="l2beat-base-project" />

The subtraction above deducts the **inclusion** delay and not the rest of the path. That is a
modelling choice, not an error, and it turns on a question the published number does not answer: does
"exit" mean getting a withdrawal *initiated* into a state root, or getting it *finalised* on L1 with
the funds in your hand? Under the first reading, one day is the right subtrahend. Under the second,
you would also subtract proposing latency, the challenge period and the execution delay, and on
Arbitrum One that is another eight days and change on top of the
one.<Cite src="l2beat-arbitrum-project" />

Do not resolve this from a lesson. Resolve it from your own threat model, and the question that
settles it is narrow: **can the upgrade you are running from void a withdrawal that is proven but not
yet claimed?** If the upgrade replaces the bridge or the registry your in-flight claim depends on,
initiating was not exiting. You have already seen the shape of that on the OP Stack side — a single
Guardian call to `updateRetirementTimestamp` retires every existing dispute game, so a proven
withdrawal must be re-proven against a game that does not exist
yet.<Cite src="optimism-anchor-state-registry" /> An upgrade with more reach than that is not a
stretch.

<Aside kind="note">
The practical version of this is a monitoring requirement, not a philosophy question. An exit window
only helps someone who *notices the announcement*. Ten days of notice that nobody on your team is
watching for is zero days of notice. If your chain's exit window is the reason you sleep, then
watching the upgrade-initiation event on L1 and L2 is a production alert with a pager attached to it,
in the same tier as your database being down.
</Aside>

## Zero is a number that appears on good chains

Here is the finding that reorders most people's mental model of the L2 landscape.

Arbitrum One, 26 August 2026: sequencer failure **self sequence**, proposer failure **self propose**,
exit window **None** — "there is no window for users to exit in case of an unwanted upgrade since
contracts are instantly upgradable".<Cite src="l2beat-arbitrum-project" />

Base, same day: sequencer failure **self sequence**, proposer failure **self propose**, exit window
**None**, with the note that upgrades need approval by two parties, the Base Coordinator Multisig and
the Base Security Council.<Cite src="l2beat-base-project" />

Two chains with the strongest available ratings on every chokepoint this module has taught. Both with
an exit window of zero on the instant path. Both Stage 1, and both missing the same two Stage 2
requirements: upgrades unrelated to on-chain provable bugs provide less than 30 days to exit, and the
Security Council's actions are not confined to on-chain provable
bugs.<Cite src="l2beat-arbitrum-project" /><Cite src="l2beat-base-project" />

This is not a gotcha about two chains. It is the structural point the module has been walking towards:
**the escape hatch and the upgrade key are orthogonal.** Forced inclusion constrains the sequencer.
Permissionless proposing constrains the proposer. Neither constrains the address that can replace the
contract those mechanisms live in. A `forceInclusion` with no access-control modifier on it is a
guarantee about `SequencerInbox` as currently deployed, and says nothing about what
`SequencerInbox` will be one block from now.<Cite src="nitro-contracts-sequencer-inbox" />

Note also which asymmetries from earlier lessons are upgrade-shaped. The Arbitrum Inbox's entry points
carry `whenNotPaused onlyAllowed`, both gated by the rollup or its
owner.<Cite src="nitro-contracts-abs-inbox" /> The OP Stack portal's `paused()` reads through to
`systemConfig.paused()`, and pausing blocks proving and finalising while leaving deposits
open.<Cite src="optimism-portal2-source" /> Every one of those switches belongs to somebody. The
question "who?" has the same answer as "who can upgrade?" more often than the contract layout
suggests.

## The walkaway test

Put the module into one procedure. Assume the operators are actively hostile, and assume every
emergency body — Security Council, guardian, multisig — has simply vanished. Can a user get their
assets to L1 using only L1 contracts and their own key?

Answering it forces you through the chokepoints in order, and each step is a contract read you can do
today.

1. **Can I enter the queue?** Is the entry point pausable or allow-listed, and by
   whom?<Cite src="nitro-contracts-abs-inbox" />
2. **Can I complete inclusion alone?** Is there a permissionless call, or does the queue need the
   operator to drain it? This is the *self sequence* versus *enqueue via L1* distinction, and it is
   per chain.<Cite src="l2beat-arbitrum-project" /><Cite src="l2beat-zksync-era-project" />
3. **Can I get a state root posted?** Is proposing whitelisted; if so, is there a permissionless
   removal, and is it armed? On Arbitrum-family chains that is `removeWhitelistAfterValidatorAfk`,
   dead if `validatorAfkBlocks` is zero.<Cite src="nitro-contracts-rollup-user-logic" />
4. **Can I afford to?** The bond is the gate that has no access-control modifier and stops you
   anyway. Arbitrum's documentation recommends an assertion bond of 3,600 ETH for Arbitrum One, plus
   555 and 79 WETH challenge bonds per level.<Cite src="arbitrum-bold-economics" />
5. **Can I claim without help?** Proof maturity delay, dispute-game finality airgap, and a game that
   is proper, respected, finalised and resolved for the
   defender.<Cite src="optimism-portal2-source" /><Cite src="optimism-anchor-state-registry" />

A chain passes only if every answer is yes. L2BEAT compresses the same procedure into one Stage 1
requirement — users are able to exit without the help of the permissioned operators — and marks it
satisfied for Base on 26 August 2026.<Cite src="l2beat-base-project" /> Linea fails at step two: no
mechanism to have transactions included if the sequencer is down or censoring, with the Operator role
becoming public only after six months of no finalised blocks.<Cite src="l2beat-linea-project" />

ZKsync Era fails at step three in a way worth naming precisely, because it looks like a pass. Its
row records *replace proposer*: only whitelisted proposers can publish state roots, so on failure
withdrawals are frozen, and governance can attempt to change proposers via an
upgrade.<Cite src="l2beat-zksync-era-project" /> That is a real remedy and it is not yours. It routes
through the upgrade authority, which under the test's own premise has vanished — and under the
scenario you actually fear is the adversary. A remedy that requires the party you are escaping is a
"no" at step three however it is worded.

### The test's own blind spot

There is a subtlety in the framing that is easy to miss and changes what the result means.

The test asks you to assume the Security Council has *vanished*. On a Stage 1 chain, the Security
Council is also the body permitted to upgrade instantly — L2BEAT's framework allows exactly
that.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" /> So "assume it vanished" is the **favourable** assumption. It removes
the entity that could have shortened your exit window to zero.

The scenario you actually fear is not a council that disappeared. It is a council that is present,
functional, and aligned with the operator you are escaping. Against that adversary the walkaway test
returns a pass and the exit window returns zero, and the exit window is the one that decides.

So use the test for what it is good at — it is an excellent instrument for finding the step where
your runbook says "wait", and it is the origin of the stages framework's insistence on grading
governance rather than proof type.<Cite src="l2beat-stages-intro" /> Just do not read a pass as
safety. Read it as: *the mechanisms work; now go read the exit window row.*

## Every step of the escape is an L1 transaction

The last constraint is not a property of any chain. It is a property of the guarantee itself, and it
cannot be engineered away, because it is *where the guarantee comes from*.

Queueing the message, forcing inclusion, posting an assertion, proving the withdrawal, claiming it —
each one is an L1 transaction that has to be included in an L1 block and paid for in L1 gas. That is
not overhead attached to the escape hatch. It is the escape hatch. The reason nobody can stop you is
that the mechanism runs on a chain the operator does not control, and the price of running on that
chain is its fee market.

Two consequences, and the second is specific enough to be actionable.

### The price responds faster than you can act

Ethereum's base fee moves by at most one eighth per block.<Cite src="eip-1559" /> Compounding at that
ceiling:

```text
doubling      ln 2   / ln 1.125  ≈  5.9 blocks   ≈ 71 seconds
100×          ln 100 / ln 1.125  ≈ 39.1 blocks   ≈ 7.8 minutes
1000×         ln 1000 / ln 1.125 ≈ 58.6 blocks   ≈ 11.7 minutes
```

at 12-second slots.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos" /> Eight minutes of saturated demand is a hundredfold
base fee. Note what you cannot do about it: the base fee is not a bid you can beat, and paying more
priority fee reorders you inside a block without creating another one. Under sustained demand the
binding constraint is blockspace, and blockspace is fixed.

Blobs do not help here either, and it is worth saying why rather than leaving it as an assumption. The
escape path is execution gas and calldata on L1 contracts — an inbox call, a portal call, a factory
call, a proof. None of it is blob data. The blob fee market that made your chain cheap is not the
market your exit is priced in.

### The OP Stack has a second fee market on the same path

This is the part almost nobody models, and it is specified.

Deposits buy L2 gas on L1 through a gas burn, with a fee market and a **hard cap on the amount of gas
provided to all deposits in a single L1 block**.<Cite src="op-specs-guaranteed-gas" /> The documented
defaults:<Cite src="op-specs-guaranteed-gas" />

| Variable | Value |
|---|---|
| `MAX_RESOURCE_LIMIT` | 20,000,000 |
| `ELASTICITY_MULTIPLIER` | 10 |
| `TARGET_RESOURCE_LIMIT` | `MAX_RESOURCE_LIMIT / ELASTICITY_MULTIPLIER` |
| `BASE_FEE_MAX_CHANGE_DENOMINATOR` | 8 |
| `MINIMUM_BASE_FEE` | 1 gwei |

**Subgoal: convert the cap into an exit rate.** A deposit's minimum declared gas limit is
`byteCount * 40 + 21000`, so a deposit carrying no calldata declares at least
21,000.<Cite src="optimism-portal2-source" />

```text
per L1 block   20,000,000 / 21,000  ≈  952 minimal deposits
per day        952 × 7,200 blocks    ≈  6.9 million
```

using 7,200 L1 blocks per day at 12-second slots.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos" /> That is a **ceiling
on the whole chain's forced-exit throughput**, independent of how much anyone is willing to pay, and
it is not reachable in practice — because the target is a tenth of it.

**Subgoal: price the sustained case.** `TARGET_RESOURCE_LIMIT` is 2,000,000, one tenth of the
cap.<Cite src="op-specs-guaranteed-gas" /> Running at the cap means running at ten times target, so
the deposit base fee rises by its maximum every block, and
`BASE_FEE_MAX_CHANGE_DENOMINATOR` of 8 makes that the same 12.5 per cent
compounding.<Cite src="op-specs-guaranteed-gas" /> The ETH cost of a deposit is the guaranteed gas
times that base fee, converted into an L1 gas burn:

```text
L1 gas burnt  =  (guaranteed gas × L2 deposit base fee) / L1 base fee
```

which the specification states, along with a stipend crediting the gas spent processing the
deposit.<Cite src="op-specs-guaranteed-gas" /> So a sustained mass exit multiplies its own per-deposit
cost by 1.125 every twelve seconds, on the *same* schedule the L1 base fee is compounding on. Two
independent fee markets, one path, both responding to the same crowd.

**Subgoal: read the design intent, because it explains why this will not be tuned away.** The cap is
not incidental. The specification's rationale is denial-of-service resistance: an attacker who could
buy all of a block's deposit gas would frontrun a victim's deposit and make it revert, and the stated
mitigation is a large resource limit **and** a large elasticity
multiplier.<Cite src="op-specs-guaranteed-gas" /> Raising the cap to help a mass exit weakens exactly
the protection that keeps a single deposit from being griefed. The parameter is doing a job, and the
job is not evacuation.

### The two stacks amortise a crowd very differently

One more comparison falls out of the mechanisms, and it runs opposite to the direction most people
would guess.

On the Arbitrum side the cursor is positional and monotonic: `forceInclusion` names a
`_totalDelayedMessagesRead` and advances past every message at or below it, reverting with
`DelayedBackwards` if you name a position already consumed.<Cite src="nitro-contracts-sequencer-inbox" />
A crowd therefore **shares** the cost of chokepoint two. Ten thousand queued messages need ten
thousand cheap submissions, and then exactly one force call — paid by whoever calls first, benefiting
everyone behind them. The queue is the amortisation.

On the OP Stack there is no such call, and that is normally the stronger property: nothing has to be
invoked because derivation obliges nodes to include your deposit.<Cite src="op-specs-deposits" /> But
it also means there is nothing to share. Every user's deposit consumes its own slice of the same
20,000,000-gas per-block budget, and each pays the compounding deposit base fee
individually.<Cite src="op-specs-guaranteed-gas" />

So the guarantee that is stronger for one censored user is the weaker one for ten thousand of them at
once, and the guarantee that depends on somebody being willing to pay is the one that scales when
everybody is. Neither team designed for the crowd; the difference is a side effect of the shape they
chose.

The conclusion is not that escape hatches are useless. It is that "users can always exit" describes an
**individual right, exercised against a shared and priced resource**. Those two halves get quoted as
one sentence and only the first half is in the contract. Size your product's exposure against the
second half, and the honest question for a product holding user balances is not "can users exit" but
"what fraction of our users can exit in the first hour, at what cost, and who tells the rest?"

**The conclusion is defensible on the adversary it names and incomplete on the one that matters most.**
Against an upgrade authority willing to use the instant path, both chains give you zero notice, and no

risk is not the dominant term — you are choosing which governance to be a counterparty to for that
whole period, and the exit window tells you how much warning you get before that counterparty changes
the deal. Zero on the emergency path is a fact about both chains and an argument for a monitoring
alert, not an argument for indifference between them.

## What this unlocks

**The module's practice** is the only exercise here that converts belief into knowledge: submit
through the L1 path on a testnet and complete it yourself. Every claim in these five lessons is
checkable against a deployment in an afternoon, and the gap between "the docs say anyone can call it"
and "I have called it" is the entire difference between a runbook and a hope. The research behind this
module found no verified account of a mainnet L2 sequencer outage in which users forced their way out
at scale, which means the mechanism's real-world track record is a claim about code rather than an
observed property of a system.

**The reflection prompt** asks for the first page of your team's runbook with every step naming a real
function on a real address. You now have the eight reads that page depends on. Write it while nothing
is on fire, and mark honestly which steps you have never executed — that annotation is worth more than
the runbook.

**Where this connects outward.** The stages framework grades governance rather than proof type, and
you have just seen why: the proof system is downstream of the key that can replace
it.<Cite src="l2beat-stages-intro" /> The same reasoning is what makes L2BEAT's own edge-case work
insist that forced-transaction delay bounds are an open question rather than a published
number.<Cite src="l2beat-stages-edge-cases" /> Carry the habit rather than the figures: for any
guarantee a chain advertises, find the address that can withdraw it, and find out how much notice you
get.

**The one sentence to keep.** A rollup is escapable when a user can complete the whole path alone —
and *stays* escapable only for as long as the party holding the upgrade key allows. On the chains
holding the most value today, that second clause is measured in blocks, and the deposit you can always
make is not the withdrawal you can always
complete.<Cite src="op-specs-deposits" /><Cite src="l2beat-arbitrum-project" />
