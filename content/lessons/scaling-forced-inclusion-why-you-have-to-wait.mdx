A sensible engineer, reading that forcing a transaction takes twenty-four hours, concludes that
forcing must be expensive or difficult. It is neither. The call in the previous lesson does a hash
comparison and a storage write. There is no proof to generate, no committee to convene, no
computation that takes a day.

The delay is not a cost. It is a **purchase**. The chain is buying two things with it, and if you
know what they are you can tell instantly whether a given chain's number is defensible or merely
copied. Then we work the arithmetic of the mechanism that spends the delay back when you need it —
and find that it takes longer to help than almost anyone assumes.

## Purchase one: the queue must not be able to rewrite history

Start from something you already know. A forced inclusion is a child-chain state change derived from
an L1 event. [Derivation](/c/derivation) is a pure function of the L1 chain: feed it a different L1
chain, and it produces a different child chain.

Now let a [reorg](/c/reorg) happen. Suppose the delay were zero and messages were forceable in the
block after submission.

**Subgoal: identify what a shallow L1 reorg does to a queued message.** The transaction that called
`sendL2Message` sat in an L1 block. A reorg removes that block. In the new canonical L1 chain, the
message either never existed or landed at a different queue position with a different
`blockNumber`, `timestamp` and `baseFeeL1` — three of the seven fields the message hash commits
to.<Cite src="nitro-contracts-sequencer-inbox" />

**Subgoal: propagate that to the child chain.** Derivation reads the new L1 chain and produces a
different ordering. Every child-chain block built on top of the forced message is now invalid. The
child chain does not merely drop one transaction: it reorgs to the depth of everything that followed.

**Subgoal: price the exposure.** Ethereum's proof of stake divides time into 12-second slots grouped
into 32-slot epochs, and finalises with a two-epoch delay.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos" /> Two epochs
of 32 slots at 12 seconds is 768 seconds — about thirteen minutes in the good case. Anything
younger than that is a block that can still disappear. A child chain that ingests unfinalised L1
messages inherits every L1 reorg as a child-chain reorg, which is precisely the failure that soft
confirmations exist to avoid.

So the *floor* under the delay is L1 finality, and every real chain sits well above the floor.
Arbitrum documents both ends of the range and does not entirely agree with itself, which is worth
seeing rather than smoothing over. The sequencer deep dive states that on Arbitrum One and Nova the
sequencer includes delayed messages in the feed only after they are finalised on
Ethereum.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" /> The chain-configuration page states that on an L2 settling
to Ethereum the sequencer releases delayed messages after 40
blocks.<Cite src="arbitrum-chain-finality" /> Forty blocks is eight minutes, which is *less* than
two epochs. Both pages are current; they describe the same subsystem; take the conservative reading
and, if the answer matters to your product, read the node's `finalize-distance` configuration rather
than either page.<Cite src="arbitrum-chain-finality" />

<Aside kind="note">
That configuration is exposed deliberately, and the trade is stated plainly. A chain settling to
Arbitrum One or Nova can set `--node.delayed-sequencer.use-merge-finality=false` and
`--node.delayed-sequencer.finalize-distance=1` to get roughly one-minute deposits — with the noted
consequence that a reorg of unfinalised blocks on the settlement chain may reorg the chain
above.<Cite src="arbitrum-chain-finality" /> Fast bridging and reorg exposure are the same dial. Any
chain advertising near-instant deposits has turned that dial, and its risk disclosure should say so.
</Aside>

Note the asymmetry this creates, because it is easy to misread. The reorg argument constrains how
soon the *sequencer* may voluntarily ingest a delayed message — minutes. It does not by itself
explain twenty-four hours. For that we need the second purchase.

## Purchase two: normal sequencing has to stay the default

Imagine the delay set to thirty minutes on a busy chain. What happens is not that users get a faster
escape hatch. What happens is that the delayed inbox becomes the mempool.

Any user who cares about censorship — every arbitrageur, every liquidator, every bot with a
deadline — now has a strictly better strategy than trusting the sequencer: submit to L1, wait out a
short window, force. The [sequencer](/c/sequencer) loses its exclusive right to order recent
transactions, [soft confirmations](/c/soft-confirmation) stop meaning anything because any of them can
be pre-empted by a forced message, and the chain's throughput advantage evaporates because ordering
is now decided by an L1 queue.

The delay is what makes the escape path an *exception* rather than an *option*. It is priced to be
uncompetitive with the happy path, and that pricing is the point. Arbitrum's documentation frames the
same trade from the other end when it describes the force-inclusion window as a window the sequencer
is given: the delay is the sequencer's exclusive period, and forcing is what happens when the
sequencer fails to use it.<Cite src="arbitrum-tx-lifecycle-deep-dive" />

### The case that forced twenty-four hours specifically

There is a third argument, and it is the one that produced the modern design. Arbitrum's own
motivating example is a chain settling to Arbitrum One whose parent-chain sequencer is censoring. In
that setting every assertion and every sub-challenge move must bypass the sequencer, so each move
waits out the force window. Resolution then takes 24 hours multiplied by the number of moves; the
documentation's illustration is fifty sequential moves giving fifty
days.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" />

Read that as a security result, not a latency complaint. A force window used once is an
inconvenience. A force window used inside a loop is a denial-of-service weapon against the proof
system, wielded by whoever controls sequencing. Any chain that stacks a proof system on top of a
chain with a long force window inherits that multiplication.

## Spending the delay back: the buffer, worked

The response was not a smaller constant. It was a delay that shrinks under evidence of censorship —
Arbitrum's Censorship Timeout, originally called the Delay Buffer, proposed to be enabled by default
for Arbitrum One and Nova alongside BoLD.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" /> The effective window is the
lesser of `delayBuffer` and `delayBlocks`, where `delayBlocks` is a constant currently set to 24 hours
and the buffer ranges from 30 minutes to 48 hours.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" /> In the contract
that is one line: `delayBufferableBlocks` returns the buffer if it is below `delayBlocks`, otherwise
`delayBlocks`.<Cite src="nitro-contracts-sequencer-inbox" />

Everyone quotes that. Almost nobody works out how fast it moves. It is four lines of arithmetic and
the answer is surprising.

**Subgoal: get the replenishment into blocks per block.** The buffer library adds
`elapsed * replenishRateInBasis / BASIS` with `BASIS` fixed at
10,000.<Cite src="nitro-contracts-delay-buffer" /> The proposed rate for Arbitrum One is 5 per
cent — 500 basis points.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" />

```text
gain per elapsed block = 500 / 10000 = 1/20 = 0.05 blocks
```

That is the documented "one minute for every 20 minutes in which no messages are delayed beyond the
threshold".<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" />

**Subgoal: bound the depletion.** Depletion is `delay - threshold` when positive, and then the
library clamps it: if the unexpected delay exceeds the elapsed interval, it is reduced to the elapsed
interval.<Cite src="nitro-contracts-delay-buffer" /> The comment explains why — two batches each
carrying delayed messages that waited the same 100 blocks should count as one 100-block delay, not
two hundred.<Cite src="nitro-contracts-delay-buffer" /> The consequence for us is a hard rate limit:

```text
loss per elapsed block ≤ 1 block
net change per elapsed block ≥ −1 + 1/20 = −0.95 blocks
```

**The buffer cannot fall faster than 0.95 blocks per L1 block.** Under total censorship, wall-clock
time and buffer depletion run at nearly the same rate, and that single fact settles everything below.

**Subgoal: load the proposed parameters.** For Arbitrum One the proposal is a buffer of 14,400
parent-chain blocks — two days — with a threshold of 150 L1 blocks, thirty minutes; Nova's threshold
is 300 blocks, one hour.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" /> At 12 seconds a slot,
`delayBlocks` of 24 hours is 7,200 blocks.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos" />

**Subgoal: find when the buffer starts to matter.** The effective window is the lesser of the two. The
buffer starts full at 14,400, which is above 7,200, so the window is 7,200 blocks — twenty-four
hours — and *stays* there until the buffer crosses below `delayBlocks`.

```text
buffer must fall     14,400 − 7,200 = 7,200 blocks
minimum time         7,200 / 0.95  ≈ 7,579 blocks ≈ 25.3 hours
```

**Subgoal: find the floor and the time to reach it.** Depletion saturates below at the threshold, not
at zero — the library returns `threshold` rather than continuing down, so the sequencer has a
recovery margin.<Cite src="nitro-contracts-delay-buffer" /> The floor is 150 blocks, thirty minutes.

```text
total fall           14,400 − 150 = 14,250 blocks
minimum time         14,250 / 0.95 = 15,000 blocks = 50 hours exactly
```

### Why the clamp is there, and why it is the reorg argument again

It is tempting to read that 0.95 rate limit as conservatism. It is not; it exists to stop the buffer
mechanism from breaking the very property the delay was bought to protect.

The library's own worked case is worth following. Two batches are posted, each sequencing delayed
messages that waited the same 100 blocks. Without the clamp, each posting would decrement the buffer
by 100, counting one 100-block delay as 200. The comment then names the real danger: if the buffer
were 300 blocks, decrementing it while processing the first batch could let the second delayed
message become force-includable *before the sequencer could post the second batch* — and the
documentation calls this out as child-chain reorg risk in edge
cases.<Cite src="nitro-contracts-delay-buffer" />

Sit with that shape, because it recurs. A mechanism whose whole job is to shorten a delay can, if it
shortens it too eagerly, create a race between a forcer and an honest sequencer that is mid-post.
Both would then be writing valid history into the same positions. The clamp makes the buffer fall no
faster than time itself, which guarantees the sequencer's remaining margin is never consumed faster
than it accrues. The saturation floor at `threshold` rather than zero does the same work from the
other end: the library keeps a recovery margin so that a sequencer coming back from an outage can wait
out finality before queueing, rather than being forced to race.<Cite src="nitro-contracts-delay-buffer" />

That threshold has a second job worth naming, because it explains the parameter's slightly odd size.
The library treats the inbox as *synced* when the elapsed distance since the last update is within the
threshold, and describes that as the sync validity window during which no proofs are
required.<Cite src="nitro-contracts-delay-buffer" /> The threshold is therefore not a free knob: it is
simultaneously the depletion floor, the definition of "expected delay", and the width of the
no-proof-needed window. Setting it small tightens the escape hatch's floor and widens the range in
which the mechanism has to do work. Arbitrum One's 30 minutes and Nova's one hour are two answers to
that trade on two chains with different traffic.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" />

**Subgoal: state the result honestly.** The censorship timeout does not make the escape hatch fast
during an incident. For the first day of a total outage it changes nothing at all, because the buffer
has to fall seven thousand blocks before it even becomes the binding constraint. It reaches its
thirty-minute floor after roughly two days of continuous censorship. It is a defence against
*sustained* censorship — exactly the fifty-move challenge scenario it was designed for — and not a
defence against a bad Tuesday.

**Subgoal: price the recovery, because it is the asymmetry that gives the mechanism teeth.** Refilling
from the floor back to full is 14,250 blocks at 0.05 per block:

```text
14,250 / 0.05 = 285,000 blocks ≈ 39.6 days
```

Which is the documentation's own framing: one day of buffer replenished every twenty days, roughly
95 per cent uptime.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" /> Two days of censorship costs the operator forty
days of a shortened window. That ratio, not the floor, is the deterrent.

Roughly what is the effective force window at the start of the second outage, and what does that

<Answer>
**First outage.** Thirty hours is 9,000 L1 blocks. At the bounded worst-case drain of 0.95 blocks per
block the buffer falls by at most 8,550, from 14,400 to about 5,850.<Cite src="nitro-contracts-delay-buffer" />
Since 5,850 is below `delayBlocks` of 7,200, the effective window at the end of the outage is about
5,850 blocks — roughly 19.5 hours rather than 24.

**Recovery.** Five clean days is 36,000 blocks, replenishing 36,000 × 0.05 = 1,800 blocks. The buffer
returns to about 7,650, which is back *above* 7,200.

**Second outage.** The effective window is the lesser of 7,650 and 7,200, so it is 7,200 blocks — a
full 24 hours again.<Cite src="nitro-contracts-sequencer-inbox" /> The user who queued just before it
waits the standard day.

**The point.** Five days of good behaviour bought the operator back the entire benefit the first
outage had cost it, because the buffer only has to climb above `delayBlocks` — not back to its
maximum — for the window to be a full day again. The buffer punishes *sustained* censorship and
forgives *episodic* censorship almost completely. An operator that fails for a day a week is, as far
as this mechanism is concerned, well behaved. Note also that the honest answers here are bounds: the
drain rate is a worst case from the clamp, so the real buffer may be higher and the window longer,
never shorter. Under pressure, read `forceInclusionDeadline` from the contract rather than
recomputing this.<Cite src="nitro-contracts-sequencer-inbox" />

## The number is per chain, and the spread is wide

Everything above describes one chain family's configuration. The parameters are settable — chain
owners decide whether to use the feature at all and may change the defaults for their
chain.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" /> Read them from the deployment, not from a page.

The spread across live chains is larger than the discourse suggests. L2BEAT's sequencer-failure rows
on 26 August 2026 record: Arbitrum One, force with up to a **1 day** delay;<Cite src="l2beat-arbitrum-project" />
Base, force with up to a **12 hour** delay;<Cite src="l2beat-base-project" /> Scroll, force with up to
a **7 day** delay;<Cite src="l2beat-scroll-project" /> Linea, **no mechanism** to have transactions
included if the sequencer is down or censoring, with the operator role becoming public only after six
months of no finalised blocks.<Cite src="l2beat-linea-project" /> A factor of fourteen separates the
shortest from the longest among the chains that have a mechanism, and one of the four has no number
because it has no mechanism.

## The delay you cannot shorten

One closing constraint, because it survives every parameter change above. The buffer shortens the
wait between *queueing* and *forcing*. It does nothing about the wait between queueing and being
*able* to queue — and, as the previous lesson showed, the Inbox entry points carry `whenNotPaused`
and an allow list controlled by the rollup owner, while `forceInclusion`
carries neither.<Cite src="nitro-contracts-abs-inbox" /><Cite src="nitro-contracts-sequencer-inbox" />

The operational reading is unromantic. **Queue early.** A message sitting in the delayed inbox is an
option whose strike price is one L1 transaction and whose expiry is never. A message you have not
queued is a plan. During an incident the difference between those two is the entire difference
between the runbooks that work and the ones that get written afterwards.

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson** shows the OP Stack reaching the same guarantee with no queue, no forcing call and
no buffer — a sequencing window baked into the derivation rules, so that inclusion is not something
anyone does but something nodes cannot avoid. Comparing the two designs against the two purchases in
this lesson is the fastest way to see what is essential and what is one team's choice.

**Later in this module** the delay becomes an input to a subtraction: an exit window is only
protection if it exceeds inclusion delay plus proposing plus proving. A 24-hour force window inside a
10-day exit window is comfortable. The same window inside a zero-day one is decorative.

**Your own chain.** Two commands are worth running today rather than during an incident: read
`delayBlocks` and, if the chain is delay-bufferable, call `forceInclusionDeadline` for a recent block
number. The pair of numbers you get back is the first honest line of your runbook.
