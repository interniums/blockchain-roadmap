A challenger's software is asked a question by an L1 contract, and the question is this:

> What was the value of the fault-proof VM's entire machine state — registers, program counter,
> memory root, thread stacks — immediately after instruction number 4,398,046,511,103 of the program
> that derives L2 block 138,209,441 from its predecessor?

There is exactly one right answer, it is 32 bytes long, and producing it requires running a MIPS
emulator over four trillion instructions. The challenger answers. The opponent answers differently.
Now the contract asks about instruction 2,199,023,255,551.

This lesson is about the machine that makes that exchange terminate, and terminate cheaply. You have
seen the one-sentence version — bisect, then execute one step. Here we build it: the tree, the two
kinds of move, why the depth is exactly 73, what the on-chain VM must and must not be able to do,
and why the whole thing collapses if a single instruction can produce two answers.

## The reduction that makes any of this possible

**Subgoal: turn a disagreement about a result into a disagreement about a step.** Both parties agree
on the starting state — it is the anchor state, a previously confirmed output root that the game is
initialised with.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" /> They disagree about the ending state.
An execution trace is the sequence S₀, S₁, …, Sₙ where each Sᵢ₊₁ = VM(Sᵢ, Pᵢ), with Pᵢ an optional
proof of any external data the transition reads.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" />

If two traces share S₀ and differ at Sₙ, then there is a smallest index k where they first differ.
At k−1 they agree; at k they do not. So the pair (agreed pre-state, disputed post-state) is a
complete, self-contained accusation: *you claim the VM applied to the state at k−1 gives X, I claim
it gives Y, and only one of us can be right about a deterministic function.*

That is the entire reduction. Everything else in the protocol exists to find k without anyone
transmitting the trace.

**Subgoal: count what finding k costs.** Each round of questioning halves the interval still in
dispute, so the number of rounds is ⌈log₂ N⌉ for a trace of N steps. Put the OP Stack's real numbers
in: a standard chain runs `maxGameDepth` 73, so the tree has 2⁷³ leaf positions and any dispute is
settled in at most 73 moves down one path.<Cite src="op-specs-configurability" /><Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-reference" />
Seventy-three transactions, each carrying a 32-byte claim, versus re-executing the batch. That ratio
is the whole trick, and the exponential bond schedule from the previous lesson is priced against
exactly those 73 depths.

## The tree is two trees stacked

Here is the part the one-sentence version leaves out, and it matters because it explains both
parameters.

The game tree has a **split depth** as well as a maximum depth. At and above `splitDepth`, subgame
roots are **output roots** — commitments to the full state of L2 at a given block number. Below it,
subgame roots are commitments to the fault-proof VM's state at a given instruction
step.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" /> The standard values are `splitDepth` 30 and
`maxGameDepth` 73.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-reference" />

**Subgoal: read the two numbers as capacities rather than as settings.**

The spec's own justification matches: the split depth must be large enough to represent all L2
blocks since the anchor state, and the max depth large enough that the VM execution trace fits in
the leaves below it.<Cite src="op-specs-configurability" /> Sanity-check the top half against
reality: at a two-second L2 block time, 2³⁰ blocks is roughly 68 years of chain history. The
capacity is not tight, and it is not meant to be — a game must remain playable even if the anchor
state is very old.

Sanity-check the bottom half too. Below the split, one leaf is one MIPS instruction, and 2⁴³ of them
is the ceiling on how much work deriving a single L2 block may take inside the fault-proof VM. That
is a real constraint on the fault proof program, not a formality.

<Aside kind="note">
This is also why the clock rules have a special case here. When a move would leave the responder
with less than the clock extension, the clock is topped up — but the top-up is `clockExtension * 2`
for the move at depth `splitDepth − 1`, precisely because that move starts execution-trace bisection
and the responder must first generate the instruction trace off-chain.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-reference" /><Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" />
A protocol parameter encoding "this move requires running an emulator first" is a good sign you are
reading a spec written by people who ran it.
</Aside>

## Positions, and why there are two ways to disagree

Claims do not carry an interval. They carry a **position**, encoded as a generalized index: for a
node at depth d(n) with index idx(n) at that depth, the gindex is 2^d(n) + idx(n).<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" />
The high-order bit encodes the level and the remaining bits give a unique path.

A position's **trace index** is the deepest, right-most trace position it covers. So a claim commits
to an execution trace that terminates at its position's trace index: for trace index n, the claim's
state witness hash is a commitment to Sₙ.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" />

That framing — every claim is "the trace ends here, in this state" — is what gives the two moves
their meanings.

- **Attack.** The move you make when you disagree with a claim. The attacking position is the
  claim's gindex multiplied by 2, and it commits to half of the claim's trace.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" />
- **Defend.** The move you make when you disagree with a claim but *agree with its parent*. A
  defence at position n commits to the first half of the trace range of n + 1.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" />

**Subgoal: see why one move is not enough.** Suppose your opponent's claim covers steps 1 through
1,000 and asserts a wrong state at step 1,000, but the actual divergence is at step 900. If your
only move were "attack", you would be forced to dispute the first half — steps 1 through 500 — where
you actually agree with them. Attack says *the error is at or before your midpoint*; defend says
*the error is after it*. Two moves, because a binary search needs to be able to go both ways.

Some positions in the tree are therefore never occupied, and the spec says so plainly: they are not
needed, because a complementary valid position with the same trace index always exists — gindex 5
and gindex 2 share a trace index, for example.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" />

**Subgoal: notice what "left-most" buys.** Bonds resolve toward the left. If a subgame root resolves
incorrectly, its bond goes to the *left-most* claimant that countered it, which creates an incentive
to identify the earliest point of disagreement.<Cite src="op-specs-bond-incentives" /> There exist
defensive positions that cannot be countered even when they hold invalid claims — positions on the
same level as an honest claim but to its right — and the left-most payout rule is what makes them
worthless.<Cite src="op-specs-bond-incentives" />

The consequence is worth stating explicitly, because it changes what an honest challenger's software
has to do. The OP Stack documentation is direct: the maximum depth of a game is 73, but there can be
any number of claims and counter-claims within it, since many actors can participate in the same
game and effectively combine multiple disputes into one.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-explainer" />
Challengers are grouped by parity — challengers dispute claims at even depths, defenders at odd
ones.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" />

There is also a category of claim that is neither honest nor winning and must still be countered.
A **freeloader claim** is a counter to a bad claim made with the wrong pivot direction: it does not
cause the game to resolve incorrectly, but if left untouched its author reclaims their own bond. The
spec's conclusion is operational: the honest challenger must always counter freeloader claims for
incentive compatibility to be preserved.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" /> Honest software
therefore does strictly more work than "dispute the lies".

## The one step L1 actually runs

At `maxGameDepth`, positions correspond to indices in the execution trace, and the only way to
dispute a claim is to **step**.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" /> The interface is small
enough to read in full:

```solidity
/// @param _claimIndex The index of the challenged claim within `claimData`.
/// @param _isAttack   Whether or not the step is an attack or a defense.
/// @param _stateData  The preimage of the claim at the given prestate.
/// @param _proof      Proof to access memory nodes in the VM's merkle state tree.
function step(uint256 _claimIndex, bool _isAttack, bytes calldata _stateData, bytes calldata _proof)
    external;
```

<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" />

There are two step types, and they mirror the two moves. An **attack step** supplies the previous
state in the trace as input and expects the disputed claim's state as output, proving an invalid
transition. A **defence step** takes the disputed claim's state as input and expects the next state
as output, proving that the claim was an invalid attack. In both cases a claim must already exist in
the tree committing to the value supplied.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" />

That last requirement is the load-bearing one. You cannot invent a convenient pre-state at the last
moment; you can only step against states that somebody already bonded a claim on. The bisection
rounds are what produce those commitments.

### The oracle, and the reason it is a whole subsystem

The VM's state transition may need external data — a block header, an account proof, a preimage of a
hash it is asked to expand. It reads that data from a **PreimageOracle**, and the data has to be
on-chain before the step can run.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" />

Two routes exist. Some data is *local* to the game: an identifier table maps 1 to the parent L1 head
hash at proposal time, 2 to the starting output root, 3 to the disputed output root, 4 to the
disputed L2 block number, and 5 to the L2 chain ID.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" /> Global
keccak256 preimages are context-free and can be posted directly when small.

When they are not small, the design gets genuinely interesting. **Large preimage proposals** let a
submitter stream a preimage in over several transactions along with commitments to the intermediate
state of the keccak256 function after absorbing and permuting each 1088-bit block. The data is
progressively merkleised on-chain, one leaf per 136-byte chunk — exactly the keccak256 rate — each
leaf hashing the input chunk, the block index, and the 5×5 state matrix after that
permutation.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" /> A challenger who spots a wrong intermediate
state performs a single-step dispute *inside the hash function*: prove the agreed pre-state leaf,
prove the disputed post-state leaf, and let the contract absorb one block and run one SHA3
permutation on-chain.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" />

Read that twice. The bisect-then-execute-one-step pattern is applied recursively to the process of
loading data for the bisect-then-execute-one-step pattern. Standard parameters give large-preimage
proposals their own 24-hour challenge window, with a minimum proposal size of 126,000 bytes below
which the simple path is used instead.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-reference" /> And the clock
rules accommodate it: the top-up at depth `maxGameDepth − 1` is `clockExtension` *plus* that
24-hour preimage challenge period, because the move before a step may need to land a large preimage
proposal first.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-reference" />

## The VM: small enough to live in Solidity, faithful enough to be believed

Cannon is the OP Stack's fault-proof VM, and it is two programs that must agree exactly:

- **`MIPS64.sol`**, an EVM implementation that verifies the execution of a single MIPS instruction.
- **`mipsevm`**, a Go implementation that produces the proof for any MIPS instruction so it can be
  verified on-chain.<Cite src="op-docs-cannon" />

The program running *inside* Cannon is the fault proof program: `kona-client`, Rust compiled to the
`mips64-unknown-none` target as a big-endian 64-bit MIPS ELF binary.<Cite src="op-docs-cannon" />
It derives the L2 state from data its companion `kona-host` fetches, and it is built so that the
same inputs produce not only the same outputs but the *same execution trace* — which is what lets
every participant generate identical state witness hashes and therefore dispute the same instruction
index.<Cite src="op-docs-cannon" />

### Why the memory model is the interesting part

The mipsevm is 64-bit, so the addressable range is the full [0, 2⁶⁴ − 1]. Representing that on-chain
is impossible, so memory is stored as a **binary Merkle tree of fixed depth 59 with 32-byte leaves**.
Check the arithmetic — it is the cleanest derivation in the whole system:

```text
2^59 leaves × 32 bytes per leaf = 2^59 × 2^5 = 2^64 bytes
```

That is exactly the address space.<Cite src="op-docs-cannon" /> Nodes combine as
`out = keccak256(left ++ right)`, and individual leaf nodes are *not* hashed.<Cite src="op-docs-cannon" />
Pages are 4,096 bytes, which is also the minimum allocation granularity for the program inside the
VM.<Cite src="op-docs-cannon" />

**Subgoal: work out what the on-chain half therefore needs.** `MIPS64.sol` is mostly stateless. It
does not hold the memory space; it takes the memory Merkle root plus **up to two memory Merkle
proofs — one for the instruction being fetched, and one for a potential load, store, or certain
syscall instructions.**<Cite src="op-docs-cannon" /> Two proofs, because one MIPS instruction can
touch at most two memory locations: the word it is read from, and the word it operates on. The
off-chain `mipsevm` caches the last two pages for exactly the same reason — instructions are read
from one page while loads and stores hit another.<Cite src="op-docs-cannon" />

Everything about the memory design follows from a single requirement: *an L1 transaction must be
able to carry the whole justification for one instruction.*

### Determinism has a price, and the price is the kernel

Neither the on-chain nor the off-chain implementation has access to a kernel. The `syscall`
instruction simulates a minimal subset of a Linux kernel — enough to allocate memory, read from and
write to certain file descriptors, and exit. A defined set of further syscalls are implemented as
no-ops, and anything outside the supported set halts the VM.<Cite src="op-docs-cannon" />

This is the concrete meaning of "the VM must be deterministic". Not an abstract property: a specific
list of things the fault proof program is forbidden to do, because they have no on-chain
counterpart. If a single instruction could yield two results, the dispute could not be settled at
all — the loser would simply produce the other answer.

Endianness gets the same treatment. The mipsevm always handles big-endian words regardless of the
machine running Cannon, which means `MIPS64.sol` needs no byte swapping since the EVM is big-endian
too.<Cite src="op-docs-cannon" /> A representation choice made to shrink a Solidity contract.

### The VM is a plug, not a foundation

The dispute game is modular: every game is created through a `DisputeGameFactory` with a `GameType`,
a `uint32` selecting which registered implementation the factory clones.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-reference" /><Cite src="op-specs-dispute-game-interface" />
The registry as documented includes `CANNON` (0), `PERMISSIONED_CANNON` (1), `ASTERISC` (2),
`ASTERISC_KONA` (3), `OP_SUCCINCT` (6), `CANNON_KONA` (8), `ZK_DISPUTE_GAME` (10) and
`KAILUA` (1337), among others.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-reference" />

The Karst upgrade — Upgrade 19, which activated on Sepolia on 17 June 2026 and on mainnet on
8 July 2026 at the superchain default — promoted `CANNON_KONA` (game type 8) to the respected game
type, making `kona-client` the primary fault proof program used for withdrawals.<Cite src="op-docs-karst-hardfork" />
`op-geth` and `op-program` do not support Karst; the legacy Go program reached end of
support.<Cite src="op-docs-karst-hardfork" /><Cite src="op-docs-cannon" />

Note precisely what changed and what did not. The program running on the VM changed language and
implementation. The tree, the moves, the step, the bonds and the trust model did not. That
separation is the point of the game-type indirection, and it is where lesson six will start.

## Arbitrum solves the same problem with a different shape

Worth seeing, because the differences are informative rather than cosmetic.

BoLD does not use one tree with a split depth. It plays the same bisection game at **three levels of
granularity**, each opened as a sub-challenge:<Cite src="arbitrum-bold-deep-dive" />

    { label: 'BigStep level', cells: ['BigSteps, each of 2²³ WASM steps', '2¹⁹  (2¹⁹ × 2²³ = 2⁴²)'] },
    { label: 'SmallStep level', cells: ['single WASM steps', '2²³'] },
  ]}
  caption="Arbitrum's BoLD bisects three times over three units where the OP Stack bisects once over two."
  alt="BoLD's three sub-challenge levels: block level up to 2^26 child-chain blocks between two assertions, BigStep level up to 2^19 BigSteps of 2^23 WASM steps each, SmallStep level up to 2^23 single WASM steps."
/>

The reason for the levels is stated as a space argument: playing one flat bisection game over 2⁴²
hashes would be unreasonable, because a history commitment would have to materialise all of
them.<Cite src="arbitrum-bold-deep-dive" /> The unit of a claim is an **edge** carrying a history
commitment — a Merkle commitment to a list of hashes from a start state to an end state — and a
bisection move on an edge produces two children committing to each half. The documentation's worked
example: an edge committing to 33 hashes bisects into two edges of 17 hashes
each.<Cite src="arbitrum-bold-deep-dive" />

The final move is a **one-step proof** submitted to an on-chain WASM emulator, which runs the
disputed step and declares a winner.<Cite src="arbitrum-bold-deep-dive" /><Cite src="arbitrum-bold-gentle-intro" />
Same terminal move, different instruction set: WASM — strictly, WAVM — rather than
MIPS64.<Cite src="arbitrum-bold-deep-dive" />

<Aside kind="warn">
The BoLD economics page describes the space of disagreements as of max size 2⁶⁹, while the three
documented per-level maxima multiply to 2²⁶ × 2⁴² = 2⁶⁸.<Cite src="arbitrum-bold-economics" /><Cite src="arbitrum-bold-deep-dive" />
One bit apart, most likely a leaf-counting convention — history commitments over n steps have n + 1
leaves — but the docs do not reconcile it and neither will this lesson. Cite the per-level figures,
which are stated as protocol maxima, rather than the headline.
</Aside>

The deeper structural difference is that Arbitrum charges a separate challenge bond *per level*
(555 WETH at big-step, 79 WETH at small-step on Arbitrum One), where the OP Stack charges a bond per
*move* on a smoothly rising curve.<Cite src="arbitrum-bold-economics" /><Cite src="op-specs-bond-incentives" />
Two ways of pricing the same resource-exhaustion risk. The next two lessons are about what that risk
actually is.

<Check type="predict" concept="interactive-bisection">
A chain operator proposes cutting `maxGameDepth` from 73 to 40 to "make disputes cheaper and
faster", keeping `splitDepth` at 30. They point out, correctly, that the deepest bonds dominate the
cost and that 40 rounds still terminates quickly.

Work out what actually happens. Then say what you would change instead, if the goal is genuinely to
reduce the capital an honest challenger must hold.

<Answer>
**1. The change silently caps the work an L2 block may do.** With `splitDepth` 30 and `maxGameDepth`
73, the execution-trace subtree is 43 levels deep, giving 2⁴³ ≈ 8.8 trillion VM steps per L2
block.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-reference" /> At depth 40 the subtree is 10 levels: 2¹⁰ =
1,024 steps. A thousand MIPS instructions cannot derive an L2 block — it is not enough to parse a
header. The spec's stated requirement for the max depth is that it be large enough for the VM
execution trace to fit in the leaves.<Cite src="op-specs-configurability" /> This configuration
violates it outright.

**2. The failure mode is not "disputes get cheaper". It is "honest parties cannot reach a step".**
Bisection bottoms out at `maxGameDepth`, where the only permitted move is `step`.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" />
If the real divergence lies beyond the representable trace, the leaves do not commit to real machine
states, and the step cannot be constructed against a claim that means anything. The game still
resolves — by clock — which is precisely the wrong way for it to resolve.

**3. The saving they are aiming at is real but comes from the wrong lever.** They are right that
depth dominates cost: depths 70 through 73 alone account for roughly 210 ETH of the ~691 ETH total
along a path.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-reference" /> But that is a consequence of the bond
schedule, not of the tree. The gas amount scales from 400,000 at depth 0 to 300,000,000 at max
depth, and the 300,000,000 endpoint was chosen to cover roughly double the cost of a max-size large
preimage proposal.<Cite src="op-specs-bond-incentives" /> Depth and cost are coupled through that
endpoint, not through the depth alone.

**4. What I would change instead — and what each change gives up.**

- *Lower the top-end gas figure.* Directly reduces the deep bonds. Gives up deterrence against the
  most expensive attack the schedule was sized for, which is preimage-related. You must first
  establish that a max-size large preimage proposal cannot occur on this chain, and that is a claim
  about the fault proof program, not about the game.
- *Raise `splitDepth`.* Moving the split down the tree buys execution-trace levels without changing
  the total depth, at the cost of output-root capacity: the split must still be large enough to
  represent every L2 block since the anchor state.<Cite src="op-specs-configurability" />
- *Make honest capital poolable rather than smaller.* This is Arbitrum's answer to the same problem —
  trustless bonding pools, so that a high bond does not imply a small set of possible
  defenders.<Cite src="arbitrum-bold-deep-dive" /> It leaves the deterrence intact and attacks the
  centralisation consequence directly.

**5. The general lesson.** `maxGameDepth` is not a tuning knob for cost. It is a statement about how
much computation the VM may be asked to prove. Reading a parameter as a cost dial when it is
actually a capacity is how proof systems get quietly disabled by well-meaning configuration changes.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson** takes the number you have just derived — at most 73 moves down one path — and
uses it to explain the seven-day challenge window. The window is not sized against how long a proof
takes to compute. It is sized against how long an adversary could keep a defender's moves out of L1
blocks, and the research that formalises it does so as a function of the number of protocol moves
required and the budgets on each side.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-proof" />

**The lesson after that** attacks the game rather than the state: not trying to win, but trying to
make the honest side run out of clock, capital, or attention. Everything in this lesson — freeloader
claims, the left-most payout rule, per-level bonds, the exponential schedule — is a defence against
that class of attack, and it reads much better in reverse.

**And practically**, the toy bisection game in this module's practice set is worth building. Two
parties, a Merkle root over a trace, `log₂ N` on-chain rounds, and a single-step settlement. Once you
have written a `step` that compares a claimed post-state against one you computed, the phrase
"L1 executes one instruction" stops being a slogan.
