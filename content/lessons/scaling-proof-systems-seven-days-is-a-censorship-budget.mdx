Find the sentence in your own team's incident runbook that says something like: *if a bad state root
is proposed, we will challenge it.*

Now count what that sentence assumes. Software running. Software correctly configured for this
specific chain. A rollup node synced to the canonical chain and believed. Historical data still
retrievable. ETH liquid, in the right account, in the right amount. A transaction reaching an L1
block. And all of it inside a clock that the adversary is also playing against.

Seven of those are operational facts about an organisation. One is cryptography. This lesson is
about the seven, and about the parameter that exists to give them room: the challenge window, which
is not a compute budget, is not one number, and is not the same object as the delay your users
experience.

## "Seven days" is at least four different parameters

Start by taking the headline apart, because on the OP Stack the user-visible week and the game's own
clock are separate settings that happen to coincide.

| Parameter | Standard value | Contract | What it governs |
|---|---|---|---|
| `maxClockDuration` | 302,400 s = 3.5 days | `FaultDisputeGame` | Total chess-clock time **each side** may accumulate |
| `clockExtension` | 10,800 s = 3 hours | `FaultDisputeGame` | Minimum time guaranteed to respond when a clock is nearly spent |
| `proofMaturityDelaySeconds` | 604,800 s = 7 days | `OptimismPortal2` | Delay between proving a withdrawal and finalising it |
| `disputeGameFinalityDelaySeconds` | 302,400 s = 3.5 days | `AnchorStateRegistry` | Delay after a game resolves before its result counts as final |
| `withdrawalDelaySeconds` | 302,400 s = 3.5 days | `DelayedWETH` | Delay between unlocking a bond and withdrawing it |
| `challengePeriodSeconds` | 86,400 s = 24 hours | `PreimageOracle` | Window to dispute a large-preimage proposal |

**Subgoal: notice that the game's clock budget is not stated as seven days anywhere.** It is 3.5
days *per side*. Two sides, so a game that runs both clocks to exhaustion consumes seven days of
wall time — but that is an arithmetic consequence of a per-team parameter, not a configured total.
The specification's stated reason for the 3.5-day figure is that it allows enough time for honest
actors to counter invalid claims.<Cite src="op-specs-configurability" />

**Subgoal: read each delay's own justification rather than assuming they share one.** They do not.

- `proofMaturityDelaySeconds` of 7 days is described as an excessively safe upper bound that leaves
  enough time to consider social-layer solutions to a hack, and enough time for other network
  participants to challenge the integrity of the corresponding output root.<Cite src="op-specs-configurability" />
- `disputeGameFinalityDelaySeconds` of 3.5 days exists to give the **Guardian** role time to
  blacklist a resolved dispute game before withdrawals proven against it can be
  finalised.<Cite src="op-specs-configurability" />
- `withdrawalDelaySeconds` of 7 days on `DelayedWETH` exists so the Guardian can recover funds if
  bonds were allocated incorrectly.<Cite src="op-specs-configurability" />

Three different actors are being given time: the public, the Guardian, and the Guardian again. Only
the first is about fraud proofs at all. If you have ever wondered why the delay never seems to shrink
as proving technology improves, this table is the answer — proving speed is not an input to any of
these three numbers.

**Subgoal: compose them into the timeline a user actually experiences.** Chain the parameters in the
order they bind:

Note that the delays overlap rather than stack cleanly, which is exactly why quoting one number is
misleading in both directions. In the uncontested case the seven-day maturity delay dominates and
you get the familiar week. In the contested case the game itself can eat the clock budget first.

## What the window is actually sized against

Arbitrum's documentation states the reasoning in one sentence: the dispute period is seven days
because that is seen as the maximum period of time an adversary could delay Ethereum before social
intervention, originally proposed by Vitalik Buterin.<Cite src="arbitrum-bold-economics" />

Read the shape of that claim. It is not *how long a proof takes*. It is not even *how long a dispute
takes*. It is **how long censorship can plausibly persist before the base layer's social layer
reacts** — a claim about Ethereum, not about the rollup.

Arbitrum One's own configured challenge period is 6.4 days by default, changeable by the
DAO.<Cite src="arbitrum-bold-gentle-intro" /><Cite src="arbitrum-bold-deep-dive" /> The same
documentation set also describes the dispute window as "approximately seven days" in its economics
discussion, so read 6.4 days as the parameter and seven days as the design
target.<Cite src="arbitrum-bold-economics" />

**Subgoal: connect the window to the number of moves, because that is the load-bearing link.** The
academic treatment of this question models an attacker who censors a defender's transactions by
bribing block proposers. At each step, the attacker either censors — depleting the defender's time
allowance at the cost of the bribe — or lets a transaction through, conserving funds for later. The
paper analyses three game-theoretic models and determines **the challenge period length required to
ensure the defender's success, as a function of the number of required protocol moves and the
players' available budgets.**<Cite src="arxiv-economic-censorship-games" />

That is the sentence to carry out of this lesson. The window is not a constant; it is a function
with two arguments, and you met one of them in the previous lesson. A game of `maxGameDepth` 73
requires up to 73 moves down a path.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-reference" /> Each move is a
transaction that must land on L1 against an adversary who would rather it did not. **Deeper tree,
more moves to censor, longer window required for the same safety.** The clock extension exists in
the same spirit: when a team's clock is nearly spent, a move tops it back up to at least three
hours, so an attacker cannot win purely by draining time at the last
instant.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" />

**Subgoal: work out whose clock is running, because it is not obvious.** A claim inherits the clock
of its *grandparent* claim in the tree, which is what makes the clock per-team rather than
per-claim: claims at alternating depths belong to alternating teams, so a claim's grandparent is the
previous claim on its own side.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" /><Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-reference" />
Making a move resumes the clock for the disputed claim and pauses it for the newly added one — a
chess clock, exactly as the specification names it. A move against a claim becomes impossible once
the parent of that claim has accumulated `maxClockDuration`.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" />

The extension has a stated reason that only makes sense once you know about freeloader claims: a
challenger who must counter one inherits a malicious party's clock, so without a top-up the attacker
could hand the defender an almost-empty clock and win on time. The extension applies only to the
potential grandchild's clock, which bounds the total extension a game can accumulate and makes it
scale with `MAX_GAME_DEPTH`.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" />

BoLD attacks the same problem from the other end, by bounding the total. Its guarantee is that
disputes resolve within a fixed period, and the documentation states the composition explicitly:
assertions have a 6.4-day dispute window, resolution adds another, so a dispute opened at the last
second delays confirmation to a maximum of **14 days**.<Cite src="arbitrum-bold-economics" /> The
gentle introduction adds the operational trimmings — two challenge periods, plus a two-day grace
period for the Security Council to intervene, plus a small delta for
computation.<Cite src="arbitrum-bold-gentle-intro" />

The claim being made there is a bound, not a speedup. Nothing got faster. What changed is that the
worst case became a number.

## The honest challenger, written out as a requirements list

Now the operational half, and this is where documentation is far more useful than theory, because
the people who wrote it had to run the thing.

`op-challenger` is the software that plays the honest side. It relies on a **synced, trusted rollup
node** plus a trace provider such as Cannon, and its decision rule is stated in terms of that
node: if the trusted node agrees with a proposed output, do nothing; if it disagrees, post a
counter-claim.<Cite src="op-docs-challenger" />

Then comes the sentence that should change how you read the phrase "honest challenger":

> The honest challenger assumes that their rollup node is synced to the canonical state and that the
> fault proof program is correct, so it is willing to put its money on the line to counter any
> faults.<Cite src="op-docs-challenger" />

Honesty here is *relative to your own node*. A challenger with a desynced or misconfigured node is
not dishonest; it is confidently wrong, and it bonds real ETH on being wrong. The proof system does
not distinguish those cases, and neither will your bond.

The migration guide turns the rest of the assumption into a shopping list:<Cite src="op-docs-migrating-permissionless" />

- **Capital.** Maintain a minimum balance of 50 ETH, with access to a large pool of ETH for attack
  scenarios, and monitoring to ensure funds are always available. Note what this is *for*: in a
  permissioned game you rarely post claims, so the requirement is invisible; in a permissionless
  game the challenger posts a bond with every move.
- **Disk.** About 50 GB, enough to cover a couple of invalid games — more if the challenger must
  respond to more of them. Nominally zero when nobody is lying.
- **The prestate must match exactly.** The challenger refuses to interact with games whose on-chain
  absolute prestate does not match the one it holds. A hash mismatch is not a degraded mode; it is a
  challenger that silently does not defend your chain.
- **Verification that the configuration is right.** `op-challenger run-trace --run cannon-kona` runs
  the fault proof program in the Cannon VM against live L2 blocks, and you wait for
  `Successfully verified output root` in the logs. Any errors indicate a misconfiguration.

And one requirement that catches people because it is about data rather than money.

<Aside kind="warn">
**No blob archiver, no defence — but only below a certain depth.** The op-challenger FAQ addresses
this directly: an actor without access to historical data who is pushed into the bottom half of the
game, around 32 claims deep, would log errors and be unable to respond. They would have their
3.5 days of clock to fix it by switching to a beacon node that has the blobs.<Cite src="op-docs-challenger" />
The saving grace is structural: an actor is only pushed that deep if the disputed block is older
than the blob retention period, roughly 18 days, and while valid proposals resolve regularly, each
one becomes the anchor for new games — so normally only about 3.5 days of blocks are ever in
dispute.<Cite src="op-docs-challenger" />

Which means the data requirement is a *conditional* dependency that appears only after the proof
system has already been failing for a while. That is the worst possible shape for an operational
requirement, because it is invisible in every drill.
</Aside>

## Permissionless, or a `uint32` that says so

Which brings us to the difference between a proof system that binds anyone and one that does not.

**Subgoal: locate where permissionlessness physically lives.** Every dispute game is created through
the `DisputeGameFactory` with a `GameType`, a `uint32`. The **respected game type** — the type whose
results the system accepts for withdrawals and anchor state updates — is stored in the
`AnchorStateRegistry` and can be changed by the Guardian via
`setRespectedGameType`.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-reference" />

The migration guide shows the transaction. It is one call:

```text
cast calldata "setRespectedGameType(uint32)" 0
# respectedGameType in AnchorStateRegistry: 1 (PERMISSIONED) → 0 (CANNON)
```

<Cite src="op-docs-migrating-permissionless" />

That single call is what "the chain went permissionless" means. The guide also notes the
consequence, which is not cosmetic: all in-progress withdrawals must be re-proven against a new
`FaultDisputeGame` created after the update.<Cite src="op-docs-migrating-permissionless" />

**Subgoal: notice that the arrow points both ways.** The configurability spec states that the
respected game type may be changed to `PERMISSIONED_CANNON` as a fallback to permissioned proposals
in the event of a failure in the fault proof system.<Cite src="op-specs-configurability" /> The
fault proofs explainer describes the same capability from the security side: the Guardian can pause
withdrawals, blacklist games, or revert to a permissioned system, and the stated trust assumption is
therefore that the Guardian does not act to intervene.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-explainer" />

So "is challenging permissionless on this chain?" is a question with a live answer, held in a
storage slot, owned by a role. It is not a property of the stack, and it is not a property of the
proof family.

**Subgoal: understand why every OP Stack chain starts permissioned.** Not policy — a circular
dependency. The absolute prestate must be built from the chain's genesis and rollup configuration,
which are themselves outputs of deploying the L1 contracts; the migration guide names this
explicitly as the reason chains cannot be deployed directly to the permissionless fault proof
system, and `op-deployer` deploys new standard chains with the permissioned game, type 1, as the
initial game type.<Cite src="op-docs-migrating-permissionless" /><Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-reference" />
Every chain's default state is the one where the proof system binds nobody. Getting out of it is a
project someone has to fund and finish.

### How the maturity framework grades this

L2BEAT's Stages framework, on the page as last updated 23 July 2025, asks at Stage 0 whether the
project uses a proper proof system and whether **at least five external actors can submit a fraud
proof** — a quantified floor, not a yes/no.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" /> The framework's Stage 2
requirement asks whether the fraud proof system is fully permissionless and open to
everyone.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" /> The framework has been revised several times since its
introduction in June 2023 and is expected to keep evolving, so treat the live page as the
authority on which requirement sits at which rung rather than any secondary
summary.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />

The most useful thing on that page for this lesson is not a requirement at all, but the assumption
attached to Stage 1:

> If the proposer set is open to anyone with enough resources, we assume at least one live proposer
> at any time (i.e. 1-of-N assumption with unbounded N). We don't assume it to be
> non-censoring.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />

That is the one-honest-challenger assumption written down as an assumption rather than as a
guarantee — including the clause that it is *not* assumed to be non-censoring, which is precisely
why the window has to be long enough to survive censorship. The framework is also explicit that it
measures decentralisation and not security: a team can deploy a very unsafe Stage 2 project, for
instance with an experimental proof system or unaudited contracts.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" /> Stage
is a statement about who can override the system, not about whether the system is correct.

Your product holds user funds on an OP Stack chain that migrated to permissionless fault proofs
eight months ago. Your risk register says: "Fault proofs are permissionless. Invalid state roots
will be challenged."

Rewrite that entry so it is true. For each condition you add, say how you would check it — and name
the one that is hardest to verify from outside.

<Answer>
**Rewritten entry, as a conjunction rather than a fact:** *Invalid state roots will be rejected
provided that at least one party is running a correctly configured challenger, holds sufficient
liquid ETH for bonds at the depths a dispute reaches, can retrieve the data a step requires, and can
land transactions on L1 within a 3.5-day per-side clock — and provided the Guardian does not revert
the respected game type or blacklist the resolving game.*

**Checks, in order of how cheaply they can be done from outside.**

1. *Is the respected game type still permissionless?* Read it. `respectedGameType()` on the
`AnchorStateRegistry` returns the live `uint32`; the Guardian can change it with
`setRespectedGameType`.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-reference" /><Cite src="op-docs-migrating-permissionless" />
This is a one-call check and it is the only one that is genuinely easy, so do it on a schedule
rather than once.

2. *Are games actually being created and resolved?* `gameCount()` and `findLatestGames` on the
`DisputeGameFactory`, plus each game's `status()`.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-reference" /> A
chain where the respected type is permissionless but proposals stopped is not safer than one that
never migrated.

3. *Does more than one party defend?* L2BEAT's Stage 0 requirement asks for at least five external
actors able to submit a fraud proof, which is the framework's own acknowledgement that "anyone may"
and "several do" are different claims.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" /> On-chain you can only see who
*has* moved, which is a lower bound and usually zero on a healthy chain.

4. *Is the challenger funded?* The migration guidance is a 50 ETH floor plus access to a large pool,
with monitoring.<Cite src="op-docs-migrating-permissionless" /> From outside you can watch balances
of addresses that have posted claims. You cannot see the pool behind them.

5. *Does it hold the right prestate and can it fetch historical data?* This is the hard one, and it
is the answer to the question. A challenger whose absolute prestate does not match refuses to
interact with games at all,<Cite src="op-docs-migrating-permissionless" /> and one without a blob
archiver cannot respond below roughly 32 claims deep once the disputed block passes the blob
retention period.<Cite src="op-docs-challenger" /> Both failures are invisible from outside and
invisible on the happy path, because a challenger that never has to move looks identical to a
challenger that cannot move. The only external evidence would be a dispute that went unanswered —
which is the event you were trying to prevent.

**What I would actually do about number 5.** Ask the operator two questions with checkable answers:
whether they run `op-challenger run-trace` continuously and what it logs, and which beacon node or
blob archiver the challenger points at.<Cite src="op-docs-migrating-permissionless" /> An operator
who cannot answer both has a proof system that is permissionless and undefended, which is a
different risk from a permissioned one but not obviously a smaller one.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson** inverts everything here. Once you accept that safety rests on an honest party
staying funded, synced and unimpeded for days, the efficient attack is obvious: do not try to win
the dispute. Try to exhaust the defender. That is the delay attack, and the bond schedules from the
first two lessons are the counterweight.

**Then the proving side**, where the equivalent assumption is that somebody keeps proving. It fails
differently and it is priced differently, but it is the same category of dependency.

**And in your own diligence**, the shift worth making is from "does this chain have fault proofs" to
"what is the current value of `respectedGameType`, who can change it, and who is funded to play". The
first question has been answered yes on every major chain for a while now. The second is where the
variance lives, and L2BEAT's per-project risk rows are the place to start rather than
finish.<Cite src="l2beat-summary" />
