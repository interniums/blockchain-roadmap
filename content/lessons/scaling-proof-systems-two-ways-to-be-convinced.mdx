Here are two lines from two different chain operators' budget spreadsheets.

```text
Chain A, proof system operating cost, twelve months
  Jan  0.00   Feb  0.00   Mar  0.00   Apr  0.00
  May  0.00   Jun  0.00   Jul  0.00   Aug  0.00
  Sep  0.00   Oct  0.00   Nov  0.00   Dec  0.00

Chain B, proof system operating cost, twelve months
  Jan  ####   Feb  ####   Mar  ####   Apr  ####
  ... every month, forever
```

Chain A runs a fraud proof system. Chain B runs a validity proof system. The naive reading is that
Chain A's proof system is free and Chain B's is not, and the naive reading is wrong in a specific
way worth an entire lesson: **Chain A's cost is not zero, it is contingent.** It sits off the income
statement as a capital reserve that must exist, be liquid, and be defended, and in the month it is
finally spent it is measured in hundreds of ETH.

You already know the shape of both arrangements from
[two ways to convince L1](/c/optimistic-rollup). This module takes them apart. This first lesson
does the accounting, because almost every design argument you will hear about proof systems is an
argument about cost wearing a security costume, and it is much easier to see through once the four
cost lines are named separately.

## Both families adjudicate exactly the same predicate

Start by removing a difference that does not exist.

A rollup's state transition function is a pure function. Given a prior state root, the published
inputs for a range, and the rollup's rules, there is exactly one correct resulting state root. Call
the claim under adjudication **P**: *the posted root equals STF(prior root, published inputs)*.

A fraud proof and a validity proof both decide P. They differ only in **when the work of deciding
happens** and **who pays for it**:

- A **validity proof** decides P before L1 accepts the root. Someone computes a succinct argument
  that P holds, and an L1 contract checks that argument.<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" />
- A **fraud proof** decides P only if somebody objects. L1 accepts the root provisionally, and the
  decision procedure is a dispute protocol that runs only on disagreement.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-explainer" />

Neither decides anything else. Neither says the inputs were published, that the ordering was fair,
or that the contract holding your money still belongs to the people who deployed it. Hold that
thought until the last lesson of this module; it is the whole argument there.

## Family one: a contingent liability with a fat tail

The OP Stack's fault dispute game gives us real numbers, because the parameters are normative rather
than marketing. A standard chain's game is a binary tree of claims with `maxGameDepth` 73 and
`splitDepth` 30; each side plays against a chess clock with `maxClockDuration` of 302,400 seconds —
3.5 days — and a `clockExtension` of 10,800 seconds.<Cite src="op-specs-configurability" /><Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-reference" />
Every move in that game requires a bond.

**Subgoal: price the normal case, where nothing is disputed.** Creating a game costs the
initialization bond for its game type.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-reference" /> The OP Stack
documentation states that on OP Mainnet parameters, with proposals posted hourly, that is 0.08 ETH
per hour, so covering a seven-day dispute window needs roughly 14 ETH including gas.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-explainer" />
Check the arithmetic yourself: 0.08 × 24 × 7 = 13.44 ETH of bonds outstanding at any moment, plus
gas. That is the entire recurring cost of running the proof system when nobody lies. It is a
*working-capital* cost — the ETH is not spent, it is immobilised — and it recycles.

**Subgoal: price the disputed case, which is the one the reserve exists for.** Bond size is not
flat. The bond specification prices moves in gas and then applies an assumed fixed base fee of
200 gwei, with the gas charged scaling from a baseline of 400,000 gas at the top of the tree to
300,000,000 gas at maximum depth.<Cite src="op-specs-bond-incentives" /> The multiplier follows
directly from those two endpoints and the depth:

```text
x = (300,000,000 / 400,000) ^ (1/73) = 750 ^ (1/73) = 1.09493
bond(depth N) in gas = 400,000 × 1.09493^N
```

Convert the two endpoints to ETH at the assumed 200 gwei:

```text
depth  0 :   400,000 gas × 200 gwei = 0.08 ETH
depth 73 : 300,000,000 gas × 200 gwei = 60.0  ETH
```

Both land exactly on the documented figures — 0.08 ETH at depth 0 and 60 ETH at depth 73.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-reference" />

**Subgoal: total the whole path, because a dispute is not one move.** A game played to maximum depth
posts a bond at every depth along the path. That is a geometric series with ratio 1.09493:

```text
sum over depths 1..73 = 400,000 × (1.09493^73 − 1) / 0.09493 × 200 gwei
                      = 400,000 × 7,889 gas-units × 200 gwei
                      ≈ 691 ETH
```

The reference page gives the same total: roughly 691 ETH, split about 361 ETH for the side moving at
odd depths and 330 ETH for the side at even depths, with depths 70 through 73 alone accounting for
about 210 ETH.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-reference" />

**Subgoal: notice the shape of the distribution, not just its mean.** Nearly a third of the total
sits in the last four moves. That is deliberate — the deepest moves are the expensive ones to
respond to, including the large-preimage path — but it has a consequence for whoever is defending:
you cannot budget for the average move. You must be able to fund the *last* move, at the moment when
capital is already locked in every move above it.

<Aside kind="note">
The op-challenger FAQ quotes a slightly different figure — just over 631.2 ETH to play one game to
the final step, about 315.6 ETH per side.<Cite src="op-docs-challenger" /> That is the same series
summed over depths 0 through 72 rather than 1 through 73; run both sums and you get 631.4 and 691.3.
Neither number is wrong. It is a good reminder that a bond total is only meaningful with its
summation range attached, which is the same discipline this module will apply to proving costs.
</Aside>

So Chain A's twelve zeroes are honest and incomplete. The real line item is: *14 ETH immobilised
continuously, plus a standing obligation to be able to produce several hundred ETH on a few hours'
notice, in a market where the adversary chose the timing.* The op-challenger documentation states
the requirement without softening it: the combined funding of all honest actors must exceed the
funding available to an attacker, and operators should have significant funds available at short
notice.<Cite src="op-docs-challenger" />

Arbitrum prices the same liability differently and arrives at the same shape. BoLD requires a
one-time assertion bond — 3,600 WETH on Arbitrum One and Nova — plus per-level challenge bonds of
555 WETH at the big-step level and 79 WETH at the small-step level.<Cite src="arbitrum-bold-deep-dive" /><Cite src="arbitrum-bold-economics" />
Same structure: a standing deposit to participate at all, and a much larger contingent draw when
somebody actually fights.

## Family two: a subscription with a hardware floor

Now the other family, and the most useful thing to notice is *how the people specifying it choose to
write the requirement down.*

The Ethereum Foundation's definition of realtime proving for an L1 zkEVM is not a price. It is a
four-part engineering envelope:<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" />

| Dimension | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Latency | ≤ 10 s for the 99th percentile of mainnet blocks |
| On-prem capital expenditure | ≤ 100,000 USD |
| On-prem power | ≤ 10 kW |
| Proof size | ≤ 300 KiB, with no trusted setups |
| Security | ≥ 128 bits, with 100 bits accepted in initial months |

Two of those five lines are a hardware budget and one is an electricity budget. That is the tell.
Proving is not a fee somebody charges; it is a physical process with a power draw, and the
Foundation wrote the spec that way because the target is home proving by solo stakers, where the
binding constraint is that most residential supplies deliver about 10 kW.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" />
The latency figure follows from the same physical reasoning: a 12-second slot minus roughly
1.5 seconds of network propagation leaves 10 seconds.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" />

**Subgoal: notice the workload attached to that latency.** The EF target is one L1 block. Not an L2
batch, not a day of L2 activity. When Succinct reported meeting it, the claim carried its full
scope: 99.7% of L1 Ethereum blocks proved under 12 seconds and 95.4% under 10 seconds, on a cluster
of 16 NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPUs, measured over 954 blocks between block 23,807,739 and 23,812,008,
published 18 November 2025.<Cite src="succinct-realtime-proving" /> Workload, hardware, sample size,
block range, date. That is what a proving number looks like when it is usable. Most are not
reported that way, and lesson five of this module is about what to do when they are not.

The cost line, unlike the fraud-proof line, is **unconditional**. The cluster runs whether or not
anyone is attacking, whether the chain is busy or idle, whether it is Sunday or a Tuesday. It scales
with throughput, because more gas is more proving. And it has no equivalent on the optimistic side,
where in the normal case nothing at all is computed for the purpose of proving.

  reality="Verification is cheap forever. Generation is a recurring hardware and energy bill per batch, which is exactly why the Ethereum Foundation's realtime-proving definition is written as a capital-expenditure ceiling and a power ceiling rather than as a fee."

## The four costs, side by side

Naming them separately is the point of this lesson, because arguments about proof systems almost
always conflate at least two.

Read the last row carefully, because it is the row people assume is empty on the right. It is not.
A validity rollup does not depend on anyone *watching*, but it absolutely depends on someone
*proving*. If the prover stops, no new state roots are accepted, and withdrawals freeze just as
surely as if a challenger had gone dark. The dependency changed shape; it did not disappear.

## What neither family gives you

Both mechanisms decide P and nothing else. That means all of the following remain untouched by which
proof family a chain uses:

- **Whether the inputs were published at all.** P is a claim about a function of published inputs.
  If the inputs are not available, the claim is unfalsifiable and irrelevant.
- **Whether the ordering was fair.** Reordering within the rules produces a valid state transition.
  Both proof systems accept it, because it is not fraud.
- **Whether the contracts still do what they did yesterday.** An upgrade replaces the verifier, the
  game implementation, or the portal. Neither proof constrains that.
- **Whether the mechanism is switched on.** A chain can hold a fully specified dispute game and
  operate a permissioned variant of it. That is a configuration, not a proof property.

The first of those belongs to [data availability](/c/rollup-validium-optimium); the last two are
where this module ends.

<Check type="explain" concept="fraud-proof">
An operator is choosing between a fault proof system and a validity proof system for a new chain,
and asks you which is cheaper to run. Their board wants one number.

Explain why the question as posed cannot be answered, and say what you would put in front of the
board instead — using only what this lesson established.

<Answer>
**The question conflates a flow with a reserve.** The validity side's cost is a *flow*: a compute
and energy bill that accrues every batch, whether or not anything goes wrong. The optimistic side's
main cost is a *reserve*: capital that must exist and be liquid but is not consumed in the normal
case. Adding them into one number requires a cost of capital and an attack probability, and the
operator has neither of those to hand — so any single figure is an assumption wearing a result's
clothing.

**What I would put in front of the board is four lines, not one.**

1. *Recurring compute.* Validity: continuous, hardware-bound, scaling with throughput. Optimistic:
zero in the normal case.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" />

2. *Standing capital.* Optimistic: on OP Mainnet parameters, hourly proposals at 0.08 ETH each over
a seven-day window means about 14 ETH permanently immobilised, plus gas.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-explainer" />
Validity: effectively none.

3. *Contingent capital, and its timing.* Optimistic: playing one game to maximum depth costs roughly
691 ETH in bonds across the path, about 361 ETH for the side moving at odd depths and 330 for the
other, with roughly 210 ETH of that concentrated in depths 70 to 73.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-reference" />
This is the line that decides the answer, and it is the line a single number hides, because the
adversary chooses when it lands. On Arbitrum One the equivalent standing figure is a 3,600 WETH
assertion bond with 555 and 79 WETH per-level challenge bonds.<Cite src="arbitrum-bold-economics" />

4. *Liveness dependency.* Optimistic: someone funded and uncensored must be watching during the
window.<Cite src="op-docs-challenger" /> Validity: someone must keep proving. Both are people
problems; neither is zero.

**The one framing sentence.** The optimistic system converts a compute bill into a capital reserve
and a delay. Whether that is a good trade depends on the operator's cost of capital and on whether
they can actually raise several hundred ETH inside a chess clock — which is an organisational
question, not a cryptographic one.

**What I would not say.** That one is "cheaper". Published proving-cost figures are not comparable
across sources; some quote cost per proof and others per transaction, and the values span orders of
magnitude across different years and workloads. Lesson five of this module works through why.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson** opens the fraud proof and shows the mechanism the bond curve is pricing: a
binary search over an execution trace, ending in a single instruction replayed inside an on-chain
virtual machine. Once you have seen why the tree is 73 deep, the exponential bond schedule stops
looking arbitrary.

**Then the challenge window**, which is not a compute budget and not a proving budget. It is a
censorship budget, and the research that sizes it does so as a function of the number of protocol
moves a defender must land and the budgets on each side — which is a direct consequence of the tree
depth you will have just met.<Cite src="arxiv-economic-censorship-games" />

**And in your own diligence**, the four-line table above is the artefact worth keeping. When a chain
tells you its proof system, you have learned one fact and you still owe yourself four questions:
what recurs, what is reserved, what is contingent, and who has to stay alive. The rest of this
module answers them one at a time — and the last lesson explains why the answers do not sort chains
into the ranking everyone expects.<Cite src="succinct-op-succinct" />
