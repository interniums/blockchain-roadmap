Four figures, all of them describing "what a ZK proof costs", all of them published by people who
measured something real:

Not one pair is comparable. Two are per proof and two per transaction, the two per-transaction
figures do not measure the same transactions, and the two per-proof figures are two years apart on
different hardware for different workloads. Averaging them, ranking them, or picking the one that
supports your slide are all the same mistake.

This lesson gives you the arithmetic that converts between the units where conversion is possible,
the questions that turn a figure into a fact, and the one place that publishes cost and latency with
their scope attached. Then it does the same for latency, where the delay users experience is
dominated by a term that has nothing to do with proving.

## Why per-transaction and per-proof cannot be reconciled

Start with a real table rather than an argument. When Succinct published OP Succinct on
10 September 2024, it included its measured proving costs for three OP Stack chains, with the block
ranges attached:<Cite src="succinct-op-succinct" />

| Chain | ¢ / tx | ¢ / block | tx / block | gas / block | Block range |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Base | 0.61 | 62 | 103.4 | 23.9M | 19,299,000 – 19,299,500 |
| OP Mainnet | 1.34 | 21 | 15.6 | 5.7M | 124,900,000 – 124,900,500 |
| OP Sepolia | 1.11 | 5.8 | 5.2 | 1.9M | 16,800,000 – 16,800,500 |

**Subgoal: confirm the per-transaction column is derived, not measured.** Divide block cost by
transaction count, then by gas instead:

```text
per transaction            per million gas
Base       62 ¢ / 103.4 = 0.60 ¢     62 ¢ / 23.9 Mgas = 2.59 ¢
OP Mainnet 21 ¢ /  15.6 = 1.35 ¢     21 ¢ /  5.7 Mgas = 3.68 ¢
OP Sepolia 5.8¢ /   5.2 = 1.12 ¢    5.8 ¢ /  1.9 Mgas = 3.05 ¢
```

The left column reproduces the published one to rounding, so "cost per transaction" is not a
property of the prover — it is the cost of proving a block divided by however many transactions
happened to be in it.

**Subgoal: read the two spreads.** Per transaction, OP Mainnet costs 2.2× Base, from the same tool
in the same post on the same day. Per million gas the spread collapses to 1.4×. The post explains
the residue: gas per transaction on Base is 60% of the other two chains — 231k against 365k — a
fixed per-block overhead is amortised across more transactions on a busier chain, and OP Mainnet
carries frequent BN precompiles underpriced relative to the proving cost they
incur.<Cite src="succinct-op-succinct" />

Proving cost tracks work done, and gas is the chain's own meter for work done. That is the closest
thing to a portable unit this topic has, and almost nobody publishes in it.

## The number is a model, and two of its three terms are not cryptography

Read how that table was produced: run the state transition program over a range of real blocks, take
the RISC-V cycle count, calculate the proving time that requires on commodity GPU hardware, and
amortise.<Cite src="succinct-op-succinct" /> No invoice appears anywhere in that pipeline. The
figure is:

```text
cost  =  cycles  ×  seconds per cycle on chosen hardware  ×  dollars per GPU-hour
```

The first term is the zkVM's, the second the hardware's, the third a rental market. The Ethereum
Foundation's Ethproofs board makes that third term explicit, charting a GPU price index alongside
proof cost on the stated reasoning that proving economics depend on GPU rental costs, and that the
gap between the two lines is the efficiency gained beyond cheaper
hardware.<Cite src="ethproofs-metrics" /> So a proving cost from eighteen months ago is not a stale
version of today's number. It is a different quantity against a different GPU market, and it moves
without a line of prover code changing.

## Where to get a figure with its scope attached

Ethproofs is a block proof explorer built by the Ethereum Foundation, aggregating submissions from
zkVM teams and publishing cost, latency and proving time per proven
block.<Cite src="ethproofs-learn" /> It is the only source this module found that ships a proving
cost with its workload, cluster and date already attached. Read on **26 August 2026**, its
real-time proving cohort showed two eligible provers out of six evaluated:<Cite src="ethproofs" />

| Team | zkVM | Guest | Cluster | Performance | Liveness | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Axiom | OpenVM 2.1 Preview | revm | 16 × NVIDIA 5090 | 99.96% | 99.96% | $0.0054 |
| ZisK | ZisK | revm | 8 GPU | 73.72% | 99.35% | $0.0079 |

The unit is dollars per proof of **one Ethereum L1 block**, on the cluster named in the same
row.<Cite src="ethproofs-learn" /><Cite src="ethproofs" /> The metrics page states the direction of
travel as a target rather than an achievement: cost trending toward $0.01, latency toward ten
seconds.<Cite src="ethproofs-metrics" />

Succinct's own measurement, published 18 November 2025, is a clean example of a well-scoped vendor
claim: 99.7% of L1 Ethereum blocks proved under 12 seconds and 95.4% under 10 seconds, on 16 NVIDIA
RTX 5090 GPUs, over a random set of 954 blocks between block 23,807,739 and
23,812,008.<Cite src="succinct-realtime-proving" /> Workload, hardware, sample size, block range,
date. An earlier post from the same team is widely quoted at "more than 93% under 12 seconds"; no
source reconciles the two, so cite the post you read rather than the pairing.

<Aside kind="note">
**Same numerator, two denominators, 2.5× apart.** The Ethproofs board reports two reliability
figures on 26 August 2026. Across **301,398 block slots**, aggregated over every evaluated prover,
33.8% were proved in ten seconds or less — with 5.78% stunned, 0.04% paralyzed and **60.4%
offline**, against a cohort target of at least 70%. Across the **119,185 proofs actually produced**,
85.4% were eligible.<Cite src="ethproofs" />

Those are the same successes counted twice: 33.8% of 301,398 is about 101,900, and the eligible-proof
count of 101,768 is 85.4% of 119,185. The whole gap is slots where a prover produced nothing. A
vendor benchmark measures the second denominator, because it runs when the cluster is up. An
operator planning to depend on proving cares about the first.
</Aside>

## Latency: four claims spanning two orders of magnitude

The honest position on latency is that there is no single number to teach. Here is the published
range, each claim with the scope its own source gives it:

| Claim | Scope as stated | Source and date |
|---|---|---|
| Seconds | One L1 Ethereum block, 16-GPU cluster | Succinct, Nov 2025<Cite src="succinct-realtime-proving" /> |
| "16 minutes to 16 seconds" | Ethereum L1 blocks, ecosystem-wide, over an unstated interval | Ethereum Foundation, Dec 2025<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" /> |
| ~190–200 s per batch | Polygon zkEVM, described as predictable | arXiv survey of prior work, Mar 2025<Cite src="arxiv-zk-rollup-bottlenecks" /> |
| ~240 s for a 5,000-tx batch | ZKsync Era, described as elastic | arXiv survey of prior work, Mar 2025<Cite src="arxiv-zk-rollup-bottlenecks" /> |
| "Tens of minutes" **and** "on the order of minutes" | OP Succinct rollups — both phrases, in the same post | Succinct, Sep 2024<Cite src="succinct-op-succinct" /> |

Two further figures circulate widely — roughly 30 minutes for a ZKsync batch, roughly 75 minutes for
Linea — which this module could not trace to a first-party page and therefore does not publish as
fact. They are recorded so you recognise them.

Study the last row. One post carries both "on the order of tens of minutes" and "on the order of
minutes with a sufficiently large cluster".<Cite src="succinct-op-succinct" /> Not a contradiction:
one is the cluster a rollup has, the other the cluster it might buy. A reader who lifts either
sentence without its qualifier has manufactured a fact.

### The delay a user experiences is mostly not proving

Decompose the wall clock between an L2 transaction executing and its state root being final on L1:

Two of those six lines are proving. The rest are batching and settlement, and in production they
dominate.

OP Succinct's proposer generates range proofs in parallel, then aggregates and submits **a single
ZKP every hour**, matching OP Mainnet's cadence at the time and configurable by the
operator.<Cite src="succinct-op-succinct" /> A prover that finished instantly would leave up to an
hour of latency in place, because nothing is submitted until the cadence fires. Cross-check on a
different chain: L2BEAT's liveness row for Base, read on 26 August 2026, records state updates every
**51 minutes 25 seconds** on average — with an anomaly of 3 hours 47 minutes on 20 August between
13:49 and 17:36 UTC — each proposal covering 600 L2 blocks split into sub-ranges of
30.<Cite src="l2beat-base-project" /> Whatever the prover's per-range time is, the observable rhythm
is the cadence.

  belief="ZK rollups are final immediately, and optimistic rollups take seven days."
  reality="Both give a soft confirmation in about a second. The seven days is the optimistic withdrawal window, sized against censorship rather than computation, and a validity rollup replaces it with proving time plus an aggregation cadence plus L1 finality — minutes, not zero."
  why="Two unrelated delays wear the same word, finality, and only one of them has a memorable number attached."

## Even the target moves

The Ethereum Foundation's realtime-proving envelope — ten seconds, $100,000 of capex, 10 kW of power
— was set out in the first lesson. Two things about it belong here.

**The workload in that latency target is one L1 block**, not an L2 batch, and the ten seconds is a
12-second slot minus roughly 1.5 seconds of propagation.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" /> Check
that the thing being proved is the thing you care about.

**And the target has already moved.** The Foundation's follow-up of 18 December 2025 reports the
performance definition met — latency falling from 16 minutes to 16 seconds, costs collapsing 45× —
then resets the bar around security: milestone M2 at 100-bit *provable* security with proof size
relaxed to ≤ 600 KiB, milestone M3 at 128 bits with the size back to ≤ 300 KiB and a formal
soundness argument for the recursion architecture.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" /> The
reason is blunt: foundational STARK conjectures were mathematically disproven, and what was
advertised as 100 bits might actually be 80.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" />

M2 carried a stated deadline of end of May 2026. On 26 August 2026 the Ethproofs board still showed
M2 as the current milestone.<Cite src="ethproofs" /> Cite the date you read, always.

<Aside kind="note">
**The paper everyone cites, read properly.** The arXiv analysis of ZK-rollup performance bottlenecks
is a real measurement study run on a desktop: an Intel Core i7-14700F with 32 GB of RAM, Circom and
Groth16 under Hardhat, batch sizes of 4, 8 and 16 transactions, and a Powers of Tau ceremony that
took over 30 minutes at n = 19 and crashed the machine at n = 20.<Cite src="arxiv-zk-rollup-bottlenecks" />

Its **structural** findings transfer completely: verification time is constant in batch size — that
is what succinctness means; generation time grows with batch size and is the primary bottleneck;
per-transaction cost falls with batch size as overhead is amortised, the same effect the OP Succinct
table shows independently. Its **absolute** figures transfer not at all — as the paper implies when
it notes that running Polygon zkEVM reportedly needs about 1 TB of main
memory.<Cite src="arxiv-zk-rollup-bottlenecks" /> Separating a study's transferable structure from
its non-transferable constants is the skill.
</Aside>

Set aside every disputed figure and the shape still stands, which is why the shape is what this
module teaches. Proving is **recurring**, running for every batch whether the chain is attacked or
idle, where the optimistic normal case computes nothing. It is **hardware-bound**, a bill of
GPU-hours at a market rate.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" /><Cite src="ethproofs-metrics" /> It
**scales with gas, not transactions**. And it carries a **liveness dependency**: if the prover
stops, withdrawals freeze — a different shape from a challenger going dark, the same category of
failure.

<Check type="explain" concept="proving-cost">
A vendor's one-pager lands on your desk:

```text
Our zkVM proves your rollup for $0.002 per proof,
with sub-10-second latency and 99.9% reliability.
```

Every number in it may be true. Write the reply that turns it into something you could put in a
model.

<Answer>
**On the $0.002.**

1. *A proof of what, and per how much gas?* Ethproofs reports cost per proof of a single Ethereum
block — $0.0054 for a 16×5090 cluster on 26 August 2026 — while the OP Succinct table reports 62¢ to
prove one Base block over its sampled range.<Cite src="ethproofs" /><Cite src="succinct-op-succinct" />
Two orders of magnitude apart, both correct, different workloads. Gas makes them comparable: on the
one table carrying both columns, per-transaction cost spans 2.2× across three chains while cost per
million gas spans 1.4×.<Cite src="succinct-op-succinct" />

2. *At what GPU price, and measured or modelled?* The published methodology for such figures is
cycle count converted to GPU-seconds converted to dollars, not an
invoice.<Cite src="succinct-op-succinct" /> The quote embeds a rental rate that moves independently
of their software, which is why Ethproofs charts a GPU price index beside the cost
curve.<Cite src="ethproofs-metrics" /> Without the assumed hourly rate it cannot be re-based.

**On the sub-10-second latency.**

3. *Which percentile, on which cluster, over how many blocks?* The Foundation's definition is P99 of
mainnet blocks on hardware capped at $100,000 and 10 kW.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" /> A
well-formed vendor claim looks like Succinct's: 95.4% under 10 seconds on 16 RTX 5090s across 954
named blocks.<Cite src="succinct-realtime-proving" /> A bare "sub-10-second" is missing three fields.

4. *What is my submission cadence?* The question that changes the model most, and the vendor cannot
answer it. OP Succinct submits one aggregated proof per
hour;<Cite src="succinct-op-succinct" /> L2BEAT records Base's state updates at roughly one every
51 minutes.<Cite src="l2beat-base-project" /> If I settle hourly, a prover ten times faster changes
my users' experience by nothing. Latency binds only once the cadence is shortened to meet it.

**On the 99.9% reliability.**

5. *Over what denominator?* On 26 August 2026 the same successes read 33.8% over block slots and
85.4% over proofs actually produced, the difference being 60.4% of slots where no proof
arrived.<Cite src="ethproofs" /> A figure over attempts tells me about the prover; a figure over
slots tells me about my chain.

**What goes in the model.** Not a price: a cost per million gas, an assumed GPU hourly rate, my own
settlement cadence, and a liveness assumption with a named fallback for the week the prover is down.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**The final lesson** closes the argument the first one opened. Both families are now fully priced —
a contingent capital reserve with a fat tail on one side, a recurring hardware bill on a moving GPU
market on the other — and neither total tells you how much to trust the chain. The last lesson shows
what does, and why the boundary between the families has stopped being a property of the stack.

**In diligence**, the transferable move is the unit conversion. When two vendors quote incomparable
figures, find the quantity that drives the cost — here, gas — and normalise both to it. Numbers that
still disagree afterwards are a real difference; numbers that converge were a marketing choice.

**And in your own systems**, the denominator question generalises. Uptime over requests-served and
uptime over requests-attempted differ by exactly the failures you most want to know about — which is
the gap between 33.8% and 85.4% on one board on one day.<Cite src="ethproofs" />
