On 26 August 2026, four projects on L2BEAT's layer-2 summary carried a Stage 2 rating: Facet, Honeypot
v2, Aztec and Ethscriptions. Their value secured, added together, does not reach a million
dollars — Facet at roughly $658K is the largest, Honeypot v2 is a bug-bounty appchain holding about
a thousand dollars, and Aztec is listed at under a thousand.<Cite src="l2beat-summary" />

Every chain on that page securing more than ten million dollars is Stage 0 or Stage 1.

That is not a criticism of anyone. It is the single most useful fact about the framework: **the top
rung is reachable, and the chains that carry real money have not taken it.** Understanding why is
more valuable than memorising the requirements, and it turns out the answer is not cowardice.

## What the framework grades, and what it explicitly does not

Start with the disclaimer, because it is load-bearing and gets skipped. L2BEAT states that the Stages
Framework discusses maturity in terms of decentralisation, not in terms of security, and goes further:
it is very easy for a team to deploy a very unsafe but Stage 2 project — perhaps because the proof
system is highly experimental, or because the contracts have not been properly
audited.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />

So the ladder measures **how much power permissioned actors retain**, and nothing else. Decentralisation
is a proxy for security only if a team decentralises at the point where the risk from permissioned
operators exceeds the risk from bugs.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" /> Hold that conditional; the middle
of this lesson turns it into arithmetic.

The framework was first introduced in June 2023, from an idea Vitalik Buterin proposed on Ethereum
Magicians in November 2022, and L2BEAT has revised it several times since.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />
The revisions have gone one way: the bar has risen, particularly around Security Council
composition.<Cite src="l2beat-stages-security-council" />

## Stage 0: six things, and each one rules out a specific lie

L2BEAT's Stage 0 requirements are a checklist, and it is worth reading each as "what would be true if
this were absent".<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />

**Does the project call itself a rollup?** It must self-identify. This sounds like paperwork and is
not: it distinguishes rollups from projects that socially signal they may eventually deviate from
Ethereum consensus.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" /> Absent it, you cannot even hold the team to the
definition.

**Are L2 state roots posted on L1?** Posting roots is what allows withdrawals at all. Absent it, the
bridge has nothing to pay against.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />

**Is data availability provided on L1?** All data necessary to reconstruct the L2 state must be
available on L1.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" /> Absent it, you are on a validium, and the first lesson's
derivation applies: you hold a commitment to data you cannot obtain.

**Is node software capable of reconstructing state from L1 data source-available?** Absent it, the
data is technically public and practically unusable, because nobody outside the team can turn bytes
into state to check a root against.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />

**Does the project use a proper proof system?** For a fraud-proof system this is what lets invalid
roots be rejected; for a ZK rollup it is what makes a root acceptable in the first place. L2BEAT adds
a subtlety worth noting: if state diffs are used for data availability, the proof system must also
ensure that all state changes are included in the diff.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />

**Are there at least five external actors that can submit a fraud proof?** The fraud-proof system must
allow a minimum of five external actors to perform the watching-and-disputing
role.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" /> Absent it, the one-honest-party assumption has no population to
draw from.

## Stage 1: one principle, and a footnote that changes it

Stage 1 used to be a list. It is now a single guiding principle, and the precision matters:

> The only way, other than bugs, for a rollup to indefinitely block an L2-to-L1 message such as a
> withdrawal, or to push an invalid one, is by compromising at least 75% of the Security
> Council.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />

Three words in that sentence are doing heavy work.

**"Indefinitely."** Bounded liveness failures are permitted, deliberately, so that teams can pause a
system and hand control to the Council when they spot a threat.<Cite src="l2beat-stages-edge-cases" />
Which immediately raises the question of how bounded is bounded, and L2BEAT's edge-case document is
candid that this is unresolved: it walks through the standard OP Stack arrangement, where a pause
expires after three months and two pauses can be chained, giving a liveness-failure bound of six
months.<Cite src="l2beat-stages-edge-cases" /> Six months of frozen withdrawals is compatible with
Stage 1.

**"At least 75%."** L2BEAT's glossary sets the composition bar: a properly configured Security Council
has at least eight members and a threshold above 75%.<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" />

**"Other than bugs."** The framework assumes the code does what it says. Every real loss in the
security track sat in the gap that assumption opens.

Then the footnote, which is where most readers stop reading and should not. L2BEAT states the
assumption explicitly: if the proposer set is open to anyone with enough resources, at least one live
proposer is assumed at any time — a 1-of-N assumption with unbounded N — and **it is not assumed to
be non-censoring**.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />

Read that again. Stage 1 assumes somebody will keep proposing. It does not assume they will propose
*your* transaction. Censorship resistance and proposer liveness are separate properties, and the
rating only speaks to the second.

There is one more Stage 1 provision: upgrades initiated by entities outside the Security Council are
allowed if they provide at least a seven-day exit window.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" /> Note the
carve-out this creates — the requirement binds the *outside* path, which is why several Stage 1 chains
carry an emergency path with no window at all.<Cite src="l2beat-arbitrum-project" />

## Stage 2: three requirements, one of which is a promise about time

Stage 2's requirements are short and hard.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />

**Is the fraud proof system permissionless?** Anyone, not just an allowlist, must be able to submit.

**Do users have at least 30 days to exit in case of unwanted upgrades?** Thirty days, including for
upgrades initiated by a DAO. There is one exception: where an on-chain bug-detection system exists —
L2BEAT's example is two valid contradicting ZK proofs, as in Polygon zkEVM's emergency mode — instant
upgrades are allowed for detected bugs.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />

**Is the Security Council restricted to acting only on errors detected on chain?** Its power must be
limited to adjudicable on-chain bugs.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />

Notice the shape of the exception. Stage 2 does not forbid emergency intervention; it demands that
the trigger be something a contract can *verify* rather than something a committee can *assert*. That
is the whole difference between the top two rungs: not whether humans can act, but whether the
condition under which they may act is checkable by code.

## When is Stage 2 actually the better choice?

L2BEAT invites the calculation directly: given some perceived probabilities of bugs and of actors
being compromised, it is possible to compute which stage gives the best
security.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" /> Here is that calculation in the simplest form that still
carries the conclusion. It is a reconstruction of the shape of the argument, not anyone's published
model.

**Subgoal: define the two failure modes so they can be compared.** Let `q` be the probability that
the proof system or the contracts contain a bug severe enough to lose funds within the period you
care about. Let `p` be the probability that any one Security Council signer is compromised or coerced
in the same period, independently.

**Subgoal: write each stage's failure probability.**

```text
Stage 2:  fails ≈ q                         (a bug lands, and nobody may intervene)
Stage 1:  fails ≈ P(collusion) + q·(1 − r)  (the council colludes, or a bug lands
                                             and the council fails to rescue, r = rescue rate)
```

**Subgoal: compute the collusion term for a compliant council.** Take the minimum compliant
configuration — eight members, threshold above 75%, so six of eight — at `p = 0.05`:

```text
P(≥6 of 8) = C(8,6)·0.05⁶·0.95²  +  C(8,7)·0.05⁷·0.95  +  0.05⁸
           = 28 × 1.5625e-8 × 0.9025  +  8 × 7.8125e-10 × 0.95  +  3.906e-10
           ≈ 3.95e-7  +  5.94e-9  +  3.9e-10
           ≈ 4.0e-7
```

**Subgoal: state the crossover and check it against reality.** Ignoring the rescue term for a moment,
Stage 2 beats Stage 1 when `q` is below roughly 4 in 10 million. That is the bar: you would have to
believe the probability of a fund-losing bug in the proof system and contracts, over your holding
period, is under one in two and a half million.

Now put a fact next to that. Scroll — a production validity rollup — performed an emergency verifier
replacement in February 2026 because of a bug in its guest prover
program.<Cite src="l2beat-scroll-project" /> Whatever `q` is for a modern rollup, it is not 4e-7.

**Subgoal: read the conclusion, which is not the one people expect.** For chains securing billions
with young proof systems, **Stage 1 is currently the correct engineering answer**, and the ladder is
not a queue everyone should be rushing up. The rescue term makes this stronger: a council that can
intervene converts some bug outcomes into inconveniences, which is worth a great deal when `q` is
large. The opening observation — Stage 2 exists and holds under a million dollars — stops looking
like an industry failure and starts looking like people doing the arithmetic.

<Aside kind="note">
This also tells you when the answer flips. As `q` falls — proof systems mature, formal verification
of the L1 contracts lands, multiple independent provers are required rather than one — the crossover
moves and Stage 2 starts winning. The zkSecurity work on formal approaches to rollup security is
aimed at exactly that variable.<Cite src="zksecurity-l2-formal" /> Watch the proof-system maturity
column, not the stage column, if you want to predict which chains climb next.
</Aside>

### The rating goes down as well as up

One more entry, because it is the best available proof that the stage number is a live measurement
rather than a badge. On 1 June 2026, Scroll's independent 9-of-12 Security Council was replaced by a
3-of-4 team admin multisig, and its rating dropped from Stage 1 to Stage
0.<Cite src="l2beat-scroll-project" />

Any curriculum, including this one, that prints a stage assignment is printing a photograph. The
research this module was built on recorded Scroll as Stage 1 as of May 2026. It is Stage 0 today. Both
statements were accurate when made, and only one of them is useful now.

## What each rung buys a product team

Translate the ladder out of governance language and into the things you would actually write into a
risk register.

**Stage 0 buys you: verifiability, and a bounded theft surface.** The data is on Ethereum, so you can
independently compute what you own. The proof system means the operator cannot post a state root that
simply reassigns your balance. What it does not buy you is any protection against the operator
changing the rules — a Stage 0 chain can have an exit window of zero and a single team multisig,
which is precisely the configuration L2BEAT flags as CRITICAL on Scroll
today.<Cite src="l2beat-scroll-project" /> **Operationally: your worst case is "the operating company
turns hostile or is compromised, and there is nothing between that event and the escrow."**

**Stage 1 buys you: a threshold and a clock.** Unilateral override now requires a supermajority of a
properly composed council rather than one team's signers, and non-council upgrades come with at least
seven days' notice.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" /> **Operationally: you gain a monitoring surface.** A
seven-day window is long enough to build an alert on — watch the timelock, page someone, exit. That
is a concrete engineering task that does not exist at Stage 0, and it is the largest single practical
difference between the two rungs. What Stage 1 does not buy you is protection against the emergency
path, which on several Stage 1 chains still has no window at all.<Cite src="l2beat-arbitrum-project" />

**Stage 2 buys you: the removal of the monitoring requirement.** Thirty days' notice on every path,
with intervention restricted to bugs a contract can adjudicate.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />
**Operationally: you stop needing a governance-watching process**, because the notice period exceeds
any plausible response time. That is the real product benefit, and it is worth noticing that it is an
*operations* benefit rather than a cryptographic one.

Read as a sequence, the ladder is: at Stage 0 you carry counterparty risk you cannot mitigate; at
Stage 1 you carry counterparty risk you can mitigate with staffing; at Stage 2 you stop staffing it.
Which is a perfectly ordinary way to describe any vendor relationship, and that is the most useful
frame for it.

## Reading a project page in five minutes

Concretely, for a product decision, in this order:

1. **Data availability row.** If it does not say the data is on Ethereum, stop; the word "rollup" does
   not apply and everything downstream changes.<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" />
2. **Exit window row.** This is the upgrade-key question in one line. "None" means an unwanted change
   can land before you can leave. Check both the regular and the emergency
   path.<Cite src="l2beat-linea-project" />
3. **Proposer failure row.** "Cannot withdraw" means a single stalled whitelist freezes your
   money.<Cite src="l2beat-zksync-era-project" />
4. **Sequencer failure row.** Self-sequence, enqueue-only, or no mechanism — this determines whether
   your incident runbook has a step you can execute.<Cite src="l2beat-arbitrum-project" />
5. **Milestones and incidents.** Emergency upgrades and governance changes appear here, dated. It is
   the cheapest available history of how the team behaves under
   pressure.<Cite src="l2beat-scroll-project" />
6. **Stage.** Last, as a summary of the five rows above — never as a substitute for them.

correct.

deploy involved. This is also why a stage claim needs a date attached, always.

## Where this pays off

**The sequencer module**, which starts next, takes the one power this ladder deliberately does not
grade — who orders your transactions — and works out what it is worth. The answer is: a great deal
operationally, and almost nothing to your stage rating, which is itself the point.

**The proof-systems and forced-inclusion modules** later in this track go under each of these rows.
When you read "Fraud proofs (INT)" or "Self sequence", you will know the mechanism the phrase is
compressing.

**The security track's threat modeling** applies here without translation. The five rows above are a
trust-boundary map somebody else drew for you and keeps current. Very few dependencies come with one.

**Your own systems.** Write down, today, the stage and exit window of the chain your product runs on,
with the date you read them. Put a calendar reminder to re-read the page in a quarter. That is the
entire operational takeaway of this module, and Scroll is the reason for the reminder.
