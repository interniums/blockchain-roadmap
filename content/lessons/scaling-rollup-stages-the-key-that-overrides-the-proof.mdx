On 23 February 2026, a validity rollup replaced its on-chain verifier in an emergency, because of a
bug in the guest prover program. L2BEAT records it on Scroll's project page under milestones and
incidents: **emergency verifier upgrade**.<Cite src="l2beat-scroll-project" /> Scroll is a validity
rollup — L2BEAT lists its proof system as OpenVM.<Cite src="l2beat-summary" />

Sit with that sequence. A chain whose entire security story is "an L1 contract checks a
cryptographic proof, so you do not have to trust us" discovered that the thing being proved was
wrong, and fixed it by having somebody swap the checker out. Nothing about the incident was
scandalous — it is, on the facts as recorded, a team responding correctly to a real bug. That is
precisely what makes it the right opening. **The capability was there, it was used, and it worked.**

If a key can replace the verifier to fix it, the same key can replace the verifier to break it. This
lesson is about the consequences of that sentence, which turn out to reorganise everything the
previous three lessons established.

## What a validity proof actually asserts

Write down the statement the verifier checks. Not the marketing version — the logical one.

```text
∃ w :  STF(s_prev, w) = s_new
       ∧  commit(w) = published_data
```

There exists a witness `w` — the ordered transactions and the state they touched — such that running
the state transition function on the previous state with that witness yields the claimed new state,
and such that `w` matches the data that was published. A [validity proof](/c/validity-proof)
establishes that, succinctly, and the L1 contract verifies it.

Now list what the statement quantifies over. It quantifies over witnesses. It does not quantify over:

- the code of `STF` — the proof shows the transition follows *the rules the circuit encodes*, and
  says nothing about whether those are still the rules tomorrow;
- the code of the verifier — the contract that evaluates the proof is not itself inside the
  statement;
- the code of the bridge — the contract that releases escrowed funds is not mentioned at all;
- who may submit — the sequencer's control over ordering is orthogonal;
- whether `published_data` was published anywhere reachable — that is the
  [data availability](/c/data-availability) question, and a proof over withheld data is
  [valid and useless](/c/validium-withholding-risk).

That is a long list of things a proof does not cover, and every one of them is governed by a
contract that sits behind a proxy.

### The precedence relation, derived

You know [proxies](/c/transparent-proxy) and [delegatecall](/c/delegatecall-proxy) cold from the
Solidity track, so the mechanism needs no explanation. What needs deriving is the ordering.

**Subgoal: write the bridge's payout condition.** Strip a withdrawal down to its essential shape:

```solidity
function finalizeWithdrawal(bytes32 root, bytes calldata proof, Withdrawal calldata w) external {
    require(verifier.verify(root, proof), "bad proof");
    require(_included(w, root),           "not in root");
    _release(w.to, w.amount);
}
```

**Subgoal: identify every value an attacker could control to make `_release` fire.** Two paths.
Path one: forge `proof` such that `verify` returns true for a `root` of the attacker's choosing —
this is breaking the soundness of the proof system, and it is the attack everybody models.
Path two: change what `verifier` points at, so that `verify` returns true unconditionally.

**Subgoal: compare the difficulty of the two paths.** Path one requires a cryptographic break or a
circuit bug. Path two requires one transaction from whoever holds upgrade authority. There is no
sense in which path two is harder, and every sense in which it is faster.

**Subgoal: state the resulting bound.** The system's effective security is the **minimum** of the
proof system's soundness and the integrity of the upgrade authority — not the maximum, and not some
average. This is the [weaker-link principle](/c/security-is-the-weaker-link) applied to governance
rather than to data, and the zkSecurity formal treatment of rollup security is built around exactly
this kind of decomposition: the guarantee you have is the conjunction of every assumption, and a
conjunction is only as strong as its weakest term.<Cite src="zksecurity-l2-formal" />

**Subgoal: notice that this is not a proxy-specific argument.** Nothing above depends on the upgrade
being implemented as a proxy. A chain that hard-codes its verifier address but lets an admin set a
"respected game type" pointer, or that keeps a settable configuration contract, has the same
structure with different plumbing. The question is never "is there a proxy" — it is **"is there any
address whose owner can change what the bridge believes?"**

### It is a contract set, and you grade the shortest path

One more structural point before the evidence. Upgrade authority on a real chain is not a single
owner address; it is a layered arrangement — a proxy admin, behind a timelock, behind a multisig,
alongside a Security Council with its own powers, alongside an emergency path that skips some of the
above. Reading only the top layer produces a comfortable and wrong answer.

**Subgoal: enumerate what has to be checked, not just the bridge.** The escrow that holds the funds;
the verifier or dispute-game contract; the configuration that names *which* verifier is currently
respected; the contract that sets the sequencer and proposer allowlists; and the admin of each of
those.

**Subgoal: apply the right aggregation.** For a delay, you take the **minimum** across paths, not the
advertised one. A chain with an eleven-day governance timelock and a Security Council that can act
immediately has an exit window of zero, because an attacker who has the Council does not use the
timelock. This is the same reasoning as the blast-radius test from threat modeling — you ask, for
every path, "if this one is compromised, does the escrow move?" and the answer defines the zone.

**Subgoal: notice why this is under-audited.** These are Solidity contracts on Ethereum with
`onlyOwner` modifiers and proxy patterns, exactly the code the security track trained you to review.
They are simply not in anybody's audit scope, because the chain is filed under infrastructure and
infrastructure is assumed rather than reviewed — which is precisely the gap the formal treatment of
rollup security sets out to close.<Cite src="zksecurity-l2-formal" /> The
[access-control questions](/c/access-control-failure) you would ask of a protocol apply unchanged.

## What the live rows say

Everything above is an argument. Here is what L2BEAT's per-project risk rows said when read on
26 August 2026 — with the reminder, which this whole module keeps making, that these move.

**Linea — validity proofs, Stage 0.** Exit window: **none**. The stated reason is that there is no
window for users to exit in case of an unwanted upgrade since contracts are instantly upgradable, and
the risk summary reads: funds can be stolen if a contract receives a malicious code upgrade, with a
**0-second delay on code upgrades**.<Cite src="l2beat-linea-project" />

**ZKsync Era — validity proofs, Stage 0.** Non-emergency upgrades go through a 4-day-3-hour delay,
but the risk summary states funds can be stolen if a contract receives a malicious code upgrade,
where the delay is 4d 3h to 8d 3h *unless the upgrade is initiated by the EmergencyUpgradeBoard, in
which case there is no delay*.<Cite src="l2beat-zksync-era-project" /> The same page adds a second
mechanism worth knowing about: the central operator can censor withdrawal transactions by
implementing a `TransactionFilterer`, with no delay at all.<Cite src="l2beat-zksync-era-project" />

**Arbitrum One — fraud proofs, Stage 1.** Exit window: **none** for the emergency upgrade path, ten
days for the regular path.<Cite src="l2beat-arbitrum-project" /> A Stage 1 chain, passing the
walkaway test, still has an emergency path whose exit window is zero.

That ten-day figure is derivable and worth deriving, because it shows what an exit window is made of.
Non-emergency upgrades are initiated on L2, wait 8 days on L2, then wait 3 days on L1; and forcing a
transaction in costs a further day, which must be spent inside the window rather than after
it.<Cite src="l2beat-arbitrum-project" />

**An exit window is notice minus the time your own exit takes.** That subtraction is why a chain can
advertise an eleven-day timelock and give users ten days, and why a chain with a one-day timelock and
a one-day force delay gives users nothing at all.

## Nine of twelve, or three of four

Scroll's page carries a second entry, and it is the one that shows the framework is live rather than
historical. On 1 June 2026 an independent 9-of-12 Security Council was replaced by a 3-of-4 team
admin multisig, and the chain dropped to Stage 0.<Cite src="l2beat-scroll-project" /> Its risk
summary now reads: funds can be stolen if a contract receives a malicious code upgrade, and there is
no delay on code upgrades — rated CRITICAL.<Cite src="l2beat-scroll-project" />

It is worth quantifying what changed, because "went from 9-of-12 to 3-of-4" sounds like a
configuration tweak.

**Subgoal: choose a model simple enough to compute and state its assumption.** Give each signer an
independent probability `p` of being compromised or coerced. Independence is a strong idealisation —
signers on one team share an employer, a laptop fleet and a jurisdiction — and it biases the result
in favour of the smaller multisig, so whatever gap it shows is a floor.

**Subgoal: compute the collusion probability for each threshold at p = 0.1.**

```text
P(≥3 of 4)  = C(4,3)·0.1³·0.9  +  0.1⁴
            = 4 × 0.001 × 0.9  +  0.0001
            = 0.0036 + 0.0001   = 0.0037

P(≥9 of 12) = Σ over k = 9..12 of C(12,k)·0.1ᵏ·0.9^(12−k)
            = 220·10⁻⁹·0.729 + 66·10⁻¹⁰·0.81 + 12·10⁻¹¹·0.9 + 10⁻¹²
            ≈ 1.60×10⁻⁷ + 5.35×10⁻⁹ + 1.08×10⁻¹⁰ + 10⁻¹²
            ≈ 1.66×10⁻⁷
```

**Subgoal: take the ratio and read it.**

```text
0.0037 / 1.66×10⁻⁷  ≈  22,000×
```

Under an independence assumption that flatters the smaller set, the change raises the modelled
probability of a unilateral override by roughly four orders of magnitude. Two things drive it: the
threshold count fell from nine to three, and — the part people skip — the *independence* fell too,
because "team admin multisig" means the surviving signers are correlated by construction. L2BEAT's
glossary sets the bar it is measuring against: a properly set up Security Council consists of at
least eight members with a threshold greater than 75%.<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" /> Those
requirements have tightened over time rather than loosened.<Cite src="l2beat-stages-security-council" />

## What the proof is still worth

None of this makes proof systems decorative, and the opposite over-correction is its own mistake.

A working proof system removes an entire class of attack: the operator cannot simply post a state
root in which your balance belongs to them and walk away with it. That is a large, real guarantee,
and it is the reason a Stage 0 rollup is a fundamentally different object from a chain with no
settlement contract at all. L2BEAT counts a proper proof system among its Stage 0
requirements — it is table stakes, not a differentiator.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" /> The framework
also asks for at least five external actors able to submit a fraud proof at that same
rung.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />

The accurate statement is narrower than the marketing and wider than the cynicism: **the proof
bounds what the operator can do while following the rules; the upgrade key bounds who writes the
rules.** Both matter. Only one of them is on the landing page.

<Check type="explain" concept="proofs-do-not-imply-trustless">
Your company is choosing between two chains for a product holding customer deposits. A colleague
argues for Chain Z on the grounds that "it has real ZK proofs, so we don't have to trust the
operator, whereas Chain A is optimistic and relies on someone watching."

Chain Z: validity proofs, Stage 0, exit window none, proposing whitelisted.
Chain A: fraud proofs, Stage 1, exit window ten days on the regular upgrade path and none on the
emergency path, proposing permissionless.

Write the counter-argument. Then name the one thing your colleague is right about.

<Answer>
**The counter-argument, in the order the risks actually bite.**

*Upgrade authority.* Chain Z's exit window is none, which means an upgrade can land before any
customer can leave — the configuration L2BEAT records for Linea today.<Cite src="l2beat-linea-project" />
Chain A gives ten days' notice on the ordinary path, the configuration recorded for Arbitrum
One.<Cite src="l2beat-arbitrum-project" /> On the risk that has
historically cost the most money — someone changing the contracts — Chain A is strictly better, and
the proof system is not involved in that comparison at all.

*Withdrawal liveness.* Chain Z's proposing is whitelisted, so if the proposer stops, withdrawals
freeze regardless of anything else — L2BEAT's *replace proposer* and *cannot withdraw*
ratings.<Cite src="l2beat-zksync-era-project" /><Cite src="l2beat-linea-project" /> Chain A's is permissionless, so a stalled proposer is an
inconvenience rather than a freeze. Note that your colleague's own criterion — "relies on someone
watching" — cuts the other way here: Chain A's dependence is on *anyone*, Chain Z's is on a specific
whitelist.

*What the proof rules out.* On both chains, the operator cannot finalise a state that steals your
balance. Chain Z establishes that with cryptography and Chain A with a challenge game plus a window.
Different mechanisms, same guarantee, and neither is the guarantee your deposits actually need most.

**Where your colleague is right, and it is not a small point.** The withdrawal wait. Chain A's
seven-day challenge window is a genuine working-capital cost that Chain Z does not impose, and if
your product sweeps to L1 frequently, that is a real operational advantage with a real number
attached. The error is not in valuing the proof — it is in valuing it as a *trust* property when it
is a *latency* property.

**The reframe worth handing back.** "Do we trust the operator?" is not one question. It is four: can
they steal (no, on both), can they freeze (yes on Z, no on A), can they censor (yes on both), can
they change the rules (yes on both, with ten days' notice on A and none on Z). Proof type answers
exactly the first one — which is why the stage framework grades the other
three.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** turns everything above into the grading scheme L2BEAT publishes, requirement by
requirement, and shows what each rung of the ladder actually buys a product team — including the
uncomfortable finding about which projects have reached the top rung and how much money they secure.

**The sequencer module** takes the third of the four questions in that answer — can they censor —
and works out precisely how much damage that permits, which is more than people expect and less than
they fear.

**Everything you learned about proxies** in the Solidity track now has a second application, and
L2BEAT's stage requirements are the checklist somebody else already wrote for
it.<Cite src="l2beat-stages-intro" /> The
audit question you would ask of a protocol's `upgradeToAndCall` is the same question, asked of a
chain: who holds it, what is the threshold, how long is the notice, and is there a second path that
skips the notice entirely. On every chain examined in this lesson, the answer to the last one was
yes.

**Your own systems.** If your product's risk disclosure says "secured by Ethereum" and does not name
an upgrade authority, it is describing the data lane and calling it custody. The fix is two
sentences and an L2BEAT link.
