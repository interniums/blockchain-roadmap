A contract on Ethereum holds escrowed deposits. Someone submits a 32-byte value and asserts: this is
the state root of the L2 after block 41,205,332. The contract has no way to run the L2. It cannot
replay ten million transactions; if it could, there would be no reason for the L2 to exist.

So it has to decide whether to believe a number, and there are exactly two arrangements available.

**Assume it is right and let someone else object.** Accept the root, refuse to act on it for a fixed
period, and give anyone who disagrees a way to prove the disagreement on-chain.

**Refuse to accept it without a proof.** Require a succinct cryptographic argument that the root
follows from the previous root and the published inputs, and verify that argument in the contract.

Everything the industry calls "optimistic" versus "ZK" is those two sentences. This lesson works out
what each actually costs, and then dismantles the three things almost everyone believes about the
comparison.

## Why a fraud proof does not re-run anything

The first arrangement has an obvious-looking problem: if the contract cannot execute the L2, how does
it adjudicate a dispute about the L2's execution? The answer is the single most elegant idea in the
whole rollup design, and it is worth deriving rather than accepting.

**Subgoal: reduce "who is right about the state" to "who is right about one step".** Both parties
agree on the starting state. They disagree about the ending state. Therefore there exists a first
step of the execution trace where their claimed intermediate states diverge. Finding that step
settles the dispute, because the step before it is agreed and the step after it is not.

**Subgoal: find that step without transmitting the trace.** Each party commits to their claimed
state at the midpoint of the disputed range. If they agree at the midpoint, the divergence is in the
second half; if they disagree, it is in the first. Either way one round halves the range. The OP Stack
specification calls this the bisection game and structures the dispute exactly this
way.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" /> Arbitrum's documentation describes the same shape:
bisection narrows the disagreement, and only then does anything execute on
L1.<Cite src="arbitrum-interactive-fraud-proofs" />

**Subgoal: count the rounds.** A range of N steps needs ⌈log₂ N⌉ halvings to reach a single step.
Put a number on it. Suppose the disputed block does 2⁴⁰ machine steps — about a trillion:

Forty on-chain messages, each carrying one hash, versus a trillion executed instructions. That ratio
is the entire trick, and it is why the dispute-game specification is written around claims and
counter-claims rather than around execution.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-dispute-game" />

**Subgoal: name what L1 finally runs.** One instruction, replayed inside a deterministic virtual
machine that the L1 contract can execute — the normative fault-proof specification defines that
machine and the program it runs.<Cite src="op-specs-fault-proof" /> OP Stack calls that machine Cannon, an on-chain
MIPS interpreter.<Cite src="op-docs-cannon" /> This is the **only** part of the L2's execution that
Ethereum ever performs.

## The seven days are a censorship budget, not a compute budget

The challenge window on both major optimistic stacks is about seven days. The universal explanation —
"generating a fraud proof takes a while" — is wrong, and the correct explanation tells you something
you can use.

**Subgoal: state what the window has to guarantee.** The safety of the whole arrangement rests on at
least one honest party noticing a bad root and getting a challenge transaction included on Ethereum.
OP Stack's own docs are explicit that this requires someone to actually be running the challenger
software.<Cite src="op-docs-challenger" /> So the window must be long enough that an adversary cannot
keep that transaction out of every L1 block for its full duration.

**Subgoal: work out how fast an adversary can price a challenger out.** Ethereum's base fee moves by
at most one eighth per block — the OP Stack fee documentation states the same 12.5% cap when it
explains how the relayed L1 base fee behaves.<Cite src="eip-1559" /><Cite src="op-docs-transaction-fees" />
To multiply the base fee by a factor f, an attacker filling blocks needs n blocks where 1.125ⁿ = f:

```text
f = 100    n = ln(100)  / ln(1.125) = 4.6052 / 0.11778 ≈ 39.1  → 40 blocks ≈ 8 minutes
f = 1000   n = ln(1000) / ln(1.125) = 6.9078 / 0.11778 ≈ 58.6  → 59 blocks ≈ 12 minutes
```

**Subgoal: read the result the right way round.** Making L1 inclusion expensive is fast — minutes.
*Keeping* it expensive is what costs money, and the cost is linear in duration because every block of
the campaign is paid for at the elevated fee. A window of minutes would be trivially outlastable. A
window of days converts censorship from a stunt into a sustained, publicly visible, extremely
expensive campaign. **That is the quantity the seven days is buying.**

**Subgoal: notice what this does not settle.** Nothing above proves seven days is enough, and the
research does not treat it as settled either — economic censorship games against fraud proofs are an
active area, and BoLD's design goal is specifically to bound the delay an adversary can
impose.<Cite src="arxiv-economic-censorship-games" /><Cite src="arbitrum-bold-deep-dive" /> The
honest statement is that the window is a parameter chosen against an adversary model, not a constant
derived from one.

The bottom link is the one everybody studies. The four above it are operational facts about a person
or an organisation, and OP Stack's documentation says so in as many words when it describes
`op-challenger` as the software an honest party actually runs.<Cite src="op-docs-challenger" />

Two of those links have names worth carrying. **Permissionless challenging** is whether *anyone* may
submit — a whitelisted challenger set turns the proof system into a decorative one, and moving off
the whitelist is a specific migration a chain has to perform rather than something that happens by
default.<Cite src="op-docs-fault-proofs-explainer" /> **Bonded challenging** is what funds the
watching: both sides post bonds and the loser's bond pays the winner, which simultaneously pays for
honest vigilance and prices spam.<Cite src="arbitrum-bold-deep-dive" />

## What a validity proof removes, and what it does not

The second arrangement replaces the argument with a proof. A succinct proof that the transition was
computed correctly is verified by an L1 contract, and because correctness is established rather than
assumed, there is no window and no dependence on anyone watching.<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" /> The
contract doing the checking is what L2BEAT's glossary calls the verifier: an entity in a ZK rollup,
often a smart contract, that verifies proofs submitted by a prover.<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" />

Now be precise about which delay that removes, because this is where the industry's vocabulary does
the most damage.

There are two different waits in an optimistic rollup, and they are not the same object:

- **The confirmation wait** — from submitting a transaction to being able to act on its outcome.
- **The withdrawal wait** — from that outcome existing to the L1 bridge paying against it.

A validity proof removes the second. It does nothing to the first. Both rollup families give you a
soft confirmation from the sequencer in about the same time — OP Mainnet's own documentation puts
soft finality at roughly two seconds, with a subblock pre-confirmation at about 200
milliseconds.<Cite src="op-mainnet-transaction-finality" /> A validity rollup's users are sitting on
exactly the same kind of operator promise while the prover works.

And the prover does work. Proving is a recurring compute bill per batch that optimistic rollups
simply never pay.<Cite src="arxiv-zk-rollup-bottlenecks" /> How large a bill, and how long a wait,
is a question this lesson will not answer with a number, because the sources do not agree.

<Aside kind="warn">
**Proving latency and proving cost are unresolved, and the disagreement is not small.** Figures in
circulation include seconds (SP1 Hypercube on a 16-GPU cluster, proving *Ethereum L1 blocks* — a
different workload from an L2 batch),<Cite src="succinct-realtime-proving" /> tens of minutes as a
generic claim, and production figures of roughly half an hour and roughly an hour and a quarter
attributed to two different validity rollups by secondary sources. Cost figures are worse: they are
quoted variously per proof and per transaction, which are not the same unit, and the values span
three orders of magnitude. **Sources disagree and this lesson does not pick one.** If you need a
number, measure the specific chain, state the unit, and date it.
</Aside>

## The line between the two is dissolving

Treating "optimistic" and "ZK" as chain categories was already shaky, and current deployments have
made it untenable. Validity proving is available as a mode on the OP Stack rather than as a property
of the stack.<Cite src="succinct-op-succinct" /> The dispute-game interface was built for exactly this
kind of pluggability, with game types selected by configuration rather than baked
in.<Cite src="op-specs-dispute-game-interface" />

L2BEAT's scaling summary, read on 26 August 2026, shows the consequence directly. It carries a
**risk type** column and a separate **proof system** column, and they do not line up the way the
folklore expects: Base Chain is listed as Optimistic with proof system SP1; Arbitrum One as
Optimistic with BoLD; OP Mainnet as Optimistic with OPFP; Taiko Alethia as Validity with SP1
Hypercube / RISC Zero.<Cite src="l2beat-summary" />

Taiko's project page goes further and shows what a modern proof configuration actually looks like:
every proposal range is verified by exactly two proofs chosen from SGX running Geth, SGX running
Reth, SP1 and RISC0, with at least one SP1 or RISC0 proof required, and proof submission gated by a
whitelist of two provers.<Cite src="l2beat-taiko-project" /> Trusted hardware and cryptographic
proofs, in the same sentence, on the same chain. "Is it a ZK rollup?" has stopped being a question
with a useful answer.

<Check type="predict" concept="validity-rollup">
Two chains hold your funds. Chain V is a validity rollup: proofs are generated and verified on L1
roughly every thirty minutes. Chain O is an optimistic rollup with a seven-day challenge window. Both
run a single sequencer that emits soft confirmations in about two seconds.

You are building a payments product. For each of the following, predict which chain is better, or say
the two are equivalent — and name the mechanism that decides it.

1. Crediting a merchant's balance in your own database three seconds after the customer pays.
2. Sweeping the merchant's funds to an Ethereum address at the end of each day.
3. Surviving a week in which the sequencer's operator is compromised and reorders unconfirmed
   traffic to its own benefit.
4. Keeping your unit economics stable when volume triples.

<Answer>
**1 — equivalent, and this is the one people get wrong.** Both give you a soft confirmation from the
same kind of source at the same time. Proof type has no bearing on it. What you are trusting at three
seconds is one company's process, on both chains identically.

**2 — Chain V, decisively.** This is the withdrawal wait, and it is the only comparison where the
proof type is the deciding mechanism: roughly half an hour of proving versus seven days of challenge
window. If daily sweeps are core to your product, that difference is your working-capital
requirement.

**3 — equivalent, and neither is good.** The sequencer's ability to reorder unconfirmed traffic is
bounded by neither proof system, because reordering is not an invalid state transition. Both proof
systems reject invalid state; ordering within the rules is not invalid. This is the subject of the
sequencer module.

**4 — Chain O, at the margin.** Chain V pays a per-batch proving bill that Chain O does not — a real,
recurring compute cost with meaningful hardware requirements<Cite src="succinct-realtime-proving" /> —
while both pay for data. Which dominates depends on numbers this lesson refused to publish, so the correct answer is
"measure it" — but the *shape* is that Chain V has a recurring compute cost line and Chain O has a
latency and bonded-capital cost line instead.

**The pattern.** Of four product questions, proof type decides exactly one. That ratio is the reason
the next two lessons are about exits and upgrade keys rather than about cryptography.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** takes the honest-challenger assumption seriously. A fraud proof protects you if
somebody is watching, funded, and able to reach L1. Each of those is a separate liveness assumption
about people, and the same lesson shows that the equivalent assumption on a validity rollup — that
somebody will keep proposing state roots at all — is the one that quietly freezes withdrawals on real
chains today.<Cite src="l2beat-arbitrum-project" /><Cite src="l2beat-taiko-project" />

**The lesson after it** is the point the whole module is built around. Everything here is an argument
about how an L1 contract decides whether to accept a root. None of it is an argument about who is
allowed to replace that contract.

**Your own systems.** If your product's treasury policy was written around "we're on a ZK rollup so
it's final immediately", the second bullet above is the sentence to re-read. Finality and
withdrawability were never the same event.
