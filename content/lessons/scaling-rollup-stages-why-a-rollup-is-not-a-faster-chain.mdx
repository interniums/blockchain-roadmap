Deploy the same vault contract to two chains. Both cost a fraction of a cent per transaction. Both
confirm in about two seconds. Both have a bridge, a block explorer, a wallet integration, and a page
that says "secured by Ethereum".

On one of them, if every employee of the operating company walked into the sea tomorrow, you could
still compute your balance from data nobody can withhold, submit a withdrawal to a contract on
Ethereum, and get your money. On the other, you could not — because the only complete copy of the
chain's history sits on machines those employees controlled.

Nothing about throughput, fees, or confirmation latency distinguishes the two. The word **rollup**
names the difference, and this module is about learning to check which one you are actually on.

## The definition is about where the data goes, not how fast it runs

L2BEAT's glossary states it in one sentence: a rollup is a blockchain that inherits consensus and
data availability from another blockchain called L1, and rollups enable trust-minimised bridges with
the base layer via proof systems, either optimistic or
zero-knowledge.<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" /> The same glossary draws the boundary from the other
side: if data is stored somewhere other than a permissionless L1, then the project is not a rollup
but a validium or an optimium.<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" />

Unpack that into the two properties a chain has to hold simultaneously.

**Property one — anyone can reconstruct the state without permission.** The transaction data needed
to rebuild the chain is published somewhere nobody can decide to stop serving. On Ethereum that is
[blob space](/c/blob).

**Property two — Ethereum's contracts can reject a claimed result.** The chain's settlement runs in
L1 contracts, so a state the rules reject never becomes the state the bridge pays out against.

Neither property mentions speed, and speed is the thing every marketing page leads with. Hold that
gap in mind for the whole module: **the interesting facts about an L2 are never the ones on its
landing page.**

### Why a state root on its own buys you nothing

You already know from [state roots](/c/state-root) that a root is a binding commitment to a
key-value map. That is exactly why publishing only the root is insufficient, and it is worth walking
the argument rather than accepting it.

**Subgoal: state precisely what a user needs in order to leave.** To withdraw, you must construct a
transaction that the L1 bridge accepts. That means producing the account's current value plus a
[storage proof](/c/storage-proof) against the state root the bridge trusts. A proof is a path of
sibling hashes. You cannot produce sibling hashes you do not have.

**Subgoal: check whether the root gives you those siblings.** It does not. A Merkle root is a
one-way function of the tree. Given only the root, recovering any leaf or any sibling requires
inverting a hash, which is precisely the property [collision and preimage resistance](/c/preimage-resistance)
promises you cannot do.

**Subgoal: identify what does give you the siblings.** Rebuilding the tree yourself. That means
replaying every transaction from genesis, which means having every transaction. So the operator's
choice about *where the transaction data lives* determines whether the exit path exists at all —
before any question about proofs, sequencers or governance arises.

This is why the definition is a data-location question wearing a security-property costume. A chain
that checkpoints a root to Ethereum and keeps its transaction data on its own servers has given you
a number you can verify against nothing.

## The data lane has a hard ceiling, and it is smaller than you expect

The reconstructibility property costs real bytes on Ethereum, so it is worth knowing the size of the
pipe every rollup shares. Every step here is arithmetic you can redo.

**Subgoal: fix the size of one blob.** EIP-4844 defines a blob as 4,096 field elements of 32 bytes
each.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> That is 4096 × 32 = **131,072 bytes**, exactly 128 KiB.

**Subgoal: fix how many blobs a block carries.** Blob capacity is no longer set by named hardforks.
EIP-7892 introduced blob-parameter-only forks that change the target and maximum on their own
schedule.<Cite src="eip-7892" /> BPO2, specified in EIP-8135, activated on 7 January 2026 and set
the target to 14 and the maximum to 21.<Cite src="eip-8135" /><Cite src="fusaka-mainnet-announcement" />
Whether a further BPO fork has raised those numbers between then and now is not established in this
lesson — that is exactly the kind of parameter to read from the Ethereum Foundation's blog rather
than from a curriculum.

**Subgoal: turn blobs per block into bytes per second.** Ethereum produces one block per 12-second
slot.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos" /> At the target of 14 blobs:

```text
14 blobs × 131,072 bytes = 1,835,008 bytes per block
1,835,008 ÷ 12 s        ≈ 152,917 bytes per second
                        ≈ 13.2 GB per day
```

At the maximum of 21 the same arithmetic gives roughly 229,376 bytes per second. That is the whole
data budget, shared by every rollup on Ethereum.

**Subgoal: convert bytes into transactions using a measured compression figure, not a guessed one.**
Rollups compress hard before posting — Arbitrum uses Brotli, and L2BEAT records Arbitrum One's
batches as Brotli at its highest compression setting.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" /> The honest way
to get bytes-per-transaction is to read what a live chain actually posted. L2BEAT publishes exactly
that. Over the year ending 26 August 2026, Scroll posted 18.01 GiB of data, averaging 958.39 bytes
per L2 user operation.<Cite src="l2beat-scroll-project" />

```text
152,917 bytes/s ÷ 958.39 bytes/op ≈ 160 user operations per second
```

Roughly 160 operations per second across all rollups combined, at one chain's measured compression
ratio. Treat that number as an order of magnitude and nothing more: the ratio swings with
transaction mix, with the encoding a chain chooses, and with whether it posts full transaction data
or state diffs — L2BEAT records Linea as posting transaction data where most validity rollups post
diffs.<Cite src="l2beat-linea-project" />

<Aside kind="note">
The interesting consequence is not the specific figure but its shape. **Rollup capacity is bounded by
Ethereum's data lane, not by how fast an L2 can execute.** A chain that wants past that bound has
exactly one move available: publish its data somewhere other than Ethereum. At that point it has
stopped satisfying the definition, and L2BEAT will file it under
[validium or optimium](/c/rollup-vs-validium) rather than rollup.<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" /> The
throughput ceiling and the trust boundary are the same constraint seen from two sides.
</Aside>

## The thing you actually depend on is the bridge

There is a clause in L2BEAT's definition that is easy to read past and changes what you are assessing.
A rollup without a bridge, or considered without its bridge, is called a **sovereign
rollup**.<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" /> Which tells you that the bridge is a separable component,
and separable components are exactly the ones worth naming individually.

Work out which component your money is in.

**Subgoal: locate the asset.** Bridged ETH on an L2 is not on the L2 in any custodial sense. The real
ETH sits in an escrow contract on Ethereum. What exists on the L2 is an accounting entry that the
escrow contract has agreed to honour under conditions written in its own code.

**Subgoal: name what has to hold for the escrow to pay you.** It has to accept a state root covering
your balance, accept a proof against that root, and release the funds. All three happen in L1
contracts.

**Subgoal: notice what has dropped out.** The L2's consensus, its client software, its uptime, and
its team's continued existence are not on that list. Correspondingly, neither is the L2's own
excellent engineering a defence if the escrow contract's rules are wrong or can be changed.

This is why the rest of the module keeps looking at Ethereum rather than at the L2. **The L2 is where
your transactions execute; the L1 contract set is where your claim on the money lives**, and those
two live in different threat models. When a chain is described as "secured by Ethereum", the honest
expansion is "the data is on Ethereum and the escrow is on Ethereum" — both true, and neither of them
a statement about who can rewrite the escrow.

<Aside kind="warn">
Keep the distinction sharp, because the failure that costs people money is not "the L2 broke". It is
"the L2 was fine and the L1 contract set behaved in a way nobody read". That is a
[contract review](/c/upgrade-risk) problem in a domain that looks like an infrastructure problem,
which is why it goes unstaffed.
</Aside>

## The sequencer is one role, and it is not the dangerous one

Almost everything a user experiences on an L2 is produced by a single process. Arbitrum's own
documentation describes the sequencer as responsible for ordering incoming transactions and managing
the batching, compression and posting of transaction data to the parent
chain.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" /> Reading the same page carefully, the sequencer's work splits
into three distinct jobs:

1. **Accept and order.** Transactions arrive at one endpoint and are ordered as received, unless the
   chain overrides that policy.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" />
2. **Broadcast.** The accepted ordering goes out on a real-time feed, which is what produces the
   confirmation your wallet shows.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" />
3. **Post.** Batches are compressed and submitted to the `SequencerInbox` contract on
   L1.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" />

The third job carries a detail worth sitting with. Arbitrum's docs state plainly that only the
sequencer can call the batch-submission methods on the `SequencerInbox` contract, and that this
exclusivity ensures no other party can include messages directly.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" />
That is a permission written into an L1 contract — not a temporary operational convenience.

Now separate sequencing from the two roles it gets confused with:

- **Proposing** — publishing the claimed state root that the bridge pays withdrawals against.
- **Upgrading** — holding the authority to replace the L1 contracts, including the one that verifies
  proofs.

These are three different powers, held by three different mechanisms, failing in three different
ways. A chain can decentralise any one of them and leave the other two untouched. Most of this track
is an argument about which of the three actually determines whether you can get your money out, and
the answer is not the one everybody talks about.

## What "confirmed" is standing in for

One more piece of the picture before the module gets to proofs. OP Stack's own documentation defines
soft finality as the point where the sequencer has processed your transaction and included it in a
block, and states outright that soft finality relies on a single sequencer operated by the chain
operator, and that until the transaction's data lands on Ethereum the sequencer is technically able
to reorg the transaction away.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-finality" />

Read that as a vendor telling you the truth in its own docs. The green tick is a claim by one
company's process about what it intends to do. The module on sequencers takes that apart properly;
for now, note that the confirmation you see and the guarantee the word "rollup" makes are separated
by minutes and by an entirely different trust assumption.

<Check type="explain" concept="rollup">
Four chains describe themselves. For each, say whether it satisfies the rollup definition, and name
the specific property that is missing when it does not.

1. Chain A executes off-chain, publishes every transaction as blob data on Ethereum, and posts a
   state root to an L1 contract that verifies a validity proof before accepting it.
2. Chain B executes off-chain, posts a state root to Ethereum every hour, and serves transaction
   history from its own archive nodes and a public S3 bucket.
3. Chain C executes off-chain, publishes its transaction data to an external data-availability
   network with its own validator set, and posts a validity proof to Ethereum.
4. Chain D executes off-chain, publishes every transaction as blob data on Ethereum, and posts a
   state root to an L1 contract that accepts it from a whitelisted address with no proof at all.

<Answer>
**1 — a rollup.** Both properties hold: the data is on a permissionless L1 so anyone can rebuild the
state, and settlement runs through an L1 contract that can reject a claim.

**2 — not a rollup.** The reconstructibility property fails. An S3 bucket and a set of archive nodes
are both revocable: whoever operates them can stop serving, and no contract on Ethereum obliges them
to continue. The state root you hold is a commitment to data you cannot obtain, which is the
situation the middle of this lesson derives is useless for constructing a withdrawal.

**3 — not a rollup by L2BEAT's definition; a validium.**<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" /> This is the
case worth being precise about, because it feels like a rollup and has a real proof. The proof
establishes that the state transition was computed correctly. It does not establish that the inputs
were published. With data withheld, the state is provably correct and you still cannot compute your
own balance to build a withdrawal — [the failure mode](/c/validium-withholding-risk) that has no
analogue on a rollup.

**4 — not a rollup, and this one is missing the second property rather than the first.** The data is
reconstructible, so anyone can *detect* a wrong state root. Nothing on L1 can *reject* it. Detection
without rejection means the honest observer gets to write a very well-evidenced blog post while the
bridge pays out against the wrong root.

**The pattern.** The two properties fail independently and produce different symptoms. Case 2 and
case 3 leave you unable to prove what you own; case 4 leaves you able to prove it and unable to act
on it. A single word — "rollup" — is doing the work of both, which is why the rest of this module
grades chains on structure rather than on labels.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** takes the second property seriously: there are exactly two ways to arrange for
an L1 contract to be able to reject a claimed state, and the difference between them is smaller and
stranger than the industry's vocabulary suggests.

**The third lesson** takes the first property to its conclusion. Being able to reconstruct the state
is necessary for an exit and nowhere near sufficient — there are four separate chokepoints between
"I can compute my balance" and "the money is on L1", each with a different party able to stall it.

**The fourth lesson** is where the module's real argument lands. Everything above is about execution
and data. None of it constrains the party who can replace the contracts that check the execution and
hold the data commitments, and that party is the one whose compromise costs you money.

**The sequencer module** that follows takes the single process introduced here and makes it concrete:
the feed you can connect to yourself, the confirmation that is a promise rather than a proof, the
revenue that pays for the whole arrangement, and the three proposals for stopping the arrangement
that have each, so far, failed to displace it.

**Your own systems.** The cheapest exercise here is to open the L2BEAT project page for whichever
chain your product is on and read the "Data availability" row before you read anything
else.<Cite src="l2beat-summary" /> If it does not say the data is on Ethereum, everything you assumed
from the word "rollup" is up for renegotiation.
