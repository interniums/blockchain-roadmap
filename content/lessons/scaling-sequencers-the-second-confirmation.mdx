An exchange receives a deposit on an L2. Somewhere in its code is a line that decides the balance is
now the user's, and that line names a moment. Two seconds after submission? When the batch reaches
Ethereum? When Ethereum finalises the block containing that batch?

The three answers differ by roughly a factor of a thousand in latency and by three entirely different
trust assumptions. Most teams pick the first one by accident, because it is what the RPC's default
block tag returns, and never write down what they chose.

This lesson is the second half of the timeline. It is short because the mechanism is simple; it
matters because the number everybody quotes for it — seven days — is wrong by a factor of about four
hundred, and the chains' own documentation says so.

## Three states, and one of them is badly named

OP Stack nodes track three states: **unsafe**, **safe**, and **finalized**. The documentation is
direct about the naming problem — "unsafe" can give people pause, but it is simply the protocol's
technical term for the soft-finality stage, meaning the transaction is in a block whose data has not
yet been posted to Ethereum.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-finality" />

These map onto JSON-RPC block tags you already use: the unsafe head is served as `latest`, and the
safe and finalized heads back the `safe` and `finalized` tags.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-finality" />
That mapping is the practical payload of this lesson. **The tier system is already exposed by every
compliant L2 RPC. Nothing needs building; something needs choosing.**

The middle state is the one nobody talks about, and it deserves its own paragraph. Once the
transaction's data is in an Ethereum block, sequencer trust is no longer required for ordering — but
the Ethereum block itself can still reorg until Ethereum finalises
it.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-finality" /> So `safe` is not "half final". It is a precise
statement: **you have stopped trusting a company and started trusting Ethereum's short-term
liveness.** That is a change of counterparty, not a change of confidence level.

### What `finalized` on an L2 RPC is actually computing

The tag exists on the L2 endpoint, so it is tempting to read it as the L2's own consensus reaching a
decision. It is not, and the difference matters when you reason about correlated failure.

**Subgoal: identify where the L2's finalized head comes from.** OP Stack chains in the standard
configuration are rollups that use Ethereum's consensus mechanism to order and finalise transactions
rather than running a separate consensus protocol, and therefore inherit Ethereum's ordering and
finality properties.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-finality" />

**Subgoal: trace the derivation.** The batcher publishes an L2 block's data to Ethereum as blob data
or as calldata. When that Ethereum block is finalised by Ethereum's consensus, the L2 block it carried
is marked finalized.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-finality" />

**Subgoal: state the consequence.** An L2's `finalized` head is a *function of* L1 finality applied to
batch inclusion. There is no separate quorum, no separate validator set, and nothing the L2 operator
can do to make the number advance faster — but also nothing they can do to make it advance *wrongly*
once the data is on Ethereum. The tag genuinely means what you want it to mean, and it means it for a
reason that has nothing to do with the L2's own software.

**Subgoal: notice the failure mode this creates.** Because the L2's finalized head is derived from
L1, anything that stalls Ethereum finality stalls every L2's finalized head simultaneously. Products
gated on `finalized` across several L2s do not have diversified finality risk; they have one risk
seen through several endpoints.

## Deriving the timeline

The wall-clock figures are not magic constants; every one of them decomposes.

**Subgoal: fix the epoch length.** Ethereum's finality machinery works in epochs of 32 slots at 12
seconds each.<Cite src="ethereum-org-pos" />

```text
32 slots × 12 s = 384 s = 6.4 minutes per epoch
```

**Subgoal: convert epochs to finality.** Under normal conditions an Ethereum block is finalised
roughly two to three epochs after it is proposed — about 13 to 19
minutes.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-finality" />

```text
2 epochs = 12.8 min      3 epochs = 19.2 min
```

<Aside kind="warn">
The clean mechanism number is two epochs, 12.8 minutes. Measured figures do not always land there:
one benchmark cited in this project's research reported a median closer to 15.9 minutes over a
24-hour window. **Sources disagree and this lesson does not pick one.** Take 13 to 19 minutes as the
range and treat any single figure with suspicion, because the quantity genuinely varies with
participation and network conditions.
</Aside>

**Subgoal: add the batch submission step.** OP Mainnet produces enough data to post batches every few
minutes.<Cite src="op-mainnet-transaction-finality" /> Stack the two:

Which is exactly the figure OP Mainnet's documentation publishes for hard
finality.<Cite src="op-mainnet-transaction-finality" /> Arbitrum describes the same two-tier structure
in its own vocabulary: soft finality from the sequencer's real-time feed, hard finality when
transactions are included in batches posted to and finalised on the parent
chain.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" />

### The batch interval is a business decision, and it is yours to check

Here is where the derivation stops being universal. That "few minutes" is not a protocol constant. It
depends on the chain's batcher configuration and throughput: high-throughput chains fill their
batches quickly and post every few minutes, while chains tuned for lower data costs may hold data back
for up to their configured maximum channel duration, **which can be several
hours**.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-finality" /> The sequencing window — 12 hours in the standard
configuration — is the hard upper bound for the step.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-finality" />

So on a quiet chain, hard finality is not 15 to 30 minutes. It is however long the operator has
decided to wait before paying for a blob, which is a cost optimisation made by someone whose
incentives are not identical to yours. On a chain where you hold user funds, **the batch posting
interval is a number to measure rather than assume**, and it moves when the chain's volume moves.

There is a matching failure mode to watch for. If the sequencer is accepting transactions but cannot
publish them, the safe and finalized heights stall while the unsafe height keeps
climbing.<Cite src="op-docs-sequencer-outages" /> The gap between `latest` and `safe` is therefore not
just a latency figure; monitored over time it is your detector for one of the two sequencer outage
types, and the one with no other symptom.

## The seven-day number is about withdrawals, and only withdrawals

OP Stack's documentation addresses this misconception under its own heading, twice, in two different
pages, which tells you how much support load it generates. Transactions on OP Stack chains do not take
seven days to finalise; the seven-day delay applies only to *withdrawals* of ETH and ERC-20 tokens
through the Standard Bridge, and does not affect transaction finality on the
chain.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-finality" /><Cite src="op-mainnet-transaction-finality" />

A related belief travels with it and is also addressed directly: fault-proof challenges created in
response to withdrawal claims **do not reorganise the chain**. Challenges only remove bad claims,
without affecting the Standard Bridge or the chain
itself.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-finality" />

Separate the two objects cleanly and neither confusion survives:

- **Chain finality** — when is the ordering irreversible? Minutes.
- **Bridge withdrawal** — when can I move the asset to Ethereum? Seven days on an optimistic chain's
  standard bridge, or proving latency on a validity chain.

They are different questions about different systems, and they share a word.

## Choosing the line, in money

The decision is not "which is safest". It is a comparison of two costs, and it is worth writing out
because the arithmetic usually surprises people.

**Subgoal: name the two costs.** Waiting costs you conversion, abandoned carts, and support load; call
it `F` per transaction. Acting early costs you the value at risk multiplied by the probability of
reversal in that window; call it `V × P`.

**Subgoal: choose the line where the loss stops being the bigger number.** Credit at the earlier
moment when `V × P < F`. Which immediately tells you the shape of the right policy: **the line should
move with `V`, not be fixed for all transactions.**

**Subgoal: put rough magnitudes on `P` for each tier and be honest about their provenance.**
Reversal at the soft tier requires the sequencer to reorg its own blocks — extremely rare in the
observed record, and technically possible at any moment.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-finality" />
Reversal at `safe` requires a short Ethereum reorg. Reversal at `finalized` requires breaking
Ethereum's finality. Those three probabilities differ by many orders of magnitude, and none of them
has a published, measured value that this lesson could hand you. What you can do is order them
correctly and let `V` pick the tier.

**Subgoal: convert that into policy rather than into a constant.** A coffee purchase clears at
`latest`. A five-figure settlement clears at `safe`. Anything that leaves your system irreversibly —
a payout, a fiat transfer, a physical shipment — clears at `finalized`. Arbitrum's own guidance points
the same way: it names centralised exchange deposits and withdrawals as the case where certainty of
finality is mandatory.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" />

<Check type="explain" concept="hard-finality">
A payments team writes: "We credit on `latest`. Our L2's sequencer has never reorged a block, and
waiting 20 minutes would destroy our conversion rate. This is the right trade."

The reasoning is defensible. Say what is missing from it, and describe what a complete version of the
same decision looks like.

<Answer>
**What is defensible.** The trade itself. For low-value, high-frequency payments the friction cost of
waiting genuinely dominates the expected loss, and a policy that names `latest` deliberately is
better than one that lands there by default.

**What is missing — three things.**

*A cap.* "We credit on `latest`" with no value threshold means the same policy applies to a
five-dollar payment and a five-hundred-thousand-dollar one. The expected-loss arithmetic says the line
should move with the amount at risk, and a single tier cannot do that.

*A different rule for irreversible side effects.* Crediting a database row is reversible. Shipping
goods, releasing fiat, or sending a payout is not. Those should sit behind `safe` or `finalized`
regardless of what the balance display does — and both tags are already available on the same
RPC.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-finality" />

*A monitor on the assumption.* "The sequencer has never reorged" is an observation about the past
being used as a control. Make it an actual control: alert on the gap between `latest` and `safe`
exceeding the chain's normal batch interval. That gap widening is the visible symptom of a
transaction-submission outage, which otherwise has no symptom at
all.<Cite src="op-docs-sequencer-outages" />

**The complete version.** Same headline decision, plus a value threshold, plus a separate tier for
irreversible actions, plus one alert. Roughly a day of work, and it converts an implicit bet into a
documented one with a stated maximum loss — which is the thing you will be asked for the first time
something goes wrong.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** asks what happens if the operator you have been trusting for those two seconds
stops behaving. The answer bounds the damage more tightly than most people assume, and the bound is
worth knowing precisely, because overestimating it makes people discount the risks that are real.

**The infrastructure track's finality boundary** work transfers directly: the rule that a pipeline
must not write derived state ahead of a durability tier it can defend is the same rule, stated for
data instead of for money.

**Your own systems.** Query `eth_getBlockByNumber` with `latest`, `safe` and `finalized` against your
L2's RPC right now and subtract the block numbers. The difference is the exposure your product is
currently accepting, expressed in blocks, and you will know within a minute whether anyone has ever
thought about it.
