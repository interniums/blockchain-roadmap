Three designs exist for removing the single sequencer. All three are well specified, all three have
been written up more than once, and each has a deployment record that reads differently from its
design document.

This lesson takes them in order, works out what each actually fixes, and ends on the sentence the
whole elective has been building towards — which is that none of them touches the risk the previous
module spent five lessons on, and that there is a live chain proving it today.

## Design one: an in-protocol sequencer committee

The most conservative option is to keep sequencing inside the rollup and give the job to a set rather
than to a process. Arbitrum's documentation describes exactly this as its long-term vision: a
transition from a centralised sequencer to a decentralised, fair sequencing model in which a committee
of servers collectively determines transaction ordering, requiring a supermajority, and it notes that
the Timeboost ordering policy is designed to be compatible with decentralised
sequencing.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" /><Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost" />

That is a vision statement in a documentation page. Read it as a statement of direction, not of
deployment — and note that the same page describes the current single-sequencer arrangement in the
present tense throughout, with the decentralised model in the future.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" />
L2BEAT's glossary likewise defines the sequencer as a party, singular, responsible for ordering and
executing transactions on the rollup and submitting the data to L1.<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" /> The
vocabulary the whole ecosystem uses has the centralisation baked into it.

**Subgoal: quantify what a committee buys on liveness, because that is its strongest claim.** Model
each node as independently available with probability `a`, and require `k` of `n` to be up.

```text
single operator, a = 0.99
  unavailability      = 1%  →  0.01 × 8,760 h = 87.6 hours per year

committee of 4, need 3 of 4, a = 0.99
  P(up) = C(4,3)·0.99³·0.01 + 0.99⁴
        = 4 × 0.970299 × 0.01 + 0.960596
        = 0.038812 + 0.960596
        = 0.999408
  unavailability      = 0.0592%  →  5.2 hours per year
```

Roughly a seventeen-fold reduction in downtime from four nodes. That is a real, sizeable, ordinary
engineering win — the same one you get from any quorum-replicated service, and it is worth being
unromantic about it: **committee sequencing is high availability, and high availability is the
correct frame for what it delivers.**

**Subgoal: check the independence assumption, which is doing most of the work.** Four nodes run by one
company, in one cloud account, on one software build, are not independent. The 5.2-hour figure is what
you get from genuinely uncorrelated operators; a committee of one organisation's machines buys the
much smaller improvement that redundancy inside a single failure domain buys. Who runs the committee
members matters more than how many there are.

**Subgoal: name what it does not change.** Ordering value now accrues to the committee instead of to
one operator, which redistributes MEV without creating a contested market for it. And the L1 contract
set is untouched.

## Design two: a shared sequencer network

The second proposal moves sequencing outside the rollup entirely, to a network that orders for several
rollups at once. The claimed benefits are genuine and specific: **cross-rollup atomicity**, so a single
transaction can span two chains, and **liveness that does not depend on any one operator**. The cost
is equally specific: a new dependency and a new trust assumption sitting outside the rollup's own
contracts.

The design has not been the constraint. Adoption has.

**Astria.** One of the two leading efforts, reported to have shut down in December 2025 by secondary
coverage of the sector.<Cite src="orochi-sequencer-centralisation" /> Treat the date as reported
rather than primary — this project could not confirm it from a first-party announcement, and the final
block height circulating alongside it is not independently verified, so it is not repeated here. The
shutdown itself is well attested, and it is checkable in thirty seconds: the project's main site no
longer resolves, while its documentation host still serves.

**Espresso.** The surviving network, and the more instructive case, because what it survived as is not
what it was proposed as. Its own current documentation describes it as a **decentralised settlement
layer**, and is explicit about the division of labour: each application environment has its own
sequencer that orders transactions and streams them to Espresso; Espresso finalises that stream
through decentralised proof-of-stake consensus; and each application then executes its own logic
against the finalised stream.<Cite src="espresso-docs" /> The architecture page states it even more
plainly — each application's execution ordering rules, meaning how transactions are sequenced inside a
block, are set by the application itself, not by Espresso, and Espresso does not execute the
transactions inside those blocks.<Cite src="espresso-rollup-architecture" />

Read that against the original pitch. The surviving shared-sequencer network's own documentation says
integrated chains keep their own sequencer. Its product is consensus finality over what those
sequencers produced, plus trust-minimised readability of that finalised state by other
systems.<Cite src="espresso-rollup-architecture" /> The consensus protocol is HotShot, from the
HotStuff family, and compromising Espresso-finalised ordering would require controlling at least a
third of staked ESP.<Cite src="espresso-docs" />

That is a real and useful product. It is not the thing that removes your chain's single sequencer.

### What cross-rollup atomicity would actually require

The atomicity benefit is the one that cannot be obtained any other way, so it is worth understanding
precisely what it demands. It is more than "one party orders both chains".

**Subgoal: state the property.** A transaction spanning rollups A and B either takes effect on both or
on neither. Sell on A, buy on B, and never end up having done one.

**Subgoal: work out what one sequencer gets you.** Ordering both chains means the same party can place
the two halves in corresponding positions in both orderings. That is necessary and it is not
sufficient, because *ordering* is not *execution*. Half A executes under A's rules and half B under
B's, and either can revert for reasons the sequencer cannot see when it chooses the order.

**Subgoal: name the missing piece.** Somebody has to be able to make the second half's success a
condition of the first half's, which means one chain's state transition has to depend on the other's
outcome. That is conditional execution across two state machines, and it needs a mechanism inside
both chains' execution — not just a common orderer in front of them.

**Subgoal: check the claim against what Espresso's docs describe.** Espresso finalises the ordering
and does not execute; each environment runs its own state transition function, and cross-environment
coordination is handled afterwards, by attaching succinct proofs to cross-chain messages that the
destination verifies without re-executing the source.<Cite src="espresso-rollup-architecture" />

So the surviving design achieves cross-chain coordination through **verified messages against a common
finalised record**, not through atomic co-execution. That is a coherent and probably more tractable
answer than the original one, and it is a different product. When someone says shared sequencing gives
you atomicity, ask which of the two they mean.

## Design three: based sequencing

The third proposal removes the sequencer rather than replacing it. In a **based rollup**, L1 validators
order the rollup's transactions as part of building L1 blocks. Taiko's documentation states the
consequence in the form that matters: there is no additional proof-of-stake network, no sequencer
committee and no external coordination layer, and the rollup inherits Ethereum's consensus
directly.<Cite src="taiko-based-rollups" /> The term was coined by Ethereum researcher Justin Drake to
describe rollups that delegate sequencing to L1.<Cite src="taiko-based-rollups" /><Cite src="ethresearch-based-rollups" />

The MEV row is the one that changes the economics conversation from the previous lesson: value flows
to Ethereum validators rather than being captured by a centralised
sequencer.<Cite src="taiko-based-rollups" /> That is economically aligning and it is also, from the
chain operator's point of view, the deletion of a revenue line — which is a large part of why this
design has few adopters.

### The trade-offs the design brings, named by its own authors

Taiko's documentation lists them without hedging.<Cite src="taiko-based-rollups" />

**Block time is bounded by L1.** Without preconfirmations, the fastest a based rollup can confirm is
one L1 block time — twelve seconds. Taiko addresses this with based preconfirmations, which is to say
it reintroduces a soft-confirmation layer, because users will not accept twelve seconds.

**Proposer competition wastes gas.** Multiple proposers may submit simultaneously and losers pay for
nothing. In practice the preconfirmation system coordinates this by electing a single preconfer per
epoch.

Follow that second one through, because it is where the design meets reality. Under the current Unzen
rollout, the normal proposing path is preconf-whitelisted; proposing becomes permissionless only as a
fallback, if forced inclusions age out.<Cite src="taiko-based-rollups" /> Censorship resistance in the
meantime is provided by a `ForcedInclusionStore` contract that lets any user force a transaction into
the L2 regardless of what the current preconfer chooses to
include.<Cite src="taiko-based-rollups" />

L2BEAT's live rows describe the same arrangement from the outside, read on 26 August 2026: sequencer
failure is rated **enqueue via L1**, with an inclusion becoming due after 9 minutes 36 seconds, after
which a whitelisted proposer cannot publish another proposal without processing up to ten due
inclusions; and proposer failure is rated **cannot withdraw**, with proposing gated by a
`PreconfWhitelist` that selects a single active operator for the current epoch and has no
permissionless path.<Cite src="l2beat-taiko-project" />

That 9 minutes 36 seconds is 576 seconds, which is exactly 48 Ethereum slots — a reminder that these
deadlines are slot counts underneath.

L2BEAT's stage specification raises a sharper objection to the whole preconf-router pattern, and it is
worth carrying because it is not obvious. Most censorship-resistance guarantees on Ethereum today come
from the long tail of self-building proposers that do not use mev-boost. Proposers who decline to
opt in to mev-boost will also, arguably, decline to stake into a preconf router — so restricting
sequencing rights to a staked whitelist of opted-in L1 proposers removes precisely the population that
was providing the censorship resistance, which is why the addition of a preconf router suggests the
need for an additional forced-transaction mechanism.<Cite src="l2beat-stages-edge-cases" />

**Based sequencing inherits Ethereum's censorship resistance only to the extent that it inherits
Ethereum's whole proposer set.** Gate it behind an opt-in, and you inherit the opted-in subset.

## The fourth option, which is already shipped

There is a sequencing path on every chain in this lesson that is fully permissionless today, and
nobody calls it decentralised sequencing because it is slow.

**Subgoal: describe the L1 inbox as a sequencer.** Anyone may submit a transaction to it. Nobody can
be excluded. The ordering it produces is Ethereum's, so it inherits Ethereum's censorship resistance
exactly. Those are the properties every design in this lesson is trying to obtain.

**Subgoal: state its performance honestly.** Its effective block time is the force-inclusion delay:
up to twelve hours on OP Stack, up to a day on Arbitrum's default, nine minutes thirty-six seconds
before an inclusion becomes due on Taiko Alethia.<Cite src="l2beat-taiko-project" /> A sequencing
layer with a twelve-hour block time is not a product, which is why it is described as an escape hatch
rather than as a sequencer.

**Subgoal: extract the reframe.** Every rollup already has permissionless sequencing. What the fast
path buys is **latency**, and what it costs is **a trusted operator for the duration of that
latency**. All three designs in this lesson are attempts to keep the latency while reducing the trust,
and their difficulty is entirely in that combination rather than in either half alone.

**Subgoal: use it as a design test.** When you evaluate any new sequencing proposal, ask what its
*fallback* looks like when the fast path fails. Taiko's answer is the `ForcedInclusionStore` plus a
permissionless proposing fallback when inclusions age out.<Cite src="taiko-based-rollups" /> A design
whose fast path has no permissionless fallback has not decentralised sequencing; it has changed who
the operator is.

### Why the fifth row cannot move

This is worth deriving rather than asserting, because it is the elective's closing argument.

**Subgoal: write down what sequencing authority is, mechanically.** It is a permission on a specific
L1 function: who may call the batch-submission method on the inbox contract, or who may call `propose`
on the proposal contract. Arbitrum's docs state the permission explicitly — only the sequencer can
call those methods.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" />

**Subgoal: write down what upgrade authority is, mechanically.** It is a permission on a different
function: who may point the proxy at new implementation code, or who may set the configuration naming
which verifier is respected.

**Subgoal: check whether changing one changes the other.** They are separate access-control entries on
separate functions, frequently in separate contracts. Rotating the first from one address to a
committee, an external network, or the L1 proposer set is a write to one permission set. It has no
effect on the other. There is no mechanism by which it could.

**Subgoal: state the conclusion and test it against reality.** A chain can fully decentralise its
sequencing and remain Stage 0. This is exactly why L2BEAT's stage framework grades governance and
exits rather than sequencing.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />

And the test passes, on the most decentralised sequencing design in production. Taiko Alethia
delegates ordering to Ethereum's proposers, and on 26 August 2026 it is rated **Stage 0**, with an
exit window of **none**, because its contracts are instantly
upgradable.<Cite src="l2beat-taiko-project" /><Cite src="l2beat-summary" /> Meanwhile Base, Arbitrum
One and OP Mainnet — every one of them a single company's sequencer — are Stage
1.<Cite src="l2beat-summary" />

**The chain that solved sequencing centralisation scores worse than the chains that did not, because
the ladder measures something else.** That is not a flaw in the ladder. It is the ladder telling you
which risk it thinks is bigger, and pointing at the evidence.

<Answer>
**1 — improves, substantially, and you can estimate by how much.** Nine independent organisations
needing six to agree is a quorum system, and the availability arithmetic at the top of this lesson
applies. The word doing the work is *independent* — nine organisations in nine failure domains, not
nine machines in one account.

**2 — improves, and by less than it looks.** Censoring one user now requires the ordering quorum to

the disclosure, you have made it wrong.

**5 — gets worse, at least initially, and this is the reason the designs keep not shipping.** Ordering
revenue now has nine claimants instead of one, and coordination has a cost. A company being asked to
decentralise sequencing is being asked to share its income statement in exchange for a stage rating
that will not move. The adoption record in this lesson is what that incentive produces.

## Where this unlocks

**The proof-systems module** later in this track goes under the "State validation" row you have been
reading off L2BEAT — bisection, one-step proofs, the fault-proof VM, and the honest comparison of
proving cost and latency that this elective has deliberately refused to reduce to a number.

**The forced-inclusion module** goes under "Sequencer failure" and "Proposer failure" the same way,
including the contracts and the exact calls a runbook needs to name.

**The data-availability module** goes under the row this elective started from, and works out what
happens on the chains that answer it with something other than Ethereum.

**The OP Stack module** turns the derivation pipeline — the thing that produces deposit-only blocks
when a sequencer dies — into something you can run.

**What you can now do that you could not before.** Open any L2's project page and read it as a threat
model rather than a scorecard: five rows, each naming a party and a failure, with the stage as a
summary rather than a verdict. Then ask the only question that has consistently predicted where the
money went — **who can change the contracts, how many of them are there, and how much notice do I
get?** Sequencing is the part everybody talks about. It is not the part that has cost anyone their
funds.
