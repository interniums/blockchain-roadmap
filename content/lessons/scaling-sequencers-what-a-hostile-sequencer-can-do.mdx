Run the threat model you already know how to run, on a component you have probably never put in it.

**Actor:** the company operating your chain's sequencer, or whoever has taken control of that process.
**Capability:** it receives every transaction before anyone else, chooses the order, and is the only
party permitted to submit batches to the L1 inbox contract.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" />
**Question:** what is the worst outcome?

The two answers in circulation are both wrong. "They could take everything, it's basically
custodial" is wrong, and the reason it is worth correcting is not pedantry: people who believe it
discount the risks that are real, because everything looks equally catastrophic. "It's fine, it's
secured by Ethereum" is wrong in the other direction, and it is how a product ends up with an uptime
commitment it cannot keep.

This lesson draws the boundary precisely, with the blocking mechanism named for every entry on the
"cannot" side, and then prices the three things on the "can" side that actually cost money.

## The cannot column, with mechanisms

Three capabilities are genuinely out of reach, and each is blocked by something specific you already
understand.

**It cannot forge a transaction you did not sign.** Every L2 transaction carries a signature over its
contents, verified during execution exactly as on L1. The sequencer receives signed payloads; it
holds no key of yours, and the state transition function will not apply an invalidly signed
transaction. The blocking mechanism is [ECDSA](/c/ecdsa-secp256k1) plus the fact that verification
happens inside the state transition rather than at the door.

**It cannot finalise a state transition that violates the rules.** This is what the proof system is
for. On a validity rollup the L1 contract rejects a root without an accepting proof; on an optimistic
rollup a wrong root can be challenged and overturned inside the window. Either way the bridge does
not pay against a state the rules reject.

**It cannot unilaterally move the escrow.** The assets backing bridged balances sit in L1 contracts
whose withdrawal logic the sequencer does not execute. Note the precise scope of this one — **the
sequencer role cannot; the upgrade authority can**, and on most chains those two powers are held by
the same organisation even though they are different mechanisms. That distinction is the whole of the
previous module, and it is why this lesson bounds the *sequencer* rather than the *operator*.

## The can column, in ascending order of how much it costs you

### Reorder

Ordering within the rules is not a rule violation, so no proof system objects to it. Arbitrum's
default is first-come-first-served, and the docs are explicit that chains can override that policy —
Timeboost is the named override.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" /> Whatever value is extractable by
choosing an ordering belongs, by default, to whoever chooses.

### Delay and censor

The sequencer can hold your transaction. Forced inclusion is the counter, and its cost is the
force-inclusion delay: up to a day on Arbitrum One's default, up to twelve hours of sequencing window
on OP Stack.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" /><Cite src="op-docs-forced-transaction" />

Two chain-specific escalations are worth carrying, both read from L2BEAT on 26 August 2026, because
they show the bound is not uniform across chains.

**ZKsync Era.** The central operator can censor withdrawal transactions by implementing a
`TransactionFilterer`, **with no delay**.<Cite src="l2beat-zksync-era-project" /> That is a censorship
capability that the force-inclusion path does not route around, because it operates on the L2 side of
the queue.

**Linea.** L2BEAT's risk summary states that funds can be frozen if the sequencer refuses to include
an exit transaction, and rates it CRITICAL.<Cite src="l2beat-linea-project" /> Freezing is not theft,
and a user whose funds are frozen indefinitely will not find that distinction comforting.

There is a second-order effect that the delay figure hides, and it is the one that converts censorship
into loss. Forced inclusion gets your transaction *ordered*; it does not get it ordered *in the state
you wanted*. A top-up that arrives a day late arrives into a position that has already been
liquidated; a hedge that arrives twelve hours late arrives into a different price. The censoring party
does not need to steal anything — it only needs to choose which of your transactions is late, and let
the market do the rest. **The damage from censorship is not the delay; it is everything that changes
during the delay**, and that quantity is unbounded even though the delay is not.

So the honest form of the bound is: **a hostile sequencer cannot take your money, and on some chains
it can keep you from ever reaching it, and on every chain it can make reaching it worth less than it
was.** Where a chain sits between those depends on its sequencer failure row, not on its proof type.

### Halt

If the single sequencer stops, the chain stops. Nobody is robbed — that is what the escape hatch is
for — and every product on the chain is unavailable.

**Subgoal: express that in the unit your SLA is written in.** A 99.9% monthly availability commitment
allows about 43.2 minutes of downtime per month:

```text
30 days × 24 h × 60 min = 43,200 min
43,200 × 0.001          = 43.2 min of allowed downtime
```

**Subgoal: compare it against the escape path's granularity.** If the outage is long enough that
users have to be routed through forced inclusion, the relevant unit is the sequencing window — twelve
hours on OP Stack.<Cite src="op-docs-forced-transaction" />

```text
12 h = 720 min
720 / 43.2 ≈ 16.7
```

**Subgoal: read the result.** One such event consumes roughly seventeen months of a 99.9% budget. And
you cannot buy it back: your availability is bounded above by the sequencer's, and no amount of
redundancy on your side changes that, because there is nothing to fail over to. **Liveness, not
theft, is the concrete cost of single-operator sequencing**, and it is the number your uptime
commitments have to price.

Remember also that only one of the two outage shapes announces itself. A transaction-submission
outage leaves users able to transact while the safe and finalized heights stall — no error, no alert,
unless you built one.<Cite src="op-docs-sequencer-outages" />

### Reorg its own unsafe blocks

The last entry is the narrowest and the easiest to misread. The sequencer can reorganise blocks whose
data has not yet landed on Ethereum; once blocks are safe, reorganisation becomes much harder, and
once finalized, effectively impossible.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-finality" />

Two things follow.

**The window is the batch interval, not a protocol constant.** On a high-throughput chain posting
every few minutes it is minutes. On a chain tuned for low data cost it can be much longer, because
the operator has chosen to hold data back.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-finality" /> The chain with
the cheapest fees has, all else equal, the longest reorg window — which is not a trade anybody
advertises.

**It is bounded by the sequencing window even in the worst case.** After the window expires, nodes
derive blocks deterministically from L1 whether the sequencer cooperates or
not.<Cite src="op-docs-forced-transaction" /> So "the sequencer rewrites history indefinitely" is not
on the table; "the sequencer rewrites the last several minutes" is.

## MEV: the structural difference from L1

On Ethereum, the right to order transactions in a block is contested every slot. Proposers change,
builders compete, and the value of the ordering right is discovered in a market. You know this
machinery.

On an L2 with one sequencer, that right belongs to one party, permanently, with no market. Nothing
forces an auction. Nothing forces a rebate. Whether any of the extractable value is shared is
**policy set by the operator, and policy can change.** That is a structural difference from L1, not a
temporary one, and it survives every proposed sequencer fix except one.

### Timeboost, as the concrete instance

Arbitrum's Timeboost is worth studying closely because it is a live, documented answer to "what does
an operator do when it holds all the MEV". It is a transaction ordering policy — a set of rules the
sequencer is trusted to follow — and it is live on Arbitrum One and Arbitrum
Nova.<Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost" />

The stated motivation is a critique of pure first-come-first-served. Under FCFS, searchers have
incentives to win latency races by investing in off-chain hardware, which produces spam and
congestion; and all captured MEV goes to searchers, returning none of it to the chain owner or the
applications on the chain.<Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost" />

The mechanism has three parts.<Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost" />

**An express lane.** A special sequencer endpoint, `timeboost_sendExpressLaneTransaction`, whose
submissions are sequenced immediately. The sequencer accepts payloads there only if signed by the
current round's express lane controller.

**A sealed-bid, second-price auction per round.** Rounds default to 60 seconds. Bidding closes
`auctionClosingSeconds` — default 15 — before the round begins, leaving 45 seconds to bid; in the
remaining 15 an autonomous auctioneer verifies bids, picks the winner, and resolves the auction
on-chain.

**An auction contract** holding deposits and acting as the canonical source of truth for results and
proceeds. Deposits are withdrawable with a two-round, two-minute delay; the minimum reserve price
defaults to 0.001 WETH; each address may place at most five bids per round.

**Subgoal: work out why the express lane wins races.** Non-express transactions have their arrival
timestamp delayed by 200 milliseconds, while the default block time is 250
milliseconds.<Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost" />

The delay is nearly the whole block interval, so any race decided inside a block is decided in the
express lane's favour by construction. That is the product being sold, and it is sold explicitly:
a *temporary time advantage*.

**Subgoal: be precise about what is not being sold.** The docs enumerate it. The express lane
controller does not have the right to reorder transactions, has no guarantee of being first at the top
of the block, and has no guarantee of profit.<Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost" /> Users remain protected
from front-running and sandwich attacks because the mempool stays
private.<Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost" />

**Subgoal: read the pricing model the docs state.** The value of the express lane is the sum of the
MEV the controller predicts it can extract during the round, plus the MEV it extracts during control
that it did not predict.<Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost" /> The first term is what bidders can reason
about before the auction closes; the second is realised variance they cannot. In a second-price
auction with independent private values, bidding your true valuation is a dominant strategy — but the
second term means valuations here are neither fully private nor fully known even to the bidder, so
treat the clean auction-theory result as a starting point rather than a conclusion.

<Aside kind="note">
Notice what the auction does not change: the *sequencer* still holds the right and still runs the
auction. Bids may be made in any ERC-20 token, in any amount, and collected by any address, at the
full discretion of the chain owner.<Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost" /> The auctioneer is described as
autonomous and off-chain. This is an operator voluntarily converting a private extraction right into
a public market whose parameters and proceeds it controls. That is a meaningful improvement over
keeping it silently, and it is not the same thing as the right being contested.
</Aside>

### The claim that is a hypothesis

Timeboost's docs state an expectation worth flagging as an expectation rather than a result: that
rational, profit-seeking actors will spend on auctions *instead of* investing in hardware or
infrastructure to win latency races, and that this diversion will reduce MEV-driven spam on the
network.<Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost" />

That is a plausible substitution argument and it is not obviously complete. Winning the auction
requires estimating the round's MEV, and estimating it better than rivals is itself a technical
capability worth investing in. It is entirely coherent for the same firms to spend on both — the
auction to secure the lane, and infrastructure to know what to bid. Whether total spend on latency
infrastructure actually fell is an empirical question that this lesson has no measurement for, and
you should not treat a stated design intention as an observed outcome. **Ask for the measurement
before repeating the claim.**

The narrower point stands on its own, though, and it is the one that matters for the module's
argument: FCFS is not a neutral default. It routes value to whoever can be physically closest to the
sequencer's endpoint. Every ordering policy allocates the ordering right to somebody, and "first
come, first served" allocates it to capital spent on latency. The operator's choice is not *whether*
to allocate, only *how*.

## Detecting any of this from outside

Every capability above is observable, and almost nobody instruments for it. The useful framing is that
the sequencer is a **vendor dependency with no status page you should trust**, so you build your own.

**Sequencer liveness.** Poll `latest` on the L2 and alert when the block number stops advancing for
more than a few multiples of the block time. This catches the loud outage
shape.<Cite src="op-docs-sequencer-outages" />

**Batch posting health.** Track the gap between `latest` and `safe`. Its normal value is the chain's
batch interval; a sustained widening is the *only* symptom of a transaction-submission outage, in
which users transact happily while nothing durable is
happening.<Cite src="op-docs-sequencer-outages" /> This is the highest-value single alert in the
list, because it is the failure with no other tell.

**Finality lag.** Track the gap between `safe` and `finalized`. This one is not about your chain at
all — it moves with Ethereum's finality, and it moves for every L2 simultaneously.

**Censorship of your own traffic.** If your product submits transactions, measure the delay between
submission and appearance in a block, per transaction, and alert on the tail rather than the mean.
Selective censorship does not change the mean.

**Ordering surprise.** For anything with a liquidation or an auction, record your transaction's index
within its block. A drift in that distribution is the observable footprint of an ordering policy
change — including one the operator did not announce.

Four of those five are three lines of code against an RPC you already pay for. The reason they are
missing from most systems is not difficulty; it is that the sequencer is filed mentally under
"blockchain" rather than under "single-vendor dependency in the critical path", and only the second
framing generates alerts.

<Check type="explain" concept="sequencer-worst-case-is-bounded">
You run a lending protocol on a single-sequencer L2. Model the operator as fully hostile from
tomorrow morning and work out, for each of the following, whether it is possible, and if so what
bounds the damage.

1. Draining the protocol's collateral to an address the operator controls.
2. Liquidating a large borrower profitably by ordering the operator's own liquidation transaction
   ahead of everyone else's.
3. Preventing a specific borrower from ever topping up their collateral.
4. Stopping the protocol from being usable at all for a week.
5. Reversing a repayment that a user's wallet already showed as confirmed.

<Answer>
**1 — not possible.** It requires either a transaction signed by the protocol's admin, which the
sequencer cannot forge, or a state transition that violates the rules, which the proof system
rejects. Both mechanisms are named in the "cannot" column above. Carry the caveat: if the same
organisation also holds the chain's upgrade authority, the *organisation* can do it — via the L1
contracts, not via the sequencer.

**2 — possible, and it is the default rather than an attack.** Choosing an ordering is exactly the
sequencer's job, and a liquidation is a valid transaction. No proof system objects. The bound is
economic rather than technical: the operator has a reputational and regulatory cost, and on a chain
running Timeboost the equivalent advantage is sold in a public auction rather than exercised
silently.<Cite src="arbitrum-timeboost" /> Note that your protocol cannot detect the difference on
chain — a well-ordered liquidation looks identical either way.

**3 — possible, and the bound is chain-specific.** On a chain rated *self sequence*, the borrower
routes around it through forced inclusion, at a cost of up to a day's
delay.<Cite src="l2beat-arbitrum-project" /> On a chain rated *no mechanism*, they cannot route around
it at all.<Cite src="l2beat-linea-project" /> For a lending protocol this is the entry that should
alarm you most: a day of censorship during a price move is the difference between a top-up and a
liquidation, and the delay is not a bug in the escape path — it is the escape path working as
designed.

**4 — possible, and it costs you rather than robbing you.** A week of downtime is seventeen weeks'
worth of a 99.9% budget by the arithmetic above, and there is no failover. Note the asymmetry: this is
the entry with the highest probability and the lowest drama, which is why it is the one that actually
happens.

**5 — possible only before the batch reaches Ethereum**, because the sequencer can reorg its own
unsafe blocks and nothing else.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-finality" /> That window is minutes on a
high-throughput chain and potentially hours on one tuned for low data costs. After the data is on
Ethereum, ordering no longer depends on the operator.

**The pattern worth extracting.** The catastrophic-sounding entry is impossible; the boring entries
are possible, likely, and unpriced. That inversion is the reason to state the bound precisely rather
than gesture at it.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** follows the money on the other side: what the operator earns for running this
process, why the margin is thinner than the fee charts suggest, and why a chain's economics now push
towards volume rather than towards extracting more per transaction.

**The lesson after it** takes the three proposals for removing the single operator and works out
which of the entries above each one actually fixes. The list is shorter than you would hope, and the
entry it never touches is the one from the previous module.

**The security track's actor-capability framing** is what this lesson is: one actor, enumerated
capabilities, mechanism named for each denial. The reason it feels different is that the actor is a
vendor rather than an attacker, and vendors do not appear in most threat models until they behave
like attackers.

**Your own systems.** Take the five-item list in the check and rewrite it for your protocol. Item 3
is the one that will change what you build.
