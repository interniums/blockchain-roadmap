A user pays a third of a cent to swap on an OP Stack chain. Follow the money.

It does not go to one place, and one of the pieces is not revenue at all — it is a pass-through of a
cost the chain incurs on Ethereum on that user's behalf. Telling the two apart is the whole of L2
economics, and it is why "L2s print money" and "L2 margins collapsed" are both circulating as
confident claims about the same set of businesses.

This lesson takes the fee apart with the operator's own formulas, then takes one chain's published
costs apart and checks that the numbers close. It also names, explicitly, the figures this project
could not verify and will therefore not print.

## The fee has four components and four destinations

On an OP Stack chain the total is, after the Isthmus upgrade:<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-fees" />

```text
totalFee = operatorFee + gasUsed × (baseFee + priorityFee) + l1Fee
```

Each component lands in a different predeploy contract, and the table of vaults is worth
memorising because it makes the revenue question concrete:<Cite src="op-docs-fee-vaults" />

The second row is the one that should stop you. On Ethereum the base fee is burned. On an OP Stack
chain it is routed into a vault the chain operator controls the recipient
of.<Cite src="op-docs-fee-vaults" /> The mechanism you learned as monetary policy on L1 is a revenue
line on L2, and nothing in the user-facing fee display distinguishes the two.

Withdrawal from the vaults is permissionless: anyone can call `withdraw()`, the entire balance moves
rather than a partial amount, and routing follows a `withdrawalNetwork` parameter — either a direct
ETH transfer on L2, or a withdrawal initiated through the `L2ToL1MessagePasser` with a 400,000 gas
limit that must then be proven and finalised on L1.<Cite src="op-docs-fee-vaults" /> Permissionless
withdrawal is a nice design detail: the funds can always reach the configured recipient without the
operator being available, which is the same escape-hatch reasoning applied to the operator's own
money.

## The L1 data fee, derived rather than quoted

The `l1Fee` component is the pass-through, and its formula has changed three times. Working through
the current one is worth doing because the constants look arbitrary until you cancel the units.

**Subgoal: understand the Ecotone form, which introduces the two scalars.** Ecotone estimates the
compressed size from calldata gas costs — 4 gas per zero byte, 16 per non-zero — and then divides by
16:<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-fees" />

```text
tx_compressed_size = (4·zeros + 16·nonzeros) / 16
weighted_gas_price = 16 · base_fee_scalar · base_fee  +  blob_base_fee_scalar · blob_base_fee
l1_data_fee        = tx_compressed_size × weighted_gas_price
```

**Subgoal: cancel the 16s and see what they are for.** Dividing calldata gas by 16 converts "gas" into
"non-zero-byte equivalents" — a size estimate in bytes. The calldata term then multiplies by 16 again
to convert bytes back into calldata gas before pricing it at the L1 base fee. The blob term has no
such factor because blob gas is already charged per byte of blob. **The two 16s are a unit conversion
in and back out, present so that one size estimate can price two different L1 resources.**

**Subgoal: read the Fjord form, which replaces the estimator.** Fjord swaps the zero/non-zero heuristic
for a FastLZ-compressed size fed through a linear model:<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-fees" />

```text
estimatedSizeScaled = max(minTransactionSize × 1e6, intercept + fastlzCoef × fastlzSize)
l1FeeScaled         = baseFeeScalar × l1BaseFee × 16 + blobFeeScalar × l1BlobBaseFee
l1Cost              = estimatedSizeScaled × l1FeeScaled / 1e12
```

**Subgoal: derive the 1e12.** `minTransactionSize`, `intercept` and `fastlzCoef` are scaled by 1e6,
so `estimatedSizeScaled` carries a factor of 1e6. Both fee scalars are also scaled by 1e6, so
`l1FeeScaled` carries another. Their product carries 1e12, and the divisor removes exactly that. The
constant is not a magic number — **it is the product of two independent fixed-point scalings meeting
in one multiplication.**

**Subgoal: notice what the model parameters are.** `intercept` and `fastlzCoef` were determined by
linear regression over a dataset of previous L2 transactions, minimising root-mean-square error
against the change in batch size when compressed with Brotli, over historical OP Mainnet
data.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-fees" /> The fee you pay is the output of a fitted model of how
much your transaction will grow a Brotli-compressed batch. That is an unusually honest piece of
engineering, and it means per-transaction L1 cost is an *estimate* by construction, not a
measurement.

## Gross, net, and a set of numbers that close

Gross revenue is everything users pay. Net is that minus what the chain spends: L1 posting, proving
if it runs a validity system, and ordinary operations.

L2BEAT publishes the L1 half per project. Its Scroll page, for the year ending 26 August 2026, gives
a total onchain cost of $57.76K, an average cost per L2 user operation of $0.002863, an average cost
per day of $158.24, total data posted of 18.01 GiB, an average of 50.51 MiB per day, and an average
of 958.39 bytes per user operation.<Cite src="l2beat-scroll-project" />

Six numbers. Check whether they are consistent, because an aggregator's figures are exactly the kind
of thing to verify rather than absorb.

**Check one — daily cost against annual cost.**

```text
$158.24 × 365 = $57,758   vs   $57.76K   ✓
```

**Check two — daily data against annual data.**

```text
50.51 MiB × 365 = 18,436 MiB = 18.00 GiB   vs   18.01 GiB   ✓
```

**Check three — cost per operation, derived from the other four.**

```text
18.01 GiB      = 18.01 × 1,073,741,824  ≈ 19.34 × 10⁹ bytes
operations     = 19.34 × 10⁹ ÷ 958.39   ≈ 20.18 × 10⁶
cost per op    = $57,760 ÷ 20.18 × 10⁶  ≈ $0.002863   ✓
```

All three close. The published set is internally consistent, which is worth knowing both because it
raises confidence in the source and because the technique transfers: **any four of those six numbers
determine the other two, so a set that does not close is telling you something.**

Two readings of the result itself. First, the absolute magnitude — under sixty thousand dollars of
Ethereum costs for a year of operating a chain — makes it obvious that L1 data is no longer the line
item that decides whether an L2 is viable. Second, and more useful: cost per operation is the number
to compare across chains, not total cost, because total cost is mostly a statement about volume.

## Why cheap data did not create a margin

The tempting conclusion is that cheap L1 data means fat margins. The fee decomposition rules it out,
and the argument is two steps.

**Step one: the L1 cost is charged onward explicitly.** The user's fee contains an `l1Fee` component
computed from the current Ethereum base fee and blob base fee, relayed on-chain and updated every
Ethereum block.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-fees" /> It is a pass-through, sized to the cost.

**Step two: therefore both sides of the spread move together.** When the L1 data cost falls, the
amount the chain can justify charging as an L1 data fee falls with it, automatically, by formula. A
chain that kept charging the old amount would be visibly overcharging against a publicly readable
input.

So the direction is well supported: **cheap data compresses per-transaction margin rather than
expanding it, because the cheapness is passed through by construction.** What it does not support is
any specific margin figure, which moves with blob demand week to week.

The structural response is visible in the fee formula itself. The **operator fee** — introduced with
Isthmus and modified with Jovian — is a chain-configurable component with its own scalar and constant,
charged on top of everything else and routed to its own vault.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-fees" />
It exists because chains needed a revenue lever that is not a pass-through, once the pass-through
stopped covering anything.

## The data lane, on the record

The blob capacity schedule *is* verified from primary sources, and most material you will read is
stale on it. EIP-4844 introduced blobs;<Cite src="eip-4844" /> EIP-7892 made target and maximum
changeable by standalone blob-parameter-only forks rather than by named
hardforks;<Cite src="eip-7892" /> and BPO2, specified in EIP-8135, activated on 7 January 2026 with a
target of 14 and a maximum of 21.<Cite src="eip-8135" /><Cite src="fusaka-mainnet-announcement" /> A
rollup operator's own account of what that changed for its batcher is published by
Optimism.<Cite src="optimism-fusaka-is-live" />

Whether a further BPO fork has landed between then and now is **not established**, and the
target/maximum pair current today should be read from the Ethereum Foundation's blog rather than from
any curriculum, including this one.

Note also that the choice between blobs and calldata remains live per batch. Arbitrum's batch poster
uses `addSequencerL2BatchFromBlobs` by default where the parent chain supports EIP-4844, and switches
to `addSequencerL2Batch` with calldata when the blob base fee is high or blob space is
constrained.<Cite src="arbitrum-sequencer" /> A batcher that only implements one path is
unoptimised in one regime and broken in the other.

An investor memo states: "This L2 processed 40 million transactions last quarter at an average fee of
$0.004, generating $160,000 in quarterly revenue with near-zero costs now that blobs are cheap."

List everything wrong with that sentence, and say what you would ask for instead.

<Answer>
**"Revenue" is gross, and part of it is not the chain's.** The user's fee contains an explicit L1 data
component that exists to recover what the chain pays Ethereum.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-fees" />
Counting it as revenue double-counts: it is simultaneously in the top line and in the cost line.

**"Near-zero costs" conflates two different costs.** L1 data may well be small — L2BEAT's published
onchain costs for a mid-size chain run to tens of thousands of dollars a
year.<Cite src="l2beat-scroll-project" /> But if this chain runs a validity proof system it pays a
recurring proving bill that does not appear in that figure at all, and it pays ordinary
infrastructure and staffing costs that appear nowhere on chain.

**Cheap blobs cut the top line too.** The memo treats falling data cost as pure margin expansion. The
fee formula passes the L1 cost through, so the amount chargeable falls with the cost. Both sides
move.

**The revenue split is unstated.** Base fees, priority fees, L1 fees and operator fees land in four
different vaults with four separately configurable recipients.<Cite src="op-docs-fee-vaults" /> "The
chain's revenue" is not one number until somebody says which vaults are in scope and where each one
withdraws to.

**What to ask for instead.** Net revenue after L1 posting and proving, stated per period with the
period named; the split by fee component; the operator-fee configuration, since it is the only
non-pass-through lever; and cost per user operation rather than total cost, so the figure is
comparable across chains and across volume levels. Then ask what happens to each line if blob base
fee returns to a congested regime — because it has done so before and the schedule that made it cheap
is a governance parameter, not a law.
</Answer>

## Where this pays off

**The last lesson of this module** looks at the three proposals for replacing the single sequencer,
and the economics here are why the adoption record is what it is: sequencing revenue is the operator's
business model, and a proposal that moves ordering elsewhere is asking a company to give up its
income statement.

**The blobs module** later in this track goes under `l1Fee` properly — the dual market, the type-2
versus type-3 batching decision, and the posting-cadence trade-off that this lesson touched when it
noted that a chain's batch interval sets its reorg window.

**The infrastructure track's cost work** transfers directly: cost per unit of work, measured rather
than modelled, compared across vendors. An L2 is a vendor with an unusually good public cost
disclosure, and most vendors do not publish theirs on a dashboard.<Cite src="l2beat-summary" />

**Your own systems.** Pull the four vault balances for your chain and see who the recipients are. It
takes one `eth_getBalance` per address and it is the most direct available answer to "who is this
chain actually for".
