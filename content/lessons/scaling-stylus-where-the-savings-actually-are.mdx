The number everyone quotes for Stylus is "10 to 100 times cheaper". Arbitrum publishes it, on its own
gas-metering page, and the sentence it sits in is this one: compute is generally 10-100x cheaper
depending on the program.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-metering" />

The word doing the work is *compute*. Three sentences later the same page says that while the
underlying `SLOAD` and `SSTORE` operations cost as they do in the
EVM, the Rust SDK implements a caching policy that minimises their
use.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-metering" /> The VM-differences page is blunter still: state-bound
work is identical across the two environments.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-vm-differences" />

So the headline is true and almost useless on its own. What decides whether Stylus is worth using on
a given function is the ratio of two costs that move independently — one that collapses and one that
does not move at all — plus a fixed overhead that has to be earned back. This lesson gives you the
numbers to run that calculation, and the honest status of each one.

## Ink: why a second unit exists at all

Start with the mechanical fact that forces the design. WASM opcodes are orders of magnitude faster
than their EVM counterparts, so almost every operation Stylus performs costs less than 1
gas.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-metering" />

Fractional gas is not an EVM concept. There is no representation for 0.007 gas anywhere in a receipt,
a refund calculation or a block's gas accounting. Round it up to 1 and you have thrown away the
entire advantage — every cheap instruction now costs the same as `ADD`. Round it down to 0 and you
have an unmetered VM, which on a chain that must fraud-prove its own execution is not an option.

The resolution is a second unit underneath gas. Stylus meters in **ink**, and the exchange rate is
fixed at 1 gas = 10,000 ink.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-metering" /> The conversion happens
automatically as execution passes between the two VMs, and receipts are always denominated in gas —
users never encounter ink.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-metering" />

Two properties of that rate matter more than the number itself.

**It is a chain parameter, not a constant.** The ink price — how much ink one EVM gas buys — is
configurable by the chain owner, with 1:10000 as the default.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-metering" />
The documentation explains the mechanism it is there to provide: if the Stylus VM becomes twice as
fast, rather than repricing every operation, the ink price can be halved so one gas buys twice as
much ink.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-metering" /> That is a repricing lever for an entire VM,
pulled in one place. It also means that any ink figure you hard-code into a spreadsheet is a
snapshot.

**You can read it from inside a contract.** The SDK exposes `evm_ink_left()` alongside
`evm_gas_left()`, and `tx_ink_price()` returns the current rate, with the conversion being
`gas = ink * ink_price / 10000`.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-vm-differences" /> Arbitrum recommends ink
for exactly one purpose: developers optimising contracts may measure in ink to pin down the exact
cost of a routine.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-metering" /> It is a profiler unit. Treat it as one.

## Where the compute saving physically comes from

It is worth knowing the mechanism, because it tells you which of the savings are structural and
which are pricing decisions that could be tuned away.

Arbitrum walks through `ADD`. In the EVM: pay for gas, requiring multiple look-ups of an in-memory
table; consider tracing, even if disabled; pop two items off the simulated stack; add them; push the
result. In the Stylus VM: execute a single x86 or ARM `ADD`
instruction.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-metering" />

Two of those lines are the whole story. The simulated stack disappears because the program is
compiled to native code rather than interpreted. And the metering overhead shrinks because, unlike
the EVM's charge-before-every-opcode discipline, the Stylus VM strategically charges for many opcodes
at once, with the charging logic living inside the program itself rather than in an in-memory
table.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-metering" />

Neither of those is a pricing choice. Both are consequences of "compiled, separately metered VM"
rather than "interpreter". That is why the compute advantage is durable in a way that a repriced
opcode would not be.

Set that against the EVM you know. Arbitrum's own comparison uses `ADD` at 3 gas as the reference
point for EVM per-opcode granularity;<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-vm-differences" /> the WASM equivalent
is 0.007, and a 64-bit multiply is 0.016.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-opcode-hostio-pricing" /> Those
are gaps of two to three orders of magnitude per instruction. A tight arithmetic loop — the shape of
a fixed-point curve evaluation, a Poseidon round, a field multiplication — is where that gap
compounds into the "50-100x" that Arbitrum's optimisation guide lists for compute-heavy
loops.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-optimization" />

Read the caveat the table carries, though, because it is unusually explicit. The values were
determined via a conservative statistical analysis, are expected to change as Stylus matures, and may
fluctuate across upgrades. They are defined in the upstream Stylus and Arbitrator source rather than
in the precompiles, so the page tells you to confirm current values against that source before
relying on exact figures. Only one relationship is stated as fixed: 10,000 ink = 1
gas.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-opcode-hostio-pricing" />

<Aside kind="note">
Notice what is *not* in that table: anything touching the chain. No `SLOAD`, no `CALL` to another
contract, no `msg.sender`. WASM as a specification has no such instructions — which is the
observation the first lesson ended on, and the reason the next section exists.
</Aside>

## The host I/O boundary, priced

Every interaction with the chain leaves the fast native region through a host I/O. Arbitrum prices
the transition itself: suspending WASM execution so the Stylus VM can perform a task natively in the
host costs about 0.84 gas.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-opcode-hostio-pricing" />

The per-byte terms on `read_args` and `write_result` count bytes after the first
32.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-opcode-hostio-pricing" /> Those two rows are the ones that surprise
people: reading your own calldata and writing your own return value are metered operations with
per-byte costs, and `write_result` is more than three times the price of `read_args` per byte. A
function that returns a large array is paying for that array twice — once to build it in memory and
once to hand it back across the boundary.

`keccak` is instructive in the other direction. On the EVM, `KECCAK256` costs 30 gas plus 6 gas per
32-byte word.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-optimization" /> The Stylus hostio is 12.18 plus 2.1 per
word — cheaper, but by a factor of roughly three, not a hundred. Arbitrum's own optimisation guide
says so plainly: keccak is already cheap per byte on the EVM, so hashing is not a headline
saving.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-optimization" /> If your hot path is "hash a lot of things",
Stylus is a modest win. If it is "do a lot of arithmetic between hashes", it is a large one.

## The fact that decides everything

Storage is not cheaper. Not somewhat cheaper, not cheaper in aggregate — the same.

Arbitrum's VM-differences page states that both environments use the same 256-bit key-value store,
that EVM and WASM contracts can share storage, and that `SLOAD` and `SSTORE` costs are
identical.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-vm-differences" /> The optimisation guide's comparison table
gives storage operations a relative saving of "None
(1x)".<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-optimization" /> The refund logic matches the EVM
exactly.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-vm-differences" />

So the numbers you measured in the EVM track carry over unchanged. A cold `SLOAD` is 2,100 gas and a
warm one 100; an `SSTORE` from zero to non-zero on a cold slot is 20,000 plus the 2,100 cold
surcharge, and non-zero to non-zero is 2,900 plus the same surcharge.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" />
[Cold-versus-warm access](/c/cold-vs-warm-access) works the way EIP-2929 defined it, and clearing a
slot refunds under the EIP-3529 cap of 4,800 gas per slot, with the transaction total bounded at one
fifth of the gas used.<Cite src="eip-2929" /><Cite src="eip-3529" /><Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-optimization" />

### What the SDK's storage cache actually buys

There is one genuine storage-side difference, and it is smaller than it sounds until you look at what
it replaces.

The Stylus SDK caches storage within a call. The first read pays the full `SLOAD` cost; additional
reads of the same slot in the same call are cheaper because they are served from the cache; and the
cache is automatically flushed at the call boundary.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-vm-differences" />

Now do the arithmetic on what that saves, because it is not the expensive part.

**Subgoal 1 — price the first read.** It is a cold `SLOAD`: 2,100 gas.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" />
The cache does not touch it. Nothing can; the slot has to be fetched.

**Subgoal 2 — price the reads it does eliminate.** The second and subsequent reads in the same call
would have been *warm* `SLOAD`s at 100 gas each.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /> That is what the
cache removes.

**Subgoal 3 — compare against competent Solidity.** A Solidity developer who hoists the read into a
local variable eliminates exactly the same 100-gas-per-read cost, by hand, in one line. Arbitrum's own
optimisation guide recommends the identical hoist for Stylus code in large
loops.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-optimization" />

**Subgoal 4 — state the conclusion precisely.** The cache is a default-correctness feature, not a cost
advantage. Naive Rust beats naive Solidity on a storage-touching path; careful Solidity closes the gap
entirely. Budget it as insurance against a mistake, not as a line item in a savings estimate.

## Memory: the change that makes new things possible

The EVM charges for memory with a quadratic term. You know what that does to algorithm design: large
intermediate buffers are not expensive, they are *forbidden*, and every on-chain algorithm gets
rewritten to stream rather than to hold.

Stylus prices memory by the 64 KB WASM page, and a program can use up to 8 MB by default — a
`PageLimit` of 128 pages, configurable by the chain owner.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-metering" />
The VM-differences page describes the charge as a linear cost per page, levied through
`pay_for_memory_grow`, which the SDK's `#[entrypoint]` macro wires up and which Nitro inserts calls to
automatically when allocation occurs.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-vm-differences" />

Two things here need care.

**The two documentation pages describe the curve differently.** The gas-metering page attributes the
memory saving to a novel *exponential* pricing mechanism, while stating in the same paragraph that in
practice the gas cost of a compute- and memory-heavy Stylus call grows *near-linearly* with workload
size.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-metering" /> The VM-differences page says linear cost per
page.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-vm-differences" /> Those are not the same claim about the shape of the
curve, and nothing in either page reconciles them. What both agree on, and what you can design
against, is the direction: sub-quadratic, and dramatically flatter than the EVM's. Treat the exact
curve as something to measure on the chain you are shipping to.

**The headline multiplier is a vendor figure with no independent check.** Memory is quoted at
100-500x cheaper.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-metering" /> No third-party benchmark corroborates
that range. It comes from Offchain Labs, about Offchain Labs' own product.

What is not in dispute is the qualitative consequence, and it is the more interesting one. An 8 MB
working set changes which algorithms are expressible at all: a large in-memory table, a cryptographic
scratch space, a full proof transcript. That is a category change, not a discount.

## The overheads a small function will never earn back

Stylus has fixed costs that Solidity does not, and they are the reason "port everything" is wrong even
when the compute saving is real.

**Activation.** The first time a WASM is deployed it must be activated, which charges a configurable
`ActivationGas` amount — 0 by default — plus a fixed computation charge of 1,659,168 gas, plus a
dynamic data fee that scales with the size of the compiled program.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-metering" />
The good news is that it is per-codehash: you do not have to activate future copies of the same
program, so the same template can be deployed many times paying activation
once.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-metering" />

**Per-call initialisation.** Calling a Stylus contract incurs a minimum init cost of 8,832 gas when the
program is not cached, or 352 gas when it is, plus a dynamic init term proportional to the program's
complexity.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-metering" /> The documentation draws the conclusion for you:
if a contract does next to nothing, it may be cheaper in
Solidity.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-metering" />

Hold those two numbers next to each other, because the ratio is the point. **Uncached, every single
call pays 8,832 gas before your code runs.** That is more than four cold `SLOAD`s. Cached, it is 352 —
roughly three warm `SLOAD`s. The difference between a Stylus contract that is in the chain's cache and
one that is not is a factor of twenty-five on the fixed entry cost of every call it ever receives.

### Working the break-even

Take a function that does real arithmetic — say it costs 40,000 gas in Solidity and 4,000 gas of
metered WASM compute in Stylus, a 10x compute saving inside Arbitrum's stated range.

**Subgoal 1 — restate the per-call comparison honestly.** Solidity: 40,000. Stylus, uncached:
8,832 + 4,000 = 12,832, plus the dynamic init term.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-metering" /> Saving,
about 27,000 gas per call. Comfortable.

**Subgoal 2 — do it again for a small function.** Now suppose the function costs 9,000 gas in Solidity
and 900 in Stylus. Uncached: 8,832 + 900 = 9,732. Stylus is *more expensive*, despite a genuine 10x
compute saving, because the entry fee swallowed it. This is the case the documentation is warning
about, and it is not a corner case — it describes most functions in most contracts.

**Subgoal 3 — amortise activation.** At 27,000 gas saved per call, the 1,659,168-gas fixed activation
charge alone needs roughly 61 calls before the contract is ahead, ignoring the data fee. For a hot path
that is nothing. For an admin function called four times a year it is never.

**Subgoal 4 — name what the arithmetic cannot tell you.** Every input above is a chain parameter.
Arbitrum says as much: all of these parameters are configurable by the chain owner and subject to
change, and since gas numbers vary across updates, it may make more sense to clock how long an
operation takes than to go solely by the numbers in
receipts.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-metering" /> The structure of this calculation is durable. The
constants in it are not.

## Caching is an auction, and it does not renew itself

The 8,832-versus-352 gap has a mechanism behind it, and the mechanism has an ongoing cost.

Cached contracts are held in memory rather than read from disk, which is what removes most of the
initialisation work.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-caching-contracts" /> The cache is managed by an
on-chain `CacheManager` contract that holds approximately 4,000 contracts and runs an
auction: developers bid to have their contract inserted, and when the cache is full the lowest bids
are evicted to make room.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-caching-contracts" /><Cite src="nitro-contracts-cache-manager" />
Bids are held in a minimum heap encoded as `bid << 64 + index`, and a global decay parameter computed
as `decay = block.timestamp * _decay` inflates the value of bids over time — which is the documentation's
stated way of ensuring developers periodically pay to maintain their
position.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-caching-contracts" />

Three consequences for anyone running this in production.

**It is opt-in and explicit.** Accessing an uncached contract does not add it to the cache; only
explicit calls to the `CacheManager` do.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-caching-contracts" /> Traffic alone
will not get you in.

**It is competitive and losable.** If your contract is removed from the cache, calling it becomes more
expensive unless it is re-added.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-caching-contracts" /> Your per-call fixed
cost can rise by 8,480 gas because somebody else outbid you, with no change on your side at all.

**You can read the real numbers rather than guess.** `programInitGas(address)` on the `ArbWasm`
precompile returns the initialisation cost both cached and
uncached.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-caching-contracts" /><Cite src="arbitrum-precompiles-reference" />
`getMinBid` on the `CacheManager` — or `cargo stylus cache suggest-bid` — gives the current entry
price, and `cargo stylus cache status --address=...` tells you whether you are still
in.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-caching-contracts" />

One more line item, for large programs: a contract split across fragments incurs an additional
activation cost roughly equivalent to one `EXTCODEREAD` per fragment, because ArbOS must read each
fragment from state before concatenation and decompression. Caching still helps afterwards, since the
cache stores the fully reconstructed native code rather than the
fragments.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-caching-contracts" />

## The status of every number in this lesson

Arbitrum's optimisation guide contains an unusually candid paragraph, and it is the right note to end
the measurement section on. Its own comparison table — `ecrecover` at roughly 3,000 gas on the EVM
against roughly 300 in Stylus, `MLOAD` and `MSTORE` at about 3 gas per word against about 0.3 — is
followed by the statement that the Stylus-side figures and the multipliers are directional, drawn from
Offchain Labs' own benchmarks, and vary with workload, input size and ArbOS version. Benchmark your own
contract to get numbers you can rely on.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-optimization" />

That is the vendor telling you not to quote the vendor. Take it.

**Function A** reads three storage slots, adds them, writes one slot back, and emits an event.

**Function B** reads one storage slot to get a configuration value, then runs 20,000 iterations of

Predict which one benefits, quantify why, and name the one thing about function B that could still

<Answer>
**Function A gains nothing and probably loses.** Decompose its cost. Three `SLOAD`s and one `SSTORE`

emission uses the same underlying mechanism and costs the same
gas.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-vm-differences" /> The additions are the only compute, and three
`I64Add`s at 0.01 gas each is 0.03 gas of savings against a state-bound cost in the thousands. Then
add the per-call init cost: 8,832 gas uncached, or 352 cached, plus a dynamic
term.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-metering" /> Even in the best case the entry fee exceeds every
saving available. Uncached, it is not close.

**Function B is the shape Stylus exists for**, and for two independent reasons rather than one. The
20,000-iteration loop is metered per WASM instruction at ink prices — `I64Mul` at 0.016 gas,
`I64Add` at 0.01 — against EVM opcodes costing single-digit gas each, which is the regime Arbitrum's
guide labels roughly 50-100x for compute-heavy
loops.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-opcode-hostio-pricing" /><Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-optimization" />

On the EVM the buffer alone may well be the dominant cost; in Stylus it is close to a rounding error.
The single `SLOAD` is unchanged, and irrelevant next to the rest.

**What could still sink function B: the return path and the cache.** Two specifics. First, this
function returns one `uint256` — fine. Had it returned the 200 KB buffer, `write_result` charges
1.6381 gas per byte after the first 32, which on 200 KB is on the order of 300,000
gas.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-opcode-hostio-pricing" /> The rule that falls out: keep large working
sets *inside* the call. Second, if the contract is not in the `CacheManager`'s cache, every one of
those thousands of daily calls pays 8,832 gas instead of 352, and staying cached means bidding in an
auction with a decay parameter designed to make you pay
again.<Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-gas-metering" /><Cite src="arbitrum-stylus-caching-contracts" /> That
is an operational obligation, not a one-off.

**The general rule, stated once.** Split the function's cost into state-bound, compute-bound and
memory-bound parts before reaching for a language. Stylus moves exactly two of those three, and adds a
fixed entry fee to every call regardless.
</Answer>

## What this unlocks

You can now answer the only question that matters at design time — *does this component get cheaper?*
— with a decomposition rather than an opinion. Split the hot path into state access, computation and
memory; hold state access constant; apply a large but vendor-sourced multiplier to the other two; then
subtract a per-call entry fee of 8,832 or 352 gas and amortise a 1,659,168-gas activation charge over
the expected call count.

Two of those inputs are not costs at all but obligations, and they are what the next lesson is about.
**Activation** is a second transaction your deployment script must perform and your runbook must know
about. And an activated program does not stay activated — there is a `programTimeLeft` on a chain
precompile for a reason. Deployment, for a Stylus contract, is not the last step.
