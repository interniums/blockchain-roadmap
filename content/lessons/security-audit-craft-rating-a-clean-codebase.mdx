One category is below the rest, and the paragraph explaining it is the most useful prose in the report.
Assembly is used extensively for gas optimisation; some functions skip input validation to save gas; and
the conclusion, in the report's own words, is that the complexity of the system and the low-level
optimisations make a steep learning curve, and it is highly likely that an inexperienced developer will
introduce bugs inadvertently.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" />

That is not a finding. It is a prediction — about the bugs that will be written *after* the engagement
ends — and no count of current defects could have produced it.

## Why the second deliverable exists

Trail of Bits' own explanation is worth reading as a piece of reasoning rather than as marketing. Bugs
often indicate deeper issues that can be challenging to understand and address; given the time-boxed nature
of reviews, security engineers may not have the opportunity to identify all bugs caused by these problems,
and those problems may continue to cause issues in the future even after the initial bugs are
fixed.<Cite src="tob-blockchain-maturity" />

Three claims, each doing work:

**Findings are symptoms.** A rounding bug is an instance; the absence of a systematic approach to
arithmetic is the cause. Fix the instance and the cause keeps producing instances.

**A time-boxed review cannot enumerate the instances.** This is the honest admission underneath the whole
practice. Six engineer-weeks buys a sample, and the sample size is a budget decision.

**Therefore the useful deliverable is about the process, not the artefact.** The rubric was developed from
performing hundreds of security audits over more than a decade, identifying the control families where
flaws are commonly found and where improvements are frequently
needed.<Cite src="tob-blockchain-maturity" />

There is also a framing point they make explicitly, and it is the one clients respond to: the maturity
evaluation helps demonstrate positive progress toward safety rather than just detection of bugs, which they
call a negative indicator.<Cite src="tob-blockchain-maturity" /> A finding count can only go down as
attention goes up. A maturity table can go up.

## The categories, and why the list is not fixed

The published set is nine: Arithmetic; Auditing; Authentication/access controls; Complexity management;
Decentralization; Documentation; Low-level manipulation; Transaction ordering risks; Testing and
verification.<Cite src="tob-blockchain-maturity" />

Immediately after listing them, the same source adds the qualifier that most summaries drop: this
control-family approach is applied for all clients, blockchain or otherwise, and the controls are
**adjusted based on the target of the review** — with cryptography and application-security teams having
their own recommended controls.<Cite src="tob-blockchain-maturity" />

You can watch that happen. The Uniswap v4 report's appendix defines ten categories, adding **Cryptography
and Key Management** — the safe use of cryptographic primitives and functions, along with robust mechanisms
for key generation and distribution — even though it does not appear in that engagement's rating
table.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" />

So: **copy the category names verbatim from the report you are working under, and expect them to vary.**
The value is in the questions, not in the labels.

| Category | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| Arithmetic | Is there a *systematic* approach to correctness — rounding, precision, overflow — or a collection of habits? |
| Auditing | Could an incident responder reconstruct what happened from the events emitted? |
| Authentication / access controls | Is there an enumerable role and capability matrix, or is it "whatever `onlyOwner` is on"? |
| Complexity management | Can a reviewer hold a function in their head? What happens when a new engineer joins? |
| Decentralization | What can the privileged actor do, how fast, and can users exit first? |
| Documentation | Do the specifications state invariants, or only the happy path? |
| Transaction ordering | Is the protocol safe under adversarial ordering? |
| Low-level manipulation | Is the assembly justified, documented, and cross-checked against a high-level equivalent? |
| Testing and verification | Do the tests encode invariants, or only paths? |

## The ratings, and a disagreement this curriculum will not hide

Here the sources do not agree, and pretending otherwise would be exactly the failure this module is
about.

**The blog post announcing the rubric names four:** weak, moderate, satisfactory, or
strong.<Cite src="tob-blockchain-maturity" />

**The criteria document the blog links to names five:** Missing (not present / not implemented); Weak
(several and/or significant areas of improvement identified); Moderate (adequate procedure, but it can be
improved); Satisfactory (above average, but it can be improved); Strong (only small potential areas of
improvement identified).<Cite src="tob-code-maturity-criteria" />

**A published report's own appendix names eight:** Strong (no issues found, and the system exceeds industry
standards); Satisfactory (minor issues, compliant with best practices); Moderate (some issues that may
affect system safety); Weak (many issues that affect system safety); Missing (a required component is
missing, significantly affecting system safety); Not Applicable (the category is not applicable to this
review); Not Considered (the category was not considered in this review); Further Investigation Required
(further investigation is required to reach a meaningful
conclusion).<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" />

The three lists are reconcilable — five assessment ratings plus three non-assessment statuses, with the
blog naming only the middle four — but this curriculum has not established which is canonical, and the
firm's own documents present them differently. **Treat the scale as report-local.** Read every rating
against the legend printed in the report that produced it.

The practical consequence is sharper than the taxonomy. Three of those eight values are *not ratings at
all*: Not Applicable, Not Considered, and Further Investigation Required each mean nobody formed a
judgement.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> **Never read a category's absence, or a non-rating, as
a pass.** "Not Considered" on Decentralization is not a clean bill of health; it is a hole in the
engagement with a name.

### How a rating is determined

The criteria document publishes an actual procedure, which is what makes the ratings reproducible rather
than impressionistic. The process varies with the codebase — use cases, size, complexity, audit goals,
timeline — but the general approach is:<Cite src="tob-code-maturity-criteria" />

1. If **any** "Weak" criterion applies, Weak is applied.
2. If **none** of the Weak criteria apply and **some** Moderate criteria apply, Moderate can be applied.
3. If **all** Moderate criteria apply and **some** Satisfactory criteria apply, Satisfactory can be applied.
4. If **all** Satisfactory criteria apply **and** there is evidence of exceptional practices or security
   controls, Strong can be applied.

Two properties of that algorithm are worth internalising, because they explain most surprising ratings.

**Weak is disjunctive; everything above it is conjunctive.** One weak criterion drags the category down;
one satisfactory criterion does not lift it. This is deliberately asymmetric, and it means a category with
excellent practices in four dimensions and a gap in the fifth is Weak, not "Satisfactory with a note".

**Strong requires evidence beyond compliance.** All satisfactory criteria *plus* something exceptional. In
the Uniswap table, Arithmetic reached Strong on a codebase where high-level operations use checked
arithmetic by default, low-level arithmetic matches the high-level counterparts, and rounding directions
are considered and explicitly documented — with widespread `unchecked` usage, but special consideration for
operations that could overflow and documented edge cases.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> Strong
is not the absence of `unchecked`; it is the presence of reasoning about it.

### The bars are higher than teams expect

Read one category's criteria in full and the recalibration is immediate.

**Arithmetic is Weak if** there is no explicit overflow protection with no justification; if intentional
`unchecked` usage is not sufficiently documented; if there is no specification of the arithmetic formulas
or the specification does not match the code; if no explicit testing strategy has been identified to
increase confidence in the arithmetic; or if testing does not cover critical arithmetic edge
cases.<Cite src="tob-code-maturity-criteria" /> Any one of those. The blog puts a number on the
consequence: a codebase without an automated testing method targeting arithmetic can be considered moderate
at best — and adds that if you have not incorporated fuzzing into your development process, you have fallen
behind.<Cite src="tob-blockchain-maturity" />

**Auditing at Moderate already requires off-chain infrastructure.** Not just events for all critical
functions, but an off-chain monitoring system that logs events with an implemented monitoring plan;
documentation describing the purpose of events, how they should be used and their assumptions;
documentation describing how to review logs to audit a failure; and an incident response plan describing
how the protocol's actors must react in case of failure.<Cite src="tob-code-maturity-criteria" /> Satisfactory
adds notifications and alarms, defined roles and responsibilities, and an incident response plan that is
**regularly tested through a cybersecurity incident response
exercise**.<Cite src="tob-code-maturity-criteria" />

Most teams reading that discover they are Weak on Auditing while believing they were fine, because they
emit events. Events are the first criterion of five.

**Testing and Verification at Satisfactory requires 100% of reachable branch and statement coverage in unit
tests, an end-to-end automated testing technique covering all user entry points, isolated tests that do not
depend on execution order unless justified, and mutation testing to detect missing or incorrect tests and
properties.**<Cite src="tob-code-maturity-criteria" /> Mutation testing at Satisfactory, not at Strong.

**Decentralization is Weak if** critical functionality is upgradable by a single entity and an arbitrary
user cannot opt out or exit before the upgrade triggers; if a single entity directly controls user funds;
if all decision-making is controlled by a single entity; if system parameters can be changed at any time by
a single entity; or if permission from a centralized actor is required to use the
contracts.<Cite src="tob-code-maturity-criteria" /> Moderate requires that risks related to trusted parties
are documented, that users have a documented path to opt out of upgrades or exit, that privileged actors
cannot unilaterally move funds out of or trap funds in the protocol, and that all privileges are
documented.<Cite src="tob-code-maturity-criteria" />

Read that last one against the Uniswap table's **Decentralization: Strong**, whose justification is that
the protocol is built to be decentralized and the only privileged functions relate to protocol fees, capped
at a maximum of 0.1% if enabled.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> Strong here is a *design*
property — the admin cannot do very much — not a governance-process property.

## Worked example: rating a codebase in a day

A 4,000-line vault protocol, eight contracts, one upgradeable proxy. You have a day. Produce a table.

**Subgoal: collect evidence per category before assigning anything.** Ratings without cited evidence are
opinions, and the published tables always pair a rating with a paragraph naming specific code — TOB-UNI4-5
for a missing event, the absence of a two-step ownership transfer, the count of 590 provided
tests.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> Open a document with nine headings and fill it with
observations first. Assign nothing yet.

**Subgoal: run the Weak criteria first, because they are disjunctive and they terminate early.** For
Arithmetic: is there a written specification of the formulas, and does it match the code? If the answer is
"there are NatSpec comments", that is not a specification, and the category is Weak regardless of how good
the code looks.<Cite src="tob-code-maturity-criteria" /> This ordering saves hours — one criterion can
close a category.

**Subgoal: for Auditing, ask about the monitor and the plan, not about the events.** "Do you have an
off-chain system consuming these events, and a written incident response plan naming who does what?" Most
teams answer no to both, which is Weak, and the useful part of the conversation is that they expected
Satisfactory.<Cite src="tob-code-maturity-criteria" />

**Subgoal: for Decentralization, enumerate the upgrade path and time it.** Who can upgrade, in how many
transactions, with what notice, and can a user exit inside that window? A proxy upgradable by a single
multisig with no timelock hits the Weak criterion about critical functionality upgradable by a single
entity with no user opt-out.<Cite src="tob-code-maturity-criteria" /> Note that this is a *design* finding
that no test suite would ever produce.

**Subgoal: for Testing and Verification, ask what the tests assert, not how many there are.** Coverage
percentage is a weak signal — and on this stack a misleading one, since coverage tooling is the most
fragile part of the toolchain. The question that separates Moderate from Satisfactory is whether an
automated technique targets the *critical components*, and the question underneath that is whether the team
has written invariants at all. If they cannot name their ten most important ones, that answer is the
rating.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" />

**Subgoal: write the justification paragraph before the rating word.** Force yourself to write "the
codebase does X, which means Y" and let the rating fall out of the algorithm. This prevents the most common
failure — picking a rating that feels socially right and then finding evidence for it — and it produces the
paragraph the client will actually act on.

**Subgoal: use the non-ratings honestly.** If you did not examine transaction ordering because the scope
excluded the AMM integration, write Not Considered, not Satisfactory.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" />
A table with two honest non-ratings is more valuable than a table of nine confident guesses, and it tells
the client precisely what to buy next.

**Subgoal: predict the next engagement.** Close with one sentence per Weak or Moderate category saying what
class of finding it will produce. That sentence is the deliverable's whole reason for existing, and it is
the thing a finding count structurally cannot say.

A team shows you their audit report: 12 findings, all Low or Informational, zero High or Medium. The
maturity table reads Arithmetic: Moderate; Auditing: **Weak**; Authentication: Satisfactory; Complexity
Management: Satisfactory; Decentralization: **Weak**; Documentation: Moderate; Low-Level Manipulation:
**Not Applicable**; Testing and Verification: Moderate; Transaction Ordering: **Not Considered**.

They are about to launch with $50 million of deposits and want to know if they are ready. What do you tell
them, and in what order?

<Answer>
**Lead with the two Weaks, because under the published algorithm a Weak means at least one weak criterion
applied, and the weak criteria are the ones that describe absent systems rather than imperfect
ones.**<Cite src="tob-code-maturity-criteria" />

**1. Decentralization: Weak is the launch-blocking rating.** The weak criteria are specific: critical
functionality upgradable by a single entity with no user opt-out or exit before the upgrade; a single entity
in direct control of user funds; all decision-making controlled by one entity; parameters changeable at any
time by one entity; permission from a centralized actor required to use the
contracts.<Cite src="tob-code-maturity-criteria" /> At least one of those is true of a system about to hold
$50 million. Ask which, then ask the two questions that move it to Moderate: *are the privileged actors
unable to unilaterally move funds out of or trap funds in the protocol, and is every privilege
documented?*<Cite src="tob-code-maturity-criteria" /> If the honest answer is that the multisig can drain
or freeze, the depositors' risk is not the contract logic that produced twelve Lows — it is the key. That
is the same boundary the formal-verification module ended on, arriving here through a rubric instead of a
proof.

**2. Auditing: Weak means they cannot detect an incident, which compounds every other risk.** Moderate
already requires an off-chain monitoring system with an implemented plan, documentation of what events
mean and how to review logs after a failure, and an incident response plan naming how actors must
react.<Cite src="tob-code-maturity-criteria" /> Weak means some of that is missing. Combined with the
Decentralization rating this is the dangerous pair: an admin key powerful enough to matter, and no system
that would notice it being used. Their only real lever in an incident is speed, and speed requires
detection.

**3. "Not Considered" on Transaction Ordering is a hole in the engagement, not a pass.** The published
legend defines it as the category not being considered in this
review.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> For a vault taking deposits, ordering risk is a live
question — deposit and withdraw sandwiching, share-price manipulation between transactions, the
[first-depositor](/c/inflation-first-depositor-attack) class. Ask why it was excluded. If the answer is
"out of scope", that is a scoping decision worth revisiting before launch, not a result.

**4. "Not Applicable" on Low-Level Manipulation is the one benign entry, if it is true.** It means the
category does not apply to this review.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> Verify it cheaply: grep
for `assembly`. If there is none, this is a real and unusual positive — a codebase with no low-level
manipulation is a codebase with a whole class of risk absent.

**5. Only now, the twelve findings.** Twelve Lows and Informationals from an engagement of unstated length
is not evidence of health. It is consistent with a healthy codebase, with a short engagement, and with a
scope that excluded the interesting parts — and the report's effort figure and scope statement are how you
tell those apart, which is the next lesson.

**6. What I would actually recommend, in launch order.** Document every privilege and publish it; add a
timelock with a user exit window on the upgrade path; stand up event monitoring with a written response
plan and one rehearsal; get transaction ordering into scope for a follow-up; and open a bug bounty on day
one rather than after the first quarter.

**The sentence that reframes the question they asked.** "Ready" is not a property of the finding count. On
their own report's evidence, the code is probably fine and **the operations are not** — and every large
loss of the last two years has been at that boundary. The maturity table told them this and the finding
count could not have. That is what the second deliverable is for.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**Next**, the last piece of report literacy: scope, commit, engineer-weeks, and the fix review. Those four
determine what "audited" bought, and the maturity table is only interpretable next to
them.<Cite src="tob-publications-repo" />

**Immediately usable**, though: this rubric does not require an engagement. Run it on your own codebase
this week, honestly, and the Weak categories are your roadmap. The published guidance frames it exactly
this way — a self-evaluation protocol for developers, security engineers and
leadership.<Cite src="tob-blockchain-maturity" />

**And when you read other firms' reports**, expect the shape to recur with different names. Cyfrin's public
corpus of 243 reports as of 26 August 2026 uses its own severity columns and its own
conventions;<Cite src="cyfrin-audit-reports-repo" /> the Arbitrum Stylus review applies the same rubric to
a Rust and WASM virtual machine rather than to Solidity.<Cite src="tob-arbitrum-stylus-review" /> The
categories travel because the questions are about engineering practice, and engineering practice is not
language-specific.
