One finding. A rounding error that lets a caller extract 0.02% more than their share, repeatable
indefinitely, on a protocol holding $200 million, on Optimism.

Submit it to a firm and it comes back **High severity, Medium difficulty**. Submit it to Sherlock and it
comes back **High**. Submit it to Code4rena and it might come back **2 — Medium**, and if the mechanism
depended on front-running it might come back invalid.

Three labels, one bug, and none of the graders was wrong. They were answering different questions using
different published rules, and the answers are not convertible.

This lesson is about those rules — read verbatim, because paraphrase is exactly where the confusion comes
from — and about what to do when you have to write, read or defend a severity across the boundary.

## System one: severity times difficulty

The firm convention prints two independent axes on every finding. Trail of Bits publishes both scales in
an appendix of every report, so they are checkable rather than folklore.

**Severity — how bad if it fires:**<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" />

| Level | Definition |
|---|---|
| Informational | The issue does not pose an immediate risk but is relevant to security best practices |
| Undetermined | The extent of the risk was not determined during this engagement |
| Low | The risk is small or is not one the client has indicated is important |
| Medium | User information is at risk; exploitation could pose reputational, legal, or moderate financial risks |
| High | The flaw could affect numerous users and have serious reputational, legal, or financial implications |

**Difficulty — how hard to make it fire:**<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" />

| Level | Definition |
|---|---|
| Undetermined | The difficulty of exploitation was not determined during this engagement |
| Low | The flaw is well known; public tools for its exploitation exist or can be scripted |
| Medium | An attacker must write an exploit or will need in-depth knowledge of the system |
| High | An attacker must have privileged access, may need to know complex technical details, or must discover other weaknesses to exploit this issue |

Three things about these tables surprise people.

**There is no Critical.** High is the top of the scale. A report from this firm cannot contain a critical
finding, so counting criticals across firms compares vocabularies rather than codebases.

**"Low" severity is partly a statement about the client.** Read it again: *the risk is small **or is not
one the client has indicated is important***.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> Severity is
calibrated against what the client said they care about, which is one of several places where the scope
conversation reaches into the findings.

**"Undetermined" exists on both axes.** Both scales carry an explicit "we did not determine this" value,
and reports use it — the Arbitrum Stylus review's exposure analysis lists two findings at Undetermined
severity alongside 2 High, 6 Medium, 10 Low and 21
Informational.<Cite src="tob-arbitrum-stylus-review" /> That is a professional convention worth
importing into your own work: a scale with a slot for *unknown* prevents the pressure to round unknowns
down to Low.

### The grid, read as four different calendars

Five severities and four difficulties give twenty cells. The useful reading is not the cell but the
quadrant, because each quadrant implies a different response.

Collapse the two axes into one number and every one of those distinctions is gone. A High/Low and a
High/High both become "high risk", get the same ticket priority, and get worked in whatever order the
tracker sorts them. The information that would have told the team which to do tonight and which to do next
sprint was in the second column, and you deleted it.

## System two: the competitive convention, read verbatim

The received summary is that competitive platforms derive one severity from impact times likelihood. That
summary is close enough to be useful and wrong in a way that matters, because the two largest platforms'
published criteria are not the same function — and one of them explicitly says likelihood is not an input.

### Sherlock: thresholds, and likelihood excluded

Sherlock states that it is exclusively focused on high and medium-severity
findings.<Cite src="sherlock-issue-validity" /> Its criteria are unusual in being *quantitative*:

**High:** direct loss of funds without extensive limitations of external conditions, where the loss of the
affected party is significant. Significant is defined: users lose more than 1% **and** more than $10 of
their principal; or more than 1% and more than $10 of their yield; or the protocol loses more than 1% and
more than $10 of the fees.<Cite src="sherlock-issue-validity" />

**Medium:** causes a loss of funds but requires certain external conditions or specific states, or the loss
is highly constrained; or breaks core contract functionality, rendering the contract useless or leading to
loss of funds relevant to the affected party. Relevant is 0.01% **and** more than $10 on the same three
categories.<Cite src="sherlock-issue-validity" />

And then, in a highlighted note directly beneath:

> **Likelihood is not considered when identifying the severity and the validity of the
> report.**<Cite src="sherlock-issue-validity" />

That single line should change how you read every "impact × likelihood" description of this convention.
Under Sherlock's published rules, the "requires certain external conditions" clause is doing constraint
work inside the *impact* definition, not multiplying by a probability.

Two further rules are worth carrying because they are the ones that surprise submitters:

**Repeatability collapses into impact.** If a single attack can cause a 0.01% loss but can be replayed
indefinitely, it will be considered a 100% loss and can be medium or high depending on the
constraints.<Cite src="sherlock-issue-validity" /> The dust-sized rounding leak you were tempted to file
as Low is a High if it repeats.

**Severity is chain-dependent.** Issues that depend on front-running get their severity downgraded if the
deployment chain has a private mempool: High becomes Medium, and Medium becomes
invalid.<Cite src="sherlock-issue-validity" /> The same code, deployed twice, produces two different
severities — and the report also has to explain how the issue arises with *unintentional* front-running,
because "the attacker will monitor the mempool looking for the victim's transaction" is explicitly called
out as invalid.<Cite src="sherlock-issue-validity" />

### Code4rena: three levels, and likelihood in named places only

Code4rena's scale is `3 — High`, `2 — Medium`, and `QA`, where QA absorbs Low risk and
Governance/Centralization risk, and informational issues are
discouraged.<Cite src="code4rena-severity-classifications" />

**High:** assets can be stolen, lost or compromised directly — or indirectly if there is a valid attack
path that does not have hand-wavy hypotheticals.<Cite src="code4rena-severity-classifications" />

**Medium:** assets are not at direct risk, but the function of the protocol or its availability could be
impacted, or value could leak with a hypothetical attack path with stated assumptions but external
requirements.<Cite src="code4rena-severity-classifications" />

Likelihood does appear — but in named, bounded places rather than as a general multiplier: loss of real
amounts depends on specific conditions and likelihood considerations; privilege escalation issues are
judged by likelihood and impact; and for speculation on future code, based on likelihood considerations
the judge may assign a severity rating in any way they see
fit.<Cite src="code4rena-severity-classifications" />

Two structural rules do more work than the severity definitions:

**Trusted roles are assumed to behave.** All roles assigned by the system are expected to be trustworthy
and to invoke privileged functions responsibly; reckless admin mistakes are invalid, and calls are assumed
to be previewed. Direct misuse of privileges belongs in a QA
report.<Cite src="code4rena-severity-classifications" /> A whole class of finding that a firm would rate
under Decentralization is, by rule, not a competitive finding.

**Weird tokens are out of scope unless listed.** Non-standard and fee-on-transfer token findings are out
of scope unless explicitly listed as supported, with USDT as the single named exception — and judges are
told to invalidate such findings immediately.<Cite src="code4rena-severity-classifications" />

<Aside kind="warn">
Notice what those two rules have in common: they resolve severity by reference to **the scope document**,
not to the code. On a competitive platform the README is normative. Sherlock goes further and says so
directly — the protocol team can use the README, and only the README, to define the protocol's
invariants, via one specific question about properties that should hold even if breaking them has low or
unknown impact; issues breaking those invariants can be assigned Medium regardless of
impact.<Cite src="sherlock-issue-validity" /> A contest submitted without reading the README is a
submission written under rules you have not seen.
</Aside>

## Why the two systems do not translate

Line up the shapes and the mismatch is structural, not cosmetic.

| | Firm convention | Competitive convention |
|---|---|---|
| Axes printed | Two: severity and difficulty | One: severity |
| Top of scale | High<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> | High<Cite src="code4rena-severity-classifications" /> |
| Bottom of scale | Informational, plus Undetermined | QA, informational discouraged<Cite src="code4rena-severity-classifications" /> |
| Privileged-actor abuse | A finding, and a maturity-category input | Out of scope by rule; QA at best<Cite src="code4rena-severity-classifications" /> |
| Effect of exploitation cost | Recorded on the difficulty axis, severity unchanged | Folded into severity, or excluded entirely<Cite src="sherlock-issue-validity" /> |
| Deployment chain | Context in the description | Can change the label outright<Cite src="sherlock-issue-validity" /> |
| Quantitative thresholds | None published | Sherlock: 1% / 0.01% and $10<Cite src="sherlock-issue-validity" /> |

The clean way to see the non-translation: take a finding that is catastrophic but requires privileged
access. In the firm system it is **High severity, High difficulty** — a real finding, with the privilege
recorded as a difficulty and probably re-appearing under Decentralization in the maturity assessment. In
the Code4rena system, the same finding runs straight into the rule that privileged roles are expected to
be trustworthy, and it lands in a QA report.<Cite src="code4rena-severity-classifications" /> Not because
anyone thinks admin compromise is harmless, but because the contest is not scoped to it.

**So a report's severity labels are only interpretable against the rules that produced them.** Two
practical consequences:

- **Never compare severity counts across reports from different systems.** "Firm A found three Highs and
  contest B found eleven" is a comparison of vocabularies. Cyfrin's public repository, for instance,
  carries per-report counts across C/H/M/L/I/G columns — a six-column scheme that is neither of the two
  above.<Cite src="cyfrin-audit-reports-repo" />
- **State your convention when you write.** One line at the top of a finding list — "severity and
  difficulty per the Trail of Bits scale" or "impact-based severity per Code4rena's classifications" —
  costs nothing and prevents a reader from importing the wrong rules.

Code4rena invokes likelihood only in named cases — privilege escalation, speculation on future code, and loss of real amounts. The one-line summary of the convention is a paraphrase, not a rule."

## Defending a severity, which is the actual skill

A severity is a claim with two halves, and both have to be defended in the finding itself. Under either
system the questions are the same; only where the answers get written changes.

**Defending impact** means answering: *who loses what, and how much?* Sherlock's thresholds make this
concrete — more than 1% and more than $10 of principal, yield or protocol
fees<Cite src="sherlock-issue-validity" /> — and even outside that system the discipline of naming the
affected party and the quantity is what separates an argument from an adjective. "Users could lose funds"
is not a defence. "Any depositor who redeems while the queue is non-empty receives up to 3% less than
their pro-rata share" is.

**Defending likelihood or difficulty** means answering: *what does the attacker need, and can they get it?*
Capital, a privileged role, a specific block position, a listed token, a second bug. Each of these is
either cheap or expensive, and saying which is the whole content of the difficulty rating.

**The composition rule.** Findings that are individually harmless can be jointly severe, and published
reports handle this by cross-referencing: Trail of Bits' TOB-UNI4-1 and TOB-UNI4-2 each note that the
other raises their severity.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> If you have a chain, say so in both
findings, because whichever one the client triages first is the one that has to carry the argument.

<Check type="explain" concept="severity-vs-difficulty">
You find this in a perpetuals protocol deployed on both Ethereum mainnet and Optimism:

> `closePosition` computes the exit price from the oracle **before** applying the funding-rate update, so a
> position closed in the same block as a funding update settles at a stale rate. The discrepancy is at most
> 0.05% of position value per occurrence. Anyone can call `closePosition` on their own position; the
> funding update is permissionless and can be triggered by anyone in the same transaction.

Rate this under (a) the firm convention, (b) Sherlock's criteria, and (c) Code4rena's classifications.
Where the systems disagree, say which disagreement is real and which is an artifact of the rules.

<Answer>
**(a) Firm convention: Medium-to-High severity, Low difficulty.**

*Severity.* 0.05% per occurrence sounds small, but the attacker controls both halves — they close their own
position and they can trigger the funding update in the same transaction — so it is repeatable at will,
bounded only by position size and gas. Repeatable value extraction from other participants' funds is a
serious financial implication affecting numerous users, which is the High
definition.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> I would write High and defend it on repeatability, and
I would be prepared to be argued down to Medium if the protocol can show a per-block or per-position
constraint I missed.

*Difficulty: Low.* No privileged access, no second bug, no capital beyond a position. Anyone can script
it — which is the Low definition almost word for word: the flaw is well known in shape and public tools
exist or can be scripted.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" />

*Why the pair is more useful than either number.* High/Low is the quadrant that says **stop the release**.
If it had come back High/High — say, if only a whitelisted keeper could trigger funding — the correct
response would have been different: fix it, and separately document the keeper privilege as a trust
assumption with a monitor on it. Same severity, different calendar, and the difficulty axis is where that
lives.

**(b) Sherlock: High.**

Direct loss of funds without extensive limitations of external conditions — the attacker needs nothing they
do not already have.<Cite src="sherlock-issue-validity" /> On the threshold test, 0.05% per occurrence
clears the Medium bar of 0.01% immediately; and the repeatability rule is decisive: a single attack causing
a small loss that can be replayed indefinitely is considered a 100% loss and can be medium or high
depending on the constraints.<Cite src="sherlock-issue-validity" /> There are no constraints here, so High.

Note what did *not* enter the reasoning: how likely anyone is to actually do it. Sherlock says likelihood
is not considered when identifying severity or validity.<Cite src="sherlock-issue-validity" /> Under the
firm convention that same consideration is present, but as a separate printed axis rather than as a
discount on severity.

**(c) Code4rena: 3 — High, and it survives the scope rules.**

Assets can be stolen indirectly through a valid attack path with no hand-wavy hypotheticals — the path is
two calls in one transaction.<Cite src="code4rena-severity-classifications" /> Check it against the rules
that kill submissions: it is not conditional on a user mistake; it does not require a privileged role, so
the trusted-roles rule does not apply; it is not speculation on future code; and it is not a
weird-token issue.<Cite src="code4rena-severity-classifications" /> It stands.

**Where the disagreement is real, and where it is an artifact.**

*Artifact:* the fact that the firm prints two labels and the platforms print one. All three systems
identify the same defect with the same consequence; the firm simply records the exploitation cost in a
second field instead of folding it in. Nothing is lost in the firm's version and something is lost in the
others', but the underlying judgement is identical.

*Real, and this is the one worth arguing about:* **the two deployments.** If the mainnet deployment's
version of this bug required front-running someone else's transaction, Sherlock's rule would downgrade it
on a private-mempool chain — High becomes Medium, Medium becomes invalid.<Cite src="sherlock-issue-validity" />
Optimism is named in that rule as an example of such a chain. It does not apply *here*, because the
attacker front-runs nobody; both calls are their own. But notice how close the finding came to having two
different severities on two chains for one commit. **Severity is a property of a deployment, not of a
codebase**, and this is the cleanest demonstration of it in either rulebook.

*Also real:* if the funding update had been keeper-only, the three systems diverge sharply. Firm: High
severity, High difficulty — still a finding. Code4rena: the trusted-roles rule pushes it toward QA. That is
not a disagreement about danger; it is a disagreement about what was purchased, and it is the reason the
next-but-one lesson is about reading a report for what the engagement actually bought.

**What I would write in the finding either way.** The impact sentence with a number in it —
*0.05% of position value per occurrence, repeatable within a single transaction by any position holder* —
and the capability sentence — *requires no role, no capital beyond the position, and no second bug.* Those
two sentences are the defence. The label is downstream of them, and a reader operating under a different
rulebook can re-derive their own label from them, which is the real reason to write them.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**Next**, the observations that should not carry a severity at all — gas notes, style, hardening — and why
promoting them costs more than leaving them out.

**Then the maturity assessment**, which is where the findings the competitive rules exclude go to be
useful. A privileged-role capability that is out of scope for a contest is exactly the evidence a
Decentralization rating rests on.<Cite src="tob-blockchain-maturity" />

**And a habit worth starting now.** When you read any public report — Trail of Bits' corpus is the largest
consistently formatted one available<Cite src="tob-publications-repo" /> — find the severity appendix
before the findings. Two minutes at the back of the document tells you what every label in the front of it
means, and it is the difference between reading a report and reading a scoreboard.
