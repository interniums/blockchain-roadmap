Here is a finding, in the shape it was published, from a real engagement:

```text
1. Gas for WASM program activation not charged early enough

Severity: Medium            Difficulty: Low
Type: Data Validation       Finding ID: TOB-STYLUS-1
Target: arbos/programs/programs.go

Description
The gas for activating WASM programs is not charged early enough in the activation
code to prevent denial-of-service attacks.
[...code figure...]
However, if Brotli's decompression fails, the user will not be charged for activating
the program, which can be expensive.

Exploit Scenario
Eve creates a specially crafted compressed WASM bytecode with a corrupted bit at the
end, with the purpose of slowing down the Arbitrum chain. The corrupted bit causes a
failure during decompression, allowing her to avoid paying full price for her program,
making her attack cheaper than expected.

Recommendations
Short term, charge gas as early as possible during WASM program activation; gas should
be charged even if activation fails for any reason.

Long term, review each computationally expensive operation that can be arbitrarily
triggered by users to ensure it is properly priced.
```
<Cite src="tob-arbitrum-stylus-review" />

Two hundred and forty words. Read them again and notice what is *not* there: no adjectives, no severity
argument, no reference to best practice, no "this is a critical vulnerability". The document is doing
several distinct jobs and each sentence belongs to exactly one of them.

That structure is not stylistic. Each section exists because a specific reader needs a specific thing, and
a finding missing a section fails in a predictable way. This lesson is about which section does which job,
and what happens when one is absent.

## The reader is an engineer who does not want to believe you

Before the template, the audience. Your finding is read by someone who wrote the code, who is behind on a
launch, and who has a strong prior that it is fine. They are not hostile. They are *busy*, and their
default action is to add your finding to a list they will re-triage on Friday.

Everything in the anatomy is designed against that default:

- The **header block** lets them decide in four seconds whether to read on.
- The **description** makes the mechanism undeniable without requiring them to trust you.
- The **exploit scenario** converts "this is wrong" into "this costs us", which is what gets scheduled.
- The **proof of concept** removes the argument entirely.
- The **recommendation** removes the excuse that the fix is unclear.

A finding that skips one of these does not get discounted proportionally. It gets deferred, and deferral
is indistinguishable from rejection.

## The header block: triage in four seconds

```text
Severity: Medium            Difficulty: Low
Type: Data Validation       Finding ID: TOB-STYLUS-1
Target: arbos/programs/programs.go
```

**Severity and difficulty are two separate axes**, and the next lesson is entirely about why. For now,
note the pairing above and contrast it with a finding from the same firm's Uniswap v4 Core review, which
is rated **Informational severity with High difficulty**.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> Cheap
to exploit and moderately damaging versus hard to exploit and barely damaging: two very different
positions on a remediation calendar, and a single "risk" number would have blurred both into "medium-ish".

**Type is a controlled vocabulary, not a description.** Trail of Bits publishes its category list in an
appendix of every report: Access Controls, Auditing and Logging, Authentication, Configuration,
Cryptography, Data Exposure, Data Validation, Denial of Service, Error Reporting, Patching, Session
Management, Testing, Timing, and Undefined Behavior.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> A fixed
vocabulary is what makes the category breakdown at the front of the report mean anything — the Uniswap v4
review's six findings resolve to Data Validation 3, Undefined Behavior 2, Auditing and Logging
1.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> That distribution is a statement about the codebase, and it
only exists because nobody invented a category.

**The finding ID is a handle for the rest of the engagement.** It appears in the client's issue tracker,
in the fix commit message, in the fix review, and in the next audit. Findings without stable identifiers
cannot be tracked to closure.

**Target pins location — and the commit is the part people drop.** The published header above names a
file; the report's Project Targets section carries the repository and the exact version, in the Uniswap
case commit `7a72031574fc4548ca8fce197114cf87d5a2c037`.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> Without
the commit, line numbers are noise within a week. This is the same discipline as pinning a fork block in a
[fork test](/c/fork-testing-foundry): a claim about code is only checkable against a specific version of it.

## The description: make the mechanism undeniable

The description has one job — to make the reader able to verify the claim themselves — and it does it in
three moves.

**Move one: state what the code does.** Neutrally, in one sentence, before any judgement. "The gas for
activating WASM programs is not charged early enough in the activation
code."<Cite src="tob-arbitrum-stylus-review" />

**Move two: show it.** Include the snippet, with the file and line beneath it as a numbered figure. The
snippet is not decoration; it is what converts "the auditor says" into "I can see". Both reports use
figure captions carrying path and line — `src/libraries/Pool.sol#L312-L314` and
similar.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" />

**Move three: name the gap.** One sentence connecting the code to the wrongness: "However, if Brotli's
decompression fails, the user will not be charged for activating the program, which can be
expensive."<Cite src="tob-arbitrum-stylus-review" />

### The move that buys the most credibility: arguing against yourself

The best example in either report is a finding that spends half its description explaining why it does not
currently work. Trail of Bits' TOB-UNI4-1 identifies a strict-equality check on a fee comparison, then
writes:

> Note that the issue is not currently exploitable, as: we could not find a realistic way to increase the
> fee above 100%; the following operations in `computeSwapStep` would revert. [...] This issue's severity
> can be higher if combined with TOB-UNI4-2.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" />

Three things are happening there and all three are worth stealing.

**The limitation is stated by the auditor, not discovered by the client.** If the client finds the
limitation first, every other finding in the report is now suspect. If you find it first, the reader
calibrates on you as accurate rather than as alarmist — which is the currency you spend on the *next*
finding.

**Severity is composed across findings.** TOB-UNI4-1 and TOB-UNI4-2 are both individually harmless and
both note that the other raises their
severity.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> Real exploit chains are built out of components like
this, and a report that only rates findings in isolation systematically underprices exactly the
combinations attackers use.

**"Not currently exploitable" is not "not a finding".** It is a finding whose difficulty is High and whose
long-term recommendation matters more than its short-term one, because the thing preventing exploitation
is an accident of the current code, and the current code changes.

## The exploit scenario: the section that promotes an observation

An exploit scenario names four things: **an actor, a capability that actor holds, an ordering of actions,
and what the actor obtains.**

> Eve creates a specially crafted compressed WASM bytecode with a corrupted bit at the end, with the
> purpose of slowing down the Arbitrum chain. The corrupted bit causes a failure during decompression,
> allowing her to avoid paying full price for her program, making her attack cheaper than
> expected.<Cite src="tob-arbitrum-stylus-review" />

Actor: Eve, an ordinary user with no privileges. Capability: submitting a program. Ordering: craft, submit,
decompression fails, gas not charged. Gain: the attack costs less than it should.

This is the same object as the [threat scenario](/c/threat-scenario) from the threat-modelling module,
written after the fact instead of before it. There, you enumerated actors by capability and asked what each
could do; here, you have found something one of them can do, and you write it in the same grammar. The two
artifacts should be legible to each other — a finding whose actor does not appear in the client's threat
model is telling you something about the threat model.

**The diagnostic use is more important than the documentation use.** If you cannot write the scenario
concretely — if it comes out as "an attacker could potentially manipulate the state to cause unexpected
behaviour" — you have one of two things. Either it is an observation that belongs in an informational
section, or you do not yet understand your own finding. Both are useful to discover *before* you send it.

Notice also which findings lack this section. In the Uniswap v4 report, the informational findings have no
exploit scenario, because there is no consequence to narrate.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" />
**The presence of the section is what distinguishes a vulnerability from an
observation** — which is the subject of two lessons from now.

## The proof of concept: removing the argument

A runnable reproduction — typically a Foundry test against the client's repository at the audited
commit — ends the debate about whether the finding is real. It has stopped being a nice-to-have.

Sherlock's published criteria make it close to mandatory in specific categories. A proof of concept is
recommended for non-obvious issues with complex vulnerabilities or attack paths, issues with non-trivial
input constraints, issues involving precision loss, reentrancy attacks, and attacks related to gas
consumption or reverting message calls — and then the sharp clause: **if the original report does not
include a proof of concept, it will be considered invalid if the issue cannot be clearly understood
without one.**<Cite src="sherlock-issue-validity" /> Not downgraded. Invalid.

Code4rena approaches the same problem from the impact side, requiring that a High-severity claim rest on a
valid attack path that does not have hand-wavy hypotheticals.<Cite src="code4rena-severity-classifications" />
A proof of concept is the least hand-wavy artifact available.

Three properties make a proof of concept worth writing even when nobody demands one:

**It converts your belief into a fact.** You may be wrong. Writing the test is how you find out at your
desk rather than in the client call, and the false-positive you catch this way is the cheapest one you
will ever handle.

**It becomes the regression test.** The same test validates the fix, and then lives in the client's suite
forever. This is the single largest piece of durable value an audit leaves behind, and it costs you
nothing extra.

**It fixes the severity argument in advance.** Most severity disputes are actually disputes about whether
the preconditions are reachable. A test that reaches them from a realistic starting state settles that
without adjectives.

<Aside kind="note">
If the finding came out of a fuzz campaign, you are most of the way there already. A fuzzer's
[shrunk](/c/shrinking) counterexample is a minimal input sequence that reproduces the failure, which is
exactly the skeleton of a proof of concept — and shrinking has done the work of stripping the incidental
calls that make a raw trace unreadable. The remaining work is to replace the harness with the real entry
points and to give the actors names.
</Aside>

## The recommendation: two horizons, deliberately

Trail of Bits splits every recommendation into short term and long term, and the split is worth stealing
wholesale.

> **Short term**, charge gas as early as possible during WASM program activation; gas should be charged
> even if activation fails for any reason.
>
> **Long term**, review each computationally expensive operation that can be arbitrarily triggered by
> users to ensure it is properly priced.<Cite src="tob-arbitrum-stylus-review" />

**Short term fixes the bug.** Specific, minimal, mergeable this week, and phrased so the reviewer of the
fix commit can check it.

**Long term fixes the reason the bug was possible.** Notice that the long-term item is not about WASM
activation at all — it is a review of a *class*: every expensive user-triggerable operation. That is a
claim that the finding is an instance rather than an accident, and it is the sentence that changes how the
team writes the next contract.

The same split appears in the Uniswap report: short term, use `>=` instead of `==`; long term, create
tests for which the different fee limits are not set to
100%.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> Again the long term is a testing-practice change, not a
code change. This is where a finding stops being a bug report and becomes an argument about engineering
practice — and it feeds directly into the codebase maturity assessment two lessons from now.

## Worked example: promoting a concern to a finding

You are reviewing a staking contract and something bothers you: `claimRewards` reads `block.timestamp`
into a local, then calls the reward token's `transfer`, then writes `lastClaimed`. You have a feeling.
Drive the feeling to a document.

**Subgoal: name the defect, not the symptom, and write the title first.** "Reentrancy in claimRewards" is
a symptom and may be wrong. "State update after external call allows repeated reward claims" names the
mechanism and the consequence, and writing it first tells you what you still have to prove: *repeated*,
and *claims*.

**Subgoal: establish the capability before the exploit.** Can the reward token call back? If it is a fixed
in-house ERC-20 with no hooks, the answer may be no, and the finding collapses into an informational note
about [checks-effects-interactions](/c/checks-effects-interactions) ordering. If it is ERC-777 or a
configurable token list, the callback exists and you have an actor. **This step is where most reentrancy
findings should die**, and killing them here costs you ten minutes instead of costing the client an
afternoon.

**Subgoal: write the scenario in four parts and see whether it holds together.** Actor: Eve, who controls a
token accepted as a reward asset. Capability: her token's `transfer` calls back into the staking contract.
Ordering: stake, wait, call `claimRewards`, re-enter `claimRewards` before `lastClaimed` is written, repeat.
Gain: rewards for one period paid N times. If any of the four parts required a word like "somehow", stop
and go back.

**Subgoal: reproduce it before you rate it.** A Foundry test with a malicious token, an attacker contract,
and an assertion on the drained balance. Run it at the audited commit. If it does not reproduce, you have
just avoided a false positive; write down why it failed, because the reason is often itself a finding
about an undocumented protection.

**Subgoal: rate both axes with the reproduction in hand.** Severity: direct loss of protocol funds, so
High. Difficulty: the attacker needs their token listed as a reward asset — a privileged action by an
admin — which pushes difficulty toward High under a scale where High means the attacker must have
privileged access or discover other weaknesses.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> High severity,
High difficulty. That pairing is a *specific* recommendation — fix it, but the listing procedure is the
compensating control, and it should be documented as one.

**Subgoal: write both recommendations, and make the long one about the class.** Short term: move the
`lastClaimed` write above the `transfer`, or add a guard. Long term: a codebase-wide review for state
writes occurring after external calls, plus an invariant asserting that rewards paid per period never
exceed rewards accrued — which hands the client something a fuzzer can consume long after you are gone.

**Subgoal: reread it as the engineer who wrote it.** Is there a sentence they can disagree with that you
have not already addressed? "Our reward token can't do that" is the obvious one. Pre-empt it in the
description: state which token property the finding depends on, and what would have to be true for it to
be false. A finding that survives its own steelman is a finding that gets fixed.

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="finding-anatomy">
Here is a submission from a junior reviewer. Rewrite it into the anatomy, and say what you would have to
go and find out before you could send it.

> **Critical: Unsafe use of transfer in withdraw()**
>
> The `withdraw()` function in Vault.sol uses `token.transfer()` without checking the return value. Many
> tokens like USDT do not return a bool, and some return false instead of reverting. This is a critical
> vulnerability that could lead to loss of funds. Recommendation: use SafeERC20.

<Answer>
**What is right about it.** The underlying observation is real and well known, the recommendation is
correct, and it names a concrete token whose behaviour differs. That is more than many submissions have.

**What is missing, in order of how much it costs.**

**1. No target and no commit.** "Vault.sol" is not a location. It needs the file, the line range, and the
commit hash the review was conducted at — the same pinning every published report
uses.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> Without it the finding is unverifiable in a month and
untrackable through a fix.

**2. No exploit scenario, and this is the fatal one.** There is no actor, no capability, no ordering, no
gain. The scenario is also *not obviously writable*, which is the real problem: for the finding to cause
loss, the deployment must actually use a non-compliant token. If the vault is deployed with a single,
known, compliant asset, there is no path to loss and this is a hardening note, not a critical. **Writing
the scenario is how you discover which of those two you have** — and that is why the section is the gate
between an observation and a vulnerability.

**3. The severity is asserted, not argued, and "Critical" is almost certainly wrong.** Two independent
problems. First, severity here depends entirely on the token set, which the submission never establishes.
Second, Code4rena's criteria require a High to rest on assets being stolen, lost or compromised through a
valid attack path without hand-wavy hypotheticals,<Cite src="code4rena-severity-classifications" /> and
they treat non-standard and fee-on-transfer token findings as **out of scope unless the tokens are
explicitly listed as supported**, with USDT as the single named exception.<Cite src="code4rena-severity-classifications" />
So on a Code4rena contest, this submission's fate depends on a scope document the reviewer did not read.

**4. No proof of concept.** Sherlock's criteria recommend one for exactly this shape — non-trivial input
constraints — and state that a report without one is invalid if the issue cannot be clearly understood
without it.<Cite src="sherlock-issue-validity" /> Here a proof of concept is also cheap: a mock token that
returns `false`, a deposit, a withdraw, an assertion that the accounting decremented while no tokens moved.

**5. The recommendation has no long term.** "Use SafeERC20" fixes this call site. The class-level version
is: adopt a safe-transfer wrapper across the codebase, and add an invariant that internal accounting
changes only when the corresponding token balance changed.<Cite src="tob-arbitrum-stylus-review" />

**6. The title names the API, not the defect.** "Unchecked return value from `token.transfer` lets a
failed withdrawal decrement internal accounting" states the mechanism and the consequence.

**The rewritten header.**

```text
Title:      Unchecked transfer return value lets a failed withdrawal
            decrement internal accounting
Severity:   High (if any non-compliant token is supported) / Informational (if not)
Difficulty: Low
Type:       Data Validation
Target:     src/Vault.sol#L142-L151 @ <commit>
```

**What I would go and find out before sending it.** Which tokens the deployment actually accepts, and who
can add one. Three outcomes, three different findings. *A fixed compliant asset*: informational, with a
long-term recommendation and a note for the fix review. *An admin-managed list*: a real finding whose
difficulty is High because it needs a privileged listing, and whose exploit scenario runs through that
admin — which also makes it an entry in the trust-assumption inventory. *A permissionless list*: High
severity, Low difficulty, and it should have been the first thing in the report.

**The meta-lesson.** The word "Critical" did none of the work in the original. The scope question — which
tokens — did all of it, and it takes fifteen minutes to answer. Severity is a claim about impact and
likelihood, and both have to be defended; that is the whole of the next lesson.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**Next**, the two severity conventions in daily use, why a "High" means different things depending on
which one produced it, and why difficulty and likelihood are not the same
axis.<Cite src="tob-blockchain-maturity" />

**Then**, the findings that should not be findings — where gas notes and style observations belong, and
what an inflated report costs you on the engagement after this one.

**Then the second deliverable**: the maturity assessment that exists so a client with zero criticals still
learns something. Notice that you have already met its raw material — every "long term" recommendation you
wrote is evidence for a maturity category.

**And every one of these artifacts is public.** Trail of Bits maintains its complete report corpus, every
one carrying the same anatomy, the same appendices and an effort
figure;<Cite src="tob-publications-repo" /> Cyfrin publishes theirs with per-report severity
counts.<Cite src="cyfrin-audit-reports-repo" /> Reading five findings written by people better at this than
you is the fastest available way to improve, and it costs nothing.
