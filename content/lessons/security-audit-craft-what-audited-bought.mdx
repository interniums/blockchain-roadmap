The instinct is to read the right-hand column as a worse codebase. Resist it. A Solidity exchange and a
Rust-and-WASM virtual machine embedded in a Go client are not comparable artefacts, and 47 engineer-weeks
against a moving four-repository target is not the same product as six engineer-weeks against one frozen
commit.

What the table actually shows is that **"audited" is not one thing**, and that the report tells you which
thing you got — if you read the four sections nobody reads. This lesson is those four sections: effort,
scope, the fix review, and the disclaimer.

## Engineer-weeks: the number that calibrates everything else

Trail of Bits states the effort in the engagement overview of every published report: *a team of six
consultants conducted the review from July 15, 2024 to August 2, 2024, for a total of six engineer-weeks
of effort*<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> and *a team of six consultants conducted the review
from October 30, 2023, to May 3, 2024, for a total of 47 engineer-weeks of
effort*.<Cite src="tob-arbitrum-stylus-review" />

That number is the best available proxy for how much expert attention was purchased, and it has one
correct use: **form your expectation before you read the findings.**

Six engineer-weeks on a novel exchange with heavy inline assembly is a focused review. You should expect a
short findings list, deep coverage of a few named areas, and explicit statements about what was not looked
at. When the report then delivers exactly that, it has met expectation — which is a completely different
reading from "they only found six issues".

Forty-seven engineer-weeks across four repositories over six months is a different product. You should
expect breadth, a long tail of informational findings, and findings that reference each other. Getting 41
findings from that is unremarkable; getting six would have been alarming.

## The scope statement: what "audited" actually names

A scope statement has three parts, and each one converts a marketing word into a checkable claim.

**Project goals — the questions the engagement set out to answer.** The Uniswap v4 report lists them as an
explicitly non-exhaustive set: whether internal accounting of balances or fees can be altered without
providing tokens; whether users can lose funds or have them stuck during normal operation; whether
malicious hooks can access funds or block another pool's operations; whether access controls are correctly
implemented; whether arithmetic is correct and low-level implementations match their high-level
counterparts; whether transient storage manipulation cleans up correctly and locks are correctly
implemented; whether storage can be overwritten from a different pool; whether fees and deltas are
correctly calculated; and whether prices and ticks are correct and
manipulable.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" />

Read that list as an inverted table of contents. **Anything not on it was not a question the engagement was
paid to answer.** For a hooks-based exchange, notice what is present — hook isolation, transient storage
cleanup, pool ID collision — and use the shape to predict where the findings will be. Then check whether
you were right, because that exercise is how you learn to read scope.

**Project targets — the repository, the commit, the type and the platform.** Uniswap: one repository, one
version, `7a72031574fc4548ca8fce197114cf87d5a2c037`, Solidity,
EVM.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> Stylus: four repositories with different languages — Rust,
Solidity, Go — commits that **rotate by engagement week** (`27113b8` for weeks 1 and 2, `991e082` for weeks
3 and 4), plus fourteen individually named pull
requests.<Cite src="tob-arbitrum-stylus-review" />

That second shape is worth pausing on, because it is what auditing an actively developed system looks like.
The target moved during the review, and the report says so precisely enough that a reader can reconstruct
which code each finding was written against. A report that reviews a moving target and prints one commit
has lost that.

**Project coverage — what was actually done, per component.** This is the section that distinguishes a
report from a checklist. The Uniswap review names the technique per area: manual review of the
`updateDynamicLPFee` function and LP-fee validation; manual review of protocol-fee access controls plus
stateful invariant testing focused on whether the singleton always holds enough funds to cover outstanding
protocol-fee debts; verification that native ETH is handled as a special case in all transfer-related
functions; a static invariant ensuring only `settle` and `settleFor` are
payable.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> Each claim is checkable, and each names the method, which
means a reader can judge whether the method suited the risk.

### The out-of-scope list is the part that surprises people

The Uniswap report includes a Coverage Limitations section naming two exclusions: issues previously found
in other audits that were not fixed in the target commit, and issues already known to the Uniswap team and
present in a GitHub issue or PR in the repository.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" />

Read that carefully. **Known issues are out of scope.** So "we found six issues" does not mean six issues
existed at that commit; it means six *new* ones were found beyond what was already tracked. A reader who
treats the finding count as a census of defects has misread the document — and the document told them so,
in a section between the coverage and the automated testing appendix.

## The disclaimer says the quiet part

Every Trail of Bits report carries a Test Coverage Disclaimer, and it is more informative than most
executive summaries:

> Security assessment projects are time-boxed and often reliant on information that may be provided by a
> client, its affiliates, or its partners. As a result, the findings documented in this report should not
> be considered a comprehensive list of security issues, flaws, or defects in the target
> system or codebase.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" />

And on tooling specifically: automated techniques augment manual review, but each has its limitations — a
tool may not generate a random edge case that violates a property, or may not fully complete its analysis
during the allotted time — and their use is also limited by the time and resource constraints of a
project.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> That is the fuzzing and formal-verification content of
this track, restated by the firm as a caveat on its own deliverable.

There is a third clause in the same notices section that has nothing to do with security engineering and
everything to do with due diligence:

> The sole canonical source for Trail of Bits publications is the Trail of Bits Publications page. Reports
> accessed through any source other than that page may have been modified and should not be considered
> authentic.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" />

**A PDF someone sent you is not evidence.** Neither is one hosted on the audited project's own site. If you
are relying on an audit as part of a decision about money, fetch it from the auditor's canonical
index.<Cite src="tob-reports-index" /><Cite src="tob-publications-repo" />

## The fix review: the part the report cannot contain

The report describes a commit. The moment the team pushes the fixes, it describes a commit that no longer
exists.

A **fix review** re-examines each reported issue against the remediation commit and answers three
questions: is the fix correct, is it complete, and did it introduce anything new. Firms publish these as
separate documents alongside the original review, and Cyfrin's index shows the same practice in a different
form — a report date distinct from the audit end date, with follow-up and formal-verification documents
listed as separate artefacts against the same
engagement.<Cite src="cyfrin-audit-reports-repo" /><Cite src="tob-publications-repo" />

The failure mode is specific and common enough to check for by default: **a fix that resolves the reported
instance and leaves the same pattern elsewhere in the codebase.** The finding said "line 142"; the fix
changed line 142; lines 208 and 314 have the same shape and nobody looked. This is exactly why the
short-term / long-term recommendation split exists — the long-term item is the one that would have caught
the siblings, and it is the one most likely to be marked "acknowledged" rather than "fixed".

A useful fix-review note is short and has four parts: the finding ID; the remediation commit; whether the
fix is correct and complete, with the reasoning; and whether the pattern occurs elsewhere. Writing one is
also the cheapest way to learn to read code the way an auditor does, because it forces you to check a
claim rather than to form an impression.

## An audit bounds risk; it does not remove it

Put the pieces together and the honest reading of a clean report is narrow: **a specified amount of skilled
attention found nothing in a specified place, at one commit, under a declared scope, excluding known
issues.**

That is genuinely valuable and it is not completion. The strongest available evidence on this point comes
from bug-bounty data, with the caveat that it is published by a company selling bounty programs. Immunefi's
five-year analysis, covering confirmed and paid reports across 593 programs from January 2021 through
February 2026 with contests and attackathons filtered out, reports that **93.9% of programs active five
years or longer have logged at least one confirmed, paid critical severity
disclosure**.<Cite src="immunefi-long-running-programs" />

The distribution matters more than the headline. The rate climbs monotonically with exposure: 61.4% at one
year or more, 73.9% at two, 87.2% at three, 92.9% at four, 93.9% at
five.<Cite src="immunefi-long-running-programs" /> And criticals are not once-and-done: of the 364 programs
that surfaced at least one, the average is 2.7 confirmed criticals per program, with a median of
2.<Cite src="immunefi-long-running-programs" />

Treat the vendor incentive seriously — a company selling continuous coverage benefits from the conclusion
that continuous coverage is necessary. Then notice that the monotonic shape is the hard part to explain
away. It is what you would expect if criticals are a property of complex high-value code and *time under
adversarial scrutiny* is the variable that surfaces them. Which is the same claim the Test Coverage
Disclaimer makes, from the other side.

The operational conclusion: an audit is one purchase of attention. A bounty is a standing one. Neither
replaces the other, and a report delivered is the start of the security programme rather than its
conclusion.

## Worked example: reading an unfamiliar report in fifteen minutes

You have been sent a 60-page PDF and asked whether the protocol is safe to integrate with. Here is the
order, and it is deliberately back-to-front.

**Subgoal: establish authenticity before anything else.** Fetch it from the auditor's canonical index
rather than reading the copy you were sent.<Cite src="tob-reports-index" /> Two minutes, and it is the only
step that protects against a modified document.

**Subgoal: find the effort figure and set an expectation.** Engineer-weeks, team size, calendar
window.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> Write down what you expect the findings list to look like
*before* you see it. Being wrong is informative in both directions.

**Subgoal: read the targets and diff the commit against production.** Repository, commit hash, chains. Then
go and check what is actually deployed. If the deployed bytecode does not correspond to the reviewed
commit, the report is a document about a different program and everything downstream of this step is moot.

**Subgoal: read the goals as a list of questions, and note what is missing.** For a vault: is there a
question about the oracle? About the upgrade path? About the first depositor? Absences here are the highest
information-per-second in the entire document.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" />

**Subgoal: read the coverage limitations.** Known issues excluded, previously reported issues excluded,
components deferred.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> This is where a finding count of six becomes
interpretable.

**Subgoal: read the maturity table, not the finding count.** Weak categories predict the next engagement;
Not Considered marks a hole.<Cite src="tob-blockchain-maturity" /> If the table says Decentralization is
Weak, your integration risk is the admin key, whatever the findings say.

**Subgoal: check for a fix review, and check what happened to the long-term recommendations.** If there is
no fix review, you are reading about a commit that no longer exists. If the long-term items are all
"acknowledged", the class-level causes are still present.

**Subgoal: only now, read the findings.** With the effort, scope, exclusions and maturity ratings in hand,
the findings finally mean something — and you will find you are reading them for whether they match the
scope's shape rather than for their count.

**Question 1: "Which commit was audited, and does it match what is deployed today?"**

This is first because it can end the conversation. Audited is a property of a commit, under a scope, for a
number of engineer-weeks — not of a protocol.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> A published report

*The answer that changes my decision:* anything other than "here is the commit, and here is the deployed
bytecode matching it". "We've shipped a few upgrades since" means the audited artefact and the live one are
different programs, and the badge covers the wrong one. If they cannot answer at all, I stop here — not
because the code is bad, but because nobody on their side is tracking the thing the claim rests on.

*Sub-question I sneak in for free:* is it upgradeable, and who can upgrade it? Because if it is, the
deployed code is a moving target and the audit covers a snapshot by construction.

**Question 2: "How many engineer-weeks, and what was explicitly out of scope?"**

Two halves of one question about how much attention was actually bought. The effort figure is the best
available proxy and reputable firms print it in the engagement
overview.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> The exclusions matter just as much: the Uniswap report's
Coverage Limitations section excludes issues previously found in other audits and left unfixed, and issues
already known to the team and tracked in a GitHub
issue.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" /> **Known issues can be out of scope by agreement**, so
"zero criticals" is compatible with a tracked critical that was never in the engagement.

*The answer that changes my decision:* a small figure against a large surface — say six engineer-weeks
across an eleven-contract system with an oracle and a bridge. That is not a bad audit; it is a focused one,
and the marketing sentence dropped the focus. Equally decisive: an exclusion list that contains the
component I am about to route money through.

*What does not change my decision:* the firms' names. There is no neutral ranking of firm quality; every
comparison in circulation was published by a market participant.<Cite src="sherlock-top-firms" /> "Two
top-tier firms" is a claim about procurement, not about assurance.

**Question 3: "Is there a fix review, and what does the maturity table say?"**

*Fix review*, because without one the report describes a commit that no longer exists, and the most common
remediation failure is a fix that resolves the reported instance while the same pattern survives elsewhere.
I also want to know how many long-term recommendations were implemented rather than acknowledged — the
long-term items are the ones that address the reason the bug was possible.

*Maturity table*, because it is the part of the report that speaks to my actual risk. Zero criticals is
exactly the situation the second deliverable exists for: a codebase can produce no critical findings and
still rate weak on testing, documentation and decentralisation.<Cite src="tob-blockchain-maturity" /> If
Decentralization is Weak, my $5 million is exposed to an admin key rather than to a logic bug, and no
number of clean findings addresses that. If Auditing is Weak, nobody will notice an incident quickly, which
means my exit depends on my own monitoring.

*The answer that changes my decision:* Weak on Decentralization or Auditing, or Not Considered on a
category central to my integration. Note that "Not Considered" is defined as the category not being
examined in this review — it is a hole, not a pass.<Cite src="tob-uniswap-v4-core-review" />

**What I would conclude even if all three answers are good.**

I would still not treat the audit as completion. Immunefi's five-year data across 593 programs shows 93.9%
of programs running five or more years surfaced a confirmed critical, climbing monotonically from 61.4% at
one year — and among programs that found one, the average is 2.7.<Cite src="immunefi-long-running-programs" />
That is vendor research and should be discounted accordingly, but the monotonic curve is hard to explain
away. So my last check is not about the report at all: **do they run a funded bug bounty, and do they have
a monitor with a defined response?** A protocol with a slightly thinner audit and a live bounty plus
working monitoring is a better counterparty than one with two glossy reports and neither.

**And one thing I would not ask.** "Was it formally verified?" On its own that question adds nothing —
without the bounds, the summaries and the optimistic flags, the answer is a word rather than a claim.

## What this unlocks

**The module closes here, and its thesis was one sentence:** a finding is a persuasive document addressed
to an engineer who does not want to believe you, and a report is a persuasive document addressed to a
reader who wants a badge. Both fail in the same way — by leaving out the part that makes the claim
checkable.

**The next module is competitive contests**, where the same findings are written under different economics:
a fixed pool split among many reviewers, duplicate decay, judging phases and escalation. The severity rules
you read verbatim in this module are the rules those payouts are computed
from.<Cite src="code4rena-severity-classifications" />

**The practice worth doing before you move on** is a teardown. Take one report from the public
corpus,<Cite src="tob-publications-repo" /> extract only the effort figure, the scope statement and the
maturity table, and predict the findings before reading them. Then read them and analyse the misses. It is
the single highest-yield exercise in this track, and the corpus is free.

**And then write one.** Audit a contract you did not write, produce findings in the anatomy from lesson
one, rate them on both conventions, and attach a maturity table where every rating cites specific evidence.
Every High or Medium gets a runnable proof of concept. That document — not a finding count — is the
artefact that shows you can do this work.
