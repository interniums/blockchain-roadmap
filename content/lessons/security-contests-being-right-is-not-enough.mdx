Preliminary results land. Your High is a Low. The judge's explanation is two sentences long and, as far
as you can tell, wrong — they have assumed a precondition the contract does not enforce.

You have twenty-four hours. Whether you are allowed to say anything at all was decided months ago, by
your accuracy on previous contests and by whether you filed tax paperwork.

This is the part of competitive auditing nobody advertises, and it is the most interesting mechanism
design in the space. Every platform in this module has independently concluded that **the right to argue
must be rationed**, and each rations it in a different currency. Understanding why is worth more than
understanding any one platform's rules, because the design tells you what a submission is actually worth
to the people paying for it.

## Judging is a pipeline with published durations

Sherlock publishes its judging as four phases whose lengths scale with submission volume, which is
unusual enough to be worth reading closely.<Cite src="sherlock-judging" />

**Phase 1 — real-time judging.** Begins immediately after the contest ends. Runs for the number of issues
divided by one hundred, in days, with a minimum of two; the docs' own example is 500 issues giving five
days. Its stated purpose is to crowdsource judgments, explanations and de-duplication in order to reduce
the lead judge's workload. Dedicated judges sign up in advance so that every issue is judged and
de-duplicated by the end of the period. The docs note that 90 percent of issues are often judged
accurately within 12 to 24 hours, letting the protocol team start fixing.<Cite src="sherlock-judging" />

**Phase 2 — lead judge.** Runs for the issue count divided by two hundred, in days, minimum two — three
days for 500 issues. The lead judge focuses only on the issues that phase 1 flagged as controversial, and
must write an explanation for every judgment.<Cite src="sherlock-judging" />

**Checkpoint: preliminary results.**

**Phase 3 — escalation.** Twenty-four hours. Participants may flag issues they believe the lead judge got
wrong, and **must pay with their Signal Score to flag an issue**, after which they may leave a comment
explaining their reasoning.<Cite src="sherlock-judging" />

**Phase 4 — final judgments.** The lead judge re-reviews everything flagged, over the controversial-issue
count divided by twenty, in days, minimum two. A Sherlock judge supervises and will only overturn a
decision if it is clearly wrong.<Cite src="sherlock-judging" />

Two structural facts fall out of those numbers.

**Judging is a paid, scalable operation.** Durations keyed to volume, with signed-up judges
and a mandatory written explanation for every controversial ruling, is the design of a process expecting
thousands of submissions on a schedule. It is funded accordingly: Code4rena states that judges are
incentivised to decide severity, validity and quality by receiving a share of the prize pool
themselves,<Cite src="code4rena-awarding" /> and on Sherlock the Lead Senior Watson — drawn from the top
ten percent of ranked participants — receives fixed pay stated as roughly ten thousand dollars per audit
week, and reviews the protocol's fixes afterwards.<Cite src="sherlock-watsons" /> The person ruling
against you has a professional stake in ruling consistently.

**The escalation window is the shortest phase by an order of magnitude.** Judging can run for a week or
more; the window in which you may contest it is one day. That asymmetry is deliberate: the process is
optimised for the protocol getting fixes quickly, not for a participant getting a hearing.

## Three platforms, three currencies for the right to argue

The interesting comparison is not what each window is, but what each platform decided to charge.

**Sherlock charges a reputation currency.** Flagging an issue during the escalation window costs Signal
Score.<Cite src="sherlock-judging" /> Escalation is therefore a budget: you can be right about ten
rulings and only afford to contest two, and the choice of which is itself a skill.

**Code4rena charges accuracy plus identity.** Its equivalent phase is post-judging QA, a 48-hour window
in which the sponsor team and eligible wardens raise concerns for the judge's
review.<Cite src="code4rena-competitions" /> Eligibility is a table, for competitions starting on or
after 8 January 2026:<Cite src="code4rena-signal" />

| Your signal | Max PJQA comments | With verified ID and tax info |
|---|---:|---:|
| `null`, or under 0.2 | 0 | 1 |
| 0.2 to 0.3999 | 0 | 3 |
| 0.4 and above | 0 | unrestricted |

Read the left-hand column. **Without identity verification and tax information, the comment allowance is
zero at every level of skill.** Accuracy alone buys you nothing here; it multiplies something that starts
at zero until you have done the paperwork. And PJQA is the only route — the documentation states that it
is the formal mechanism for disputes and that contacting judges directly outside it is not
permitted.<Cite src="code4rena-judging-criteria" />

**CodeHawks charges a track record in dollars.** Its appeals period is 48 hours after judging, during
which interactions are enabled on your GitHub submissions. You may always appeal your *own* submissions.
To appeal someone else's, you must hold at least one submission in that contest, have earned at least
**$200 USDC** from previous CodeHawks competitions, and have a total-to-valid submission ratio of **0.2 or
greater** — stated explicitly as a Sybil defence and a filter against inexperienced
judges.<Cite src="codehawks-appeals" />

### Two different things are called "signal", and confusing them is expensive

The naming collision is genuinely unhelpful, so pin it down.

**Sherlock's Signal Score is a currency.** You spend it. Escalating consumes it.<Cite src="sherlock-judging" />

**Code4rena's `signal` is a ratio.** It is the number of valid High and Medium findings divided by all the
Highs and Mediums you submitted. It reads `null` until you have made at least three High or Medium
submissions to finalised audits, is recalculated daily, is computed from data going back to March 2023,
and is visible to any authenticated user alongside your submissions.<Cite src="code4rena-signal" /> You
do not spend it. It is a public accuracy record that gates submission counts, PJQA comments, roles and
the proof-of-concept requirement.<Cite src="code4rena-signal" />

The practical difference: a Sherlock Signal Score can be saved up and deployed. A Code4rena `signal`
cannot be saved — every submission you make is a bet on it, and a wrong one lowers it permanently in the
window the calculation covers.

## Validity and quality are separate rulings

Before you can decide whether a ruling is worth contesting, you need to know which of two independent
judgments went against you.

Code4rena judges assess **validity** as valid, invalid or out of scope, and separately assess **quality**
as sufficient, insufficient, or low quality/spam. A submission must be judged both valid *and* of
sufficient quality to be eligible for an award.<Cite src="code4rena-judging-criteria" />

The bar for sufficient is stated as roughly the level of a draft report by a professional auditor, judged
on technical substance, with writing quality counting only where it obstructs the technical
message.<Cite src="code4rena-judging-criteria" /> A submission may be insufficient while being entirely
true, and the enumerated criteria are worth memorising:

- incorrect;
- low or incomplete effort;
- **clearly overinflated severity**;
- a proof of concept that fails the burden-of-proof test;
- an approach disrespectful of the sponsor's or judge's time.

Submissions that appear to be direct copies of other reports in the same audit are collectively deemed
insufficient.<Cite src="code4rena-judging-criteria" />

The burden-of-proof definition is the most operationally useful sentence in the whole document.
**Insufficient proof is defined as the judge needing to do additional research or coding in order to
validate the claims in the submission.**<Cite src="code4rena-submission-guidelines" /> That is a test you
can apply to your own draft before you file it: does anything here require the reader to go and find out?
The same page adds a detail that reads like it was written after a bad week — a proof of concept that
reverts must demonstrate the *precise* revert error rather than merely
reverting.<Cite src="code4rena-submission-guidelines" />

The stakes on quality are higher than on any single finding. Judges have discretion to invalidate **all**
of a warden's findings in a contest for repeated low-quality submissions, and a judge recommending
consequences under the good-citizenship rule needs agreement from two other
judges.<Cite src="code4rena-judging-criteria" /><Cite src="code4rena-submission-guidelines" /> Both halves
matter: the sanction is severe, and it is deliberately not one person's decision.

## Full credit, partial credit, and the two things you must demonstrate

Within a duplicate group, the difference between a full and a partial slice comes down to two
requirements, and knowing them tells you exactly what an escalation would have to establish.

A full-mark submission requires **identification and demonstration of the root cause**, and
**identification and demonstration of the maximum achievable impact of that root cause**. A satisfactory
demonstration is a step-by-step explanation from root cause to impact with code snippets or a coded proof
of concept — or, at the judge's discretion, a high-standard issue with less detail. Missing the root cause
is grounds for partial scoring, downgrading, or invalidation. Missing the maximum impact is grounds for
partial scoring or downgrading. Presentation quality and missing or incorrect remediation steps can also
pull a submission into partial credit.<Cite src="code4rena-judging-criteria" />

Notice the asymmetry: failing to identify the root cause can void the finding entirely, while failing to
identify the maximum impact only reduces it. That is a direct statement about what the sponsor is buying.
They can price an impact themselves; they cannot fix a bug you have not located.

## Severity inflation, priced three ways

Now the behaviour all of this is designed to suppress.

Severity inflation is submitting something you believe is a Low as a High, on the theory that a judge will
settle somewhere favourable. It is a rational-looking move against a single ruling and a losing move
against a system with memory. Each platform closes it differently:

**Code4rena closes it in the accuracy ratio.** High and Medium submissions downgraded to Low are excluded
from the `signal` calculation entirely, and the documentation gives the reason — to discourage wardens
from inflating severity.<Cite src="code4rena-signal" /> Read the mechanism carefully: an inflated claim
cannot improve the ratio, and it burns one of very few submissions failing to do so. It is then caught
separately on the quality axis, where "clearly overinflated severity" is an enumerated
criterion for insufficiency, and insufficient submissions both forfeit awards and count against
`signal`.<Cite src="code4rena-judging-criteria" /><Cite src="code4rena-signal" />

**Code4rena also closes it inside QA reports.** Wardens overstating the severity of QA issues — submitting
low-risk findings as Medium or High to angle for a higher payout — have their scores reduced by
judges.<Cite src="code4rena-submission-guidelines" />

**CodeHawks closes it at the appeals gate.** The 0.2 total-to-valid ratio required to appeal another
auditor's submission is an accuracy threshold in disguise: a habit of inflating drives that ratio down and
eventually removes your standing to participate in judging at all.<Cite src="codehawks-appeals" /> The
same page warns that excessive appeals with little or no merit can lead to *valid* concerns from that
participant being disqualified.<Cite src="codehawks-appeals" />

**And the professional consequence outlives the platform.** Everything in
[the audit-craft module](/c/false-positive-cost) about a wrong finding costing you credibility on the next
one applies here with an amplifier: the record is public, permanent, and indexed against your handle.

## Where subjectivity legitimately lives

None of this means severity is objective, and pretending otherwise produces its own bad escalations.

CodeHawks lists three factors determining a finding's severity: impact on the protocol, likelihood of
exploitation, and — stated as a factor in its own right — the **degree of judge or protocol
subjectivity**.<Cite src="codehawks-severity" /> The resulting matrix is the standard one, with High
impact against High likelihood giving High, the diagonal giving H/M, M and M/L, and Low against Low giving
Low.<Cite src="codehawks-severity" /> The page then says outright that subjectivity remains in
classification and the judge's discretion is pivotal, and that where the protocol under audit stipulates
particular criteria, those criteria are the benchmark.<Cite src="codehawks-severity" />

Code4rena says something adjacent and sharper about consistency. Standards within a contest are informed
by the overall quality of that contest's submissions, and a judge's internal bar evolves with what they
have seen. The documentation argues this is not inconsistency but consistency *in valuing submissions
relative to each other*, which is a central way performance in a competition is
measured.<Cite src="code4rena-judging-criteria" />

You may disagree with that framing. What you cannot do is treat it as an error to be appealed, because it
is the published policy. Which leads to the rule this lesson exists to install.

<Aside kind="note">
**Escalate when you have a mechanism, not an opinion.** A mechanism is a fact about the code the judge did
not have: an enforcement they assumed exists and does not, a call path they did not trace, a state they did
not consider reachable. An opinion is a different weighting of the same facts. Mechanisms are worth your
whole escalation budget. Opinions are worth none of it, because the judge already has the facts and is
applying a rubric you do not control.
</Aside>

<Check type="explain" concept="judging-and-escalation">
Preliminary results are out on a Sherlock contest and you disagree with three rulings. You can afford to
escalate two. For each, say whether to spend an escalation, and what the comment would have to contain.

1. Your High was downgraded to Medium. The judge wrote that exploitation requires the attacker to hold the
   `KEEPER` role. Reading the code again, `rebalance()` has no role check — the modifier is on the internal
   helper it calls, which is also reachable through `emergencyExit()`.
2. Your Medium was ruled a duplicate of another warden's Medium. Both describe rounding in the same
   function. Yours identifies the truncation site; theirs identifies the same site and additionally shows
   a drain across repeated calls, and was selected for the report.
3. Your Medium was downgraded to Low. The judge agrees the bug is real and that funds leak, but assesses
   the leak rate as immaterial. You think it is material.

<Answer>
**1 — escalate. This is a mechanism, and it is the strongest kind.**

The judge's stated reasoning rests on a factual claim about the code — that a role gate applies — and the
claim is checkable and false. The comment should do exactly one thing: show the unguarded path from
`emergencyExit()` to the internal helper, with line references at the contest commit, and state that the
downgrade's stated premise does not hold. Do not re-argue the severity. Correct the fact and let the
rubric do its own work.

This is what an escalation budget is for, and it is why the currency exists — it forces you to look for
this shape rather than spending on the other two.

**2 — do not escalate, and note that you are not disputing a severity at all.**

Duplication is decided by root cause: if fixing the root cause in a reasonable manner would make the
finding no longer exploitable, the submissions are duplicates,<Cite src="code4rena-judging-criteria" />
and CodeHawks states the same rule with the same worked emphasis — two reports of the same root cause are
duplicates even when submitted at different severity levels.<Cite src="codehawks-payouts" /> Same
truncation site, same fix, same group. There is nothing here a judge got wrong.

What *is* true is that you will receive less than the other warden, and that is also correct by the rules
rather than a mistake. Full credit requires demonstrating both the root cause and the **maximum achievable
impact** of that root cause; you demonstrated the first and not the
second.<Cite src="code4rena-judging-criteria" /> The other submission did both, which is why it was
selected for the report and, on Code4rena, would carry a 30 percent slice bonus for
it.<Cite src="code4rena-awarding" />

The correct response is not an escalation. It is a note in your own retrospective: *I stop at the
mechanism and do not push through to the worst case.* That is a repeatable, fixable habit, and it is worth
more than the delta on this one finding.

**3 — do not escalate.** This is the opinion case, and it is the one that feels the most escalatable.

The judge has the same facts you do and has weighted them differently. Materiality of a leak rate is
exactly the discretionary judgment that severity rubrics hand to the judge, and CodeHawks names judge
subjectivity as an explicit factor in classification rather than pretending it away.<Cite src="codehawks-severity" />
Spending an escalation to say "I weigh this more heavily" gets you a restatement of the rubric.

There is one version of this that *would* be escalatable, and it is worth being able to spot: if you can
show a **rate**, rather than an assessment. A calculation of leakage per call times realistic call
frequency, against total value, converts the opinion into a mechanism. If you can produce that number, you
have a case. If you find yourself writing "in my view", you do not, and you should keep the escalation.

**The pattern across all three.** Escalation is not an appeals process for outcomes you dislike. It is a
narrow channel for correcting a judge's factual premise, and the price exists precisely to stop it becoming
the former. On a platform with a public accuracy record, spending it badly is visible twice — once in the
currency, and once in the pattern of behaviour attached to your handle.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The severity discipline transfers directly to private work.** Everything about
[informational versus finding](/c/informational-vs-finding) in the audit-craft module gets an enforcement
mechanism here: a rubric applied by a stranger who publishes the result. Once a Medium of yours has been
called a Low in public with reasons, you calibrate — and that calibration is what a client pays for later.

**The next lesson removes the judge.** On a bug bounty there is no lead judge, no de-duplication phase and
often no escalation market at all: severity follows demonstrated impact against assets, and the disputes
that remain go to arbitration or to the program's own discretion.

**And the module's reflection prompt is now answerable.** Whatever you learn about your own calibration of
the word "Medium" will have come from a judge disagreeing with you in public. There is no private version
of that experience, which is the honest argument for entering a contest you expect to earn nothing from.
