Look at Ethena's last two rows. The same program, on the same page, applies one scope doctrine to its
smart contracts and to critical web findings, and the opposite doctrine to high-severity web findings.

That is the shape of this lesson. Everything a contest handles once, globally, at the platform level —
scope, severity, payout, dispute — a bounty handles per program, per asset class, and sometimes per
severity row. The unit of reading is not "how do bounties work". It is this table.

## The two scope doctrines

**Primacy of impact** means a report is judged by the impact demonstrated against assets, rather than by
whether the specific contract or code path was enumerated in the program's scope list. The enumerated
scope becomes a floor: hit a listed asset and you are covered; hit an unlisted one with a demonstrated
impact and you may still be covered.

**Primacy of rules** is the opposite default. The program's written terms determine what is in scope and
payable, and a demonstrated impact falling outside those terms is not rewarded.

Every program declares which applies, and the declaration is the single highest-value line on the page
for deciding whether to spend a month on a target.

## Severity starts from impact, then gets adjusted downward

The severity system underneath is not the impact-and-likelihood matrix used by contest platforms.
Immunefi classifies on a four-level scale — Critical, High, Medium, Low — and states that the scale takes
multiple factors into account but **finalises them largely by the impact that they cause**. The table is
described as mostly concerned with the consequence of a successful exploit, with an explicit caveat: if
the exploit requires elevated privileges or uncommon user interaction, the level may be downgraded to
reflect that, **or rejected**.<Cite src="immunefi-severity-classification" />

For smart contracts, the published ladder is concrete enough to self-assess against:

- **Critical** — direct theft of user funds at rest or in motion, other than unclaimed yield; direct theft
  of user NFTs; permanent freezing of funds or NFTs; unauthorised minting of NFTs; predictable or
  manipulable randomness that allows abuse of the principal; manipulation of a governance vote deviating
  from the voted outcome; protocol insolvency.
- **High** — theft or permanent freezing of *unclaimed* yield or royalties; **temporary** freezing of funds
  or NFTs.
- **Medium** — a contract unable to operate for lack of token funds; block stuffing; griefing with no
  profit motive; theft of gas; unbounded gas consumption.
- **Low** — the contract fails to deliver promised returns without losing value.<Cite src="immunefi-severity-classification" />

Two boundaries carry most of the disagreements: **permanent versus temporary freezing**, and **claimed
versus unclaimed yield**. Both move a finding between Critical and High, and both are questions about the
state of the system rather than about the code — which is why a report establishing them explicitly is
worth substantially more than one leaving them to be inferred.

## The maximum is not the payout

The headline number on a program page is a ceiling, and the arithmetic underneath it is published.

Ethena: for critical smart-contract bugs the reward is **10 percent of the funds directly affected**, up
to a maximum of three million dollars, with the amount at risk calculated at the time and date the report
is submitted — and a **minimum of one hundred thousand dollars**, stated as being there to incentivise
researchers against withholding a critical report.<Cite src="immunefi-program-ethena" /> The Graph uses the
identical shape at a different scale: 10 percent of funds directly affected up to fifty thousand, with a
fifteen thousand minimum described in the same terms.<Cite src="immunefi-program-thegraph" />

Read the ceiling backwards. A three-million-dollar maximum, at ten percent, corresponds to thirty million
dollars of funds directly affected by a single report. A program advertising a large maximum is telling
you the size of the pool it is worried about, not the size of the cheque you should expect.

Now read the minimum, because it is the most honest sentence on either page. A floor exists **to discourage
security researchers from withholding a bug report**.<Cite src="immunefi-program-ethena" /> The mechanism
design is explicit about what it is competing against: a researcher holding a critical has an alternative
to reporting, and the floor is priced to make the legitimate path dominate even when ten percent of a small
affected balance would be trivial. That is the whole economic argument for bug bounties in one clause.

### Two adjustments with no contest analogue

**Repeatable attacks depend on whether the contract can be stopped.** If the vulnerable contract can be
upgraded or paused, only the initial attack is considered for a reward, because the project can mitigate
further exploitation. If it cannot be upgraded or paused, the project considers the *cumulative* impact of
the repeatable attacks, on the reasoning that nothing prevents an attacker draining everything — warranting
ten percent of funds at risk, capped at the maximum critical reward.<Cite src="immunefi-program-ethena" />

Sit with the incentive that creates. An immutable contract is worth more to report a bug in than an
upgradeable one, for the same bug. Everything the proxy module taught about upgradeability as a risk
surface has a mirror image here: upgradeability is also a mitigation, and the bounty prices it.

**Temporary freezing is priced by duration.** Ethena's terms state that the reward doubles from the full
frozen value for every additional hour the funds are frozen, up to the cap for the High
tier.<Cite src="immunefi-program-ethena" /> Time-to-recovery becomes a variable in your report rather than
a detail, which means a report that establishes *how long* a freeze lasts is doing paid work.

## The vault: what on-chain proof actually proves

Historically, an advertised maximum bounty was an unverifiable claim. Immunefi's Vaults system addresses
that by holding a program's committed funds on-chain, in a vault built on the Safe multisig contract, under
the project's sole control via an externally owned account, multisig or other smart contract wallet, with
rewards payable on-chain in one transaction and assets deposited as stablecoins, ETH, or anything on the
Uniswap token list.<Cite src="immunefi-vaults" /> The page lists Ethereum mainnet and Optimism as
available, with other EVM chains described as coming soon.<Cite src="immunefi-vaults" />

Program pages then publish the vault's contents. On 26 August 2026:

Be precise about what those rows establish, because it is easy to over-read in either direction.

**What the vault proves.** A specific, checkable quantity of assets, at a public address you can verify
yourself without trusting the platform or the program. The thirty-day average is a second, harder-to-game
number: a vault topped up the morning you look at it and drained afterwards would show a low average. Two
verifiable numbers where there were previously none.

**What the vault does not prove.** That the advertised ceiling is backed. On these two programs the
available funds sat below the maximum in both cases, and in Ethena's by a factor of roughly two hundred and
forty. Nor does a vault mean the vault is the payment channel: Ethena's own terms state that payouts are
handled by the Ethena team directly, denominated in USD and paid in USDC on mainnet, with the conversion
computed from the average of two named price sites at the time the report was
submitted.<Cite src="immunefi-program-ethena" />

So the vault converts an unverifiable claim into a checkable number, and the checkable number is usually not
the ceiling. That is still a large improvement, and it is exactly the kind of claim to state carefully
rather than repeat as a slogan.

## The rules that govern conduct, not just scope

A contest hands you a frozen repository. A bounty hands you a live system and a list of things you may not
do to it. Immunefi's default prohibited activities include **any testing on mainnet or public testnet
deployed code — all testing to be done on local forks**; any testing with pricing oracles or third-party
smart contracts; denial-of-service attacks against project assets; automated testing generating significant
traffic; and public disclosure of an unpatched vulnerability in an embargoed
bounty.<Cite src="immunefi-severity-classification" />

The first is the one to build a habit around. [A runnable proof of concept](/c/poc-as-proof) is the
required methodology here rather than a convenience, and it must run against a local fork at a pinned
block, never against the deployment. Both programs read for this lesson require a PoC and require
KYC.<Cite src="immunefi-program-ethena" /><Cite src="immunefi-program-thegraph" />

The severity classification's default out-of-scope list closes the other obvious door: **impacts requiring
attacks the reporter has already exploited themselves, leading to damage**, are out of scope by
default.<Cite src="immunefi-severity-classification" /> Confirming a finding by executing it is not a
stronger report. It converts a payable finding into an incident with your name attached.

## The same game, priced differently elsewhere

Bounties are not an Immunefi monopoly, and the two alternatives are worth knowing because they price the
submission itself.

**Code4rena runs bounties alongside its competitions**, with a rule that makes the economics unmistakable:
each submission requires a **25 USDC deposit**, paid in-app from a connected wallet, refunded if the finding
is valid or `wontfix` and **not refunded** if it is invalid or spam. Only Critical and High issues are
accepted unless the bounty repo says otherwise, coded runnable proofs of concept are required, submissions
cannot be edited once made, and unless otherwise noted the sponsor team judges. Findings that receive no
sponsor response within fourteen days are closed by default, and where a warden appeals, appointed judges
apply the bounty criteria and their decision is binding and final.<Cite src="code4rena-bounties" />

**Sherlock's post-launch bounty** attaches a dispute apparatus instead. Qualifying customers who have had a
Sherlock audit get a hosted program with free triage from a researcher who has previously demonstrated
proficiency with that codebase, plus an arbitration suite comprising Sherlock's Protocol Claim Committee and
the **UMA Optimistic Oracle**; the customer pays a platform fee of ten percent of the research
fee.<Cite src="sherlock-post-launch-bounty" />

Note what that implies. A bounty dispute is a factual disagreement between two parties with money at stake
and no judge in the loop, so one platform's answer is to route it through an on-chain optimistic oracle. The
existence of the mechanism tells you that "the program decides" is a real failure mode the market is trying
to price.

## The strategy inversion

Everything in the two arithmetic lessons now runs backwards.

**No duplicate split, so being second is worth zero** rather than a small share. There is no partial credit
for arriving late.

**No window, so nothing forces a decision at hour 300** except the invisible race — and the fact that the
code is live, so every day spent perfecting a report is a day the vulnerability stays exploitable by
someone who will not report it.

**Depth beats breadth, and the reversal is total.** In a contest the scarce resource is uniqueness across a
codebase you have two weeks with. In a bounty it is a complete, reproducible, correctly-classified report
against one system you may study for months — where the payout scales with the funds you can demonstrate at
risk, and where establishing whether a freeze is permanent or whether the contract is pausable changes the
number.

**And the same person does both.** Code4rena's rules accept a top-50 position on Immunefi's leaderboard as
grounds to relax its submission limits for a newcomer to its own platform.<Cite src="code4rena-signal" />
Two games, one career.

<Answer>
**The estimate.** Under primacy of impact, the vault's absence from the assets list is not automatically
disqualifying — the doctrine judges the report on the demonstrated impact against assets rather than on
whether the path was enumerated. Direct theft of user funds sits at Critical on the smart-contract
ladder.<Cite src="immunefi-severity-classification" />

The reward calculation on the programs read for this lesson is ten percent of the funds directly affected,
capped at the maximum and floored at the minimum.<Cite src="immunefi-program-ethena" /> Ten percent of
$800,000 is $80,000, which is above the $50,000 floor and below the $2,000,000 cap — so **roughly $80,000**,
subject to the program's own discretion, which every program on both pages reserves.

The repeatability does *not* raise it. Because the contract can be upgraded or paused, only the initial
attack is considered for a reward; cumulative impact is counted only where the contract cannot be stopped.

**Three things this report must establish that a contest submission would not.**

1. **The funds directly affected, as a number, at the time of submission.** In a contest, severity is a
   label and the payout comes from a share formula that never asks how much money was at stake. Here the
   payout *is* a percentage of that figure, computed at the moment you file. A report that does not state
   and evidence the amount is asking the program to compute your reward for you.

2. **Whether the contract can be upgraded or paused.** This is not background colour — it is the switch
   between "only the initial attack counts" and "cumulative impact counts". You would have to check, state
   it, and be right.

3. **That you did not touch the deployment.** The reproduction must be a local fork, because testing against
   mainnet or public testnet deployed code is prohibited by default, and impacts arising from attacks the
   reporter already carried out are out of scope by
   default.<Cite src="immunefi-severity-classification" /> In a contest against a frozen pre-deployment
   commit there is no equivalent constraint, because there is nothing live to touch.

**And one thing worth doing before writing any of it.** Check the vault. The program advertises a
two-million-dollar ceiling; the vault publishes a public address and a thirty-day average balance you can
verify yourself.<Cite src="immunefi-vaults" /> That number does not obligate anyone, and on the programs read
for this lesson it sat well below the advertised maximum — but it is the only checkable evidence available
about whether the counterparty can pay, and it costs one block explorer lookup.
</Answer>

## Where this pays off

**This module closes the security track's career question.** Contests, bounties and private audits are one
practice with three payment structures. The finding is the same artefact; what differs is who grades it, how
many other people wrote it, and whether the code is live while you write.

**The scope-reading habit transfers furthest.** In a contest it is the platform's standing rules plus the
repository README; in a bounty, the rewards table, the assets and impacts tables and the prohibited-activities
list; in a private engagement, the scope statement you write yourself. Same hour, three shapes.

**And the honest summary stands.** Your first contest will most likely produce no critical finding and no
meaningful payment, and that is the expected outcome rather than a failure. What it produces instead is a
calibrated sense of what the severity words mean, a public artefact with your name on it, and a first row on
the only credential this industry actually recognises.
