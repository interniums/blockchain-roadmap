Both rows are a **High**. Both wardens were judged valid. `warden_a` was given full credit — score 1 is
a satisfactory, unpartial submission in that table's notation. The difference between 4,798.84 and
17.05 is not a difference in severity, effort, or write-up quality. It is that eighteen other people
also found `H-01`, and nobody else found `H-02`.<Cite src="code4rena-awarding" />

That ratio — a factor of roughly 280 between two findings carrying the same severity label — is the
single most behaviour-changing fact in competitive auditing, and almost nobody arrives knowing it.

## The formula, taken apart

Code4rena publishes the calculation, and separately publishes the inputs its award script takes: risk
level, validity, number of duplicates, rank, and in some cases partial-duplicate
status.<Cite src="code4rena-awarding" /><Cite src="code4rena-awarding-process" /> Note what is not on
that list — effort, hours, or how many findings you filed. Work the calculation in four steps, each
with a purpose rather than just a value.

**Subgoal one — convert a severity into a quantity of shares.** The platform starts each unique finding
with a base number of shares, called the `pie`. A High starts at 10; a Medium starts at 3. That ratio,
roughly 3.3 to 1, is the platform's price for the difference between the two severity
labels.<Cite src="code4rena-awarding" />

**Subgoal two — shrink the whole finding because it was popular.** Before anyone's share is worked out,
the pie itself is multiplied by a decay factor of `0.85` raised to the power of one less than the number
of people who reported the issue. This is the part that surprises people: the *finding* is worth less
because many people found it, independently of the fact that it will also be divided among
them.<Cite src="code4rena-awarding" />

**Subgoal three — divide what is left among the finders.** Each participant's `slice` is the decayed
pie divided by `split`, the number of submissions in that duplicate group.

Putting the three together gives the published expressions:

```text
Med Risk Slice:   3 * (0.85 ^ (split - 1)) / split
High Risk Slice:  10 * (0.85 ^ (split - 1)) / split
```

**Subgoal four — turn shares into money.** Slices are pro-rata claims on the High/Medium pool, not
currency. Your award is your slice as a fraction of every slice awarded in the audit, times the
pool.<Cite src="code4rena-awarding" /> This is why a contest with an unusually strong field pays less
per finding at identical severity: the denominator is everyone else's work.

### What the decay actually costs

The division by `split` is the part everyone predicts. The decay is the part that changes decisions, so
put a number on the gap. Below, "naive" is what you would expect if the award were divided and nothing
more — `10 / split` for a High — and "actual" is the published formula.

Arithmetic on the published formula.<Cite src="code4rena-awarding" /> The "fraction of naive" column is
just the decay term, and it has a memorable shape: **every additional four-and-a-bit people who find
your bug halve what the whole finding is worth**, before it is divided at all. Multiply that by the
division and the tail falls off a cliff.

Now run the comparison that settles the strategy question. One Medium that nobody else found is worth
3.000 shares. Ten Mediums, each of which twenty people found, are worth ten times 0.0068 — about
**0.068 shares**, some forty-four times less.<Cite src="code4rena-awarding" /> Ten valid findings,
correctly written, each a real bug, adding up to about two percent of one rare Medium.

## The shape is a design choice, not a constant

CodeHawks runs the same structure with different numbers, which is the clearest proof that the shape is
deliberate rather than inevitable. Its published calculation gives High-risk shares as
`5 * (0.9 ^ (findingCount - 1)) / findingCount` and Medium-risk shares as
`1 * (0.9 ^ (findingCount - 1)) / findingCount`.<Cite src="codehawks-payouts" />

Three parameters, three different values from Code4rena's:

- **The High-to-Medium ratio is 5 to 1**, not 3.3 to 1. CodeHawks pays relatively more for escalating
  from Medium to High.
- **The decay base is 0.9**, not 0.85. Duplicates are punished less harshly: the halving point sits at
  roughly seven-and-a-half finders instead of five-and-a-bit.
- **Low-risk findings get their own share expression** on CodeHawks, from a much smaller pool, with an
  explicit note that judges may disqualify low-effort submissions at their
  discretion.<Cite src="codehawks-payouts" />

Same skeleton, three dials set differently. When you move platforms, you are not learning new rules —
you are re-reading two numbers.

## Duplicates are decided by root cause, not by your write-up

The formula's `split` is not something you can argue with by describing your bug differently. Both
platforms define duplication the same way, and the definition is mechanical.

Code4rena: findings are duplicates if they share the same root cause, and more specifically, **if
fixing the root cause in a reasonable manner would make the finding no longer exploitable, the findings
are duplicates**.<Cite src="code4rena-judging-criteria" /> CodeHawks gives the same rule with a worked
pair: a missing zero-address check reported as "no zero address check results in loss of funds, high"
and as "users can lose precision when it doesn't check for `address(0)`, low" are duplicates, because
the root cause is identical, *despite the different severity labels*. Two low findings with genuinely
different root causes — a missing zero-address check and a divide-before-multiply — are not
duplicates.<Cite src="codehawks-payouts" />

Two consequences follow, and they run in opposite directions.

**You cannot escape a duplicate group by finding a different exploit path.** Code4rena states that when
similar exploits demonstrate different impacts, the highest and most irreversible impact is the one used
to score the finding, and the rest are graded relative to the submission selected for the
report.<Cite src="code4rena-judging-criteria" /> Rewriting the same root cause with a flashier scenario
moves you within the group, not out of it.

**But you can lose credit inside the group by describing it badly.** Full credit requires both
identification and demonstration of the root cause *and* of the maximum achievable impact of that root
cause. Missing the root cause is grounds for partial scoring, downgrading, or invalidation. Missing the
maximum impact is grounds for partial scoring or downgrading. Partial credit is assigned at the judge's
discretion at levels such as 25, 50 or 75 percent of a satisfactory
submission.<Cite src="code4rena-awarding" /><Cite src="code4rena-judging-criteria" />

So within a nineteen-way duplicate group, the published sample shows full-credit wardens receiving
17.05 USDC, 75-percent wardens 12.79, 50-percent 8.52 and 25-percent 4.26.<Cite src="code4rena-awarding" />
The whole spread inside that group is under thirteen dollars. The spread between that group and the solo
finding is nearly forty-eight hundred.

**That is the ordering to internalise.** Write-up quality moves you a little. Rarity moves you three
orders of magnitude.

## Two places where quality is nevertheless paid directly

Having just said rarity dominates, the counterweight has to be stated precisely, because it is the
reason a good write-up is not merely professional courtesy.

**The selected-for-report bonus.** For each unique High or Medium finding, the submission chosen for
inclusion in the audit report receives a **30 percent slice bonus**, and the pie is increased
accordingly so the bonus is not taken out of the other finders' shares. Code4rena's own worked example:
a High found by three wardens has a pie of `10 * 0.85^2 = 7.225`; when one submission is selected, the
pie becomes `7.225 + (2.4083 * 0.3) = 7.9475`, and that warden's slice rises from 2.4083 to
3.1308.<Cite src="code4rena-awarding" /> Roughly thirty percent more money for the same bug, decided
entirely by whose write-up the judge chose to publish.

**The rarity bonuses.** Two further pools sit on top: a **Hunter bonus** of 10 percent of the
High/Medium pool to whoever identifies the greatest number of *unique* Highs and Mediums, and a
**Gatherer bonus** of 10 percent to whoever identifies the greatest number of valid ones. The Hunter
score is deliberately narrow — only full-credit findings with **fewer than five submissions in the
duplicate set** count toward it, each scoring 10 divided by the number of duplicates for a High and 3
divided by the same for a Medium.<Cite src="code4rena-awarding" />

Read the Hunter rule as a second, independent statement of the same preference: a finding twenty people
made does not merely pay almost nothing, it does not exist at all for the rarity bonus.

## The platform has already limited your volume

There is one more reason "submit more" is not available as a strategy, and it is not economic — it is a
hard cap.

Code4rena computes an accuracy metric it calls `signal`: for a warden or team, the ratio of valid High
and Medium findings to all the Highs and Mediums they submitted. It reads as `null` until you have made
at least three High or Medium submissions to finalised audits. Submissions per audit are then capped by
it.<Cite src="code4rena-signal" />

| Your signal | Max submissions per audit | With verified ID and tax info |
|---|---:|---:|
| `null`, or under 0.2 | 1 | 2 |
| 0.2 to 0.3999 | 2 | 10 |
| 0.4 and above | 10 | unrestricted |

Those are the platform's published numbers, with a note that they are subject to change based on the
volume of low-quality submissions received.<Cite src="code4rena-signal" />

A first-time warden gets **one submission**. Not one High — one submission of any kind, since all
submission types count against the limit, High, Medium and QA report alike.<Cite src="code4rena-signal" />

And the QA route offers no escape hatch, because Lows are not individually paid at all. Low-severity and
governance or centralization findings must be submitted as a **single QA report per warden**, editable
only during a two-hour window after submission; the reports are graded A, B or C and awarded on a curve,
with **only the top three** receiving anything.<Cite src="code4rena-submission-guidelines" /><Cite src="code4rena-judging-criteria" />
Twenty valid Lows and one valid Low are the same submission, and both pay zero unless the write-up
places in the top three of the field.

This changes the character of a first contest completely. The question is never "how many findings can
I produce". It is "of everything I have, which single item is most likely to be both valid and rare",
and answering it well requires the scope reading in the next lesson and the severity calibration in the
one after.

<Aside kind="note">
The signal metric also closes an obvious loophole: High and Medium submissions that get downgraded to
Low are excluded from the signal calculation entirely, and the documentation states the reason plainly —
to discourage wardens from inflating severity.<Cite src="code4rena-signal" /> Dressing a Low as a High
therefore has no upside on the ratio and spends one of your very few submissions. The judging lesson
picks up the other end of the same mechanism.
</Aside>

## Where to spend the last day

The arithmetic produces a specific, unintuitive tactical rule, and it is worth stating as an
instruction rather than a principle.

**On the final day of a contest, do not re-read the main contract looking for something you missed.**
Everyone else is doing exactly that, in the same file, for the same reason. Anything you find there at
hour 300 is something dozens of people found at hour 20, and it lands in a twenty-way duplicate group
worth roughly two percent of naive.

Spend the day where the crowd is not. The candidates are always unglamorous: **integration boundaries**,
where this protocol meets the real behaviour of the specific external contract it composes with rather
than that contract's interface; **migration and upgrade paths**, meaning the state of the system
*during* a transition rather than before or after; **the edges of the state space**, such as empty
pools, single-participant states, and values at the boundary of a cast; and **whatever the test suite
does not touch**, since coverage gaps map where the authors' attention was not.

None of that is a trick. It is the direct consequence of a pool split by a decaying function of how many
people looked in the same place.

<Check type="predict" concept="solo-finding">
A contest has a High/Medium pool of $100,000. When judging finishes, the audit's total awarded slices
come to 40.0 shares. You have two findings in it:

1. A **High** that twelve other wardens also submitted — thirteen in the duplicate group, and you were
   given full credit but were not selected for the report.
2. A **Medium** that nobody else found. You were selected for the report, since you are the only
   submission in the group.

Estimate each award, and then say which of the two you should have spent your last three days on.

<Answer>
**Finding 1 — the thirteen-way High.**

Slice is `10 * 0.85^12 / 13`. The decay term `0.85^12` is about 0.1422, so the pie is about 1.422 and
your slice is about **0.109 shares**.<Cite src="code4rena-awarding" /> At $100,000 over 40 shares,
each share is worth $2,500, so the award is roughly **$273**.

**Finding 2 — the solo Medium, selected for the report.**

The base pie is `3 * 0.85^0 = 3.000`, and with a single submission the slice is 3.000. The
selected-for-report bonus adds 30 percent of that slice to the pie, so the pie becomes 3.9 and your
slice becomes **3.9 shares**.<Cite src="code4rena-awarding" /> That is roughly **$9,750**.

**The ratio is about 36 to 1, in favour of the Medium.** Read that sentence again with the severity
labels attached: a Medium out-earned a High by a factor of thirty-six, and the only reason was that
nobody else found it.

**Where the last three days should have gone.**

Neither finding, in the sense that matters. Once you had submitted the High, additional time on that
finding bought nothing — you were already in the duplicate group and the group's size was determined by
other people's behaviour, not yours. Once you had submitted the Medium, additional time on *it* bought
only the report bonus, which you already had.

The last three days were worth spending on **a second solo finding**, anywhere the other twelve wardens
in group one were demonstrably not looking. Even a second solo Medium — 3.0 shares, another $7,500 —
would have out-earned every duplicated High you could conceivably have found in the same time.

**Two caveats a careful reader should raise.**

The share-to-dollar conversion is not something you can compute during a contest, because the
denominator is every other warden's slices and you do not know it until awards are announced. What you
*can* compute during a contest is the ratio between your own options, which is all the arithmetic above
is really used for.

And on Code4rena, if this were your first contest, you could not have submitted both: with `null`
signal you have one submission, or two with identity verification and tax information
filed.<Cite src="code4rena-signal" /> Under that constraint the question stops being "which do I spend
time on" and becomes "which one do I file", and the answer is the Medium, by a factor of thirty-six.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson is the other half of this one.** Rarity is worth three orders of magnitude, so the
question becomes where to look — and the first answer is: nowhere the contest has already declared out of
scope. An invalid finding is not a small slice, it is zero, and it costs a submission and a point of
accuracy besides.

**The judging lesson explains the `split` you were given.** Duplicate grouping is a ruling like any
other, contestable in a bounded window, at a price.

**The bounty lesson is this arithmetic deleted.** One bug, one payout, and being second is worth nothing
rather than a small share — which flips the optimal strategy from breadth-inside-a-window to
depth-over-months.

And when you next read a published contest report, read the duplicate counts alongside the findings.
That column is a map of where a hundred people all looked at once — which is the most direct evidence
available about where they did not.
