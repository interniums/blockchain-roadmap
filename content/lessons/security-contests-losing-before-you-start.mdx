The contest opens at 14:00. At 14:01 almost everyone does the same thing: clones the repo, finds the
largest contract in the in-scope list, and starts reading.

That hour is the most expensive of the contest, and it is expensive in a way that leaves no trace. Nothing
goes wrong. You read code, you understand it, you find something. Two weeks later a judge marks it out of
scope, and the reason turns out to have been written down before you arrived — sometimes in the repository,
sometimes on a documentation page you have never opened.

An out-of-scope finding is not a small award. It is zero, it consumes one of the one-or-two submissions a
new participant is allowed, and it counts against the accuracy ratio that sets how many submissions you
get next time.<Cite src="code4rena-signal" /> No defect in this module is cheaper to fix, because fixing
it costs an hour of reading.

## Two exclusion surfaces, and only one of them is in the repo

Most advice about contest scope covers the first surface and stops. Both matter, and the second is the
one that surprises experienced engineers.

**Surface one: this contest's own documents.** The in-scope file list and the commit hash; the known
issues section; links to prior audit reports; the appendix of findings already produced by automated
tooling; and the protocol's own stated invariants. Code4rena's submission guidelines put the
requirement plainly — a submission must not have been surfaced as a known issue, with the audit repo
README named as the place that says so.<Cite src="code4rena-submission-guidelines" />

**Surface two: the platform's standing rules.** These are class-level exclusions that apply to every
competition the platform runs, live before any specific contest exists, and they are not reproduced in
the repository. They invalidate categories of finding, not instances.

The second surface is where the damage concentrates, so take it apart properly.

## The standing rules, as a checklist

These are Code4rena's, from its severity classifications page.<Cite src="code4rena-severity-categorization" />
Read them as a list of things not to spend two weeks on.

**Non-standard tokens are out of scope by default.** All non-standard or "weird" ERC-20 findings and all
fee-on-transfer token findings are out of scope unless those tokens are explicitly listed as supported in
the scope or documentation. The sole exception is USDT, which is in scope despite its non-standard
behaviour. The page instructs judges to *immediately invalidate* findings in this category that do not
meet the criterion, and discourages wardens from submitting them.

**Findings that require the user to make a mistake are QA at best.** Findings conditional on a user being
careless or entering wrong information are Quality Assurance at best and may be judged invalid outright,
on the stated reasoning that non-privileged users are expected to preview their transactions and modern
tooling has previews built in. Phishing and improper caution using a protocol fall under the same rule.

**Privileged roles are assumed trustworthy.** All assigned roles are expected to invoke their privileged
functions responsibly, and reckless admin mistakes are invalid — assume calls are previewed. Direct misuse
of privileges goes in a QA report, as do bugs reachable only through an admin mistake. Privilege
escalation is judged on likelihood and impact, and a privileged-function scenario producing a
vulnerability *under reasonable assumptions* may reach Medium.

**A fault inside an out-of-scope library is out of scope; misuse of it is not.** When an in-scope contract
inherits from or composes with an out-of-scope one, the test is where the root cause lives. Root cause
inside the out-of-scope contract: out of scope. Incorrect implementation of the inheritance, or improper
calls into the library by the in-scope contract: in scope.

**Anything not exploitable within the contest's scope is speculation about future code.** It can be valid
only if the root cause is demonstrated to lie inside the contest scope. A warden may argue that a future
change making the bug manifest is reasonably likely, and the judge may then assign severity as they see
fit. Where exploitability depends on a third-party integration, likelihood must assume a competent
integrator who has done due diligence.

**An event-only impact is capped at Low.** A submission whose consequence is a faulty event emission is
assessed on its broader impact — if the event feeds an on-chain process such as bridging or an inclusion
proof, it is assessed on that. Failure to demonstrate a broader impact caps severity at Low, as do bugs
affecting only readability, data accessibility, or front-end display.

**Some specific findings are pre-rejected by name.** Unused view functions are Low at best. Approve and
`safeApprove` front-running is explicitly not a valid vulnerability, and while `increaseAllowance` and
`decreaseAllowance` are deprecated, using them is not a finding. A protocol not supporting CryptoPunks is
informational and "cannot be argued as a vulnerability".

**Dust is Low; the yield rule has a direction.** Loss of dust amounts — rounding errors, marginal fee
variations — is QA or Low. Loss of *matured* yield is treated like any other loss of capital, so it can be
High. Loss of *unmatured* yield, or yield in motion, is capped at Medium.

<Aside kind="note">
These rules are less arbitrary than they look. "Assume the admin is trustworthy" is not a claim about
admins; it is a scoping decision that centralization risk is a governance question the sponsor already
knows about, so it belongs in the QA report rather than crowding the findings list. Disagreeing with the
policy is fine. Submitting against it is not a form of disagreement — it is an invalid submission.
</Aside>

## Prior audits: known, unless the bug outlived the fix

Prior audit reports get their own rule, and it is more nuanced than "already found, therefore invalid".

Findings from previous audit reports listed in the repo README should **generally** be treated as known
issues and out of scope, particularly where the sponsor marked them acknowledged or wontfix. Two doors
stay open. If the finding was *confirmed* and could reasonably be expected to have been mitigated before
this competition, a judge may treat it as valid because the vulnerability persisted. And if your
submission demonstrates a substantively distinct or higher-severity attack path, the judge may deem it
valid.<Cite src="code4rena-judging-criteria" />

That is a genuinely productive place to spend time, and almost nobody does. Checking whether each
confirmed finding in a prior report was actually fixed is a bounded task with a clear success criterion,
and it looks exactly where the crowd is not — which the [previous lesson](/c/solo-finding) established is
where the money is.

You may also argue an issue into scope: Code4rena's judging criteria explicitly allow a warden to make
the case that an out-of-scope item merits inclusion. But it is the judge's absolute discretion, and the
submission must carry a clear argument for why.<Cite src="code4rena-judging-criteria" /> Treat that as an
escape hatch for something genuinely serious, not as a way to recycle an invalid finding.

<Aside kind="warn">
One asymmetric rule worth remembering: QA reports must not include findings on code or issues identified
as out of scope.<Cite src="code4rena-judging-criteria" /> The QA report is not a place to put things that
did not survive scope. It is graded against other wardens' QA reports on quality and thoroughness, and
padding it with out-of-scope material is visible.
</Aside>

## On a bounty, the rules govern conduct as well as scope

Bug bounty programs carry the same two-surface structure with a difference that matters more: some of the
standing rules describe what you may *do*, not merely what you may report, and the code is live.

Immunefi publishes a default out-of-scope list: impacts the reporter already exploited themselves, impacts
requiring leaked keys or access to privileged addresses, external stablecoin depegs the attacker did not
cause, secrets in a public repository without proof they are in production use, best-practice
recommendations and feature requests. For smart contracts it adds incorrect data supplied by third-party
oracles — while explicitly *not* excluding oracle manipulation or flash-loan attacks — plus basic economic
and governance attacks such as a 51% attack, lack of liquidity, Sybil attacks, and centralization
risks.<Cite src="immunefi-severity-classification" /> Each program may vary the list, and the page says so.

The prohibited-activities list is the one to internalise before you touch anything. Prohibited by default:
any testing on mainnet or public testnet deployed code, with all testing to be done on local forks; any
testing with pricing oracles or third-party smart contracts; any denial-of-service attack against project
assets; automated testing generating significant traffic; and public disclosure of an unpatched vulnerability
in an embargoed bounty.<Cite src="immunefi-severity-classification" />

The distinction between the two surfaces collapses here. Breaking a scope rule wastes your time. Breaking a
conduct rule against a live contract is an incident.

## The scope brief: one hour, before the first contract

Here is the hour that replaces reading the biggest file first. Produce a one-page document. Each step has a
purpose, not just an output.

**Subgoal one — pin what you are actually reviewing.** Copy the commit hash and the in-scope file list into
your notes verbatim. Anything off that list is out of scope by default, and the hash is what your proof of
concept must run against.

**Subgoal two — collect the exclusions before forming any hypotheses.** Known issues, then prior audit
reports, then the automated-findings appendix. Do this before you have opinions, because once you have
found something you will unconsciously argue it past a rule you half-remember.

**Subgoal three — check the class-level rules against this codebase's shape.** If the protocol takes
arbitrary ERC-20s, the non-standard-token rule has just deleted a large and tempting surface. If it has a
broad admin surface, the trusted-roles rule sends most of what you will notice to the QA report. Knowing
that now is worth more than knowing it in week two.

**Subgoal four — make the test suite run.** Code4rena requires a coded, runnable PoC for High and Medium
submissions to Solidity audits unless the README says otherwise, and marks submissions insufficient if the
PoC does not run against the provided suite.<Cite src="code4rena-submission-guidelines" /> Discovering a
broken toolchain on the last evening is a self-inflicted invalid submission.

**Subgoal five — write down the three places you will spend your time, and why.** Naming them forces the
choice to be deliberate rather than a consequence of file size, and gives you something to compare against
the published report afterwards.

## First Flights are where this should go wrong

Every mistake above is cheaper to make when nothing is at stake, which is the argument for CodeHawks First
Flights as an entry point rather than a warm-up.

First Flights are auditing challenges on smaller codebases with, in the platform's own words, different
reward mechanisms from its standard competitions: **no monetary prize pool**, with participants earning
experience for submitted findings instead. The complexity of each Flight is stated to increase over
time.<Cite src="codehawks-first-flights-docs" />

What is *not* removed is the machinery: scope published at launch, a live submission portal described as
the same experience as a standard competition, a judge who reviews and tests every finding and confirms or
adjusts its severity, and an appeals phase afterwards with feedback you can
read.<Cite src="codehawks-first-flights-docs" /> Severity is assessed on the same impact-and-likelihood
matrix used in the paid competitions.<Cite src="codehawks-severity" />

That is the right shape for a first attempt precisely because the pool is zero. Nothing rewards inflating
a severity, nothing punishes submitting an honest belief and being told it was wrong, and there is no
twenty-way duplicate group to be disappointed by. What you practise is the judgement, and the judgement is
the part that transfers.

<Aside kind="warn">
The First Flights page contradicts itself on cadence — one section says monthly, another says weekly — and
carries a "last updated 2 years ago" stamp.<Cite src="codehawks-first-flights-docs" /> Do not plan around
either figure; check the platform, or a competitions tracker, for what is actually
live.<Cite src="sch-competitions-tracker" />
</Aside>

You are three days into a Code4rena competition on a lending protocol. The README lists the in-scope files
and one known issue ("the oracle is trusted"). You have five candidate submissions and, being new, one
submission slot. Rule each in or out, and say which you file.

1. `deposit()` reverts for fee-on-transfer tokens, stranding user funds. The protocol accepts arbitrary
   ERC-20 collateral; the docs list no supported tokens.
2. If a user passes the wrong recipient address to `withdrawTo()`, funds go to a stranger.
3. The `pause()` function can be called by the owner at any time, freezing all withdrawals indefinitely.
4. An in-scope vault calls an out-of-scope math library's `mulDiv` with arguments in the wrong order,
   producing a wrong share price on every deposit.
5. `Deposit` is emitted with the pre-fee amount, so the protocol's subgraph reports balances that do not
   match on-chain state. No on-chain process reads the event.

<Answer>
**1 — out of scope.** Fee-on-transfer token findings are out of scope unless the tokens are explicitly
listed as supported in the scope or documentation, and the page directs judges to invalidate such findings
immediately.<Cite src="code4rena-severity-categorization" /> "The protocol accepts arbitrary ERC-20s"
makes the bug real and makes the finding invalid, which is exactly the pairing that catches people.

**2 — QA at best, plausibly invalid.** Findings conditional on the user being careless or entering wrong
information are QA at best and may be judged invalid, with non-privileged users expected to preview their
transactions.<Cite src="code4rena-severity-categorization" />

**3 — QA report, not a finding.** All roles assigned by the system are expected to be trustworthy, and
direct misuse of privileges belongs in the QA report.<Cite src="code4rena-severity-categorization" /> It
would only reach Medium if you could show a vulnerability arising under *reasonable* assumptions rather
than an owner choosing to grief.

**4 — in scope, and this is the one you file.** The library is out of scope, but the root cause is the
in-scope vault's improper call into it, and issues arising from an in-scope contract's misuse of
out-of-scope functionality are valid findings.<Cite src="code4rena-severity-categorization" /> Wrong share
price on every deposit puts assets directly at risk, which is the High
definition.<Cite src="code4rena-severity-categorization" /> Argument-order mistakes are also
under-reported relative to how often they exist, so this is a plausible candidate for a small duplicate
group.

**5 — capped at Low.** A submission whose consequence is faulty event emission is assessed on its broader
impact, and where no on-chain process consumes the event, failure to demonstrate that broader impact caps
severity at Low — as do issues of data accessibility and front-end
display.<Cite src="code4rena-severity-categorization" /> A subgraph is a front end.

**What the exercise is really testing.** Four of the five are real defects. A reviewer who evaluates only
"is this true" files something worth nothing. The scope rules are not a formality wrapped around the
technical work; on a competitive platform they are half of the technical work, and they are readable in
an hour.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**Scope discipline is the transferable half of this module.** In private audit work the same reading
produces [the scope statement](/c/scope-statement) — the section of a report that decides what "audited"
means. Here it decides whether your fortnight paid.

**The next lesson is about the rules you disagree with**, and every platform has a bounded, priced window
in which to say so. Knowing which of these rules is a firm exclusion and which is a judge's discretionary
call is what tells you when arguing is worth the price.

**And the bounty lesson inverts the scope question.** Under primacy of impact, a program's enumerated
scope becomes a floor rather than a ceiling — a demonstrated impact against assets can count even where the
contract was never listed. No contest has an equivalent rule.
