Two browser tabs, opened the same morning by the same person.

The first is a GitHub repository pinned to one commit. It will not move for fourteen days. A countdown
sits at the top of the page. Somewhere between fifty and several hundred other people have the same
tab open, reading the same four hundred lines, and the money in this tab is a number that was fixed
before any of them arrived.

The second is a contract address on mainnet. It holds real deposits right now. There is no countdown,
no commit hash, no participant list, and no way to know whether the person who found this bug two
hours ago has already filed it.

Both tabs are labelled, in every article you have ever read about them, "getting paid to find bugs".
They are different games with opposite optimal strategies, and the largest single source of wasted
effort in this field is people playing one while believing they are in the other.

The fourth row is the one that reshapes behaviour, and the next lesson is entirely about its
arithmetic. The fifth row is the one nobody expects, and it is worth pausing on now because it kills
the strategy most newcomers arrive with.

## An audit contest, as a process

Code4rena's own description of the shape is the clearest available, and the other contest platforms
are variations on it rather than alternatives to it.<Cite src="code4rena-competitions" />

**Sponsors post a prize pool** to attract reviewers to their code. The pool is fixed in advance and
does not grow if the codebase turns out to be worse than expected.

**A submission phase opens**, typically lasting one to three weeks, during which the code is public
and participants — Code4rena calls them wardens — submit findings through a form. The judge and the
sponsor are already reading submissions while the phase is still running.<Cite src="code4rena-competitions" />

**A judging phase follows.** The judge decides severity, validity and quality, and decides which
submissions are duplicates of each other. Sponsor input is considered; the judge
decides.<Cite src="code4rena-judging-criteria" />

**A short review window opens after preliminary decisions.** On Code4rena this is a 48-hour
post-judging QA period in which the sponsor team and eligible wardens can raise concerns for the
judge.<Cite src="code4rena-competitions" /> After it closes, decisions are
final.<Cite src="code4rena-judging-criteria" /> Sherlock runs a structurally similar but differently
priced phase, and lesson four takes both apart.

**Awards are calculated by a published algorithm** rather than negotiated, and distributed in two
batches so that winners have time to satisfy payout requirements.<Cite src="code4rena-awarding-process" />

**A report is published** compiling the valid findings, usually along with all the submissions.<Cite src="code4rena-competitions" />

Two details in that sequence matter more than they look. The frozen commit is not a formality — it is
what makes the pool splittable at all, because every participant reviews an identical artefact and
"did two people find the same thing" is therefore decidable. And the report is a public artefact with
your handle on it, which for most first-time participants is the entire realisable value of the
exercise.

<Aside kind="note">
Scale sets the tone. Code4rena describes a community of over 10,000 auditors with an average of more
than 100 security researchers participating in each audit.<Cite src="code4rena-competitions" /> Hold
that next to the fact that the pool is fixed. You are not being hired; you are entering a field.
</Aside>

## A bug bounty, as a relationship

Now the second tab. The Immunefi listing on 26 August 2026 showed 186 live bounty programs, with the
page itself stating that its metrics are updated daily and lag report resolution by two weeks for
confidentiality.<Cite src="immunefi-bug-bounty" />

Open one and the shape is unmistakably not a contest. The Graph's program lists a start date of
4 August 2021 and a last-updated stamp of 18 August 2026 — a program that has been open for five
years.<Cite src="immunefi-program-thegraph" /> Ethena's lists 4 April 2024 and 11 August
2026.<Cite src="immunefi-program-ethena" /> There is no cohort. There is no deadline. There is
nothing to be simultaneous with.

What replaces the deadline is a race you cannot observe. A contest tells you the competition exists
and roughly how large it is. A bounty tells you nothing: the value of your finding is a step function
of whether someone else filed it first, and you have no signal about that until you submit.

The award rule inverts too. There is no split, because there is nothing to split — a bounty pays for
a bug, once. Being the second person to find a critical is worth exactly zero, where in a contest it
is worth a share.

### The cost of a submission is not zero, and on one platform it is literally priced

Here is the detail that most cleanly separates the two games, and it is easy to miss because it lives
on a page most people never open.

In a Code4rena **competition**, submissions cost you an allowance. The platform caps how many findings
you may submit per audit, keyed to an accuracy score it calls `signal`. A participant with no history
— which is every first-timer — is limited to **one submission per audit**, rising to two if they have
verified their identity and filed tax information.<Cite src="code4rena-signal" />

In a Code4rena **bounty**, submissions cost money. Each submission requires a **25 USDC deposit**, paid
in-app from a connected wallet. It is refunded if the finding is judged valid or `wontfix`. It is
**not** refunded if the finding is invalid or spam.<Cite src="code4rena-bounties" />

Sit with the second one. A bounty submission is a bet with your own money on your own calibration: the
platform has taken the thing everyone wants to do — file it and see — and priced being wrong. The
competition cap is the same statement in a different currency. "Submit everything you find" is not a
strategy a newcomer can execute on Code4rena. You get one shot, so the only question is where you point
it — which is what lesson three is about.

<Aside kind="warn">
Both figures are current platform policy and both are the kind that change. The submission-limit table
carries an explicit note that the limits are subject to change based on spam
volume.<Cite src="code4rena-signal" /> Read the numbers from the platform on the day you compete, not
from this page.
</Aside>

## What a first contest actually returns

This is the part that is usually written by someone selling a course, so read the epistemics carefully.

**There is no published distribution of participant outcomes.** Platforms publish leaderboards, which
show the people at the top. A leaderboard is a maximum, not a distribution: it tells you what the best
result was and nothing about the median, the mode, or the share of participants who earned nothing.
This research could find no trustworthy figure for any of those, and rather than pick a number from a
blog, this module states that the number is unknown. If you encounter a confident claim about "the
average competitive auditor earns X", ask where the denominator came from.

**The findings you can reach first are the ones worth least.** That is not a comment on your ability;
it is arithmetic, and the next lesson does it in full. Bugs that a first-time reader can see are bugs
that fifty other people can also see, and the award for an issue is divided across everyone who
reported it and reduced further by a decay factor on top of the division.<Cite src="code4rena-awarding" />

**A submission can be true and still worth nothing.** Code4rena's judging criteria say this
explicitly: a submission may be factually valid and still be ruled insufficient quality, and
insufficient submissions are ineligible for awards.<Cite src="code4rena-judging-criteria" /> The bar
is stated as "roughly at a level that could be found in a draft report by a professional
auditor".<Cite src="code4rena-judging-criteria" /> You already know how to write a finding from
[the audit-craft module](/c/finding-anatomy); a contest is where you discover whether a stranger
agrees.

So what does come back?

**A calibrated severity vocabulary.** You will submit something you are certain is a Medium and watch
a judge call it a Low, in public, with reasons. Reading severity rubrics does not produce that
recalibration, because rubrics feel obvious until one is applied to your own work.

**A public artefact.** The report is published with the submissions, so you have findings a third party
graded.

**The retrospective.** Read the final report against your own notes, with particular attention to the
findings you saw and dismissed. That category is where almost all the transferable learning sits, because
it is the only direct evidence you get about your own judgement rather than your own knowledge.

### CodeHawks makes the training frame explicit

Cyfrin's CodeHawks runs a beginner tier called First Flights, and its own description is unusually
honest about what it is for. First Flights are, in the platform's words, auditing challenges with
smaller codebases and different reward mechanisms from its standard competitions; unlike the standard
competitions, First Flights offer **no monetary prize pool**, granting participants experience earned
by submitting findings instead. The complexity of each Flight is stated to increase over
time.<Cite src="codehawks-first-flights-docs" />

Everything else is the real machinery: a public scope, a live submission portal described as the same
experience as a standard competition, a judge who reviews and tests every finding and confirms or
adjusts its severity, and an appeals phase afterwards.<Cite src="codehawks-first-flights-docs" />

That combination — real process, zero pool — is the correct first move, and it is correct precisely
because the pool is zero. With no money on the table there is nothing to lose by submitting your
honest severity, which is the one habit worth forming before it costs anything.

<Aside kind="warn">
The First Flights page contradicts itself on cadence: one section says a new First Flight is announced
monthly, another says one takes off every week, and the page carries a "last updated 2 years ago"
stamp.<Cite src="codehawks-first-flights-docs" /> Treat the cadence as unknown and check the platform
before planning around it. A competitions tracker is the practical way to see what is actually
live.<Cite src="sch-competitions-tracker" />
</Aside>

## The credential question

Nothing in this field certifies you. There is no exam, and the certificates that exist are sold by
people who benefit from selling them.

What there is instead is a leaderboard, and the strongest evidence that the leaderboard is the real
credential comes from a platform treating a *rival's* leaderboard as identity. Code4rena's rules let a
researcher ranked in the top 50 on the Sherlock, Cantina or Immunefi leaderboards request a bypass of
Code4rena's own submission limits, receiving the allowance of a high-accuracy warden for their first
three Code4rena audits.<Cite src="code4rena-signal" />

Read that as a market fact rather than a policy detail. A competitor's public ranking is accepted as
proof of competence by a platform that has every reason to be sceptical of it; no certificate in this
industry clears that bar. The corollary is unglamorous — a leaderboard is built from graded
submissions, so the only route to the credential runs through being graded, in public, from zero.

## Neither game ends the security story

One temptation this module should close off now. It is easy to read "we ran a contest with a hundred
reviewers" as a stronger claim than "we had an audit", and therefore as an end state.

The available evidence points the other way, though its provenance needs stating. Immunefi published
research reporting that nearly every long-running bug bounty program on its platform has eventually
surfaced a critical bug — programs whose code had already been audited, and in many cases
contested.<Cite src="immunefi-long-running-programs" /> That is vendor-published research about the
vendor's own platform, with an obvious commercial interest in the conclusion, so treat the direction as
informative and the precision as not established. Directionally it agrees with
[the audit-craft module](/c/audit-is-not-a-guarantee): a hundred reviewers for two weeks is a different
shape of assurance from two reviewers for six, and neither is a proof.

Code4rena's rules encode the same expectation structurally. Competitions can include live, deployed
code, and when they do the process changes: after submissions close, all submissions become sensitive
and hidden from every warden, and by default there is no post-judging QA phase at all — precisely so
that issues affecting deployed contracts are not shared while they are still
live.<Cite src="code4rena-live-code-rules" /> The platform's own design assumes that a contest may be
sitting on top of exploitable production code.

You have found what you are confident is a Critical: an unauthorised path to drain a vault. Say what
you would do differently in each of these two situations, and why.

1. You found it during a two-week audit contest on a frozen, **not yet deployed** commit, on day three
   of fourteen.
2. You found it in a **live, deployed** contract that is currently holding deposits, outside any
   contest, while reading the code on your own.

<Answer>
**Situation 1 — the clock you care about is the duplicate clock, not the deadline.**

The code is not deployed, so nobody's money is at risk while you write. The value of the finding
depends on how many other people file it, and that number only grows. Submit it properly and promptly:
a full write-up with a runnable proof of concept against the repo's own test suite, which Code4rena
requires for High and Medium submissions to Solidity audits unless the audit README says
otherwise.<Cite src="code4rena-submission-guidelines" />

Then, and this is the part beginners get backwards, **stop looking for more obvious bugs**. Eleven days
remain and the marginal value of a second heavily-duplicated finding is close to nothing. Spend them
somewhere other people are not looking.

Note the one case where the deployment status flips this: if the contest scope includes live code and
your finding hits it, Code4rena's "live criticals" exception makes prompt submission of a
High-or-critical issue in live contracts, with a coded proof of concept, filed before the code is fixed
or the bug documented anywhere participants could see it, the *only* submission in its duplicate set
eligible for payout from the competitive pool.<Cite src="code4rena-awarding" /> There, speed is worth
real money, and the incentive exists specifically to get live bugs disclosed fast.

**Situation 2 — you are holding a live vulnerability and the first question is legal and ethical, not
economic.**

Before anything else: find out whether a bounty program covers this contract and what its terms say.
If there is a program, its rules bind you, and Immunefi's default prohibited-activities list forbids
testing against mainnet or public testnet deployed code entirely — all testing is to be done on local
forks.<Cite src="immunefi-severity-classification" /> Confirming your finding by trying it on the live
contract is not a bolder version of the same act; it is the act the program exists to prevent.

Economically, the situation is the inverse of the contest. There is no split and no deadline, so
nothing rewards you for hurrying except the unobservable race against another finder. What *is*
rewarded is depth: a complete report with a working local-fork reproduction, because bounty programs
routinely require a proof of concept as a condition of the
report.<Cite src="immunefi-program-ethena" />

**The transferable rule.** In a contest, the scarce resource is uniqueness, and it decays with time.
In a bounty, the scarce resource is proof, and the code is live while you assemble it. Those two
sentences generate almost every tactical difference between the games.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The arithmetic comes next**, concrete enough to change what you do with the last day of a contest,
from the platforms' own published award formulas and sample results.

**Then scope reading**, because the exclusion surface is much wider than the repository README — each
platform maintains standing rules that invalidate whole classes of finding before any specific contest
begins.

**Then judging**, where every platform in this module prices the act of disagreeing: Sherlock in a
reputation currency, Code4rena in an accuracy score plus identity verification, CodeHawks in prior
earnings.

**And bounties last**, because primacy of impact, on-chain proof of assets, and rewards computed as a
percentage of funds at risk have no contest analogue at all.

All of it assumes the finding-writing craft from the previous module. The difference here is that a
stranger with a published rubric decides what your writing was worth, and tells everyone.
