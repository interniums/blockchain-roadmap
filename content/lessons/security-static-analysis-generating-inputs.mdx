Two campaigns against the same contract, on this machine, minutes apart.

The first ran a hundred and twenty-eight thousand calls and passed.

```text
[PASS] invariant_sharesNonZeroImpliesAssets() (runs: 256, calls: 128000, reverts: 93347)

| Contract | Selector | Calls | Reverts |
| Pool     | deposit  | 42823 | 25546   |
| Pool     | donate   | 42601 | 25306   |
| Pool     | withdraw | 42576 | 42495   |
```

The second ran one call and failed.

```text
[FAIL: panic: assertion failed (0x01)]
	[Sequence] (original: 1, shrunk: 1)
		sender=0x…0084 addr=[src/Pool.sol:Pool] calldata=donate(uint256) args=[101]
 invariant_noAssetsWithoutShares() (runs: 0, calls: 0, reverts: 1)
```

Same contract, same fuzzer, same seed budget. The first campaign took six and a half seconds; the
second found its counterexample in twenty-eight milliseconds and shrank it to a single
call.<Cite src="measured-static-analysis-2026-08-26" />

Nothing about the tool changed between those two runs. What changed was the sentence the author wrote
down. That is the whole subject of this lesson, and it is the reason property fuzzing sits where it
does in the assurance stack.

## The asymmetry that governs everything else

You already know the mechanics from Track 05: the runner generates arguments, drives a sequence of
calls, and evaluates your property after each. What that module did not press on is the epistemics,
and the two runs above make them concrete.

**A counterexample is proof.** `donate(101)` against a pool with no shares outstanding is a fact about
the code. It reproduces, it shrinks to a minimal witness, and no amount of further running makes it
untrue. This is the strongest result any tool in this module produces at this price.

**A pass is not the negation of that.** "128,000 calls, no violation" is a statement about the states
the campaign reached. It is evidence, it is real, and it is bounded by exactly one thing: where the
generator happened to go.

So the two outcomes are not symmetric verdicts. One is a discovery and the other is a coverage report
wearing a verdict's clothing. Every downstream mistake in this category comes from reading the second
as though it were the first.

### Why the counterexample is usable at all

Look at the failing output once more, at the part that is easy to skim past: `(original: 1, shrunk: 1)`.

A generator that finds a violation finds it inside whatever sequence it happened to be running, which
on a longer campaign is routinely dozens of calls with 256-bit arguments in them. Handed to you raw,
that is not a finding — it is a log, and turning it into a reproduction is an afternoon. So the runner
minimises: it repeatedly drops calls and reduces arguments, re-checking that the property still breaks,
until removing anything more makes the failure disappear.

The output above happened to find its violation on the first call, so there was nothing to remove. The
mechanism is what matters, because it is what makes this tier's output *actionable* rather than merely
true. `donate(101)` is a sentence you can put in a finding, hand to an engineer, and paste into a
regression test. A forty-call sequence saying the same thing is a sentence nobody reads.

The distinction generalises. A tool that tells you a property can break has given you a fact. A tool
that hands you the smallest witness has given you a fix. The [shrinking](/c/shrinking) machinery is
where a large part of a fuzzer's practical value lives, and it is invisible in every feature
table.<Cite src="crytic-building-secure-contracts" />

## The number to read is not pass or fail

Look at the passing run again, at the row for `withdraw`.

Forty-two thousand five hundred and seventy-six calls. Forty-two thousand four hundred and
ninety-five reverts. **Eighty-one calls to `withdraw` actually
executed**<Cite src="measured-static-analysis-2026-08-26" /> — 0.19 percent of the budget assigned to
that function, and 0.06 percent of the campaign.

The reason is not mysterious. `withdraw` requires the caller to hold shares, and the generator picks
senders and amounts from the whole 256-bit space, so almost every attempt bounces off a `require` at
the door. The campaign spent six seconds proving that unauthorised withdrawals revert, which nobody
was asking about.

That is the real reading of a fuzz report, and it is a habit worth building now:

- **Reverts per selector**, not reverts in total. A single door-bouncing function will dominate the
  aggregate and hide the ones that are working.
- **Which selectors got real calls**, because a function with no successful calls was not tested,
  regardless of what the verdict line says.
- **Coverage**, if the runner reports it, because that is the only number that answers "did we reach
  anywhere new".

The fix belongs to the fuzzing module and has a name there: a [handler contract](/c/handler-contract)
that sets up plausible state and clamps arguments to reachable ranges, so the budget is spent inside
the protocol rather than at its front door. The failure mode also has a name —
[revert blindness](/c/revert-blindness) — and this report is what it looks like from the outside.

## Property quality dominates tool choice

The second run is the counterweight. It used the same generator with the same defaults and found a
real defect in twenty-eight milliseconds, because the sentence was sharper.

Line the two properties up.

**"If shares exist, assets exist."** True of the contract, and nearly unfalsifiable — it holds in every
state the contract can reach, including all the broken ones. It is the kind of property that gets
written when the author is trying to write *a* property rather than *the* property.

**"If no shares exist, no assets exist."** A statement about value with no owner. Falsified
immediately by a single donation into an empty pool, which is the same shape as the
[first-depositor inflation surface](/c/inflation-first-depositor-attack) you met in Track 04: assets
arrive without shares being minted, and the next depositor pays for them.

The tool did not get better between those two runs. The specification did. This is why the fuzzing
module opens on properties rather than on tools, and it is why the ordering in this module's title —
tools, plural, in a stack — is deliberately not the order in which you should spend your first day.

## Echidna, and the mode nothing else has

Foundry's built-in runner is where most people start and where many stay. The two dedicated fuzzers
earn their place by doing things it does not.

Echidna is Trail of Bits' property-based fuzzer for EVM contracts, written in Haskell, and it is the
original — the design paper predates most of the current toolchain.<Cite src="crytic-echidna" /><Cite src="echidna-issta-2020" />
It supports several test modes — property, assertion, optimization, overflow and
exploration<Cite src="crytic-echidna" /> — and the list is worth reading as five different questions
rather than five configuration values.

*Property* mode asks whether a boolean-returning function ever returns false, with state reverted
between checks. *Assertion* mode asks whether any assertion inside the contract can be violated during
a sequence, which is the mode that composes with the assertions you already write. *Overflow* mode
targets arithmetic specifically. *Exploration* mode asks nothing at all — it drives the contract purely
to accumulate coverage, which is how you build a corpus before you have decided what to
assert.<Cite src="crytic-echidna" /> And *optimization* mode is the one below.

Echidna also runs against forked chain state through an RPC endpoint, which is what makes it usable
against a protocol you did not write.<Cite src="tob-onchain-echidna" />

**Optimization mode is the one worth changing how you think.** Every other mode falsifies a boolean:
the property held, or here is a sequence where it did not. Optimization mode maximises a numeric
value.<Cite src="crytic-echidna" />

The difference is not a convenience. Consider the question you actually want answered about a lending
market with a manipulable price source. "Can an attacker extract profit?" is a boolean, and the answer
is usually yes for some absurd sequence, which tells you little. "What is the largest profit an
attacker can extract, and what sequence achieves it?" is a number and a witness — and a number is
something a product decision can be made against. A maximum of eleven wei is a rounding artefact. A
maximum of four hundred thousand dollars is a Tuesday.

That reframing is the same one this track keeps returning to. Security questions phrased as booleans
produce answers you cannot act on; the same questions phrased as quantities produce a threshold you can
argue about.

## Medusa, and the honest comparison

Medusa is the other fuzzer from the same maintainers: written in Go, built on geth, with parallel
workers, coverage-guided input generation, mutational value generation informed by static analysis, and
an HTML coverage report. It supports both assertion and property
testing.<Cite src="crytic-medusa" /><Cite src="tob-unleashing-medusa" />

One item on that list is a callback worth noticing. **Value generation informed by static
analysis**<Cite src="tob-unleashing-medusa" /> means the fuzzer seeds its mutations with constants
harvested from the source by an analyser — the magic numbers, boundary values and addresses that appear
in the code. A generator drawing uniformly from the 256-bit space will essentially never produce the
one value a `require` compares against; a generator that has read the source produces it immediately.

That is the first tier feeding the third, and it is a concrete instance of the argument this module
closes on. The tiers are not four alternatives on a shelf. They share artefacts, and one of them
quietly makes another one work.

Read the rest of the feature list against the previous section and the two tools look like
straightforward rivals. They are not, and the reason is the most useful fact in this lesson.

**Trail of Bits maintain both.** Their launch write-up for Medusa reports an internal benchmark finding
that Medusa and Echidna perform similarly on coverage and corpus
size.<Cite src="tob-unleashing-medusa" />

Weigh that carefully in both directions. It is a vendor benchmark — but it is a vendor benchmark on
*both* of the tools compared, which removes the usual incentive to flatter one of them. And it is the
only benchmark there is: no independent, peer-reviewed comparison of the current fuzzers was
established during this module's research.<Cite src="hacken-audit-tools" /> Every side-by-side ranking
in circulation was written by someone who ships one of the tools.

So the honest teaching line is that the choice is **ergonomic rather than capability-driven**.
Parallelism and Go tooling pull one way; optimization mode, maturity and the forked-state workflow pull
the other. Which one fits your CI, your team's language comfort and your campaign length is a real
question. Which one finds more bugs is not a question anybody has answered.

## Working a campaign report

Here is the pass, applied to the passing run at the top.

**Subgoal: read the verdict last.** Start with the per-selector table, not the PASS line. The verdict
is a function of the property and the reached states, and you do not know either of those yet.

**Subgoal: find the functions that never executed.** `withdraw` succeeded eighty-one times out of
forty-two thousand.<Cite src="measured-static-analysis-2026-08-26" /> Anything that only breaks after a
withdrawal is outside this result. Write that sentence down before you write anything else, because it
is the scope of the claim you are about to make.

**Subgoal: ask what would have to be true for the property to fail, and check whether the campaign
could have got there.** For "if shares exist, assets exist", failure needs shares outstanding against
an empty pool — which needs a successful deposit followed by a successful full withdrawal that leaves
shares behind. With eighty-one successful withdrawals total, that sequence had almost no chance of
being drawn. The property was not tested. It was merely not falsified.

**Subgoal: sharpen the property before touching the tool.** The instinct after a boring pass is to run
longer or add a second fuzzer. Both cost more than rewriting the sentence, and the two runs above are
the evidence: the weak property consumed six and a half seconds to establish nothing, and the sharp one
produced a shrunk counterexample in twenty-eight milliseconds — roughly a two-hundredth of the
time.<Cite src="measured-static-analysis-2026-08-26" />

**Subgoal: record what a pass entitles you to say.** Not "solvency holds". Something closer to: "under
this handler, this property was not violated in 128,000 calls, of which fewer than a hundred exercised
withdrawal." That sentence is honest, it is useful in a review, and it names its own weakness — which is
what makes it useful.

<Check type="explain" concept="property-fuzzing">
Your team has an invariant suite that has been green in CI for four months. Someone proposes deleting
three of the eleven properties because "they have never caught anything".

Under what circumstances is that a good idea, under what circumstances is it exactly backwards, and
what one artefact would you ask to see before deciding?

<Answer>
**When it is a good idea.** When the property is trivially true — an identity the type system or the
compiler already guarantees, or a restatement of a `require` that the code cannot violate by
construction. Those cost campaign budget and produce no information, and a campaign's budget is
finite: every call spent on an unfalsifiable property is a call not spent somewhere reachable. Delete
them.

**When it is exactly backwards.** When the property is sharp and the campaign has never reached the
state that would break it. Those two situations produce an identical CI badge and opposite
conclusions. A property guarding the liquidation path, in a suite whose generator almost never
successfully liquidates, has never been tested — and deleting it removes the guard precisely where the
guard is needed. The first campaign in this lesson is that case: eighty-one successful withdrawals in
128,000 calls means the withdrawal properties were decorative, not
confirmed.<Cite src="measured-static-analysis-2026-08-26" />

**A third case, which is the one that should worry you most.** When the property is sharp, the state
*is* reached, and it genuinely holds. That property is doing the most valuable job in the suite —
holding a line nobody has crossed yet — and "never caught anything" is exactly what success looks like.
Judged by findings, a working guard rail and a decorative one are indistinguishable.

**The artefact to ask for.** Per-selector call and revert counts, plus a coverage report, for the three
properties in question. That single table separates all three cases: it tells you whether the functions
involved in each property are executing at all, whether the campaign is reaching new code or
re-deriving the same shallow region, and therefore whether four months of green means "held" or means
"never asked".<Cite src="tob-unleashing-medusa" />

If the table is not available, that is itself the answer: a suite whose coverage nobody can produce is
a suite whose green nobody can interpret, and the correct first action is to make the number visible
rather than to delete anything.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson removes the sampling.** Proving is what you do when "we did not find a
counterexample" stops being good enough — and the honest framing is that it starts from a property you
have already sharpened here, because a specification is expensive and you do not want to buy a proof of
the wrong sentence.

**The fuzzing module is where this becomes a craft.** Properties worth writing, handlers that reach
real state, [clamping](/c/input-clamping) that does not accidentally exclude the bug,
[coverage-guided](/c/coverage-guided-fuzzing) campaigns, [corpus reuse](/c/corpus-reuse) across runs,
and [fuzzing against forked state](/c/onchain-fuzzing). Everything in this lesson is the map; that
module is the terrain.

**And the asymmetry generalises past fuzzing.** A counterexample is a fact and an absence is a
coverage statement — that pattern covers monitoring alerts that have never fired, incident runbooks
that have never been executed, and audit findings that came back empty. In each case the useful
question is the same one the table above answers: was the thing reached, or merely not falsified?
