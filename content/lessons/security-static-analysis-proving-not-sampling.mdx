Two sentences from two different pull requests.

> "We ran a hundred and twenty-eight thousand calls against this property and none of them broke it."

> "No sequence of calls breaks this property."

The first is a measurement and you now know exactly how to read it. The second is a different kind of
claim — a statement quantified over everything, not over a sample — and the interesting question is not
whether a tool can produce it. Tools produce it routinely. The question is what "everything" turned out
to mean once you read the configuration that produced it.

That gap is the entire content of this lesson, and it is why the sentence you should be suspicious of
is not "we did not find a bug" but "this contract is formally verified".

## The machinery, in one pass

Proving works the way the previous-but-one lesson worked, with the search budget removed and a
specification bolted on.

The Certora Prover takes a contract's **compiled bytecode** together with rules written in its own
specification language, and compiles the pair into a mathematical formula that an SMT solver
discharges.<Cite src="certora-gentle-intro" /> Three details in that sentence carry weight.

**Bytecode, not source.** What gets reasoned about is the artefact that ships. If you worked through
[the compiler in your trusted computing base](/c/compiler-is-part-of-tcb) in Track 04, you already know
why that distinction is not academic: a proof about source is a proof about a document, and the chain
executes something a compiler produced from it.

**A separate specification language.** Rules are written in CVL, not in Solidity, and that is a real
cost — a language to learn, a file to maintain, and a second artefact that can drift from the code it
describes.<Cite src="certora-gentle-intro" /> It is also what buys the capabilities in the next
section, which Solidity cannot express.

**A solver at the end.** Which means the same three outcomes as before: proved, counterexample, or the
solver did not finish. Nothing about calling the category "formal verification" removes the third.

## What "unbounded" actually adds

The bounded tool you met earlier verifies up to the loop and call-depth limits you supply, and says
nothing past them.<Cite src="a16z-halmos-repo" /> The Prover supports reasoning that is not capped that
way, through two constructs worth naming.

**Invariants.** A statement that holds after the constructor and is preserved by every method. Prove
those two things and you have a claim about every reachable state, over any number of transactions —
without unrolling anything, because induction did the work a bound would otherwise have to
do.<Cite src="certora-gentle-intro" />

**[Parametric rules](/c/parametric-rule).** A rule quantified over *all* public methods rather than
written against one. The practical consequence is the one that matters on a real codebase: when someone
adds a function next quarter, the rule covers it automatically, and if the new function breaks the
property the proof fails in CI rather than in production.<Cite src="certora-gentle-intro" />

That second one is the strongest argument for the category, and it is rarely the one people make.
Fuzzing and bounded checking cover the functions you pointed them at. A parametric rule covers the
function that does not exist yet. On a protocol that ships every month, the difference compounds.

## What the green checkmark excludes

Here is where the claim shrinks, and every item on this list is a thing you go looking for in a report
rather than a thing the report volunteers.

**A specific property.** A proof covers the rules that were written. A protocol with four rules has
four proofs, and everything nobody wrote a rule about is untouched. "The contract is verified" is a
category error: contracts are not the unit that gets proved, properties are.

**Under a specific configuration.** Which contracts were in scope, which were modelled as arbitrary,
what the solver was allowed to assume about the environment.

**With specific functions replaced by approximations.** [Summarization](/c/summarization) replaces a
called function with a simpler stand-in so the formula stays tractable. It is often necessary and it is
always a hole: a bug living inside a summarized function is invisible, and the proof around it still
passes.

**With specific loops bounded**, and with [optimistic assumptions](/c/optimistic-assumption) switched
on where the run would otherwise not terminate — each of which converts an unproved condition into an
assumed one.

You met the miniature version of this list in the compiler's own checker two lessons ago: an engine
that defaults to off, external calls modelled as untrusted by default, partial invariant inference, and
a timeout.<Cite src="measured-static-analysis-2026-08-26" /><Cite src="solidity-smtchecker-docs" /> The
Prover's version of that list is longer and worth considerably more money, and it lives in the
assumptions section of the report.

### Where the holes come from is not random

The list above reads like a set of choices someone made carelessly. It is nearly the opposite, and the
mechanism is worth carrying because it tells you where to look.

Nobody summarizes a function or enables an optimistic assumption for fun. They do it because the run
**did not terminate**. The third outcome is the forcing function: the solver times out, the engineer
approximates something to shrink the formula, and the run goes green. Which means the approximations
cluster exactly where the reasoning was hardest — the intricate arithmetic, the deep call chains, the
loops with data-dependent trip counts.

Those are also the places bugs live. So the holes in a proof are not distributed uniformly across the
codebase; they are concentrated in its most difficult parts, by construction. A report with no
timeouts and no summarizations is describing a simple system. A report on a complex system with neither
is describing a run nobody looked at closely.

<Aside kind="stop">
The transferable skill from this whole module is one habit: **read a verification report's assumptions
section before its results section.** The results tell you what someone chose to prove. The assumptions
tell you what they had to give away to prove it, and that is where the residual risk sits.

A report with no assumptions section is not a report with no assumptions.
</Aside>

## What changed, and what most tutorials still say

This is the fastest-moving corner of the assurance stack, and two pieces of received wisdom about it
are now wrong.

**"Formal verification means buying a licence and emailing a vendor engineer."** The Prover's source is
published — a public repository under GPL-3.0.<Cite src="certora-prover-repo" /> That does not make the
expertise free or the compute free, but the older teaching that the tool itself is inaccessible no
longer describes the situation.

**"Formal verification is an EVM thing."** The project's own description now covers EVM chains, Solana
and Stellar,<Cite src="certora-prover-repo" /> with a Rust specification language for Solana
programs.<Cite src="certora-cvlr-solana" /> If you go on to the alternative-VM track, the tooling
crosses with you.

On versions, one number is safe to state because it comes from an official changelog rather than from
a release page: the Prover's release notes list **8.16.1, dated 15 June 2026**, as the most recent
entry, with 8.13.0 in May and 8.11.3 in April of the same
year.<Cite src="certora-prover-changelog" /> That cadence — three releases in three months — is itself
the useful fact. It tells you the tool is under active investment, and it tells you that any behaviour
you learn from a tutorial has a shelf life. Take version claims from the changelog, not from a blog
post.

## The order to do this in

The most common framing error in this area is treating fuzzing and proving as competing purchases. They
are not, and the reason is mechanical rather than diplomatic.

**Fuzzing is cheap at finding counterexamples and says nothing about their absence.** Proving is
expensive and says exactly that. But writing a specification is the expensive part of proving, and
writing a *wrong* specification is the expensive way to waste it. Fuzz first: a campaign will falsify a
badly-stated property in seconds, which is when correcting it costs nothing. Then prove the property
once it has survived that.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" />

There is a second reason for that order, and it is the one that decides budgets. The artefact you write
is the same artefact in both cases. The property that drove the fuzz campaign becomes the rule you
prove, and later the condition your monitoring alerts on. That is the whole argument behind
invariant-driven development: one statement, three consumers, written
once.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" /> A team that has never written the statement down
is not choosing between tools; it has nothing to feed any of them.

### What it costs, stated honestly

No public study quantifies specification effort across these tools, and any hours-and-days figure you
read — including in vendor material — is practitioner folklore rather than
measurement.<Cite src="hacken-audit-tools" /> So this module gives you the shape rather than numbers.

The gradient runs: reusing an existing Foundry suite symbolically is the cheapest entry into the
category and the highest ratio of insight to effort;<Cite src="a16z-halmos-post" /> specifying a
focused component — a math library, a token, an access-control matrix — in a dedicated specification
language is meaningfully more; and specifying a full protocol carries an ongoing maintenance cost every
time the code changes, which is the real reason it is bought rather than adopted.

The decision that follows is not "can we afford a prover". It is **which properties are stable enough
and valuable enough to justify a specification that must be maintained forever**. Properties about
arithmetic and accounting age well. Properties about business rules change with the product, and a
specification that changes with the product is a second codebase.

## Where a proof cannot reach

Even a perfect, unbounded, unsummarized proof of every property you wrote leaves a set of things
untouched, and they are not edge cases — they are the categories the loss data puts at the top.

**Whether the property was the right one.** A proof is a claim about a sentence you wrote. If the
sentence encodes a misunderstanding of the product, the proof certifies the misunderstanding.

**Everything outside the contract.** Oracles feeding it, governance changing it, the keys that hold its
admin roles, the signing path an operator uses, the front end that constructs calldata. None of that is
in the formula. This is the [scope boundary](/c/fv-scope-boundary) the formal-verification module makes
explicit, and it is where a large share of real incidents happen.

**The gap between the verified configuration and the deployed one.** A proof against one set of
constructor parameters, one token, one chain. Deploy the same bytecode with different parameters and
you have redeployed the code without redeploying the proof.

<Answer>
**1. Which properties were proved — the actual rule list, not a summary.** A proof covers rules, not
contracts.<Cite src="certora-gentle-intro" /> A bad answer here — "the accounting is correct" with no
enumerable list — means nobody on their side can tell you what was proved either, and everything below
is unanswerable.

**2. Against which commit, and is it the deployed one?** The Prover reasons about compiled
bytecode,<Cite src="certora-gentle-intro" /> so this is checkable rather than a matter of trust: the
verified artefact either matches what is on chain or it does not. A bad answer means the proof is about
a codebase, and you are exposed to a deployment.

**3. Which functions were summarized?** This is the highest-yield question in the list and the one
least often asked. A summarized function is a hole with a passing proof drawn around
it.<Cite src="certora-gentle-intro" /> A bad answer — an unwillingness to name them — tells you either
that the report has no assumptions section or that nobody has read it.

**4. Which loops were bounded, and were any optimistic assumptions enabled?** Both convert an unproved
condition into an assumed one. Enabling them is routine and sometimes unavoidable; leaving them out of
the summary is the problem.

**5. Which conditions came back unresolved?** The third outcome. A run with timeouts has holes, and a
summary that reports only proved-versus-violated has thrown that information
away.<Cite src="symbolic-execution-survey-2025" />

**6. What is outside the scope entirely?** Oracles, upgrade admin, key custody, the pause mechanism,
the off-chain keeper. Ask directly, because the answer will not be volunteered, and this is where the
loss categories that actually dominate live.

**7. What happens to the proof when they ship next month?** If the specification is parametric over all
public methods, a new function is covered automatically and a breaking one fails the
build.<Cite src="certora-gentle-intro" /> If the rules are written per-function, the proof describes a
snapshot and decays from the day it was produced.

**The one bad answer that should change your integration, not just your notes.** Question three. A team
that cannot tell you which functions were summarized is telling you they have read the checkmark and
not the report — and the difference between those two documents is the entire subject of this lesson.

## Where this pays off

**The last lesson in this module puts the four tiers together** and argues that the sequencing decision
is a scoping decision: where you spend the expensive tool is a claim about where the risk is, and that
claim comes from your threat model rather than from a tool comparison.

**The formal-verification module takes each item in the exclusion list and makes it a lesson.**
[Bounded versus unbounded](/c/bounded-verification), the two ways to write a specification,
summarization, optimistic assumptions, timeouts, and how to read a verification appendix for the things
it does not say. This lesson is the map of that territory.

**And the reading habit transfers immediately.** Assumptions before results. Applied to an audit
report, it becomes scope and commit before findings. Applied to a benchmark, it becomes methodology
before numbers. Applied to your own work, it becomes the sentence you attach to a green CI badge — the
one that says what the green actually covered.
