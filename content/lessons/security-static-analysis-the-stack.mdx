"Which one should we buy?" is the question teams ask at the end of a module like this one, and it is
the wrong unit.

Four tiers have now been on the table, and each of them answers a question the others structurally
cannot. A review that runs one of them has not chosen a tool; it has chosen a question, and left the
other three unasked.

The cost column is an ordering, not a measurement. No public study quantifies authoring effort across
these tiers, and any hours-and-days figure you meet — including in vendor material — is practitioner
folklore.<Cite src="hacken-audit-tools" />

Read the last column downward. Every entry is the reason the next row exists. That is the argument for
a stack, and it is why professional review practice sequences all four rather than ranking
them.<Cite src="hacken-audit-tools" /><Cite src="crytic-building-secure-contracts" />

## Why the order is the order

The sequencing is not simply cheap-to-expensive, although it is also that. Each tier produces something
the next one needs.

**Static analysis orients you.** On an unfamiliar codebase the printers matter more than the detectors:
an enumerated list of state-changing entry points, and a table of what each one writes against what
authorisation it performs. That table is the input to everything else, because it tells you which
functions are worth a property at all.

**Symbolic testing goes at the arithmetic.** Rounding direction, share pricing, bit packing, encoding
round trips — small, dense functions where one solver query covers an entire class of inputs and a
counterexample names a boundary rather than a number.

**Fuzzing goes at the accounting.** Solvency, conservation, path independence: properties that only
break across sequences of calls, which is precisely the terrain symbolic tools handle worst.

**Proving goes at the short list.** The properties that survived a fuzz campaign, that are stable
enough to be worth a specification you will maintain, and valuable enough to justify
it.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" />

And the artefact travels. The sentence you write as [an invariant](/c/invariant-as-spec) becomes the
fuzz property, then the rule you prove, then the condition your monitoring alerts
on.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" />
A team that has never written that sentence down is not choosing between tools; it has nothing to feed
any of them.

## What all four are blind to at once

Stack every tier and run each to exhaustion, and one set of things is still untouched — the same set,
every time, because it is not in the code.

- **Whether the property was the right one.** All four consume a claim someone wrote. None of them
  audits the claim.
- **Authorisation intent.** "Should this be callable by anyone" is a fact about the product.
- **Oracles, governance, key custody, the signing path, the off-chain keeper.** Outside the contract,
  therefore outside every model — the [scope boundary](/c/fv-scope-boundary) that no tier crosses.
- **The gap between the reviewed artefact and the deployed one.** Different commit, different
  constructor parameters, different chain.

Now put that list next to the loss data, with the inclusion criteria attached, because the two
available counts disagree about what to count. Among smart-contract *vulnerability* categories, the
systematic review of severe real-world attacks ranks **access control** first by value lost, with
logic errors next,<Cite src="sok-1b-loss" /> and the 2026 OWASP Smart Contract Top 10, built from the
previous year's incidents over the same kind of population, leads with access control and puts
business logic second.<Cite src="owasp-sc-top-10" /> Widen the frame to every documented incident,
including operational compromise that never touched a contract bug, and stolen keys and phishing both
rank above access control — the disagreement is about scope, not about the
arithmetic.<Cite src="arxiv-audit-gap-2026" /> Either way the gap between access control and
reentrancy survives, and either way the leading category is one no tier in the table reaches.

The two categories that lead that ranking are the two categories the entire stack is blind to. That
is not an argument against the stack — the stack clears the mechanical families cheaply, which is
exactly what you want it doing. It is an argument that **choosing a stack is a scoping decision, not a
procurement one**: the expensive tier goes where your threat model says the risk is, and if your threat
model says the risk is an admin key, no tier in the table helps and the money belongs somewhere else
entirely.

## The budget is attention

Every finding you put in front of a reviewer is drawn against a budget, and the budget is not compute.
The audit-craft module gives this its own name — [the cost of a false positive](/c/false-positive-cost)
— and it is the economic claim underneath every design decision in this module.

Analysis is close to free — seconds per contract, a few dollars of CI. Reading is not. A reviewer who
spends the first hour of an engagement dispositioning informational lints has spent the hour of the
engagement when they were most likely to notice something structural. This is the argument behind an
analyser that collapses its severity scale to two buckets,<Cite src="cyfrin-audit-tools" /> and it is
the argument for reading a hundred-detector suite through its impact and confidence filters rather than
top to bottom.

Three practices follow, and they are cheap.

**Every finding gets a written disposition.** Fixed, false positive, accepted risk, or out of scope —
plus one sentence of reason. A finding without a disposition is one you will re-decide from scratch
every run, which is how twenty lines of output consume attention forever.

**Suppressions are code.** Inline lint directives and config exclusions are supported by the
toolchain,<Cite src="foundry-lint-docs" /> which means they are reviewable, greppable and datable. An
undocumented suppression is a decision with no author.

**Re-examine the suppression list on a schedule.** The code moves under a suppression, and the reason
that justified it in March can stop being true in September without anything in the diff looking like
it changed.

There is a second-order effect worth naming, because it is how teams lose the tool entirely. A finding
that is dismissed without a record trains the reader to skim, and a reader who skims stops
distinguishing the informational tier from the high-impact one. After a few months of that, the tool is
still in CI, still passing, and nobody has read its output in weeks — which is functionally identical
to not running it, except that it appears on the assurance slide. Attention is not only scarce; it is
degradable, and undispositioned findings are what degrades it.

## Wiring it into CI without turning triage into theatre

The failure mode here is specific: gate the build on a triage tool's exit code, and within two sprints
the team's relationship with that tool is suppression rather than reading.

Start from what the exit codes actually do. On forge 1.7.1, `forge lint` exits **0** with a warning
present; `forge lint --deny warnings` exits **1**.<Cite src="measured-static-analysis-2026-08-26" /><Cite src="foundry-lint-docs" />
Both behaviours are correct and they encode different policies, so choose deliberately rather than by
default.

A workable division:

**Gate on the deterministic tiers.** Compilation, unit tests, high-impact and high-confidence detector
hits, and any property proof that used to pass. These are binary, they do not drift, and a failure is
unambiguous.

**Report the rest, and hold it open.** Everything else belongs in a durable record rather than in a
build log. SARIF is the standard for exactly this,<Cite src="sarif-oasis-v210" /> and Slither's
GitHub Action uploads to code scanning,<Cite src="slither-action" /> so a finding survives the run,
deduplicates across runs, and stays visible until someone dispositions it. A build log scrolls away;
a code-scanning entry does not.

**Budget the non-deterministic tiers separately.** A fuzz campaign on every pull request is a flaky
test; a long campaign on a schedule, with its corpus persisted, is a tool. The number CI should publish
from it is coverage, not the verdict.

Two mechanical details, because both cost an afternoon when discovered late. Machine-readable test
results come from `forge test --junit`, and forge **rejects `--junit` together with
`--gas-report`**<Cite src="measured-static-analysis-2026-08-26" /> — so a job that wants both needs two
invocations. And `forge test --json` still emits plain-text warning lines to stdout ahead of the JSON
object, which breaks any consumer that pipes stdout straight into a
parser.<Cite src="measured-static-analysis-2026-08-26" /><Cite src="foundry-issue-3001" /> JUnit is
the reliable path.<Cite src="foundry-ci" />

<Aside kind="warn">
One more habit from this module, applied to CI. The Foundry documentation indexes ninety-one lints;
forge 1.7.1 recognises twenty-three of them.<Cite src="measured-static-analysis-2026-08-26" /> A
pipeline built from the documentation therefore believes it is enforcing rules its binary has never
heard of, and the pipeline is green either way. Whatever you gate on, confirm the gate exists in the
version CI actually installs — a pinned toolchain and one probing command.
</Aside>

<Check type="explain" concept="tool-stack-not-tool">
You have four weeks and one engineer before a lending protocol ships. The team has a Foundry suite with
reasonable unit coverage, no invariants, no analyser in CI, and a budget that would cover roughly one
week of a specification engineer's time.

Allocate the four weeks and defend the allocation. What are you deliberately not doing?

<Answer>
**Days 1–2: orient, and enumerate.** Run two analysers over the codebase and read the printers before
the detectors: entry points, and the table of state written against authorisation
performed.<Cite src="crytic-building-secure-contracts" /> Disposition every finding in writing. The
deliverable is not the findings — it is the entry-point table with the empty authorisation cells
circled, because that is where the costliest smart-contract vulnerability category
lives<Cite src="sok-1b-loss" /> and no tool will raise it.

**Days 3–5: write the properties, before touching another tool.** Six to ten sentences about what must
always be true: solvency, conservation, monotonicity of the share price, who may change what. This is
the expensive artefact and everything downstream consumes
it.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" /> A team that skips this step spends weeks two through
four running tools against nothing.

**Week 2: fuzz, and read coverage rather than the verdict.** Build a handler that reaches real state,
run campaigns, and check per-selector call and revert counts before believing any pass. Expect the
first campaigns to be revert-blind and expect fixing that to be most of the week.

**Week 3: symbolic testing on the arithmetic, plus the compiler's own checker.** Point it at share
pricing and rounding — small dense functions where a solver covers an input class and returns a
boundary. The compiler's model checker costs a flag and produces results the same
day.<Cite src="measured-static-analysis-2026-08-26" />

**Week 4: harden the pipeline and write the honest summary.** Gate on the deterministic tiers, push the
rest to a durable record,<Cite src="slither-action" /> schedule the long campaigns, and write down what
each result entitles anyone to claim.

**The specification-engineer week: spend it, but not on a proof.** One week buys a specification of one
component, or it buys a second reviewer looking at the authorisation table and the oracle path with
fresh eyes. Before launch, on a codebase with no invariants three weeks ago, the second is worth more:
a proof of one component is a narrow claim<Cite src="certora-gentle-intro" /> and the plausible failure
here is not in that component.

**What I am deliberately not doing, and why.**

*Not proving anything.* The properties are three weeks old. Specifying an unstable property is the
expensive way to prove the wrong sentence, and the working order is fuzz until the property stops being
wrong.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" />

*Not gating the build on analyser output.* Four weeks before launch, that produces suppressions, not
fixes.

*Not shipping an on-chain fuzzing campaign against forked state.* Real capability, wrong week — it
belongs after the protocol has state worth forking.

*Not chasing the informational tier at all.* Naming conventions and unused imports on a codebase about
to hold user funds are attention spent in the wrong place, and attention is the constraint the whole
plan is built around.<Cite src="cyfrin-audit-tools" />
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next three modules are this table, one row at a time.** Fuzzing becomes a craft of properties,
handlers and campaign convergence. Formal verification becomes the discipline of reading a report's
assumptions before its results. Audit craft becomes the question of what an engagement actually bought.

**And the closing claim is the one that survives every tool release.** No single tool has adequate
coverage, the sequencing is a scoping decision, and the resource you are spending is reviewer attention
rather than compute. Tool names in this area age in months. That sentence has not needed revising in
years, and it is the one to carry out of this module.
