Open the documentation of almost any protocol and find the section headed "Roles". It reads like this:

> **Owner** — can update protocol parameters and upgrade contracts.
> **Keeper** — can call `harvest` once per epoch.
> **User** — can deposit and withdraw.

Three rows, complete, unambiguous. It came almost free: someone read the modifiers and wrote them
down. That is exactly the problem.

That table answers "who is allowed to do what". A threat model needs the answer to a different
question: **what can each party cause to happen, alone or in combination, and how fast.** Those are
not the same question, and the second one is not derivable from the modifiers, which is why the first
feels finished and is not.

## Enumerate before you analyse

The order matters and is easy to get wrong. Trail of Bits' methodology names actor analysis as its own
step, before data-flow analysis and before scenario development: identify all actors interacting with
the system, including legitimate users, administrators, and potential attackers, in order to
understand who can access what and what privileges they have.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" />

The reason to make it a separate step, with its own deliverable, is that **analysis started from an
incomplete list silently scopes out whole attacks.** If a relayer is not on the list, no scenario
involving a relayer will be written, and nothing in the process will notice. There is no error
message for an actor you forgot. This is why the Rekt Test's very first question is whether you have
all actors, roles, and privileges documented, and why it asks for the document rather than for the
knowledge.<Cite src="tob-rekt-test" />

A starting list for anything deployed on a public chain, to be pruned rather than extended:

- **Users** — anyone who can call an unpermissioned function, with whatever capital they can borrow
  for one transaction.
- **Administrators** — whoever holds upgrade, parameter, pause, treasury or role-granting powers.
  Usually several distinct parties wearing one name.
- **Keepers and bots** — parties you pay to call something on a schedule, holding a hot key by
  construction.
- **Oracle publishers** — whoever signs or posts the numbers your accounting trusts.
- **Relayers, sequencers and bridge verifiers** — anyone who conveys a message your contracts treat
  as authorisation.
- **Block builders, proposers and searchers** — parties who choose ordering and inclusion, and can
  compose your transaction with others.
- **Integrating protocols** — anyone who reads your state or holds your token, and everyone whose
  state you read.
- **Your own supply chain** — the compiler, the libraries, the CI runner, the deploy script.
- **The attacker who compromises any of the above** — not a separate actor so much as an operation
  applied to every actor in the list.

The last entry is the one that turns a list into a threat model. Every other row is a party you have a
relationship with; that row asks what happens when the relationship is with someone else.

## Capability is a role plus its speed, scope and reversibility

Now convert the list. A **capability** is a state change an actor can cause, described precisely enough
to be argued with. Compare:

| Role statement | Capability statement |
|---|---|
| The multisig is the owner. | 3-of-5 signers can replace the implementation with arbitrary code in one transaction, with no delay and no cancellation path. |
| The keeper calls `harvest`. | A single hot key can choose the reward figure credited to the vault, once per block, bounded only by a deviation check against the previous value. |
| Governance sets risk parameters. | Any holder of a simple majority of a token borrowable in size can set the collateral factor to 100% within one voting period. |

The right-hand column can be wrong. The left-hand column cannot, because it does not say anything.
That is the practical difference: **capability statements are falsifiable, role statements are
labels.**

Three attributes turn a role into a capability, and all three are things you have to go and find out:

**Scope.** What state can change, and by how much? "Can set the fee" is a role. "Can set the fee to any
`uint256`, including values that make withdrawals revert" is a capability, and it names a bug.

**Speed.** How long between decision and effect? This is the axis a timelock moves and nothing else
does. A capability exercisable in one transaction and the same capability behind a seven-day timelock
are different capabilities, because only one of them can be cancelled.

**Reversibility.** After it is exercised, what can be undone? An upgrade that changes storage meaning
is irreversible in the way that matters even if you can upgrade again, because
[layout migration](/c/layout-migration-hazard) has already reinterpreted the bytes.

## The maturity ladder is a capability ladder

Trail of Bits describes access-control maturity as four levels, and it is the clearest worked example
of capability thinking published anywhere, because each step changes what an actor *can cause* without
changing who the actor *is*.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" />

**Level 1 — a single EOA controller.** One key holds supreme authority over all administrative
functions. Depending on how often it must be used, or how quickly in an emergency, it may live in a
software wallet on an internet-connected machine. Once compromised, the attacker can upgrade
contracts, steal collateral and destroy the protocol.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" />

**Level 2 — a centralised multisig.** Powers move to an M-of-N wallet. Compromising one signer is no
longer enough. Trail of Bits is blunt that this reduces the *risk* of compromise without changing the
*scope of damage*, and names two residual problems: once the M-th signature is obtained a malicious
action executes immediately, leaving no time for a security response; and the control point is still
singular, so routine low-risk transactions require the same signing authority as a protocol
upgrade.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" /> The cited examples of that single control point
being taken are the February 2025 exchange incident, WazirX and Radiant
Capital.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" /><Cite src="halborn-radiant-capital" />

**Level 3 — timelocks and least privilege.** Two controls arrive together. A timelock creates a delay
between approval and execution, which manufactures a genuinely new capability for the defender: the
ability to cancel an approved transaction. Least privilege splits the single control point into at
least four roles — a core system role with a high threshold and a long delay for upgrades; an
operations role with medium threshold and delay for day-to-day configuration; a **pause guardian**
behind no timelock at all with a low threshold, so an emergency response is actually fast; and a
**cancel guardian** whose only job is to kill an unauthorised transaction sitting in the
timelock.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" /> Aave, Compound and Lido are given as
examples.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" />

The SCSVS asks for the pause half as a verification requirement, and adds a constraint worth carrying:
there must be a mechanism that can temporarily stop the contract's sensitive functionality in case of a
new attack, and **that mechanism should not block access to the assets for their
owners**.<Cite src="scsvs-v1-architecture" /> A pause that traps user funds has converted an attacker's
capability into your own.

The warning attached to Level 3 is the one to internalise: monitoring and alerting of the timelock is
critical, because without it the control is worthless — Trail of Bits cites the Beanstalk incident,
where a one-day timelock went unmonitored and the hack was
preventable.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" /> **A delay you do not watch is not a control, it
is a countdown.**

**Level 4 — remove the capability.** The endgame is eliminating the need for administrative action, so
that access control leaves the threat model as a category. Uniswap and Liquity are named as protocols
that strive for this while retaining extremely limited controls for fee and incentive distribution.
Trail of Bits is explicit that Level 4 is not "delegate control to a DAO" — a Level 4 protocol needs
no managed control to operate.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" /> The trade is real: no upgrade
path means no security patch, so the contracts must be simple, extensively verified, and adding an
asset means deploying a whole new independent instance.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" />

<Aside kind="note">
Notice what moves up that ladder. The actor list barely changes — there is still an administrator.
What changes is the capability: from "arbitrary code, instantly, unstoppably" to "arbitrary code, after
seven days, cancellable by a different party who is watching". Everything you can do about
key-compromise risk is a change to the right-hand column. This is also why "we use a multisig" answers
a question nobody asked; the useful sentence names the threshold, the delay, and who can cancel.
</Aside>

## Capability lives in configuration, not in the contract

One more failure mode, because it is the 2026 version of this lesson and it is not a coding mistake.

A protocol's *capability* and its *deployment's configuration* are different objects. LayerZero's
security stack lets an application configure a set of Decentralized Verifier Networks that must
independently verify a cross-chain message before it is delivered, with the required and optional
DVN sets chosen per application.<Cite src="layerzero-security-stack-dvns" /> The Kelp DAO rsETH
incident of April 2026 is analysed as a deployment that used a one-of-one verifier configuration on
that path, so a forged cross-chain message could release tokens with no corresponding burn on the
source chain — a loss reported in the range of roughly $290 to $292 million, with sources disagreeing
on the exact figure.<Cite src="blocksec-kelp-dao" />

The protocol supported N-of-M. The deployment used 1-of-1. **An actor-capability table derived from
the protocol's documentation would have recorded the wrong capability**, because the documentation
describes what the system can be configured to do. The table has to be built from the deployment: read
the actual on-chain configuration, not the design.

That generalises past bridges. A pause guardian who was never granted the role, a timelock whose delay
is set to zero on the testnet contracts you copied, a Safe with a threshold of one because the second
signer never completed setup — each is a capability that differs from the documented one, and each is
found by reading state rather than by reading code.

## The EIP-7702 correction, stated precisely

There is one capability change every threat model written before Pectra now gets wrong, and it is worth
being exact about, because the popular summary overcorrects.

The idiom is `require(msg.sender == tx.origin)`, and the belief attached to it is "the caller is not a
contract". EIP-7702 works through the three purposes that idiom served and reaches three different
verdicts.<Cite src="eip-7702" />

**Purpose 1 — ensuring `msg.sender` is an EOA. Not affected.** `tx.origin` must still be an account
capable of originating a transaction. EIP-3607 forbids transactions from senders that have deployed
code, and EIP-7702 relaxed that restriction only for accounts whose code is a valid delegation
designator; an account with any other code still cannot originate.<Cite src="eip-3607" /><Cite src="eip-7702" />
So the equality still proves the immediate caller is key-controlled.

**Purpose 2 — protecting against atomic sandwich attacks such as flash loans. Broken.** A delegated
account originates a transaction and then executes arbitrary code, so state can be arranged before and
after your call inside one transaction while the equality holds. The EIP notes this reliance was
already considered bad practice and could be circumvented by conditionally including transactions in a
block.<Cite src="eip-7702" />

**Purpose 3 — preventing reentrancy. Broken**, and the most severely affected of the three — though
the authors report finding no examples of this usage in their non-exhaustive
search.<Cite src="eip-7702" />

So the precise statement for your capability table is: **an EOA in your actor list may now execute
arbitrary code within its own transaction, while remaining `tx.origin`.** The check has not died; it
has stopped proving a second thing it was silently proving for a decade. Any inventory row that reads
"users cannot run code" is now false, and any row that reads "the caller is key-controlled" is still
true.

  belief="EIP-7702 killed the tx.origin check, so every occurrence of it is now a bug."
  reality="The EIP explicitly says purpose (1), ensuring msg.sender is an EOA, is not affected — EIP-3607 still bars code-bearing accounts other than delegated ones from originating transactions. Purposes (2) and (3), flash-loan protection and reentrancy prevention, are broken."
  why="One sentence is easier to remember than three verdicts, and the three verdicts point at different repairs."
  src="eip-7702"
/>

<Aside kind="warn">
This is testable rather than theoretical. Foundry's `signAndAttachDelegation` cheatcode attaches a
signed delegation to the next transaction, so a regression test can drive your guard from an account
that is simultaneously `tx.origin` and running attacker
code.<Cite src="foundry-cheatcode-sign-delegation" /> The module's break exercise is exactly this. Any
suite whose adversary is always a plain EOA cannot fail on a capability that only delegated accounts
have.
</Aside>

## The inventory: what you lose if this misbehaves

The actor-capability table has a twin, and it is the artifact the rest of the track consumes. A
**trust assumption inventory** lists every component or party assumed honest, available or correct,
and states what breaks downstream if it is not: oracle feeds, admin and upgrade keys, bridge
verifiers, relayers, sequencers, block builders, the compiler, the libraries, and the signing
interface.

The word doing damage is "trusted". It reads like an endorsement. It is not.

> **Trusted** does not mean *judged safe*. It means *there is no defence if this misbehaves*.

An inventory entry is therefore an exposure statement, not an approval. The purpose of writing it down
is to price it, and the format that forces pricing is three columns: the assumption, what falsifies it,
and what breaks when it is falsified. An entry with an empty third column is not finished.

<Misconception
  belief="Marking a component trusted means someone assessed it and concluded it is safe."
  reality="It records that the system has no defence if that component misbehaves. The label is a measure of exposure, not of assurance."
  why="In ordinary English trust is something you extend to the reliable, so the security sense reads backwards."
  src="tob-bybit-threat-model"
/>

The Rekt Test's second question — whether you keep documentation of all external services, contracts
and oracles you rely on — is this inventory asked as a yes-or-no, and its stated purpose is to let you
answer how you will know when an external dependency suffers a security
incident.<Cite src="tob-rekt-test" /> That question has an operational answer or it does not, and
finding out which is the entire value of the exercise.

## Building the matrix

Take the staking vault from the previous lesson and turn the two artifacts out properly.

**Subgoal: put actors on rows, capabilities on columns, and leave the cells blank at first.** Rows:
user, keeper, upgrade multisig signer, oracle publisher, CI runner, integrating lending market,
searcher. Columns: deposit, withdraw, set fee, replace implementation, choose harvest amount, pause,
grant roles, read share price.

**Subgoal: fill each cell with allowed, forbidden, or unclear — and treat unclear as the output.** The
allowed and forbidden cells restate the modifiers. The unclear cells are the findings. "Can a searcher
cause a withdrawal to execute at a share price of their choosing by ordering transactions around it?"
is an unclear cell, and it is worth more than the entire rest of the table.

**Subgoal: add a speed column and re-read the multisig row.** If "replace implementation" is
exercisable in one transaction, write that in the cell. The row now says something a reviewer can
disagree with, and disagreement is the point.

**Subgoal: apply the compromise operator to every row.** For each actor, ask what an attacker holding
its credentials can cause. The keeper row is instructive: `onlyKeeper` restricts *who* calls
`harvest`, and nothing restricts *what number* they pass. Under compromise, the keeper's capability is
not "calls a function on schedule" — it is "chooses an accounting input".

**Subgoal: write the assumption entries the matrix just exposed.** Each compromise answer that ends in
loss becomes an inventory row: assumption, falsifier, downstream breakage. The keeper row becomes:
*we assume the harvest figure is honest; it is falsified by hot-key theft or a compromised strategy
API; share price is inflated and every subsequent withdrawal is mispriced.*

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="capability-not-role">
A team presents this actor table for a lending market and asks you to sign off on it.

| Actor | Permission |
|---|---|
| Borrower | borrow, repay |
| Supplier | supply, withdraw |
| Liquidator | liquidate |
| Admin multisig (4-of-7) | listAsset, setCollateralFactor, setOracle, pause, upgrade |

Name at least four problems with the table as a threat-model artifact, and rewrite the admin row as
capability statements.

<Answer>
**Problem one: three actors are missing entirely.** The oracle publisher — whoever produces the
numbers `setOracle` points at — appears nowhere, despite being the party whose misbehaviour has the
largest and fastest financial effect. Block builders and searchers are absent, though liquidation is
an ordering-sensitive action. And the party that deploys and upgrades — the CI runner or the
deployment key — is not on the list even though the admin row's last entry depends on it.

**Problem two: no speed and no reversibility.** Is `upgrade` behind a timelock? Can anyone cancel it?
The table cannot say, so it cannot distinguish the Level 2 deployment from the Level 3
one.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" />

**Problem three: five capabilities of wildly different blast radius share one row.** `pause` and
`upgrade` in the same cell means the deployment probably has a single control point, which is the
defining property of Level 2. Least privilege would split them, and would put `pause` behind a lower
threshold and no delay precisely so that emergency response is
fast.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" />

**Problem four: it is the documented configuration, not the deployed one.** Nothing here was read from
chain. The threshold might be 4-of-7 in the design document and 1-of-1 in the deployed Safe. That gap
is the shape of the April 2026 verifier-configuration loss.<Cite src="blocksec-kelp-dao" />

**Problem five, and the one that turns this into a scenario:** the compromise operator has not been
applied. Nobody asked what a compromised admin signing session can cause, which is the single question
the last two years of loss data are about.

**The admin row, rewritten:**

- *Four of seven signers, in one transaction with no delay and no cancellation path, can replace all
  executing code, which is equivalent to draining every supplied asset.* Speed: one block.
  Reversibility: none.
- *The same four signers can repoint the oracle for any listed asset to an address of their choosing,*
  which makes every health check and every liquidation price attacker-chosen. Speed: one block.
- *The same four signers can set a collateral factor high enough to permit borrowing beyond backing.*
  Speed: one block, and the resulting bad debt is not recoverable by reverting the parameter.
- *Any single signer with a compromised signing interface can cause the other six to approve any of the
  above, because signers do not independently verify the payload.* This is not a permission and would
  never appear in a permissions table; it is a capability, and it is the one that has actually been
  exercised at scale.

**The test to carry away.** If a row of your table could not be wrong, it is not telling you anything.
Add scope, speed and reversibility until each row makes a claim somebody could refute by reading the
chain.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** takes the assumption inventory and attaches it to geography: every assumption sits
at a numbered crossing where a value moves between zones, and the scenario table is the join of the two
artifacts.

**The vulnerability-class module** opens with [access-control failure](/c/access-control-failure),
which OWASP ranks first by realised loss among smart-contract *vulnerability* categories, and which a
wider count that also scores stolen keys and phishing places third; both agree it costs far more than
reentrancy.<Cite src="owasp-sc-top-10" /><Cite src="arxiv-audit-gap-2026" /> It reads as a coding
topic and is not — most of those failures are a capability nobody wrote down. The same applies to
[upgrade risk](/c/upgrade-risk), which is this lesson's Level 2 with the consequences attached.

**Exploit archaeology** covers [governance capture](/c/governance-capture) and
[verifier quorum collapse](/c/verifier-quorum-collapse), both of which are capability-table failures
rather than code failures: a power that existed for good reasons, exercised by the wrong party or
configured below its design.

**Track 07's account work** is where the EIP-7702 correction stops being a caveat and becomes the
default assumption: callers run code, and the useful question about a caller is what it is authorised
to do rather than what kind of account it is.<Cite src="eip-7702" />

**Your own repository.** Take the "Roles" section of your README and add three columns — scope, speed,
reversibility — then fill them in from deployed state rather than from memory. The rows you cannot
fill in are the finding.
