Pick one number in your system and follow it.

Take the staking vault's reward figure. It starts as a value inside a strategy's off-chain accounting.
It is served over HTTPS. A keeper process reads it, scales it, and puts it in the calldata of a
`harvest` transaction. An RPC provider relays that transaction. The vault decodes it, multiplies it by
a share ratio, and writes the result to storage. A lending market reads that storage through a view
function and decides whether somebody's position is solvent.

That number crossed between distinct trust zones five or six times. At exactly one of those crossings
there is a `require`. At the rest there is a convention, a schema, or nothing.

This lesson is about finding those crossings on purpose, and about the two crossings that carry no
calldata at all — the one where a human decides to sign, and the one where an off-chain system decides
what to tell them.

## A crossing is a numbered place, not a feeling

Trail of Bits' fourth methodology step is data-flow analysis: map how data moves between components
and across trust boundaries, identifying where sensitive information might be exposed or manipulated
and which threat actors could do so. The fifth step then works crossing by crossing — for each trust
boundary crossing, analyse potential attack vectors and develop realistic threat
scenarios.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" />

OWASP describes the same artifact in general-IT terms: whatever notation you use, the model must give
a clear view of trust boundaries, data flows, data stores, processes and the external entities that
interact with the system, because these often represent possible attack points and provide crucial
input for the subsequent steps.<Cite src="owasp-threat-modeling-cheatsheet" />

The operational move that makes this a technique rather than a discussion is **numbering**. Give every
crossing an identifier — DF-1, DF-2, and so on — and the analysis becomes enumerable. Every scenario
you later write cites a number. Every number that no scenario cites is either genuinely safe or
unexamined, and you can tell which by looking. Without numbers you get a meeting; with numbers you get
a checklist whose completeness is visible.

At each crossing, ask exactly four questions:

1. **Who chooses this value?** Not "where does it come from" — who has write access to it, including
   under compromise.
2. **What is validated on arrival?** Type, range, freshness, signature, monotonicity — say which, and
   say "nothing" out loud when the answer is nothing.
3. **What is the worst admissible value?** Not the worst imaginable one; the worst that passes
   validation. This is the question that finds the bug.
4. **What happens if the producer is compromised?** Assume the honest party is now the attacker and
   read the consequence.

<Aside kind="note">
Question 3 is the one people skip, and it is the one that pays. "The oracle could be wrong" produces
nothing. "The oracle can return any `uint256` and the only check is that it is non-zero, so the worst
admissible value is `type(uint256).max`, which makes every position solvent" produces a finding, a
test and a mitigation in one sentence.
</Aside>

## Decoding is not validation, and you already know why

There is a specific reason engineers under-count crossings inside their own contracts, and it comes
from a true fact applied one step too far.

Solidity's ABI decoder does check things. It bounds-checks offsets, rejects malformed dynamic data,
and reverts on values that cannot inhabit their declared type — that is what
[decoding is validation](/c/abi-decode-validates) established. The mistake is concluding that a
decoded value has therefore been checked.

**Decoding validates shape. It never validates meaning.** A `uint256` that decodes cleanly is a
well-formed integer, not a plausible reward. An `address` that decodes cleanly is twenty bytes, not a
strategy you approved. Every crossing that carries a value whose *semantics* matter needs a second
check that the decoder cannot possibly perform, because the decoder has no idea what the number is
for.

This is also why the SCSVS phrases its architecture requirement as documenting trusted relations with
other contracts *and significant data flows*, rather than documenting function
signatures.<Cite src="scsvs-v1-architecture" /> The signature is the shape. The flow is the meaning.

## The crossings with no calldata

Now the half of the map that has no Solidity on either side. These are the crossings your compiler,
your fuzzer and your auditor cannot see, and they are where the last two years of losses happened.

**Developer workstations.** Whoever controls the machine controls what the engineer sees and signs.
This is a data flow: intent goes in, a payload comes out, and something in between renders one as the
other.

**Build and CI.** Source crosses into artifact, carrying the compiler, every dependency resolved at
build time, and every credential the runner holds. The compiler is *inside* your trusted computing
base, not beside it — [toolchain trust](/c/toolchain-trust) is an exploit class built on correct source
compiling to incorrect bytecode.

**The front end.** User intent crosses into a transaction. Everything the user believes about what
they are doing was rendered by code outside your audit scope, served from infrastructure that is not in
your threat model unless you put it there.

**The signing interface.** The narrow, decisive one. Covered in its own section below.

**RPC endpoints.** Every read your keeper, front end, monitor and deploy script performs crosses this.
The April 2026 rsETH incident is analysed as combining a forged cross-chain message with RPC
infrastructure poisoning, and the reported loss figures — roughly $290 to $292 million — vary by
source.<Cite src="blocksec-kelp-dao" /> An RPC provider that lies to your monitor is a provider that
has removed your ability to know anything.

**Key custody procedure.** Not a system at all — a sequence of human steps. It is still a data flow,
it still has a producer and a consumer, and it still fails.

## Blind signing: when the display is produced by the attacker

Here is the crossing that has cost the most money, stated precisely.

**Blind signing is any approval flow in which the signer's view of a transaction is produced by a
system that could itself be compromised, so the displayed intent and the signed payload can differ.**

It is a trust-boundary failure that is invisible to contract review by construction. The contracts
execute exactly what was signed; the signature is valid; every access check passes. The failure
happened one zone earlier, when a human formed an incorrect belief about the bytes in front of them.

Trail of Bits identifies two distinct issues stacked on that crossing in February 2025: malware that
modified the wallet interface, *and* the limitation of blind signing on hardware wallets, which do not
display complete semantic information on what is being signed by the
user.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" /> Read those as two separate controls failing. The first is
"the thing that told me what I am signing was lying". The second is "the device I trust could not have
told me either way".

  belief="A hardware wallet means I know what I am signing, because the payload never leaves the device unsigned."

  why="Key isolation and payload legibility are two different properties, and the device is excellent at the first, which produces confidence about the second."

### Why structured data was not enough

You already know EIP-712: it defines a hashing scheme for structured data with a domain separator
binding a chain id and a verifying contract, so that a signed message cannot be replayed across chains
or contracts and two distinct messages cannot hash alike.<Cite src="eip-712" />

It solves *ambiguity*. It does not solve *legibility*, and the distinction is the entire content of
this section.

ERC-7730 exists because of that gap, and its motivation says so directly: most data to sign, even
enriched with the data structure description such as ABIs or EIP-712 types, is not self-sufficient in
terms of correctly displaying it to users for review. The failures it enumerates are the ones a signer
actually hits — function names are developer-oriented and do not translate to clear user intent; fields
are tied to primitive types displayable in many ways, so an integer might be a percentage or a date;
and some fields need metadata that is nowhere in the ABI, such as the decimals and ticker required to
render a token amount.<Cite src="erc-7730" />

Its answer is a JSON file, written by the application developers who best understand how the contract
is used, enriching the ABI or the EIP-712 types with formatting and interpolation information so
wallets and simulation engines can build a meaningful display from raw data. It is Standards Track and
currently **Draft**, so treat it as the shape of the answer rather than as deployed
infrastructure.<Cite src="erc-7730" />

The teaching point survives whatever happens to that particular ERC: **an ABI is a description of
encoding, and rendering intent to a human requires information an encoding does not carry.** That is a
permanent property, not a tooling gap.

### The control that actually works is independence

The mitigation is structural rather than contractual. Trail of Bits' recommendation is a secondary
verification path: recompute the transaction hashes with a verification script, on a separate secure
workstation, and compare them byte-by-byte against what the hardware wallet
displays.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" />

The script they point at is worth reading as a threat-modeling artifact in its own right. It computes
a Safe transaction's EIP-712 domain and message hashes from transaction details retrieved from the
Safe transaction service API, and it ships an explicit trust assumption list — you trust the script,
Linux, Foundry, the Safe transaction service API, the hardware wallet's secure screen, and, in
simulation mode, your RPC provider's data. Two of those six carry a documented removal procedure: an
interactive mode in which you enter and independently verify the calldata yourself removes the
dependency on the transaction service, and running your own node removes the dependency on the RPC
provider.<Cite src="pcav-safe-tx-hashes" />

That is what a mature crossing looks like. Not "we verify transactions" — six named assumptions, two
of them with a stated price for removing them.

<Aside kind="warn">
The trap in secondary verification is **fake independence**. A second display fed by the same source
as the first is not a second opinion; it is the same opinion rendered twice. If your verification
script pulls the payload from the service the compromised front end also talks to, it detects a lying
front end and not a lying service — which is why the interactive mode exists and why the recommendation
specifies a *separate* workstation.<Cite src="pcav-safe-tx-hashes" /><Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" />
When you add a verification step, write down which of the two systems it makes redundant. If the answer
is "neither", you have added a step and no assurance.
</Aside>

<Aside kind="note">
One capability note carried from the previous lesson: post-Pectra, the account originating a
transaction may itself be executing delegated code, so "a person is at the keyboard" and "no code is
running in this frame" are no longer the same claim.<Cite src="eip-7702" /> That does not change the
blind-signing analysis, but it does mean an actor row reading "EOA, therefore inert" is stale wherever
it appears on your data-flow map.
</Aside>

## The value nobody validates because someone else already did

One more pattern before the worked example, because it is the most common crossing failure in
multi-party systems.

A message crosses a boundary carrying its own authorisation. LayerZero's security stack has
applications configure a set of Decentralized Verifier Networks that independently verify a message
before delivery, with the required and optional verifier sets chosen per
application.<Cite src="layerzero-security-stack-dvns" /> The consuming contract does not re-derive
truth; it checks that the configured verifiers signed off, and mints.

That is a perfectly reasonable design, and it means **the verifier configuration is the security
property**. The April 2026 rsETH analysis describes a deployment using a one-of-one verifier
configuration on that path, so a forged message could release tokens on the destination chain with no
corresponding burn on the source.<Cite src="blocksec-kelp-dao" />

Draw that as a crossing and the question answers itself. DF-n: a cross-chain message enters from the
verifier zone. Who chooses this value? Whoever the configured verifier set is — one machine, in this
deployment. What is validated on arrival? That the configured set signed. Worst admissible value? Any
message at all. Producer compromised? Unbacked supply.

None of those four answers requires reading a line of the bridge's Solidity. All four require reading
the deployed configuration.

## Numbering the vault

Run the technique end to end on the staking vault, with the zones from the first lesson already drawn.

**Subgoal: list crossings by walking values, not by walking functions.** Function-first enumeration
produces one crossing per `external` function and misses everything off-chain. Value-first gives:

**Subgoal: answer question 2 for each, and write "nothing" where it is true.** DF-1 has arithmetic
checks. DF-3 has `onlyKeeper` — which authenticates the *caller* and validates nothing about the
*value*. DF-2 has whatever the keeper process bothers to check, which in most real repositories is
JSON schema validation and a timeout. DF-5 has nothing on your side at all; it is the consumer's
problem, and the consumer assumes you would not publish a lie.

**Subgoal: answer question 3 where question 2 came back thin.** For DF-3: the worst admissible reward
is whatever passes `harvest`'s bounds. If there are no bounds, it is `type(uint256).max`, and share
price is now arbitrary — which makes DF-5 an arbitrary input to somebody else's solvency check. Two
crossings compose into a loss that neither one contains.

**Subgoal: notice which crossings have two producers.** DF-6 is the interesting one. Its nominal
producer is the signer's intent. Its effective producer is the signing interface, and they are only
the same thing while the interface is honest. Any crossing where the nominal and effective producers
differ under compromise is a blind-signing crossing, whatever it is called locally.

**Subgoal: check the map against the zone map for orphans.** Every zone from the first lesson should
touch at least one crossing. If the CI zone appears nowhere in your DF list, you drew a zone and then
forgot to ask what flows through it — which is exactly how deployment-pipeline attacks stay invisible.

<Check type="predict" concept="data-flow-crossing">
A protocol adds a "rebalance" feature. An off-chain optimiser computes target weights, an operator
reviews them in a dashboard, and a bot submits `setWeights(uint256[] calldata weights)` guarded by
`onlyOperator`. The contract checks that the array length matches the asset count and that the weights
sum to 10,000.

Enumerate the crossings this feature introduces, answer the four questions at the weakest one, and say
which of the checks in the contract is doing security work.

<Answer>
**The crossings.** At least four, and only one of them is in Solidity.

- **DF-a:** optimiser output into the dashboard. Producer: whoever runs the optimiser, plus whatever
  market data it consumes. Validated on arrival: nothing, typically — the dashboard renders what it is
  given.
- **DF-b:** dashboard into operator's belief. This is a blind-signing crossing wearing different
  clothes. The operator approves a set of numbers whose consequences are rendered by the same system
  that proposed them.
- **DF-c:** operator approval into the bot's transaction. Producer under compromise: whoever holds the
  operator key or the bot's hot key. In many deployments the operator's "approval" is a click, and the
  bot signs with its own key — meaning the human is not on the crossing at all.
- **DF-d:** calldata into `setWeights`. The only one with a `require`.

**Four questions at the weakest one — DF-b or DF-c depending on the deployment.** Who chooses the
value? The optimiser, in practice, because a reviewer confirming plausible-looking numbers is not an
independent source. What is validated on arrival? Nothing that the producer did not also compute.
Worst admissible value? Any weight vector summing to 10,000 — including one that concentrates
everything into a single asset chosen by the attacker. What if the producer is compromised? The
protocol rebalances into an attacker-selected position, legitimately, with a valid signature and a
passing access check.

**Which contract check is doing security work.** Neither of them is doing much, and saying so is the
finding.

The length check prevents a decode-shaped mistake. The decoder already rejects malformed arrays, so
this catches an honest length mismatch, not an attack.

The sum-to-10,000 check reads like an invariant and is a normalisation check. It constrains the shape
of the vector and not its content: `[10000, 0, 0]` and `[3333, 3333, 3334]` both pass. Any attack that
consists of choosing *which* asset gets the weight sails straight through it. This is the most common
way a check looks protective and is not — it enforces a property nobody was attacking.

**What would do security work**, in order of cost: a per-asset cap so no single weight can exceed some
fraction; a per-rebalance delta bound so weights cannot move more than a few percent per call; a
minimum interval between calls; and a timelock on large deltas so the [cancel path](/c/upgrade-key-risk)
exists at all. Each of those constrains content rather than shape, and each is derived by answering
question 3 rather than by reading the code.

**The general lesson.** A check that the value is well-formed is not a check that the value is
acceptable, and the second one can only be written by someone who has asked what the worst admissible
value does.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** takes the "worst admissible value" answers and turns them into invariants —
statements that must hold on every path, which a fuzzer, a prover and a monitor can all consume. Most
good invariants are discovered at a crossing, by someone asking question 3.

**The last lesson in this module** joins this map to the actor-capability table: a threat scenario is
one actor, one capability, one numbered crossing and one falsified assumption. The numbers you assign
here are the index into that table.

**The vulnerability-class module** covers [oracle manipulation](/c/oracle-manipulation) and
[access-control failure](/c/access-control-failure), which are the two classes that live at crossings
rather than inside functions — one is question 1 answered wrongly, the other is question 2 answered
with "nothing".

**Exploit archaeology** covers [signer-UI compromise](/c/signer-ui-compromise) and
[bridge message authority](/c/bridge-message-authority) as their own classes, both of which are this
lesson's crossings with incidents attached.<Cite src="halborn-radiant-capital" />

**The infra track's monitoring work** is where a crossing becomes an alert. The general point applies
now: a boundary you cannot observe is a boundary you cannot defend, and instrumenting a crossing is
usually cheaper than validating it.<Cite src="nist-sp-800-53" />
