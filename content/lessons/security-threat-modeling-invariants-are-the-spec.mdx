Here is a security requirement, copied from the kind of specification document real protocols ship:

> The vault must remain solvent at all times. Users should not be able to withdraw more than their
> fair share, and the protocol should always be able to honour outstanding redemptions.

Everyone nods. Nobody disagrees. It is also **not a specification**, and you can prove that in one
move: name the test that fails when it is violated. You cannot, because the sentence has no truth
value at any given moment. "Should", "fair" and "able to" are not properties of a state.

Now the same requirement as something a machine can evaluate:

> `totalAssets() >= totalSupply() * lastRecordedSharePrice / 1e18`, after every externally reachable
> call, for every reachable state.

That version can be false. That is the entire difference, and it is the difference between a document
and a specification.

## The definition, and the reframe that follows from it

Trail of Bits defines an invariant as a statement about a program that must always hold, regardless of
its state or execution path.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" /> Two clauses, both
load-bearing. *Regardless of its state* rules out "after setUp". *Regardless of its execution path*
rules out "when called in the expected order".

Most engineers meet invariants for the first time as `invariant_` functions in a fuzz harness, and
that first meeting installs a belief that is hard to shake: that an invariant is a kind of test.
Invert it.

> The invariant is the specification. Fuzzing, formal verification, runtime monitoring, on-chain
> post-conditions and manual review are five different **consumers** of the same statement.

Trail of Bits makes exactly this argument — that invariants are not limited to fuzzing and formal
verification, and lists monitoring through external tools watching for transactions that break them,
on-chain invariants executed directly within the contract as post-conditions, and manual review
focused on verifying key invariants.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" />

The practical consequence is the one worth carrying out of this module: **tool choice is downstream of
specification quality.** A protocol with no written invariants cannot be meaningfully fuzzed, verified
or monitored at any budget, because all three techniques take the statement as input. Buying a Certora
engagement without a specification buys you an expensive way to discover you do not have one.

## Two kinds, and why the split is about state rather than difficulty

**Function-level invariants** focus on specific computations and typically do not change state — the
`pure` or `view` functions.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" /> Monotonicity, bounds,
rounding direction, algebraic identities. Trail of Bits' worked example is a lending protocol's
interest computation: the function computing interest earned over time is an increasing monotonic
function, so the return value increases as time
increases.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" />

They are cheap to state, cheap to check, and the natural first target for symbolic tools because the
state space is small. They also say nothing about composition: a pricing function can be perfectly
monotonic and still be called at the wrong moment by the function above it.

**System-level invariants** span the entire system's state and transitions — for example, ensuring
that assets are always greater than or equal to liabilities, or that no user has a token balance
greater than the total supply.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" />

These are the ones that catch accounting and ordering bugs, and they require accumulated state to
check, which is why [stateful fuzzing](/c/stateful-fuzzing) exists as a distinct technique from
throwing random arguments at one function. The Track 05 module on
[invariant testing](/c/invariant-testing) is the mechanics; this lesson is where the statements come
from.

<Aside kind="note">
The split is not "easy versus hard". It is "does checking this require history". `x * y = k` after a
swap in a fee-less pool is checkable from two values before and two after — Trail of Bits gives it as
the AMM example, with the caveat "assuming no fee" attached, which is itself a lesson in how much
precision a real invariant needs.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" /> "The protocol's
solvency never decreases outside of accounted fees" needs a running total that no single call
contains.
</Aside>

Three things about that table repay attention.

**The description column is plain English, and that is deliberate.** It can be as simple as how you
would describe it verbally.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" /> The precision arrives when
someone writes the executable form; the table's job is to make the claim exist.

**Complex invariants get a Hoare-triple shape** — pre-condition, command, post-condition — which
despite the formal-sounding name is the same structure as Arrange/Act/Assert or Given/When/Then, and
row ToB1 is written that way.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" /> Use it whenever an
invariant only holds under a condition, because *the condition is the part people forget and the part
attackers use.*

**The testing-strategy column is a decision, not a label.** It is where you commit to who consumes the
statement, and it belongs in the same row as the statement so the two cannot drift.

### The three questions to ask before writing any code

Trail of Bits frames the design phase as three questions, and the second is the one with real
engineering consequences:<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" />

**What are the main invariants?** The recommendation is concrete — ask the team to identify the ten
most essential invariants so they can keep them in mind at every stage, and if they cannot answer,
dedicate more time to identifying them.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" /> A team that
cannot produce ten does not have a specification problem to solve later; it has one now.

**How will these invariants be checked?** This influences the code's design. Invariants that will be
monitored require the emission of relevant events, and invariants that will run on-chain benefit from
specific code isolation.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" /> That is the sentence that
turns this from documentation into architecture: **deciding to monitor a property is deciding to emit
an event, and that decision is cheap before the contract is written and awkward afterwards.**

**How will they be kept in sync with the code?** Specifications drift as requirements change, and
having a process to keep them aligned is what determines whether the artifact survives past
launch.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" />

<Aside kind="note">
You do not have to start from a blank page. Trail of Bits maintains `crytic/properties`, a set of
pre-built security properties for common Ethereum operations, and points at it directly as a source of
inspiration when creating invariants.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" /><Cite src="crytic-properties" />
Their example row ToB0 is implemented there as an assertion that a user's balance is not higher than
total supply.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" /> The standard-token properties are the
right thing to inherit; the ones specific to your protocol are the ones only you can write, and they
are also the ones that catch [business-logic flaws](/c/business-logic-flaw).
</Aside>

## Five consumers, one statement

Once the statement exists, the same text feeds every assurance activity you will meet in the rest of
this track.

**Fuzzers** search for a counterexample by generating call sequences. Trail of Bits names Medusa,
Echidna and Foundry.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" /><Cite src="crytic-echidna" /><Cite src="crytic-medusa" /><Cite src="foundry-invariant-testing" />
Cheap, probabilistic, excellent at finding the sequence you did not imagine, and never a proof of
absence.

**Formal verification tools** attempt a proof over input classes rather than samples — Halmos, Certora
and KEVM are the named ones.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" /><Cite src="a16z-halmos-repo" /><Cite src="certora-prover-repo" />
Expensive in engineer time, and the payoff is a different kind of answer, not a stronger version of the
same one.

**On-chain post-conditions** enforce the statement at runtime, inside the transaction, and revert. Very
strong — and the subject of the next section, because they are the one with a sharp edge.

**Monitoring** watches production for transactions that break the invariant.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" />
This is the only consumer that works on the deployed system against real adversaries, and the only one
whose output is an alert rather than a test result.

**Manual review** focuses the reviewer on verifying key invariants.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" />
The under-appreciated benefit is onboarding: handing a security engineer your invariant list is how
they understand the system's critical parts in an afternoon rather than a week, and Trail of Bits calls
this out as one of the places the approach shines — it also lets you reason about code review
coverage.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" />

<Aside kind="warn">
Monitoring has a requirement the other four do not: **you must know what you will do when it fires.**
Trail of Bits states that for each monitored invariant you must define how to interpret and debug why
it broke, who in the organisation has the relevant knowledge, and what actions are available — pausing
the system, changing a parameter, upgrading the
contracts.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" /> An alert with no defined response is a
notification, not a control. This is the same failure as the unmonitored timelock from two lessons
ago, arriving from the other direction.
</Aside>

## On-chain invariants are a double-edged tool

The most tempting move, once you have a solvency statement, is to put it in the contract as a
post-condition. Sometimes that is right. Two constraints say when it is not.

**Cost and complexity.** Trail of Bits notes that making every invariant part of the on-chain code may
not be possible: some require complex computation such as unbounded loop iteration, which increases
gas cost or the risk of bugs in the invariants
themselves.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" />

**The invariant can be the bug.** They give a specific example — issue TOB-UNI-005 in their Uniswap V3
report, a broken invariant that could have allowed a malicious user to drain any Uniswap pool — and
draw the conclusion directly: on-chain invariants are a double-edged sword carrying unique benefits and
risks, which is why the candidates should be identified during the design phase and treated with
special care.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" /> A property enforced in the critical path
is code, subject to every failure mode any other code has, and it runs on every call including the
honest ones.

### Pricing the on-chain option, with measured numbers

The placement decision has an arithmetic side, and it is worth doing before the argument rather than
after. The numbers below were measured on this machine — forge 1.7.1, solc 0.8.36, optimizer off — and
they are environment-dependent, so take the shape and re-measure the
magnitudes.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" />

**Reading state costs by warmth, not by variable.** A cold `SLOAD` measured 2,100 gas and a warm one
100.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /> An invariant that touches ten previously untouched slots
therefore starts at roughly 21,000 gas before any arithmetic — on every call, forever.

**A running total is cheaper than a loop, and is not free.** Maintaining an accumulator so the check is
`O(1)` costs an `SSTORE`: 20,000 for zero to non-zero, 2,900 for non-zero to non-zero, 100 for a warm
no-op, each plus 2,100 when the slot is cold.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /> The accumulator is
also new state that can itself be wrong, which is the TOB-UNI-005 hazard in miniature.

**The monitoring path is priced in logs.** Emitting the values a monitor needs costs 375 gas per
topic.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /> Three topics is roughly 1,125 gas plus data — an order of
magnitude below a multi-slot on-chain check, and it moves the evaluation off-chain where an unbounded
loop is free.

That comparison is the real content of the placement decision. **On-chain enforcement buys prevention
and pays on every honest call; monitoring buys detection and pays once, in log gas.** Which you want
is a function of how fast you need to know and whether you can act, not of which sounds more rigorous.

<Aside kind="note">
The module's measure exercise is exactly this, and it carries a trap worth knowing before you hit it:
a read whose result is never used is eliminated as dead code even with the optimizer off, so a naive
`gasleft()` probe measures nothing. Assign each read into a storage sink. The harness overhead is about
16 to 17 gas and must be subtracted.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" />
</Aside>

## What the evidence says about invariants stopping real attacks

Do not let the word "invariant" import totality. There is an empirical study of exactly this question,
and its numbers are more useful than the vibe in either direction.

The Trace2Inv work studied 23 prevalent invariants across 8 categories, either deployed in high-profile
protocols or endorsed by leading auditing firms and security experts, and evaluated them against 42
smart contracts that fell victim to 27 distinct exploits on
Ethereum.<Cite src="trace2inv-fse24" /> The findings:

- The most effective single invariant guard **blocked 18 of the 27 exploits**, with minimal gas
  overhead.<Cite src="trace2inv-fse24" />
- Most invariants remained effective even when experienced attackers attempted to bypass
  them.<Cite src="trace2inv-fse24" />
- Combining multiple guards blocked **up to 23 of the 27**, with false positive rates as low as
  0.32%.<Cite src="trace2inv-fse24" />

Read all three lines together. A single well-chosen runtime invariant catching two thirds of a real
exploit benchmark is a stronger result than most defensive techniques can show. And **four of the
twenty-seven were not blocked even by a combination**, which is the honest ceiling: an invariant
constrains the states you thought to constrain.

## Converting a real requirement, three times

Take three prose requirements from the staking vault's documentation and drive them to the artifact.

**Subgoal: strip the modal verbs and find the quantity.** "Users should not be able to withdraw more
than their fair share" contains one measurable quantity — the ratio of assets to shares — and one
undefined word. Replace the word: *for every account, redeeming all shares returns at most
`totalAssets * shares / totalSupply`, rounded down.* Now it can be false.

**Subgoal: classify by whether checking it needs history.** That statement is checkable within one
call from four values, so it is function-level and a prover can attack it. Contrast with *the sum of
all shares equals `totalSupply`*, which needs every account, and *the vault's assets never decrease
except through an accounted withdrawal or fee*, which needs a running total across a sequence. Two
system-level, one function-level, from one paragraph of prose.

**Subgoal: name the pre-condition explicitly, in Hoare form.** The rounding claim only holds when no
harvest is mid-flight. Written out: *pre-condition — no reentrant call is in progress; command — call
`redeem` for the account's entire balance; post-condition — assets received are at most the pro-rata
share, rounded down.* The pre-condition you were about to leave implicit is the
[read-only reentrancy](/c/read-only-reentrancy) window, and writing it down is how you notice you have
one.

**Subgoal: assign a consumer per row and justify it.** The rounding claim goes to formal verification,
because it is a pure arithmetic property over a bounded computation and a fuzzer will sample a
vanishing fraction of the space. The share-conservation claim goes to a stateful fuzz campaign with a
[handler](/c/handler-pattern), because it needs sequences. The assets-never-decrease claim goes to
monitoring, because the interesting violations involve external protocols the fuzzer does not have.

**Subgoal: price the one you were tempted to put on-chain.** The conservation claim would need every
account, which is unbounded — so it is not an on-chain candidate at any gas price. The rounding claim
could be a post-condition on `redeem` at the cost of a few warm reads. Emitting the two values instead
costs 375 per topic and moves the check to a monitor.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /> Write the
decision down with the number next to it, and the argument stops being about taste.

**Subgoal: check the row against the threat model.** Each invariant should trace back to an assumption
from your inventory or a crossing from your data-flow map. The conservation claim is what a
[donation attack](/c/donation-attack) falsifies. The assets-never-decrease claim is what a compromised
keeper at crossing DF-3 falsifies. An invariant with no such trace may be worth having; an assumption
with no invariant is a gap.

A team hands you five statements from a document titled "Protocol Invariants". Classify each as a
usable invariant, a repairable statement, or not an invariant at all — and for the repairable ones,
write the fixed version. Then say which consumer each surviving statement should have.

1. "The contract should never run out of gas."
2. "For any user, `balanceOf(user) <= totalSupply()`."
3. "Only the owner can pause the protocol."
4. "The exchange rate never decreases."
5. "After any sequence of deposits and withdrawals, the sum of all user balances equals
   `totalDeposited` minus `totalWithdrawn`."

<Answer>
**1 — not an invariant.** Gas exhaustion is not a property of state; it is a property of a particular
call under a particular limit, and it is not something that "holds" or "fails" over reachable states.
It is a real concern and it belongs in the threat model as a liveness issue at a crossing — for
example, whether an unbounded loop can be pushed past the block or per-transaction limit by an
attacker who controls its length. It cannot be reduced to a boolean over state, so it cannot be handed
to a fuzzer or a prover as written.

**2 — usable as-is, and it is Trail of Bits' own row ToB0.**<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" />
It is a boolean over state, it holds on every path, and a violation is meaningful on its own. Consumer:
fuzzing, and it is available pre-written in `crytic/properties` rather than something you should be
authoring.<Cite src="crytic-properties" /> Note that it is a *weak* invariant — it catches accounting
corruption, not unfairness — which is fine. Weak invariants that always hold are worth more than strong
ones that are almost true.

**3 — repairable, and the repair is instructive.** As written it is an access-control requirement, not
a statement about state. The invariant form quantifies over callers: *for every address that is not the
owner, a call to `pause` reverts and `paused()` is unchanged.* That is checkable, and it is exactly the
class a symbolic tool is good at, because it is a claim about all inputs to one function. Consumer:
formal verification or a targeted fuzz property. Also note what it does **not** say — nothing about
whether the owner *should* be able to pause, which is the [capability](/c/capability-not-role) question
from two lessons back and belongs in a different artifact.

**4 — repairable, and this is where the money is.** "Never decreases" is almost certainly false as
stated: a loss event, a fee, or a slashing in the underlying strategy legitimately decreases it. An
invariant that is false in normal operation gets silenced within a week, which is worse than not having
it. The Hoare form forces the exception into the open: *pre-condition — no loss has been reported by
the strategy since the last observation; command — any sequence of user-facing calls; post-condition —
the exchange rate is greater than or equal to its value before the sequence.* The pre-condition is now
a named event you must emit, which is the design consequence Trail of Bits warns
about.<Cite src="tob-invariant-driven-development" /> Consumer: stateful fuzzing, plus a monitor,
because the production violations will come from the strategy rather than from your code.

**5 — usable, and it is the only system-level one in the list.** It quantifies over sequences, needs
accumulated state, and a violation means value has been created or destroyed. Consumer: a stateful
fuzz campaign with a handler; it is not an on-chain candidate because summing all user balances is
unbounded. If you want the on-chain version, you maintain ghost accumulators and pay an `SSTORE` per
update — 2,900 gas for a non-zero to non-zero write, plus 2,100 when the slot is
cold.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" />

**The pattern across all five.** The statements that survive share one shape: a quantifier, a boolean
over state, and an explicit pre-condition where one is needed. The statements that fail are either
about the machine rather than the state, or about permission rather than property. Both failure modes
are common in documents titled "Invariants", and spotting them is most of the skill.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** joins these statements to the scenario table. A threat scenario names the
assumption it falsifies, and the machine-checkable form of an assumption is an invariant — which is
why the two artifacts are written together and reviewed together.

**Track 05's [invariant testing](/c/invariant-testing) module** is the mechanics of the fuzzing
consumer: handlers, ghost variables, runs and depth, and the revert metrics that tell you your campaign
tested nothing.<Cite src="foundry-invariant-testing" /> The statements from this lesson are its input.

**This track's fuzzing and formal-verification modules** are the other two consumers at depth, and the
distinction that matters there is what each tool proves rather than what each tool's syntax looks
like.<Cite src="crytic-building-secure-contracts" />

**[Invariant monitoring](/c/invariant-monitoring) on the infra track** is the consumer that runs against
real adversaries, and it inherits the requirement stated above: define the response before you define
the alert.

**The Rekt Test question you can now answer.** "Do you define key invariants for your system and test
them on every commit?"<Cite src="tob-rekt-test" /> Both halves are this lesson — the defining, and the
every-commit part that keeps the specification and the code from drifting, which is the same discipline
the SCSVS asks for when it requires that every introduced design change be preceded by threat
modeling.<Cite src="scsvs-v1-architecture" /> Business-logic flaws remain among the largest loss
categories precisely because they are invisible to tools that have not been told what the protocol was
supposed to do.<Cite src="sok-1b-loss" />
