Two sentences about the same system. One of them is a finding.

> What if the oracle is wrong?

> The strategy operator, who can choose the reward figure at crossing DF-3 with no upper bound in
> `harvest`, publishes a value large enough to inflate share price by 40%; the lending market at DF-5
> reads that price and issues loans against shares that are not backed; the falsified assumption is
> "the harvest figure is honest"; the impact is bad debt on a third-party protocol; the mitigation is a
> per-harvest delta cap plus a monitor on the ratio.

The first sentence cannot be acted on, argued with, or closed. Everybody agrees with it and nothing
happens. The second names six things, every one of which somebody can check and disagree with — and
disagreement is what you want, because a claim nobody can refute is a claim nobody can act on either.

This lesson is about producing the second kind of sentence at volume, collecting them into an artifact
that survives, and keeping that artifact from describing a system that no longer exists.

## The six clauses

A **threat scenario** is not a worry with more words. It has a fixed shape, and the shape is what makes
it enumerable:

1. **An actor** — from your enumeration, including the compromise operator applied to any row.
2. **A capability** — from the capability table, with its scope, speed and reversibility.
3. **A numbered crossing** — DF-something, from your data-flow map.
4. **The assumption it falsifies** — from your trust-assumption inventory.
5. **The impact** — what is lost, by whom, and whether it is recoverable.
6. **The mitigation** — the control you propose, or an explicit decision to accept.

Clauses 1 through 4 are joins onto the three artifacts you built in the previous three lessons. That
is the point of building them: a scenario is generated, not invented. Trail of Bits' methodology places
scenario development last for exactly this reason — for each trust boundary crossing, analyse potential
attack vectors and develop realistic threat scenarios showing how design vulnerabilities could be
exploited.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" /> The crossings are the loop variable.

That also gives you a completeness test that a free-form discussion cannot have. **Every numbered
crossing either appears in at least one scenario or has a written note saying why it does not.** A
crossing with neither is not safe; it is unexamined, and the difference is visible on the page.

## Prompts, when the crossings stop generating ideas

Staring at a crossing and asking "what could go wrong" works for two or three crossings and then stops.
The standard fix is a prompt list, and the standard prompt list is STRIDE — a mature and popular
mnemonic originally developed by Microsoft employees, which groups threats into six general prompts so
engineers can systematically consider how each might materialise in the specific system being
evaluated.<Cite src="owasp-threat-modeling-cheatsheet" />

Each category is a violation of a desirable security attribute:<Cite src="owasp-threat-modeling-cheatsheet" />

| Prompt | Violates | Ask, at this crossing |
|---|---|---|
| **S**poofing | Authentication | Can someone convince the consumer they are the legitimate producer? |
| **T**ampering | Integrity | Can the value be altered between producer and consumer? |
| **R**epudiation | Accounting | Can an actor deny having done this, or destroy the record? |
| **I**nformation disclosure | Confidentiality | Does anything cross that should not be visible to the consumer's zone? |
| **D**enial of service | Availability | Can the crossing be blocked, delayed, or made too expensive to use? |
| **E**levation of privilege | Authorization | Can something that crossed as data end up executing as code? |

OWASP is careful that STRIDE is illustrative rather than mandatory, and that other approaches may be
used alongside or instead of it — there is no universally accepted industry standard for the process
and no right answer for every use case.<Cite src="owasp-threat-modeling-cheatsheet" />

Take that seriously, because **STRIDE maps imperfectly onto this domain and you should know where the
seams are.** Repudiation is largely handled for you, so that row produces nothing on-chain and
everything off-chain, in your operational records. Information disclosure inverts: everything is public
by default, so the question is not "can this leak" but "who acts on it first", which STRIDE has no
letter for. And the two classes that dominate real losses — economic manipulation of a value that is
nominally correct, and compromise of a party you legitimately trusted — sit awkwardly across Tampering
and Elevation of Privilege rather than in either.

<Aside kind="note">
Better domain-specific prompts, drawn from the last two lessons and worth running alongside STRIDE at
every crossing: *what is the worst admissible value here* (not the worst imaginable one); *what happens
if this producer is the attacker*; *what does a flash loan do to the economics of this*; *who reads
this value while it is mid-update*; and *what does this look like if the actor is patient and
well-resourced rather than opportunistic* — which is the [state-level actor](/c/dprk-threat-actor)
question the current loss data forces.<Cite src="sok-1b-loss" />
</Aside>

## The document, section by section

The scenarios go somewhere. That somewhere has seven parts, and each earns its place by being the
input to the next.

**Components.** Every deployed and off-chain component. The Rekt Test asks whether you keep
documentation of all the external services, contracts and oracles you rely on, so that you can answer
how you will know when an external dependency suffers a security
incident.<Cite src="tob-rekt-test" />

**Trust zones.** Each component assigned to a zone with the blast-radius reason recorded. The reason
matters more than the assignment — it is what a reviewer disagrees with.

**Data flows.** Every crossing numbered, with producer, consumer, and what is validated on arrival.
The SCSVS states this as a verification requirement: documentation must clearly and precisely define
all trust boundaries, meaning trusted relations with other contracts and significant data
flows.<Cite src="scsvs-v1-architecture" />

**Actors and capabilities.** With scope, speed and reversibility. The Rekt Test's first question asks
whether all actors, roles and privileges are documented, and names the payoff: letting developers and
external auditors identify security gaps, improper access controls, the degree of decentralisation, and
exposure in specific compromise scenarios.<Cite src="tob-rekt-test" />

**Trust assumptions.** Each with what falsifies it and what breaks downstream.

**Scenarios.** The table, each row citing one crossing and one assumption. The Rekt Test asks this as
"do you document the best ways to attack your system?"<Cite src="tob-rekt-test" />

**Residual risk.** The section that makes the document worth writing.

### Why residual risk is the load-bearing section

Every threat model ends with things you are not going to fix. Some mitigations cost more than the
asset, some destroy the product, some are not available at your stage. That is normal and it is not the
problem.

The problem is *how* the decision gets made. Without a residual-risk section, a risk is accepted by
**omission**: nobody writes it down, nobody signs off, and after an incident nobody can say whether it
was considered. With one, it is accepted **by name**, by a person, on a date, with a stated reason.

Trail of Bits routes this into machinery the organisation already has: threat model results should feed
the security roadmap and risk register, because a model that does not inform future risk-management
decisions will not provide value. Their prioritisation guidance is a risk-based approach ranking
threats by impact potential and exploitation likelihood, a defence-in-depth strategy investing across
prevention, detection and response rather than exclusively in preventive controls, and regular
assessment of whether the implemented controls deliver the expected risk
reduction.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" />

That middle clause is the one teams skip. **Detection and response are mitigations.** A scenario whose
mitigation is "we will see it within one block and can pause" is a mitigated scenario, provided the
monitor exists and somebody is on call — which is why the previous lesson insisted that an alert
without a defined response is a notification.

<Aside kind="warn">
Pause deserves a specific caution here, because it is the mitigation teams reach for when nothing else
fits. Trail of Bits notes that the pause guardian is not a golden bullet: attackers have become more
advanced and attacks are often conducted in private mempools to prevent proactive identification, so
the potency of pause as a mechanism to reduce attack impact will likely decline over
time.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" /> A pause is also itself a capability someone holds — it
belongs in your actor table as a row, not only in your mitigation column. The infra track takes this up
as [pause design patterns](/c/pause-design-patterns).
</Aside>

## Cadence: the document decays

A threat model describes a system at a moment. Systems change. The failure mode is not that the
document is wrong on the day it is written — it is that it is right, gets filed, and is quietly wrong
eighteen months later while everyone still believes it.

OWASP states the requirement plainly: threat modeling is not something performed once and never again.
A threat model should be maintained, updated and refined alongside the system, and it should be
integrated seamlessly into a team's normal SDLC process — treated as a standard and necessary step, not
an add-on.<Cite src="owasp-threat-modeling-cheatsheet" />

Trail of Bits puts numbers on that for this domain: treat threat modeling as an ongoing process rather
than a one-time exercise, and make regular model updates **quarterly or semi-annually, as well as after
significant architectural changes, new feature launches, or changes in
operations**.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" />

The second half of that sentence is the operative part. Calendar triggers are a floor; event triggers
catch drift, and in this domain they fire constantly — a new integration, a new keeper, a new signer, a
new chain, a new RPC provider, a change to who holds the deploy key. Each adds an actor, a zone or a
crossing, and none is a code change your CI would flag.

The SCSVS turns the event trigger into a verification requirement — verify that every introduced design
change is preceded by an earlier threat modeling.<Cite src="scsvs-v1-architecture" /> Trail of Bits'
version is the same discipline stated as a workflow: proposed design changes should be accompanied by
an accounting of how their adoption would affect the system's threat model and attack
surface.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" /> That is a checklist item on a design document, and it is
the cheapest possible implementation of cadence.

### Where it plugs into the rest of the process

Trail of Bits names four touchpoints, and they are worth adopting wholesale because each one turns the
document into something a different team already needs:<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" />

- **The SDLC.** Threat model early in design rather than after the design is finalised. This reduces
  the cost of fixing insecure designs and gives the team a better idea of what they are defending
  against while they still have choices.
- **Risk management.** Results feed the security roadmap and risk register.
- **Incident response.** Base tabletop exercises and IR planning on scenarios from the threat model, so
  that the incidents you rehearse are the ones you predicted. The Rekt Test's third question — whether
  you have a written and *tested* incident response plan — is this
  touchpoint.<Cite src="tob-rekt-test" />
- **Security testing and auditing.** Use the model to guide the scope and focus of penetration tests,
  code reviews and audits, which are much more effective when threat actors and their capabilities are
  well understood.

The last one pays off immediately. A firm handed a threat model spends its first days on your actual
risk instead of reconstructing your architecture, and your unmitigated scenarios become the brief —
a better use of a fixed budget than "here is the repository".

## From worry to row, twice

Do the conversion twice, deliberately, because the second one is the kind teams never produce.

**Subgoal: pick a crossing rather than a topic.** Start at DF-3, the harvest calldata crossing from the
data-flow lesson. Do not start from "oracle risk"; start from a numbered place, because a topic
generates prose and a place generates a row.

**Subgoal: name the actor with the compromise operator already applied.** Not "the keeper" — *an
attacker holding the keeper's hot key, or whoever controls the strategy's reporting API*. This is where
most scenarios die, by naming a party the reader already trusts.

**Subgoal: state the capability with its bound, or state that it has none.** *Can set the credited
reward to any value that passes `harvest`; there is no delta bound, so the ceiling is whatever the
arithmetic accepts.* If you cannot state the bound, you have found the finding before finishing the
sentence.

**Subgoal: name the assumption from the inventory, verbatim.** *"The harvest figure is honest."* Using
the inventory's exact words matters: it makes the row a join rather than a restatement, and it means
fixing the assumption updates every scenario that cites it.

**Subgoal: write impact in units somebody outside security cares about.** *Share price inflated;
withdrawals at the inflated price transfer value from remaining holders; a third-party lending market
issues loans against unbacked collateral, producing bad debt on a protocol we do not control.* That
last clause is what gets this prioritised, and it is invisible if you stop at your own contract's edge.

**Subgoal: propose a mitigation and price it.** *Per-harvest delta cap enforced on-chain, plus a
monitor on the assets-to-shares ratio.* One preventive control and one detective one, which is the
defence-in-depth split.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" /> The empirical evidence supports combining:
on a benchmark of 27 real exploits the best single runtime invariant guard blocked 18, while combining
guards reached up to 23 with false positive rates as low as 0.32%.<Cite src="trace2inv-fse24" />

**Subgoal: now write one that contains no Solidity at all.** DF-6, the upgrade-payload crossing. *Actor:
an attacker with code execution on a signer's workstation. Capability: control what the signing
interface renders, without touching any key. Crossing: DF-6. Falsified assumption: "what a signer sees
is what a signer signs." Impact: implementation replaced, total loss, irreversible. Mitigation:
independent recomputation of the transaction hash on a separate machine, compared byte-by-byte;
dedicated signing workstations; disable `delegatecall` in the wallet configuration or bind signatures
to a specific implementation.*<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" /> If your scenario table has no rows
of this shape, it is a code-review plan wearing a threat model's headings.

A team's threat model contains these four entries. For each, say whether it is a scenario, and if not,
what is missing and how you would repair it. Then say what the table as a whole tells you about the
process that produced it.

1. "Reentrancy in `withdraw` could drain the vault."
2. "If the multisig is compromised, we lose everything."
3. "A searcher observes a large pending deposit at DF-1 and front-runs it, capturing the price

<Answer>
**1 — not a scenario; it is a vulnerability class with a function name attached.** No actor, no
crossing, no assumption. It is also the wrong artifact: whether `withdraw` is reentrant is answered by
reading the code and by a fuzz campaign, not by threat modeling. Repair it by asking what assumption
would have to be false — *"no externally observable value is inconsistent across an external call"* —
and then the scenario writes itself, and notably it is about the *reader* of your state rather than
about `withdraw`. That is the [read-only reentrancy](/c/read-only-reentrancy) shape, and it involves a
crossing (DF-5) that the original sentence never mentions.

**2 — not a scenario, and the most common non-scenario in real documents.** It has an actor and an
impact and nothing in between: no capability, no speed, no cancellation path, no crossing. Repair it by
splitting into at least three rows, because "the multisig is compromised" is three different attacks —
a stolen key, a coerced signer, and a compromised signing interface — with three different mitigations.
The third is the one that has actually happened at scale, and it is invisible while all three share one
line.

**3 — a scenario.** All six clauses present, and the mitigation is specific enough to implement and
test. One refinement a reviewer would ask for: the mitigation should say whether that parameter's
*default* is safe, because a protective parameter with a permissive default protects nobody.

**4 — not a scenario, but a legitimate residual-risk entry, and incomplete as one.** A residual-risk
entry needs a scenario's body plus two fields: who accepted it, and when. As written, nobody can tell
whether "stale data" means the front end shows an old balance or the liquidation bot misses a window,
and those have different costs. Repair: state the worst outcome, name the accepting party, date it, and
note the trigger that reopens the decision. This is also the class of assumption implicated in the
April 2026 verifier-configuration loss, whose analysis describes RPC infrastructure poisoning alongside
the forged message.<Cite src="blocksec-kelp-dao" />

**What the table says about the process.** Three of four rows have no crossing number, which means the
data-flow map either does not exist or was not used to generate the rows. The scenarios were produced
by brainstorming rather than by enumeration, which produces exactly this distribution: the famous bug
class, the catastrophic hand-wave, one good row someone actually thought about, and an accepted risk
with no owner. Regenerate from the crossings and the shape changes immediately — you get coverage
instead of highlights.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this method does not give you

Close with the honest limits, because you will be selling this internally and the objections are real.

**There is no canonical, non-vendor methodology for protocols.** OWASP says outright that no
universally accepted industry standard exists for the threat modeling process and no answer is right
for every use case.<Cite src="owasp-threat-modeling-cheatsheet" /> The best-documented framing for this
domain is published by a firm that sells the service, which does not make it wrong and does mean you
should read it as an argument.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" /> STRIDE and NIST SP 800-53 are the
general-IT ancestors, and the control catalog is designed to be customised rather than applied as
written.<Cite src="nist-sp-800-53" />

**Threat modeling finds missing controls, not bugs.** It will never find an off-by-one in your
rounding. The rest of this track is for that, and the Manifesto's fourth question — did we do a good
enough job — is the one that sends you there.<Cite src="threat-modeling-manifesto" />

**Enumeration is not completeness.** You will miss actors. The value is that a missed actor becomes a
visible gap in a numbered table rather than an unexamined region nobody mapped.

**It cannot be delegated to the end.** Most protocols seek outside review at the very end of the
lifecycle, when there is little time and few opportunities to fix systemic access-control
issues.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" /> A threat model delivered the week before launch
documents decisions rather than informing them.

## Where this pays off

**The rest of this track is the other question.** [Vulnerability classes](/c/access-control-failure)
next, then exploit archaeology, static analysis, fuzzing, formal verification, audit craft and
contests. Every one of them is a technique for finding bugs in code you have already decided to write —
and the model you just built is what tells them where to look.

**Audit craft** is where the artifact becomes leverage: handing a firm your scenarios and your
unmitigated residual risk changes what a fixed budget buys, and it is also where
[what "audited" actually promises](/c/audit-is-not-a-guarantee) and
[how severity is assigned](/c/severity-vs-difficulty) get taken apart.

**Exploit archaeology** is this module run backwards. Every incident there resolves into an actor, a
capability, a crossing and a falsified assumption, and recovering those four from a post-mortem is the
fastest way to get better at producing them forward.<Cite src="blocksec-kelp-dao" />

**The infra track's monitoring and incident response** are the detection and response halves of your
mitigation column — including why [monitoring is not auditing](/c/monitoring-is-not-auditing) and how
the [signal layers](/c/three-signal-layers) fit together. The alerts worth building are the ones that
fire on a row you already wrote down.

**And the module's write exercise** is the whole thing on a repository you did not author: seven
sections, every crossing numbered, at least eight scenarios, three that no static analyser could
surface, one entirely off-chain. It takes an afternoon.
