On 21 February 2025, a group of people at a cryptocurrency exchange approved a transfer out of a cold
wallet. They were experienced. They used a multisig. Each of them looked at a screen, saw a routine
movement of funds, and signed.

What they signed changed the implementation address of the Safe multisig wallet to one the attacker
controlled, which handed over everything the wallet
held.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" /> The reported loss is the largest in the industry's history
and is quoted at different figures — roughly $1.4 billion, roughly $1.46 billion, and $1.5 billion —
because the same quantity of ETH was marked at different prices at different moments. Trail of Bits'
own analysis carries $1.5 billion in its title.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" /> Sources disagree;
this lesson does not pick one, because the number is the least interesting fact about the incident.

Here is the interesting fact. **Every contract involved behaved exactly as written.** There was no
reentrancy, no arithmetic error, no missing modifier, no uninitialised proxy. The `delegatecall` that
carried the attack is a documented, intended feature of the wallet, and you already know precisely
what it does from [borrowed code, kept storage](/c/delegatecall-proxy). A line-by-line review of the
Solidity would have returned no findings, correctly.

You have spent four tracks learning to read that Solidity. This track opens by drawing the box that
Solidity sits inside, because the largest losses of the last two years happened outside it.

## Two questions, and only one of them is about code

The rest of this track — vulnerability classes, exploit archaeology, static analysis, fuzzing, formal
verification, audit craft — answers one question: **what bugs exist in this code?** It is a good
question and there is a great deal of technique behind it.

Threat modeling answers a different one: **what would have to be true for this system to be safe, and
who is in a position to make it false?**

The two questions have different failure modes. If you only ask the first, you find bugs in the parts
you looked at and learn nothing about the parts you did not. If you only ask the second, you produce a
document full of correct architectural observations and ship a reentrancy. You need both, and the
sequencing matters: the second question tells you where to point the first.

The general-purpose framing predates blockchains by decades. The Threat Modeling Manifesto reduces the
whole activity to four questions: what are we working on, what can go wrong, what are we going to do
about it, and did we do a good enough job.<Cite src="threat-modeling-manifesto" /> OWASP's cheat sheet
organises its process around exactly those four, and is explicit that there is no universally accepted
industry standard for the threat modeling process and no right answer for every use
case.<Cite src="owasp-threat-modeling-cheatsheet" /> Take that as licence rather than as a weakness —
you are choosing a decomposition, not applying a specification.

This lesson is the first question: *what are we working on*. The unit of decomposition is not the
contract.

## A trust zone is a blast radius, not a repository

Trail of Bits describes its own methodology in five steps, and the third is the one this lesson
turns on: components are grouped into **trust zones** based on shared purpose, ownership, or level of
potential damage, and those zones are bounded by **trust boundaries**, which typically occur where
authentication and authorization are required to obtain a higher level of privilege within the
system.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" />

Read the grouping criterion again, because it is doing all the work. *Shared level of potential
damage.* Not shared language, not shared repository, not shared deployment pipeline. The operational
test is a single question you ask of every pair of components:

> If this one is compromised, is that one automatically compromised too?

If yes, they are in the same zone. If no, there is a boundary between them, and something on that
boundary has to do work — authenticate, validate, or explicitly accept the risk.

That test produces zone maps that look nothing like your directory structure. One Foundry repository
routinely spans three zones — the deployed contracts, the deployment key, and the CI runner that uses
it. One zone routinely spans several repositories: an admin multisig's signing UI, the laptops it runs
on, and the browser extension it talks to all fail together, because compromising any of them shows a
signer a lie.

### Boundaries are where the security work lives

A trust boundary is the edge between two zones, and it earns its name because **privilege changes
when something crosses it**. Data that was attacker-controlled on one side becomes trusted input on
the other. A message that was a suggestion becomes an instruction.

This is not a blockchain idea, and it is worth knowing where it comes from. Trail of Bits establishes
its set of security controls based on NIST SP 800-53 and uses those controls to guide the
engagement.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" /> That publication is a catalog of security and privacy
controls for information systems and organizations, designed to be customised and implemented as part
of an organization-wide risk process.<Cite src="nist-sp-800-53" /> You are learning the general-IT
concepts, applied where the attacker is anonymous, the transaction is irreversible and the payoff is
immediate.

The SCSVS states the requirement in one line: verify that the documentation clearly and precisely
defines all trust boundaries in the contract, meaning trusted relations with other contracts and
significant data flows.<Cite src="scsvs-v1-architecture" /> If you cannot point at a document listing
them, you do not have a boundary map — you have individual beliefs held by individual engineers, which
is the state most teams are in and cannot detect from inside.

## The contract boundary is one boundary among many

Now name the thing you have been trained on. The **contract boundary** is the set of `external` and
`public` entry points of your deployed contracts — the surface [dispatch](/c/abi-dispatch) routes to,
the surface a fuzzer drives, the surface an audit scopes.

It is a real trust boundary and a strict subset of the protocol's trust boundaries. Sitting outside
it, in every deployment that has ever held real money:

- the interface that shows a human what they are about to sign
- the workstations that interface runs on
- the custody procedure for the upgrade key
- the CI system that builds and the script that deploys
- the dependency tree the build pulls from
- the RPC endpoints everything reads through
- the oracle publishers, relayers, keepers and bridge verifiers whose output your code treats as fact
- every protocol that integrates with you, and every protocol you integrate with

None of those contain Solidity. All of them are inside the perimeter, in the sense that compromising
any one of them changes what the on-chain system does.

## Walking the February 2025 loss as a boundary problem

Take the incident apart along zone lines. Trail of Bits names four control failures, and each one is a
boundary that had no control on it.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" />

**Subgoal: locate the compromise, not the exploit.** Attackers compromised the Safe signing front end
used by all of the multisig signers, and deployed malware to manipulate the signing
interface.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" /> The crossing that failed is between "the workstation
zone" and "the signer's intent" — a boundary that exists in every protocol and appears in no Solidity
file.

**Subgoal: name the first failed control — endpoint security.** The signers likely used
general-purpose workstations for sensitive signing operations, creating a broad attack surface for
device compromise. The recommendation is dedicated signing workstations with limited connectivity, and
smart contracts that time-lock fund movement so that incident response has a window at
all.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" />

**Subgoal: name the second — a single verification interface.** The signers relied on one verification
interface with no secondary confirmation mechanism, so they could not detect manipulated transaction
details. The recommendation is to recompute the transaction hashes independently, on a separate secure
workstation, and compare them byte-by-byte against what the hardware wallet
displays.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" /><Cite src="pcav-safe-tx-hashes" /> That is a boundary
control in the most literal sense: it puts a second, independent path across the same crossing.

**Subgoal: name the third — a wallet configured to permit semantic change.** The multisig allowed
`delegatecall` operations, which is what let the attackers alter the wallet's implementation. Trail of
Bits is careful here, and you should be too: `delegatecall` operations are not a vulnerability on
their own, but their use in this system creates a design weakness, because they can change the
semantics of the transactions being signed by partially offline signers. The recommendation is either
to disable it entirely, or to design the on-chain components so a signature is valid only for a
specific implementation being called into.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" />

**Subgoal: name the fourth — no operational segregation.** Corporate infrastructure and critical
signing infrastructure may not have been properly separated, so a corporate network compromise could
reach signing operations. The recommendation is air-gapped signing with physically and logically
separate infrastructure.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" />

**Subgoal: notice the shape of all four.** Every one is a statement about a boundary and the control
sitting on it. None is a statement about a function. Three of the four are about zones that contain no
code you wrote.

<Aside kind="warn">
Trail of Bits attaches a footnote to three of those four findings that is worth carrying: the examples
are based on their external knowledge of the incident and extrapolation from the available facts, and
some may not be accurate regarding the exchange's actual security practices or what actually
happened.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" /> That caveat is a feature. A threat model is a set of
hypotheses about where controls are missing, produced from the outside, before anyone knows which one
will matter. The value is in the enumeration, not in being right about every entry.
</Aside>

This was also not the first time. The same pattern had already played out at Radiant Capital in
October 2024 at a reported loss of around $50 million, and at WazirX at around $230 million; Trail of
Bits describes the three together as attackers targeting human and operational elements rather than
exploiting code vulnerabilities.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" /><Cite src="halborn-radiant-capital" />

<Aside kind="note">
The direction of travel has a number attached, and it is worth holding next to the loss tables you
will meet in the next module. Trail of Bits, citing Chainalysis' 2024 report, states that 43.8% of all
funds stolen via hacks stemmed from compromised keys — more than any other verified attack type by a
factor of five.<Cite src="tob-beyond-private-key-risk" /> The systematic classification of severe
smart-contract attacks separately puts access control first by loss value among contract-level root
causes.<Cite src="sok-1b-loss" /> Two different datasets, two different scopes, the same finding: the
money leaves through authorisation and custody, not through clever arithmetic.
</Aside>

## Drawing zones for something you can hold in your head

Abstractions about boundaries are easy to agree with. Run one on a system small enough to be concrete:
a staking vault. Users deposit an ERC-20, receive shares, and a keeper harvests rewards from an
external strategy on a schedule. There is an admin multisig behind a proxy. Perhaps five hundred lines
of Solidity.

**Subgoal: inventory components before grouping any of them.** Vault, share token, strategy, proxy and
its admin, the upgrade multisig, the keeper's bot and its hot key, the price feed the strategy reads,
the front end, the RPC provider, the deployment script, the CI runner and its secrets, the compiler and
the library versions pinned in `foundry.toml`. Thirteen components; three of them are Solidity.

**Subgoal: apply the blast-radius test pairwise, not from intuition.** If the keeper's hot key is
stolen, is the vault compromised? Only to the extent of what `harvest` can do — different zones, then,
with the boundary being `onlyKeeper` plus whatever `harvest` validates about the numbers it is handed.
If the upgrade multisig is compromised, is the vault compromised? Completely and instantly, because the
implementation can be replaced. Same zone — or more precisely, the multisig's zone *contains* the
vault's, which is the relationship the word "privileged" is trying to describe.

**Subgoal: notice which components refuse to sit anywhere obvious.** The CI runner is the hard one. It
holds a deploy key or it does not. Answer that and it lands either in the same zone as the upgrade
multisig — which will alarm the team, and should — or safely outside. The value is that the question
gets asked out loud.

**Subgoal: count boundaries and compare against your audit scope.** A reasonable map for this vault
yields six or seven boundaries. Two are contract boundaries. A contract audit covers those two and
correctly reports nothing about the other five.

<Check type="explain" concept="trust-boundary">
For the staking vault above, classify each of the following as (a) a contract boundary, (b) a trust
boundary that is not a contract boundary, or (c) not a boundary at all — and in each case name the
privilege change, or say why there is not one.

1. The `deposit` function, callable by anyone.
2. An internal helper `_convertToShares` called only from `deposit` and `withdraw`.
3. The keeper bot reading a reward figure from the strategy's off-chain API and passing it to
   `harvest`.
4. The upgrade multisig calling `upgradeToAndCall` on the proxy.
5. A GitHub Actions workflow that runs `forge script --broadcast` on a tag push.

<Answer>
**1 — a contract boundary.** Untrusted calldata becomes trusted state. The privilege change is small
but real, and the control on it is whatever `deposit` validates. This is the boundary your existing
skills are pointed at, and [decoding is validation](/c/abi-decode-validates) is the mechanism that
makes it one.

**2 — not a boundary.** No privilege change occurs on an internal call; the caller and callee are the
same principal in the same frame. Treating internal functions as boundaries is a common way to
generate a long, useless boundary list. Correctness matters here, but it is a correctness question,
not an authorisation one.

**3 — a trust boundary, and not a contract boundary.** Two crossings are stacked: the strategy's API
into the keeper's process, and the keeper's process into `harvest`. The privilege change is that a
number chosen by whoever controls that API becomes an input to accounting. `onlyKeeper` authenticates
the *caller*; nothing authenticates the *value*. Asking "what is the worst number this API could
return, and does `harvest` survive it" is the whole of threat modeling in one question, and no static
analyser will ever ask it for you.

**4 — both, and this is the one that matters.** A contract boundary, because it is an `external` call
with an access check. Also the boundary between the deployed system and the entire apparatus of key
custody: the signers, their laptops, their signing interface, the procedure they follow. The
contract-level part is a few lines and is almost certainly correct. The other part is where February
2025 happened.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" />

**5 — a trust boundary, and the one teams forget.** A tag push crosses from "anyone with write access
to the repository, plus every dependency in the workflow" into "an actor holding a deploy key". Nobody
authored a `require` for it. Its controls are branch protection, environment approvals, secret scoping
and pinned action versions — all real, none of them in your language.

**The pattern.** Boundaries 3 and 5 are invisible to every tool in the rest of this track, and they
are the two most likely to be missing a control, precisely because nothing complains when they are.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What a team can answer before it has any of this

Trail of Bits recommends that smaller organisations start with the Rekt Test, a twelve-question
yes-or-no instrument modelled on the Joel Test, before attempting concrete threat modeling
work.<Cite src="tob-bybit-threat-model" /><Cite src="tob-rekt-test" /> Its first two questions are this
lesson stated as an audit: do you have all actors, roles, and privileges documented, and do you keep
documentation of all the external services, contracts, and oracles you rely
on.<Cite src="tob-rekt-test" />

If either answer is no, the zone map does not exist yet, and the useful move is to write the inventory
rather than argue about methodology. The next lesson is the first of those questions done properly, and
it will show you why "roles" is the wrong noun.

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** turns the actor list into a capability list, which is the difference between "the
multisig can upgrade" and "one compromised signing session replaces all executing code with no delay
and no cancellation path".

**The lesson after that** numbers every crossing on your map, so that each one indexes a row in a table
somebody can argue with — and takes the off-chain half seriously, including why a hardware wallet
screen is not the same thing as knowing what you signed.

**The vulnerability-class module** next in this track is the other question — what bugs exist — and you
will notice that [access-control failure](/c/access-control-failure) and
[upgrade risk](/c/upgrade-risk) are the two classes that sit exactly on the boundaries you just drew.
That is not a coincidence; those are the classes where the bug is an authorisation decision rather
than a computation.

**Exploit archaeology** later in this track re-reads incidents with this map in hand. When
[signer-UI compromise](/c/signer-ui-compromise) and
[verifier quorum collapse](/c/verifier-quorum-collapse) are the top entries, a curriculum organised
around contract bugs describes a minority of the losses.

**Your own systems.** The cheapest version of this lesson is thirty minutes with a whiteboard, the
blast-radius question, and an honest answer about where the deploy key lives.
