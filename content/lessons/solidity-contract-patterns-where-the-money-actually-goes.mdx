Here is a table from a 2025 systematic review of smart contract incidents. The authors selected the
50 most severe real-world attacks between 2022 and 2025 — together over $1.09 billion in losses —
and mapped each to its root causes.<Cite src="sok-root-cause-losses" />

| Vulnerability category | Incidents | Total losses (USD) |
|---|---|---|
| Access control | 13 | 417,950,000 |
| Price manipulation | 13 | 279,750,000 |
| Business logic flaws | 5 | 177,400,000 |
| **Reentrancy** | **7** | **118,501,279** |
| Input validation | 6 | 41,850,000 |
| Rounding error | 4 | 36,900,000 |
| Integer underflow | 2 | 11,800,000 |
| Dangerous delegatecall | 2 | 4,800,000 |
| Governance | 2 | 4,673,500 |
| Storage collisions | 1 | 355,000 |

Reentrancy is fourth by value.<Cite src="sok-root-cause-losses" /> The two categories above it —
access control and price manipulation — account for a combined $697.7 million in that
dataset.<Cite src="sok-root-cause-losses" />

Now count how many lessons you have read about reentrancy, in this module and everywhere else, and
how many about who is allowed to call `setOracle`.

<Aside kind="note">
Read the scope before you quote the numbers. This is 50 *selected severe* incidents on Ethereum and
related networks over a four-year window, not a census of all losses, and the authors chose them for
severity.<Cite src="sok-root-cause-losses" /> A different selection would move the totals. What does
not move is the ordering and the size of the gap: access control is roughly 3.5× reentrancy by value
here and nearly double by incident count.
</Aside>

## Why the curriculum is inverted

Reentrancy is the most *teachable* bug ever written. It has a mechanism you can draw, a stack you
can trace, a fix you can express as one moved line, and a famous origin story. Every property that
makes something good pedagogy, it has.

Access control failure has none of them. There is no mechanism. There is no trace. The bug is that a
line is not there, and the line that is not there is not visible in a diff, a stack trace, or a
disassembly. You cannot draw the absence of a modifier.

That is the whole explanation for the inversion, and it is worth saying plainly because it means the
imbalance will not fix itself. Curricula optimise for explicability; incidents do not.

## What the incidents actually look like

The same review makes a second finding that reframes the first: **incidents are rarely single-shot
failures.** Only 19 of the 50 exploits hinged on merely a code defect; the rest were chains of two
or more weaknesses, or contained a human factor that enabled the
incident.<Cite src="sok-root-cause-losses" /> The authors call these exploit chains and build a
four-tier root-cause framework around them — protocol logic design, lifecycle and governance,
external dependencies, and classic smart contract
vulnerabilities.<Cite src="sok-root-cause-losses" />

Tier two is the one worth staring at. Twelve of the incidents originate in failures of the human and
operational processes surrounding a contract, and the paper notes this tier is almost entirely absent
from traditional, code-centric vulnerability taxonomies while being a dominant cause of
failure.<Cite src="sok-root-cause-losses" /> The examples it gives are not exotic: a bridge exploit
caused by a clumsy upgrade procedure, an incomplete upgrade, bugs introduced in new code, and
retained privileges belonging to former developers.<Cite src="sok-root-cause-losses" /> The paper's
own summary of the access-control cases is that they involve exploit methods beyond mere coding
flaws — including, in its parenthetical, the lack or misplacement of a
`require(msg.sender == owner)` check.<Cite src="sok-root-cause-losses" />

So the recurring shapes are these four, and none of them is clever:

**A missing modifier.** A function that should have been gated and was not, usually one added late,
usually an administrative helper.

**An unprotected initializer.** A contract deployed and initialized in separate transactions, or an
implementation behind proxies whose own `initialize` was never disabled. The final lesson of this
module is entirely about this case.

**A privilege nobody revoked.** The developer who left, the deploy key still in the admin role, the
migration script's address that was never removed. This is a process failure that presents as a
contract vulnerability.

**A key held by the wrong kind of address.** An externally owned account holding
`DEFAULT_ADMIN_ROLE` — which, as you know, can grant itself every other
role.<Cite src="oz-docs-access-control" /> The contract is correct; the operational reality is not.

## Why tooling does not catch it

A static analyser can find reentrancy because reentrancy has a syntactic signature: state written
after an external call. It cannot find a missing authorization check, because **there is no
specification of who should be allowed.** `setFeeRecipient` with no modifier and `mint` with no
modifier look identical to a tool; one of them is a catastrophic bug and the other is how a public
faucet works. The difference lives in a design document, or in somebody's head.

That has one important consequence for how you test.

Positive tests — "the owner can pause" — are the ones everyone writes, and they pass equally well on
a contract with no access control at all. The test that finds this bug class is the **negative** one:
for every privileged function, a random address calling it must revert, with the specific error.

```solidity
function test_setOracle_revertsForStranger() public {
    vm.prank(address(0xBEEF));
    vm.expectRevert(
        abi.encodeWithSelector(
            IAccessControl.AccessControlUnauthorizedAccount.selector,
            address(0xBEEF),
            ADMIN_ROLE
        )
    );
    pool.setOracle(address(1));
}
```

Two things make this stronger than it looks. It asserts the *specific* custom error rather than "any
revert," so it fails if the function starts reverting for an unrelated reason and stops actually
testing authorization.<Cite src="oz-docs-access-api" /> And it is mechanically derivable from the
authorization table you built in the previous lesson — one test per row. A table with fifteen rows
gives you fifteen tests, and a privileged function that appears in neither the table nor the test
file is the finding.

<Aside kind="warn">
The failure mode this catches at the system level: OpenZeppelin's docs note that even when an
`AccessManager` defines permissions for a target function, they are not applied if the managed
contract is not using the `restricted` modifier for that function, or if its manager is a different
one.<Cite src="oz-docs-access-control" /> Your permission configuration can be complete and correct
and still enforce nothing. Only a call that actually reverts on-chain proves the gate exists.
</Aside>

## Pausable, and the part OpenZeppelin deliberately leaves to you

`Pausable` provides two modifiers, `whenNotPaused` and `whenPaused`; a `paused()` view; internal
`_pause()` and `_unpause()`; `Paused` and `Unpaused` events; and two custom errors, `EnforcedPause`
when the contract is paused and `ExpectedPause` when it is not.<Cite src="oz-docs-utils-api" />

Read the documentation's own note carefully, because it contains the bug: the module makes the
modifiers available, and **they will not be pausable by simply including this module, only once the
modifiers are put in place.**<Cite src="oz-docs-utils-api" /> Inheriting `Pausable` and shipping
changes nothing about your contract's behaviour. It is a toolkit, not a feature.

And the sharper edge: `_pause()` and `_unpause()` are `internal` and carry **no
authorization**.<Cite src="oz-docs-utils-api" /> The library cannot know who should hold the switch,
so it declines to guess. Exposing them is your code:

```solidity
function pause() external onlyRole(PAUSER_ROLE) { _pause(); }
function unpause() external onlyRole(DEFAULT_ADMIN_ROLE) { _unpause(); }
```

Write that wrapper without the modifier and you have shipped a public kill switch. It compiles, it
passes every test that pauses as the admin, and the negative test above is what finds it.

## A pause switch is a disclosed trust assumption

Here is the uncomfortable framing. **The ability to halt a protocol is exactly the capability an
attacker wants**, and exactly the capability a user did not agree to when they deposited. It is not a
neutral safety feature that you add and forget; it is a permanent statement about who controls
whether people can move their money.

Treat it as a disclosed trust assumption, which means four decisions made in advance and in public.

**Who holds it, and behind what address type.** An externally owned account holding the pause key is
a single point at which your protocol stops. A multisig is the minimum for anything holding real
value. `AccessManager` offers the same capability at protocol scale through
`setTargetClosed(target, true)`, which makes all calls to restricted functions on a target revert
without altering any permissions or delays — described as an incident-response mode for temporarily
shutting a contract down.<Cite src="oz-docs-access-control" /> Same power, same question, one
address.

**Pause and unpause are different capabilities.** This asymmetry is the single most useful design
rule on this page. Pausing must be fast, because incidents are fast, so it belongs with a broad set
of holders including — reasonably — your auditors or a monitoring service. Unpausing must be
deliberate, because resuming a protocol mid-incident is how a partial loss becomes a total one, so it
belongs with governance. Bundle them into one role and you have handed anyone who takes that key the
ability to stop *and start* your protocol at a time of their choosing. Split them and a compromised
pause key costs you downtime only.

**Which functions stay open while paused.** The default of "everything" is almost always wrong. If
users cannot withdraw during a pause, then your emergency response is indistinguishable from a freeze
of customer funds, and it will be reported as one. The Solidity documentation's own fail-safe
recommendation lands in the same place: a contract that detects a problem should switch into a mode
that disables most features, hands control to a fixed and trusted third party, or **just converts the
contract into a simple "give me back my Ether" contract**.<Cite src="solidity-docs-security" /> Take
that last clause literally. Deposits and swaps pause; withdrawals usually should not.

**What the exit path is.** Write down, before launch, what conditions end the pause and who decides.
A pause with no defined exit is a protocol shutdown with better public relations, and users can tell
the difference within about a week.

<Aside kind="stop">
A pause is not free even when it works. Halting a lending market stops liquidations while prices keep
moving, which can turn a contained bug into insolvency. Halting an AMM strands liquidity providers
through a volatile window. The question is never "should we be able to pause" in the abstract — it is
"which specific functions, and what breaks downstream while they are off." Answer it per function,
not per contract.
</Aside>

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="pausability">
A staking contract. The team has just added an emergency stop after an audit recommended one.

```solidity
contract Staking is Pausable, AccessControl {
    bytes32 public constant OPERATOR_ROLE = keccak256("OPERATOR_ROLE");

    function pause() external { _pause(); }

    function unpause() external onlyRole(OPERATOR_ROLE) { _unpause(); }

    function stake(uint256 amount) external whenNotPaused { }
    function unstake(uint256 amount) external whenNotPaused { }
    function claimRewards() external whenNotPaused { }
    function setRewardRate(uint256 r) external onlyRole(OPERATOR_ROLE) { }
}
```

Find every problem, ranked by what it costs.

<Answer>
**One — `pause()` is public and ungated. Anyone can halt the protocol, permanently in effect.**
`_pause()` is internal with no authorization by design; the wrapper was supposed to add
it.<Cite src="oz-docs-utils-api" /> An attacker calls `pause()`, and now nobody can stake, unstake, or
claim. They can call it again the instant an operator unpauses, for the cost of one transaction, so
the operator cannot win the race. This is a total denial of service on user funds, executable by
anyone, and it costs less than a dollar. It is also precisely the shape the loss data keeps
producing: not a clever exploit, one missing modifier.<Cite src="sok-root-cause-losses" />

**Two — `unstake` is gated by `whenNotPaused`, so a pause freezes user funds.** Even with problem one
fixed, this design means the emergency response and the nightmare scenario are the same state. Users
cannot exit during exactly the window in which they most want to. The Solidity docs' fail-safe
guidance points the other way: the degraded mode should tend toward a "give me back my Ether"
contract.<Cite src="solidity-docs-security" /> Remove `whenNotPaused` from `unstake`. Keep it on
`stake` — stopping new deposits into a possibly-broken contract is the actual point.

**Three — pause and unpause are asymmetric in the wrong direction.** Anyone can pause; a single
`OPERATOR_ROLE` can unpause. Correct is the reverse of both halves: a *reasonably broad* set can
pause (operator, guardian, monitoring service, the security firm on retainer), and a *narrower,
slower* authority can unpause. As written, the fast action is unrestricted and the deliberate action
is the one behind a role.

**Four — `claimRewards` blocked while paused is a policy decision nobody made.** It may be right;
rewards accrual during an incident is a real question. But it should be a decision with a written
reason, not a modifier copied down the file.

**Five, structural — there is no negative test.** Every problem above is caught by one test per
privileged function asserting that a stranger's call reverts with the expected custom error. A test
that `pause()` reverts for a random address would have failed on line one of this contract. That
suite is derived mechanically from the authorization table, and its absence is why this shipped.

**The fixed shape:**

```solidity
function pause() external onlyRole(GUARDIAN_ROLE) { _pause(); }
function unpause() external onlyRole(DEFAULT_ADMIN_ROLE) { _unpause(); }

function stake(uint256 amount) external whenNotPaused { }
function unstake(uint256 amount) external { }              // always open
```

With `DEFAULT_ADMIN_ROLE` held by a multisig, since whoever holds it can grant themselves every other
role in this contract.<Cite src="oz-docs-access-control" />
</Answer>
</Check>

## The disclosure obligation

One more thing follows from calling this a trust assumption. If your documentation says "audited" and
"non-custodial" and does not say "a 2-of-3 multisig can stop withdrawals," it is misleading, and the
day you use the switch is the day everyone finds out at once.

The honest version is a short section in your docs listing every privileged action, the address that
holds it, the delay, and what users should watch. That is the same table from the previous lesson,
published. It costs an afternoon, it makes you notice the rows you cannot defend, and it is the
difference between a governance action and a scandal.

## Where this pays off

**Factories and clones**, the next two lessons, add the initializer to the list of things that can be
unprotected — and an uninitialized implementation contract is an access-control bug wearing a
deployment-pattern costume.

**Proxies and upgrades**, later in this track, is the maximal version of this lesson: the upgrade key
can replace all of your code, so it is the most dangerous row in any authorization table, and the
review's own finding is that proxy and upgradeability patterns introduce severe risk when admin keys
are compromised or the governance controlling upgrades is
subverted.<Cite src="sok-root-cause-losses" />

**Track 06's threat modeling** starts from the artifact this lesson insists on — the enumerated list
of privileged actions and their holders — and turns it into attacker scenarios.

**Track 14's monitoring and incident response** is where the pause switch becomes operational: who is
paged, who has the key at 3am, how the decision is made, and how quickly a scheduled timelock
operation is noticed. A pause switch nobody is watching for is a capability you do not actually have.
