Here is the entire authorisation logic in OpenZeppelin's `Pausable`:

```solidity
function _pause() internal virtual whenNotPaused {
    _paused = true;
    emit Paused(_msgSender());
}
```

There is none. `whenNotPaused` checks that the contract is not *already* paused; it says nothing
about who is calling. `_pause` is `internal`, so nothing outside the contract can reach it — until
you expose it, and at that moment the entire question of who may halt your protocol becomes a
decision you make, in a function you write.<Cite src="oz-pausable-source-v570" />

The contract's own documentation is blunt about the gap. It makes `whenNotPaused` and `whenPaused`
available, and warns that functions are "not be pausable by simply including this module" — only once
you put the modifiers in place.<Cite src="oz-pausable-source-v570" />

This lesson is about that boundary — everything on the far side of it, why the library refuses to
cross, and the one place where people assume it has crossed and it has not.

## What it deliberately does not provide

The absences are not gaps waiting to be filled. Sorted by *why* they cannot be filled by a library:

**Because it requires market design.** No DEX or AMM. No stablecoin. A constant-product curve is
twelve lines of Solidity; the reason there is no `OpenZeppelin/AMM.sol` is that the twelve lines are
the easy part and the fee policy, the oracle exposure, the liquidity incentives and the failure
behaviour under stress are the product.

**Because it requires off-chain infrastructure.** No oracles or price feeds. No keepers or
automation. No bundler for the ERC-4337 accounts it ships. No gateway operator for the ERC-7786
bridges it ships — the standard defines an interface to a gateway; somebody has to *be* the
gateway.<Cite src="eip-7786" /> A contract cannot fetch. Everything in this category needs a party
that observes the world and posts about it, and a library is not a party.

**Because it requires organisational judgement.** Not your access-control model. Not who may pause.
Not who may upgrade. Not what the timelock delay is. Not what happens when the key holder leaves the
company. The docs frame access control as a decision to be made — who can perform each of the actions
on your system — and then give you three mechanisms to express whatever you
decide.<Cite src="oz-docs-access-control" />

**Because it is somebody else's tool.** No deployment scripts, no testing framework. Foundry and
Hardhat exist.

The pattern across all four categories is the same: the library ships the part that is a *function of
its inputs*, and stops at the part that is a function of the world or of your organisation. That is a
principled line, and once you see it you can predict what will and will not be in the next release.

## Their audit is not your audit

The library's security reputation is real and it is precisely scoped. The scope is legible, because
the repository keeps an audit table.<Cite src="oz-audits-index" />

Read three columns of it.

**Auditor.** Every report from October 2023 onward lists the auditor as
OpenZeppelin.<Cite src="oz-audits-index" /> That is a firm auditing a library maintained by the same
firm. It is not a scandal — OpenZeppelin's audit practice is one of the strongest in the industry, and
this is a normal arrangement for a vendor's own open-source code. It is also not the same evidence as
an independent review, and the table is the thing that tells you which one you have.

**Scope.** From v5.1 onwards, the scope column reads "v5.1 Changes", "v5.2 Changes", "v5.3
Changes", and so on.<Cite src="oz-audits-index" /> These are **delta audits**. Each one reviews what
the release changed, on the reasonable assumption that what came before was reviewed when it landed.
Two entries break the pattern and are worth knowing: October 2025 covers `SafeERC20`, `ECDSA` and
`SignatureChecker` specifically, and November 2025 covers `RLP` — targeted reviews of individual
components.<Cite src="oz-audits-index" />

The last time anyone audited the whole thing with scope "Everything" was **LevelK in October 2018, at
v2.0.0**, and before that New Alchemy in March 2017 at v1.0.4.<Cite src="oz-audits-index" /> The
formal verification table is smaller still: three Certora engagements, the most recent in May 2022 at
v4.7.0, covering a named handful of contracts.<Cite src="oz-audits-index" />

**Version.** The newest per-release report is February 2026 for v5.6.0.<Cite src="oz-audits-index" />
5.7.0 shipped on 29 July 2026 and sits on the npm `dev` tag — final and feature-complete, not yet
audited — while `latest` still resolves to 5.6.1.<Cite src="oz-npm-contracts" /><Cite src="oz-repo-readme" />
So "I use the audited version of OpenZeppelin" is a claim with a version number attached, and if you
installed from git you may not be making it.

### And the library is not where the losses are

Systematic mapping of real-world smart-contract attacks to root causes puts **access-control failure
far ahead of reentrancy** by value lost — first among contract-vulnerability categories, and still
multiples of reentrancy in the wider counts that also take in stolen keys and
phishing.<Cite src="sok-root-cause-losses" /><Cite src="arxiv-audit-gap-2026" /> The concrete
bugs in that category are mundane: a missing modifier, an unprotected initializer, an
over-privileged owner key, a compromised one.

None of those is a bug in `Ownable`. `Ownable` works. The failure is in the arrows — which address
holds the privilege, what else that address can do, and what happens when it is
lost.<Cite src="sok-root-cause-losses" />

Two arrows the library documents and people still get wrong:

**`renounceOwnership()` is public.** It is guarded by `onlyOwner`, and its NatSpec says exactly what
it does: leaves the contract without an owner, thereby disabling any functionality only available to
the owner.<Cite src="oz-ownable-source-v570" /> It is there on purpose — decentralising by burning the key
is a legitimate move. It is also one transaction away from every contract that inherits `Ownable`,
and it is irreversible.

**`DEFAULT_ADMIN_ROLE` is its own admin.** The source carries a `WARNING` block saying so: it has
permission to grant and revoke itself, extra precautions should be taken to secure accounts holding
it, and `AccessControlDefaultAdminRules` exists to enforce
more.<Cite src="oz-accesscontrol-v570" /> Its value is `0x00`, so it is also the default admin of
every role you declare unless you call `_setRoleAdmin`.<Cite src="oz-accesscontrol-v570" /> A
carefully separated `MINTER_ROLE`, `PAUSER_ROLE` and `BURNER_ROLE` scheme collapses to one key if that
key holds the default admin role — which it usually does, because the deployer granted it to itself in
the constructor.

## The Wizard, and what it hands you

The Contracts Wizard generates a compiling contract from a checkbox selection — token type, mintable,
pausable, votes, upgradeable and so on — and now covers several ecosystems beyond Solidity, including
Cairo, Stellar, Stylus, Polkadot and Uniswap hooks.<Cite src="oz-wizard-tool" />

It is genuinely the best available answer to one specific question: *what does idiomatic composition
against the current version look like?* That is a question that is otherwise hard to answer, because
most examples online are written against 4.x. 5.0.0 removed every preset contract from the library
explicitly in favour of the Wizard, so it is not a side project — it is where the maintainers put the
worked examples.<Cite src="oz-changelog" />

What it emits is scaffolding. Tick *pausable* and you get `Pausable` plus a public `pause()` gated by
a role. Tick *access control* and you get roles and a constructor that grants them. Tick *upgradeable*
and you get an initializer and a UUPS authorisation hook. Each tick is correct code and a new arrow:
an address that can do something, which you have not chosen yet.

The exercise that turns generated code into a design is short, and it is the module's reflection
prompt for a reason. For every privileged function the Wizard emitted, write down two things:

1. **The address type that will hold it** — an EOA, a multisig, a timelock, an `AccessManager`. Not a
   placeholder. Not "the deployer for now".
2. **The loss story** — what happens if that key is lost, and separately what happens if it is
   compromised. Those are different failures with different mitigations, and a design that survives
   one often fails the other.

Then delete every option you could not justify, and say what you gave up. A pause switch you cannot
staff is worse than no pause switch, because it is a
[disclosed centralisation vector](/c/pause-is-centralisation) that nobody is watching.

### The inventory, filled in

Here is what that looks like for a token with mint, pause, roles and UUPS upgradeability — five
capabilities, each traced to the OpenZeppelin mechanism that grants it.

| Capability | Where it comes from | Blast radius | Lost key | Compromised key |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Own the contract | `onlyOwner` on `Ownable`<Cite src="oz-ownable-source-v570" /> | Whatever you gated | Every owner-only function is dead | Attacker has all of them |
| Administer roles | `DEFAULT_ADMIN_ROLE`, value `0x00`, its own admin and the default admin of every role<Cite src="oz-accesscontrol-v570" /> | **All** roles below | No role can ever be granted or revoked again | Attacker grants themselves every other role |
| Mint | A role-gated function calling `_mint` | Total supply | Supply is fixed forever | Unbounded issuance |
| Pause | Your `pause()` calling the unauthorised internal `_pause`<Cite src="oz-pausable-source-v570" /> | Every `whenNotPaused` function | Cannot stop an incident | Attacker halts the protocol, possibly permanently |
| Upgrade | The authorisation hook on the UUPS implementation | Everything, including all of the above | Frozen at the current implementation | Attacker replaces the logic and owns the state |

Three things fall out of the table that do not fall out of the code.

**The rows are not independent.** The bottom row subsumes every row above it: whoever can upgrade can
ship an implementation that grants themselves the rest. Splitting mint and pause across two multisigs
is theatre if one EOA holds the upgrade key.

**The second row quietly subsumes the middle three.** `DEFAULT_ADMIN_ROLE` is `0x00` and is the admin
of every role you declare unless you called `_setRoleAdmin`.<Cite src="oz-accesscontrol-v570" /> A
role-separation design collapses to a single key by default, and the collapse is invisible in the
source — it is an absent call, not a present one.

**"Lost" and "compromised" want opposite mitigations.** Guarding against loss pushes you toward more
holders and recovery paths. Guarding against compromise pushes you toward fewer holders, delays and
irreversibility. A multisig behind a timelock is the usual compromise, and it is a compromise: it
makes the emergency pause slower, which is precisely when you wanted it fast. Deciding which failure
you fear more is a product decision, and it is one nobody else can make for you.

## Where this leaves you

The module has been one argument in seven parts.

**It is a library, so it composes into your code and inherits your mistakes.** Nothing runs it.
Nothing deploys it. Every privileged address in a system built on it was chosen by a person.

**One version number covers several different promises.** The audited library, `draft-` files with no
compatibility guarantee, mocks that never ship, a community repository outside the bug
bounty, and three npm tags that mean three different things.<Cite src="oz-community-contracts" />

**The upgradeable package is generated, and the storage model changed once.** That change is a
data-corruption class of bug across a major version, invisible to the compiler, and the only thing
standing between you and it is a validator you have to configure and run.

**The frontier moved.** Tokens are finished; accounts and crosschain are where the releases go. Both
are on-chain halves of systems whose other halves you have to choose and trust.

**And the boundary is the same everywhere.** The library does the part that is a function of its
inputs. Who holds the keys, what the protocol is for, and whose gateway you believe are yours.

Track 05 gives you the tooling to hold all of this to account: pin the version, run the upgrade
validator in CI, measure the gas claims rather than quoting them, and write the test that proves the
pause key is where you said it was.
