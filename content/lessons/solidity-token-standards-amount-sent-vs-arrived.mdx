A vault has been live for six weeks. Deposits and withdrawals both work. Then a withdrawal reverts
for insufficient balance, and the operator checks the numbers: the contract's internal ledger says
it owes 1,000,000 units and `balanceOf(vault)` says it holds 980,000.

Nobody stole anything. There was no exploit transaction to find. The contract has been recording a
number that was never true, once per deposit, for six weeks — and the first person to notice is the
last person to withdraw.

This lesson is about the gap between the amount a function was asked to move and the amount that
actually arrived, and about the second, stranger gap: balances that change while nothing calls the
token at all.

## The first gap: fees on transfer

Some tokens deduct on every transfer. The catalogue names STA and PAXG as tokens that take a fee,
and — the part that matters more — names USDT and USDC as tokens that do not currently charge a fee
*but may do so in the future*.<Cite src="weird-erc20" />

Read that second clause with the previous lessons in hand. Both are upgradeable, so "does this token
charge a fee" is not a property you can check once and cache; it is a property of whichever
implementation is behind the proxy at the moment your transaction executes.<Cite src="weird-erc20" />
A token that is compliant today can become fee-on-transfer by an administrative action you will not
be told about.

The catalogue also records what this has already cost: the STA transfer fee was used to drain
several Balancer pools.<Cite src="weird-erc20" />

### Worked example: how a vault becomes insolvent on purpose-built arithmetic

The token takes 2% on transfer. The vault credits the requested amount.

**Subgoal: establish the lie.** Alice deposits 100.

```text
  transferFrom(alice, vault, 100)   -> vault receives 98, token burns 2
  shares[alice] = 100                  <- credited from the argument, not the outcome
  balanceOf(vault) = 98
```

The contract's ledger and the token's ledger now disagree by 2, and nothing anywhere reports it. The
transfer returned `true`. The event says 100 left Alice.

**Subgoal: repeat until somebody notices.** Bob and Carol each deposit 100.

**Subgoal: pay the early users with the late users' money.** Alice withdraws 100 and gets it —
there are 294 units sitting there. Bob withdraws 100 and gets it; 94 remain. Carol asks for 100 and
the transfer reverts.

Name the shape precisely, because it is the general form and not a fee-on-transfer curiosity: **the
accounting error is a subsidy from the last withdrawer to every earlier one.** It is invisible while
deposits outpace withdrawals, and it surfaces as a single failing transaction belonging to somebody
who did nothing wrong. That is also why it is hard to detect in testing — a test that deposits and
withdraws in the same order, with the same user, never touches the failing path.

## The general fix: credit what arrived

Stop trusting the argument. Measure.

```solidity
uint256 before = token.balanceOf(address(this));
token.safeTransferFrom(msg.sender, address(this), amount);
uint256 received = token.balanceOf(address(this)) - before;
shares[msg.sender] += received;
```

This is not a workaround invented for this lesson. Uniswap V2's router carries a whole family of
`SupportingFeeOnTransferTokens` functions built on exactly this shape:<Cite src="uniswap-v2-router02-source" />

```solidity
uint balanceBefore = IERC20(path[path.length - 1]).balanceOf(to);
_swapSupportingFeeOnTransferTokens(path, to);
require(
    IERC20(path[path.length - 1]).balanceOf(to).sub(balanceBefore) >= amountOutMin,
    'UniswapV2Router: INSUFFICIENT_OUTPUT_AMOUNT'
);
```

There is a design detail in that family worth more than the code. The ordinary
`swapExactTokensForTokens` returns `uint[] memory amounts` — the computed path amounts. The
fee-supporting variant returns **nothing**.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-router02-source" /> You cannot quote
what you cannot predict, so the router stops pretending to and gives you a floor check instead. When
you are designing an interface around uncertain amounts, that is the honest move: replace the
promised figure with a bound the caller supplies.

### What the delta pattern costs, and where it breaks

It is not free and it is not universal. Four caveats, in the order they bite.

**It costs two extra external calls.** Each `balanceOf` is an account access to the token plus a
storage read inside it. On the first touch that is 2,600 plus 2,100 gas; on the second, with both
warm, 100 plus 100 — measured on forge 1.7.1 with solc 0.8.36.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" />
The cold reads are usually paid anyway because the transfer itself touches the same slots, so the
marginal cost of measuring is small. Small, not zero.

**It assumes `balanceOf` is honest and stable across the call.** If the token's balance can change
for a reason other than your transfer while your transfer is executing — a rebase, a hook that moves
additional value, a reentrant deposit from another user — the delta attributes that change to your
depositor.

**It breaks when one contract holds a shared balance.** Two accounting systems in the same contract,
or one contract holding both user deposits and protocol fees, measure a delta that includes anything
else that arrived in the same call. The delta answers "how much did this contract's balance change",
which is only the same as "how much did this user deposit" when nothing else can move.

**It is incompatible with reentrancy through the token.** If the transfer can call out — the next
three lessons' subject — an attacker can deposit inside your deposit, and the two deltas interleave.
Measure, and also guard.

## The second gap: balances that move on their own

A fee reduces what arrives. A rebase changes what you already have.

stETH is the token to reason about because its documentation is unusually direct. Balances are
recalculated when the Lido oracle reports the consensus-layer balance update — normally daily — and
the update lands on every address holding the token at that moment.<Cite src="lido-tokens-integration-guide" />
The rebase can in principle be negative, if validators are penalised or slashed, though the
documentation records that daily rebases have not been negative to
date.<Cite src="lido-tokens-integration-guide" />

The mechanism underneath is shares. A share is a unit of claim on the total ether the protocol
controls; the token's balance function converts shares to a balance using the current rate, and the
contract stores the sum of all shares to do it.<Cite src="lido-tokens-integration-guide" /> If that
sounds like the vault accounting from lessons nine through eleven, it is — the same construction,
arrived at for the same reason.

Now the consequence, which the documentation states as guidance: **when integrating stETH, store and
operate on shares rather than on public balances, because balances change on transfers, mints, burns
*and* rebases, while share balances change only on transfers, mints and
burns.**<Cite src="lido-tokens-integration-guide" />

That sentence is the whole integration rule, and it also tells you what breaks if you ignore it:

- **A stored amount goes stale.** "User deposited 100" is wrong tomorrow, in a direction that
  depends on validator performance.
- **An event-derived index drifts.** ERC-20 requires a `Transfer` event when tokens are transferred;
  a rebase is not a transfer, so an indexer reconstructing balances from `Transfer` logs diverges a
  little more every day.<Cite src="eip-20" /><Cite src="lido-tokens-integration-guide" /> Compare
  ERC-1155, which explicitly requires the queried balance and the event-derived balance to be
  identical — a guarantee ERC-20 never made.<Cite src="eip-1155" />
- **A cached balance is a bug with a clock.** The catalogue notes that systems caching token
  balances, Balancer and Uniswap v2 among them, operate on outdated information when balances move
  outside transfers, and that some pools had to be special-cased into the rebase procedure
  itself.<Cite src="weird-erc20" />

The two available fixes are the same two the rest of this module keeps producing: hold shares
(`transferShares` rather than `transfer`), or integrate the wrapped, non-rebasing version — wstETH,
whose balance does not move and whose value against stETH does
instead.<Cite src="lido-tokens-integration-guide" />

### Rounding is a third source of the same gap

There is a smaller effect in the same token that catches people who have already handled the big
one. Because a balance is computed from shares by integer division, transferring a *whole* balance
frequently leaves 1 to 2 wei behind on the sender, and the documentation warns that the error can
grow as the share rate grows.<Cite src="lido-tokens-integration-guide" />

So a function that reads `balanceOf(user)` and then transfers exactly that amount can fail its own
"balance is now zero" assertion. The recommended path is `transferShares`, which moves the unit the
accounting is actually kept in.<Cite src="lido-tokens-integration-guide" />

Note what this is: the amount sent is not the amount that arrived, produced by rounding rather than
by policy. Same failure, different cause, same fix — measure, or work in the unit that does not
round.

## The special case that is neither a fee nor a rebase

One more, because it defeats a naive delta check in a way that is worth seeing.

Compound's cUSDCv3 has a special case for `amount == type(uint256).max` in its transfer functions:
it transfers the user's whole balance rather than the enormous number you
passed.<Cite src="weird-erc20" /> The catalogue is explicit about the systems this breaks — vault
types that transfer a user-supplied amount and then credit the user with the same value in storage,
without checking what actually moved.<Cite src="weird-erc20" />

Think about which direction that goes. Fee-on-transfer delivers *less* than requested, which makes
the vault insolvent. This delivers less than the literal argument too — but the argument was
`type(uint256).max`, so a contract that credits the argument credits a user with the maximum
`uint256` on a deposit of whatever they happened to hold. That is not a rounding error; that is
minting the protocol's entire share supply to one address.

Same rule, and now for a second reason: credit the delta, never the argument.

## The gap you create yourself: decimals

The three gaps above are the token's doing. This one is yours, and it belongs here because it
produces the same symptom — a number in your storage that does not mean what the code assumes.

`decimals` is display metadata. It is OPTIONAL in the standard, other contracts MUST NOT expect it
to be present, and it does not appear in any arithmetic the token performs.<Cite src="eip-20" />
Balances are integers. A token with 6 decimals stores `1000000` for one unit and a token with 18
stores `1000000000000000000`, and the token itself never divides by anything.

The catalogue records the range you have to survive: USDC has 6, Gemini USD has 2, YAM-V2 has 24,
and it names the two distinct consequences — larger-than-expected precision loss at the low end, and
unexpected reverts from overflow at the high end.<Cite src="weird-erc20" />

**Subgoal: see where the factor has to live.** Suppose a vault takes USDC (6) and prices it against
an 18-decimal accounting unit at $1.00.

```text
  user deposits            1000000        1 USDC, in USDC's units
  internal unit            10**18         "one dollar" in the vault's units
  correct scale factor     10**12
```

**Subgoal: get it wrong in the cheap direction.** Omit the factor and one USDC is credited as
`0.000000000001` internal units. Harmless-looking, catastrophic in a market: the position rounds to
nothing and the user has deposited into a black hole.

**Subgoal: get it wrong in the expensive direction.** Apply the factor twice, or hard-code 18 for a
token that has 6, and one USDC is credited as a million dollars. That is the direction that becomes
an exploit rather than a support ticket, and it is the direction a test written by the same person
who wrote the bug does not catch, because both use the same constant.

The defensive shape is to read `decimals()` at integration time rather than hard-coding it — through
something like `SafeERC20.tryGetDecimals`, which treats a missing or implausible answer as failure
rather than as 18.<Cite src="oz-safe-erc20-source" /> And to hold one rule: **a raw balance is
meaningless without the address it came from.** Any function signature that takes an amount without
a token is a place where two scales can meet.

## The taxonomy, as an integration checklist

"ERC-20 compliant" is a spectrum, and the catalogue is the map. Each row below is a deviation with a
named example and the assumption it breaks — every entry drawn from the same
source.<Cite src="weird-erc20" />

| Deviation | Named examples | What it breaks |
|---|---|---|
| Returns no `bool` | USDT, BNB, OMG | The ABI decode in your caller |
| Returns `false` on success | Tether Gold | Any return-value discipline at all |
| Returns `false` instead of reverting | ZRX, EURS | Unchecked calls proceed on a failed transfer |
| Fee on transfer | STA, PAXG (and a future switch on USDT, USDC) | Credited amount ≠ received amount |
| Balance changes outside transfers | rebasing and airdropping designs | Stored amounts, cached balances, log-derived indexes |
| Upgradeable | USDC, USDT | Every behaviour you verified once |
| Blocklists | USDC, USDT | Liveness — funds can be frozen in your contract |
| Pausable | BNB, ZIL | Liveness, at the token owner's discretion |
| Non-18 decimals | USDC (6), Gemini USD (2), YAM-V2 (24) | Precision loss; overflow on the high side |
| Revert on zero-value transfer | LEND | Payout loops with a possibly-empty leg |
| Revert on zero-value approval | BNB | Approve-to-zero-then-set |
| Revert on approval to the zero address | OpenZeppelin-derived tokens | Burn-style and cleanup paths |
| Revert on transfer to the zero address | OpenZeppelin-derived tokens | Burning by transferring to `0x0` |
| Values capped below `uint96` | UNI, COMP | Unlimited approvals; large transfers |
| Multiple addresses for one balance | proxied tokens | Any per-address bookkeeping or allowlist |
| Flash-mintable | DAI | Total supply is not a bound during a transaction |
| Reentrant transfer | ERC-777 tokens | Checks-Effects-Interactions in your code |
| `type(uint256).max` means "everything" | cUSDCv3 | Crediting the argument |
| Malicious code in `name` | observed in the wild | Front ends that render metadata unescaped |

Two things to take from the table rather than from any single row.

**The deviations are independent.** A token can be upgradeable *and* blocklisting *and* six-decimal
*and* return no boolean, and USDT is three of those four. There is no "compliant / non-compliant"
bit to check.

**Most of the rows are liveness, not theft.** Blocklists, pausing and zero-value reverts do not let
anyone take your users' money; they let somebody else decide when your users can move it. That is a
different conversation with a product owner than "we might get hacked", and it is the one most teams
never have.

The rule that follows is unglamorous and is the actual deliverable of this lesson: **integration
safety is a per-token question, answered by reading the deployed source at the address you will
actually call.** Not the project's documentation, not the interface file, not a blog post listing
"the weird ones". The catalogue is a starting set of cases to test against, not an inventory of the
tokens that have them.<Cite src="weird-erc20" />

### How to actually answer it

The checks that fit in an afternoon, in the order that eliminates the most risk per minute:

**Fetch the deployed source at the address, not the project's repository.** If the address is a
proxy, follow it to the implementation and note that the answer has an expiry date, because whoever
holds the upgrade key can change it.<Cite src="weird-erc20" />

**Read three functions and nothing else at first**: `transfer`, `transferFrom`, `approve`. Their
return statements answer the previous lesson. Any arithmetic between the debit and the credit
answers this one. Any external call inside them answers the next three.

**Send one unit on a fork and diff the balances.** A single test that transfers 1 unit between two
addresses and asserts `receivedDelta == sentDelta` settles fee-on-transfer empirically, in less time
than reading about it takes.

**Grep the implementation for an admin surface**: pause, blocklist, freeze, upgrade, and any
`onlyOwner` path that touches balances. Those are the liveness rows of the table, and they are a
disclosure obligation to your users rather than a bug to fix.

**Write down the answer next to the address in your config.** The next person to add a token will
copy whatever pattern they find there.

A lending market credits collateral using the measured-delta pattern:

```solidity
uint256 before = token.balanceOf(address(this));
token.safeTransferFrom(msg.sender, address(this), amount);
collateral[msg.sender] += token.balanceOf(address(this)) - before;
```

The team is confident this handles fee-on-transfer tokens, and it does. They now want to list stETH.
Predict what goes wrong, and say whether the delta pattern is the thing that fails.

<Answer>
**The deposit itself is fine.** The delta is measured across the transfer, so whatever arrives —
including the 1-to-2-wei rounding shortfall that stETH's integer division
produces — is what gets credited.<Cite src="lido-tokens-integration-guide" /> The delta pattern does
its job.

**The stored number then stops being true, and nothing re-measures it.** `collateral[user]` is an
amount, frozen at deposit time. The next daily oracle report changes what a stETH balance means, for
every holder, with no transfer and no per-holder
event.<Cite src="lido-tokens-integration-guide" /> The market's records and the token's records
diverge from that moment and keep diverging.

**Both directions are bad.** If rebases are positive, the market holds more stETH than it has
credited — the surplus is unattributed, and whoever finds a path to claim it takes yield that
belonged to depositors. If a rebase is ever negative, the market has credited more than it holds and
is undercollateralised across every stETH position at once, without any position having moved.

**So the delta pattern is not what fails.** It answers "how much arrived", which is a
transfer-time question. The failure is that collateral is a *standing* quantity and the token
redefines standing quantities on a schedule. The two fixes are the ones Lido documents: store shares
and convert at read time, or list wstETH, whose balance does not
move.<Cite src="lido-tokens-integration-guide" />

The transferable lesson is that "measure the delta" and "store the right unit" are answers to
different questions, and a token can require both.
</Answer>

## Where this pays off

**The next three lessons** add the missing hazard: transfers that hand execution to somebody else
mid-flight. Everything here assumed the token did its work and returned. ERC-721, ERC-1155 and
ERC-777 do not.

**Lessons nine through eleven** take the share accounting that Lido's documentation describes and
make it the whole subject — ERC-4626 is that construction, standardised, with an arithmetic failure
at the bottom of it.

**The DeFi track** meets every row of the table again as a live integration decision: which assets a
market lists is a risk question, and most of the risk is in the rows about liveness rather than the
rows about theft.
