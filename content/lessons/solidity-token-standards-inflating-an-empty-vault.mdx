A vault has one share outstanding and holds 100 tokens. Its price per share is therefore 100 tokens.

Nothing about that is a bug. It is arithmetic on two numbers, both of which are true. The bug is
that an attacker chose both numbers, one of them without calling the vault at all, and the person
who is about to deposit 100 tokens is going to receive zero shares for them.

This lesson builds that state step by step, then shows why it is not a property of empty vaults
specifically.

## The donation surface

Recall the default from the previous lesson. OpenZeppelin's reference `totalAssets()` is:<Cite src="oz-erc4626-source" />

```solidity
function totalAssets() public view virtual returns (uint256) {
    return IERC20(asset()).balanceOf(address(this));
}
```

The vault's view of its own holdings is a call to the asset token, asking what the token's mapping
says. Which means: **anyone who can change that mapping entry can change the vault's accounting**,
and lesson one established exactly who can do that — anybody holding the token, by calling
`transfer(vaultAddress, amount)`.

Enumerate what such a transfer requires:

- No call to the vault. `deposit` is not invoked, `Deposit` is not emitted, no shares are minted.
- No privilege. `transfer` is permissionless by construction.<Cite src="eip-20" />
- No flash loan, no oracle, no MEV bundle, no reentrancy.
- No notification. A plain ERC-20 transfer executes entirely inside the token contract; the vault's
  code does not run, so it cannot observe, reject or react.

That last point is the one that makes this different from every other attack in this module. There
is nothing to guard, because there is no call to guard. The vault finds out the next time somebody
reads `totalAssets()`, which is to say: during the victim's deposit.

## The naive conversion, and where it breaks

A vault written without the mitigations computes shares like this:

```solidity
function _toShares(uint256 assets) internal view returns (uint256) {
    uint256 supply = totalSupply();
    return supply == 0 ? assets : assets * supply / totalAssets();
}
```

Two branches, and the first one is the tell. When supply is zero there is no rate to compute — zero
over zero — so the code invents one: one share per asset. That special case is not arbitrary; it is
the only thing you can do without a defined rate. It is also a promise that whoever deposits first
gets to set the rate.

## Worked example: the full attack

The asset has 18 decimals. The vault is freshly deployed and empty. A victim is about to deposit
100 tokens, which is `100e18` in the token's units, and their transaction is in the public mempool.

**Subgoal: take 100% of the share supply for a price of nothing.** The attacker front-runs with a
deposit of **1 wei**.

```text
  supply == 0, so shares = assets = 1
  totalSupply()  = 1
  totalAssets()  = 1
  attacker holds 1 of 1 shares
```

They now own the entire vault. The cost was one wei plus gas, and OpenZeppelin's write-up notes the
same thing: front-running the initial deposit with even 1 wei garners a 100% share of the
pool.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-inflation-defense" />

**Subgoal: move the price without minting anything.** The attacker sends `100e18` of the asset
directly to the vault's address.

```text
  totalSupply()  = 1                (unchanged — no deposit call)
  totalAssets()  = 100e18 + 1       (the token's mapping says so)
  price per share = 100e18 + 1
```

**Subgoal: round the victim's deposit to zero.** The victim's `deposit(100e18)` now executes.

```text
  shares = 100e18 * totalSupply() / totalAssets()
         = 100e18 * 1 / (100e18 + 1)
         = 0                        (integer division, floor)
```

The victim's assets are transferred in. They receive **zero shares**. No revert, no warning — the
deposit succeeded, and the receipt they hold is a balance of nothing. OpenZeppelin's article
describes the identical outcome in round numbers: the deposit's share calculation comes out at zero
because of how shares are calculated against the donated
balance.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-inflation-defense" />

**Subgoal: redeem the only share.** The vault now holds `200e18 + 1` against a supply of 1.

```text
  assets = 1 * totalAssets() / totalSupply()
         = 1 * (200e18 + 1) / 1
         = 200e18 + 1
```

Settle the ledger:

```text
  attacker paid       1 wei  +  100e18 (the donation)
  attacker received   200e18 + 1 wei
  attacker net        +100e18, minus gas
  victim  paid        100e18
  victim  received    nothing
```

The donation was never a gift. It was working capital, returned in full alongside the victim's
deposit, because the attacker held every share that existed.

## It is a front-running attack, not an opportunistic one

The attacker does not deploy this and wait. They watch for the first deposit and size the donation
against it. OpenZeppelin states both halves: an attacker would typically wait for a user to make the
first deposit and front-run that operation, and the size of the donation required to manipulate the
vault is equivalent to the size of the deposit being
attacked.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-docs" />

Two things follow that people get wrong in opposite directions.

**It is not free.** The attacker must hold and risk an amount comparable to the victim's deposit for
the duration of one transaction. Against a small first deposit the attack is cheap; against a large
one it needs capital.

**It is not confined to the first deposit.** OpenZeppelin's own framing is that this is essentially a
problem of slippage, and that new vaults are at the *greatest* risk rather than the only
risk.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-source" /><Cite src="oz-erc4626-inflation-defense" /> Which brings us to
the generalisation that matters most.

## The real precondition is scale, not emptiness

Look again at the division that produced zero:

```text
  shares = assets * totalSupply() / totalAssets()
```

This floors to zero whenever `assets * totalSupply()` is smaller than `totalAssets()` — that is,
whenever the deposit is worth **less than one share**. Emptiness is one way to get there. A high
price per share is another, and the price per share is exactly what a donation moves.

Work an example with no empty vault anywhere in it. A vault holds `1e21` assets against `1e3`
shares, so one share costs `1e18`:

Anything under `1e18` gets nothing. Anything not an exact multiple loses the remainder. That is the
same defect as the headline attack, with the vault holding a million tokens and a healthy-looking
history.

OpenZeppelin's guide gives the general rule in user-facing terms: deposit `1e9` shares' worth and the
rounding costs at most a millionth of a percent, deposit 10 shares' worth and you could lose 10%,
and deposit less than one share's worth and you get zero shares — "you basically made a
donation".<Cite src="oz-erc4626-docs" />

So the mitigation "seed the vault with a first deposit" needs to be read carefully. It raises the
cost of the specific opening move. It does not remove the mechanism, because the mechanism is that a
sufficiently high price per share rounds small deposits to nothing, and a donation is still
available to raise it.

## The four mitigations, with their costs

OpenZeppelin evaluated the ecosystem's options before designing theirs, and the write-up is unusually
honest about the drawbacks of each.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-inflation-defense" />

**A router with slippage checks.** The user goes through a wrapper that takes custody and verifies
the shares received. Straightforward, and OpenZeppelin's own assessment is that it may only
circumvent the issue rather than address it: it costs more gas because the router takes custody
first, and it introduces an additional trust assumption, since the router becomes a critical
component with permission to spend user tokens.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-inflation-defense" /> The
reference implementation's caution points users at exactly this class of wrapper as *their* defence,
independent of what the vault does.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-source" />

**Internal accounting.** Track assets in the vault's own variable so donated tokens are never
counted. OpenZeppelin's assessment: it removes the inflation risk completely and the logic is easy to
understand.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-inflation-defense" /> The cost is a different problem, and it is
lesson eleven's second half.

**Dead shares or an initial deposit.** Morpho DAO's approach is to deposit assets at initialisation
and mint the corresponding shares to the vault itself, which functions as a non-operational address —
so the loss is borne by the project rather than by a user, and the more that is deposited the harder
the attack becomes.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-inflation-defense" /> The drawbacks OpenZeppelin lists are
concrete: it does not completely resolve the issue, attacks remain possible at reduced profit, the
required amount has to be decided case by case, it discourages legitimate early liquidity providers,
and it locks a fraction of the assets permanently.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-inflation-defense" /> There is
also a deployment wrinkle — the vault's address has to be precomputed and approval granted in advance
of deployment.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-inflation-defense" />

**Virtual shares and assets.** YieldBox's design adds a virtual quantity of shares and assets to the
conversion, which mirrors dead shares without having to burn any tokens to mint
them.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-inflation-defense" /> This is what OpenZeppelin built on, and it is the
next lesson.

Note the pattern across all four. Two of them (the router, the seed) make the attack more expensive.
Two of them (internal accounting, virtual shares) change what the arithmetic reads. Only the second
pair addresses the mechanism, and each pays for it somewhere.

### The mitigation that shipped in 2020

Dead shares are not a 4626-era invention. Uniswap V2's pair contract has carried this since 2020:<Cite src="uniswap-v2-pair-source" />

```solidity
uint public constant MINIMUM_LIQUIDITY = 10**3;
// ...on the first mint only:
liquidity = Math.sqrt(amount0.mul(amount1)).sub(MINIMUM_LIQUIDITY);
_mint(address(0), MINIMUM_LIQUIDITY);   // permanently lock the first MINIMUM_LIQUIDITY tokens
```

The first liquidity provider gets 1,000 fewer LP tokens than the formula gives, and those 1,000 are
minted to the zero address where nobody can redeem them. Total supply can therefore never return to
zero, and one LP token can never be made worth an arbitrary amount, because there is always a floor
of 1,000 shares diluting any donation.<Cite src="uniswap-v2-pair-source" />

Two things to take from it. The mechanism was understood and mitigated in an AMM years before
ERC-4626 existed, and the fix cost the first depositor a small, permanent, disclosed amount — which
is exactly the tradeoff OpenZeppelin lists against dead
shares.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-inflation-defense" /> If you have wondered why the first LP in a pool
gets slightly fewer tokens than the square root suggests, this is the reason.

### Recognising it in code you did not write

The audit pattern is short enough to run over any vault, lending market or pool in a few minutes.

**Search for `balanceOf(address(this))`.** Every hit is a quantity somebody else can change with a
transfer. If the result feeds a ratio, a price, a reward rate or a share calculation, you have found
the surface.

**Read the zero-supply branch.** `supply == 0 ? assets : ...` is the special case that lets the first
depositor define the rate. Its presence means there is no virtual offset.

**Check the offset if the implementation is OpenZeppelin's.** `_decimalsOffset()` returning 0 is the
default, which is not the same as absent — the next lesson explains what the default still
buys.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-source" />

**Ask what happens at one share's worth.** Compute `totalAssets / totalSupply` for the live contract
and ask whether a realistic user deposit is above or below it. That single number tells you how much
of the deposit range rounds to zero.

**Look for the seed and check it is still there.** A vault seeded at deployment is protected only
while the seed shares exist. If they are redeemable by anyone, they are not dead shares — they are a
deposit.

<Answer>
**The attacker donates to raise the price per share until the victim's deposit rounds down, and takes
the remainder.** The seed changes the arithmetic but not its shape. Start from
`totalSupply = 1000e18`, `totalAssets = 1000e18`, so the price per share is 1.

For the victim's `50e18` to mint zero shares, the attacker needs
`50e18 * totalSupply / totalAssets` to floor to zero, which means `totalAssets` must exceed
`50e18 * 1000e18`. That is `5e40` — an absurd donation. **The seed has made total rounding-to-zero
economically impossible**, and that much is a genuine win.

**But zero is the wrong bar.** The attacker does not need the victim to receive nothing; they need
the victim to receive *less than they paid for*, with the difference accruing to existing
shareholders — which is to say, mostly to the deployer's `1000e18` shares and to whatever the
attacker holds. Any donation raises the price per share, and every subsequent deposit that is not an
exact multiple of the new price loses its remainder to the pool. The attack degrades from theft to a
tax, and the tax is paid by every depositor rather than one.

**The number that decides it** is the attacker's share of total supply. A donation of `D` raises the
pool by `D`; the attacker recovers `D * theirShares / totalSupply` of it. With the deployer holding
`1000e18` of the shares, an attacker holding a small fraction recovers a small fraction — they lose
money. That is the real defence the seed provides, and it is the same argument the next lesson's
virtual shares make, except that here it depends on the deployer never selling.

**So: better than nothing, and not a fix.** It is a capital commitment that must stay committed, it
does not survive the deployer's shares being redeemed, and the mechanism is still that price per
share is writable by anyone with the asset.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-inflation-defense" />
</Answer>

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** removes the special case entirely: give an empty vault a defined exchange rate,
and make donations captured rather than compounded. Both mitigations, with the arithmetic and the
tradeoffs.

**The security track** treats donation and first-depositor findings as a family. The recognition
pattern generalises well past vaults: any contract computing a ratio from `balanceOf(address(this))`
has published a write interface to that ratio.

**Invariant testing** on the Foundry track has this as its best worked example — the property "no
user can extract more than they deposited plus their share of yield" is checkable by a fuzz campaign
that includes plain transfers to the vault among its actions.
