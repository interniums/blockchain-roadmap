The left column is what lesson five said to stop storing. The right column is the alternative: hold
the number that does not move, and put the movement in a rate you compute at read time. Lido's own
integration guidance is precisely this — store and operate on shares, because share balances change
only on transfers, mints and burns while public balances also change on
rebases.<Cite src="lido-tokens-integration-guide" />

ERC-4626 is that construction, standardised, with an interface that lets an integrator quote and
bound an operation without knowing anything about what the vault does with the
assets.<Cite src="eip-4626" /> This lesson is the interface and its arithmetic. The next two are the
attack that lives underneath it.

## The interface, and why there are four mutators

A tokenized vault holds one underlying asset, issues shares against it, and lets holders convert back.
Every 4626 vault MUST implement ERC-20 to represent those shares, and MUST implement ERC-20's optional
metadata extensions, with `name` and `symbol` that SHOULD reflect the underlying token's in some
way.<Cite src="eip-4626" /> So a vault share is itself a token, subject to everything in the first
half of this module.

`asset()` names the underlying and MUST be an ERC-20 contract; `totalAssets()` reports the total
amount of it under management, SHOULD include compounding yield, MUST be inclusive of any fees
charged against assets in the vault, and MUST NOT revert.<Cite src="eip-4626" />

Then four state-changing functions, which look redundant until you notice what each one fixes:

```text
  deposit(assets, receiver)          -> shares      caller specifies the assets in
  mint(shares, receiver)             -> assets      caller specifies the shares out
  withdraw(assets, receiver, owner)  -> shares      caller specifies the assets out
  redeem(shares, receiver, owner)    -> assets      caller specifies the shares in
```

Two axes: in or out, and which side of the conversion is exact. You cannot make both sides exact,
because the rate is a ratio of two integers and the division has a remainder. So the standard gives
you four functions and makes you choose which quantity you want to be exact and which you will accept
as a computed result.

That is a general lesson about designing over an integer conversion, and it is why an API with one
`swap(amountIn, amountOut)` is always hiding something.

Both deposit paths MUST emit `Deposit` and MUST support ERC-20 `approve`/`transferFrom` on the asset
as the funding flow; both withdrawal paths MUST emit `Withdraw`, MUST support burning from `owner`
when `owner` is the caller, and MUST support burning from `owner` when the caller has ERC-20 approval
over `owner`'s shares.<Cite src="eip-4626" /> Note what that last clause means: **a share allowance
is a withdrawal authorisation.** Everything from lesson two applies to it.

## Three families of view function, and they are not interchangeable

This is where integrations go wrong, and the specification is unusually precise about it.

**`convertToShares` / `convertToAssets`** — the idealised rate. MUST NOT be inclusive of any fees
charged against assets, MUST NOT vary depending on the caller, MUST NOT reflect slippage or other
on-chain conditions, MUST NOT revert except on overflow from an unreasonably large input, and MUST
round down towards zero.<Cite src="eip-4626" /> The standard says these may reflect the
"average-user's" price per share rather than any particular user's.<Cite src="eip-4626" />

**`previewDeposit` / `previewMint` / `previewWithdraw` / `previewRedeem`** — what would actually
happen. MUST be inclusive of deposit or withdrawal fees, MUST NOT account for the limits returned by
the `max` functions, and MUST NOT revert due to vault-specific user or global
limits.<Cite src="eip-4626" /> Each carries a directional guarantee, and the direction always
disfavours the caller relative to the real operation:<Cite src="eip-4626" />

```text
  previewDeposit   MUST return as close to and no MORE than the shares deposit would mint
  previewMint      MUST return as close to and no FEWER than the assets mint would take
  previewWithdraw  MUST return as close to and no FEWER than the shares withdraw would burn
  previewRedeem    MUST return as close to and no MORE than the assets redeem would return
```

Read those as a contract with your slippage check. A preview never promises more than you get and
never quotes less than you pay, so a check written against a preview cannot be surprised in the
direction that costs the user.

**`maxDeposit` / `maxMint` / `maxWithdraw` / `maxRedeem`** — the bounds. MUST NOT revert, MUST return
0 if the operation is entirely disabled even temporarily, MUST NOT be higher than what would actually
be accepted, and for the deposit side MUST assume the user has infinite assets — explicitly, they MUST
NOT rely on `balanceOf` of the asset.<Cite src="eip-4626" /> `maxDeposit` and `maxMint` MUST return
`2 ** 256 - 1` when there is no limit.<Cite src="eip-4626" />

The reason to learn the three families as three is the security note attached to the middle one:

> The preview methods return values that are as close as possible to exact [...] For that reason,
> they are manipulable by altering the on-chain conditions and are not always safe to be used as
> price oracles.<Cite src="eip-4626" />

The specification then names the alternative: the convert methods are allowed to be inexact and can
therefore be implemented as robust price oracles — a time-weighted average, for
instance.<Cite src="eip-4626" />

**A vault is not an oracle.** A lending market that prices 4626 collateral with `previewRedeem` is
pricing off a number that an attacker can move inside the same transaction, and the standard warned
you in its own security section.

### Withdrawal is not guaranteed to be synchronous

One sentence in the withdrawal rules is easy to skim and expensive to miss: some implementations
require **pre-requesting** to the vault before a withdrawal may be performed, and those methods
should be performed separately.<Cite src="eip-4626" />

So "call `redeem` and receive assets" is the common case, not the guaranteed one. A vault whose
strategy has a lock-up, an unstaking period or an epoch boundary can conform to this interface and
still make redemption a two-step, multi-block process — and `maxRedeem` is where it tells you,
because that function MUST factor in global and user-specific limits and MUST return 0 when
redemption is disabled even temporarily.<Cite src="eip-4626" />

The integration consequence is a product one. Any user-facing flow that treats withdrawal as
immediate needs to read `maxRedeem` first and have a design for the case where it returns zero. A
spinner is not that design.

## Rounding direction is a security property

Shares and assets convert by integer division, so every conversion drops a remainder, and somebody
receives it. The specification states the rule as a principle and then as four cases, and the
principle is the one to memorise: it is considered most secure to favour **the vault itself** during
calculations over its users.<Cite src="eip-4626" />

- Calculating shares to issue for assets provided, or assets to transfer out for shares returned:
  **round down**.<Cite src="eip-4626" />
- Calculating shares a user must supply for given assets, or assets a user must provide for given
  shares: **round up**.<Cite src="eip-4626" />
- The `convertTo` functions are the ambiguous case, so the standard removes the ambiguity: both MUST
  always round down.<Cite src="eip-4626" />

OpenZeppelin's implementation maps this onto four lines, and reading them is faster than reading the
rules:<Cite src="oz-erc4626-source" />

```solidity
function previewDeposit(uint256 assets)  ... { return _convertToShares(assets, Math.Rounding.Floor); }
function previewMint(uint256 shares)     ... { return _convertToAssets(shares, Math.Rounding.Ceil);  }
function previewWithdraw(uint256 assets) ... { return _convertToShares(assets, Math.Rounding.Ceil);  }
function previewRedeem(uint256 shares)   ... { return _convertToAssets(shares, Math.Rounding.Floor); }
```

Floor, Ceil, Ceil, Floor. Every one of them moves the remainder into the vault, which is to say: into
the pool that all existing shareholders have a claim on.

### Worked example: why the direction is not a nicety

**Subgoal: establish a rate that is not 1.** A vault holds 1,000 assets against 1,000 shares. Yield
arrives; `totalAssets()` is now 1,100 and `totalSupply()` is still 1,000. One share is worth 1.1
assets.

**Subgoal: round toward the user on the way in.** A depositor brings 1 asset. The correct share
count is `1 * 1000 / 1100 = 0.909…`, floored to 0. Round *up* instead and they get 1 share, worth
1.1 assets, for 1 asset. Profit: 0.1 assets, taken from every other shareholder.

**Subgoal: round toward the user on the way out.** Redeeming 1 share should return
`1 * 1100 / 1000 = 1.1`, floored to 1. Round *up* and they get 2. Profit: 0.9, again from
everyone else.

**Subgoal: repeat.** Each individual error is at most one unit of the least significant place. The
attack is not the error; it is the loop. An attacker who can perform the operation N times extracts N
units, and N is bounded only by what a transaction costs. That is a different calculation on a chain
where a call costs cents than on one where it costs dollars, and it is why "the rounding error is
negligible" is a statement about a single operation being mistaken for a statement about a
system.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-docs" />

The honest counterpart, from OpenZeppelin's own analysis: rounding *correctly* also takes value from
the user, in favour of all current shareholders — and the size depends entirely on scale. Deposit
`1e9` shares' worth and the rounding costs at most a millionth of a percent; deposit 10 shares' worth
and you can lose 10%; deposit less than one share's worth and you get **zero shares**, and, in their
words, you basically made a donation.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-docs" />

Hold that last sentence. The next lesson is what happens when an attacker gets to choose how much "one
share's worth" is.

## Reading a correct implementation

The reference implementation is short enough to read in full, and three details in it are the ones
you would get wrong writing your own.

**Every mutator routes through its own preview function.** `deposit` computes shares with
`previewDeposit`, `mint` computes assets with `previewMint`, `withdraw` uses `previewWithdraw` and
`redeem` uses `previewRedeem`.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-source" /> That is not tidiness — it is what
makes the spec's directional guarantees hold by construction. The preview cannot disagree with the
operation, because it *is* the operation's arithmetic.

The consequence for anyone overriding: the source's own note says that overrides to the deposit or
withdraw mechanism must be reflected in the preview functions as well, and that the internal
`_deposit` and `_withdraw` are the right places to override because each one covers both public
entry points.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-source" /> Overriding a public function instead is documented to
have led to loss of funds.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-source" />

**The `max` functions have a dependency you cannot see from their names.** `maxWithdraw` is defined
in terms of `maxRedeem`, so overriding `maxRedeem` alone is sufficient and overriding `maxWithdraw`
alone has no effect on `maxRedeem` and creates an inconsistency between
them.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-source" /> And if `previewRedeem` is overridden to revert, `maxWithdraw`
must be overridden too, because the standard says the `max` functions MUST NOT
revert.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-source" /><Cite src="eip-4626" />

**Decimals are read once, defensively, at construction.** The constructor calls
`SafeERC20.tryGetDecimals(asset_)` and falls back to 18 if the read fails — for instance if the asset
contract does not exist yet — then `decimals()` returns that cached value plus a decimals
offset.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-source" /><Cite src="oz-safe-erc20-source" /> Two things follow. The
optional-metadata problem from lesson one is handled here rather than assumed away. And a vault
deployed against an address that is not yet a token silently gets 18, which is the wrong answer for
a six-decimal asset and will not announce itself.

The offset in that last line is the subject of lesson eleven. For now, note only that a vault's
`decimals()` is not necessarily its asset's.

## The standard defines an interface, not safe math

The security considerations open with the sentence every integrator should read before adopting a
vault:

> Fully permissionless use cases could fall prey to malicious implementations which only conform to
> the interface but not the specification. It is recommended that all integrators review the
> implementation for potential ways of losing user deposits before
> integrating.<Cite src="eip-4626" />

Conform to the interface but not the specification. That is the same failure mode as lesson four's
missing return values, one level up: the function signatures are a type, and a type is not a
behaviour.

The reference implementation makes the point concretely with two warnings of its own.

**Any mechanism that mints shares without a corresponding increase in assets alters the exchange
rate.** OpenZeppelin names a specific combination as forbidden: `ERC4626` must NOT be combined with
`ERC20FlashMint`, because flash-minting shares temporarily inflates the total supply without
increasing collateral and corrupts the exchange rate applied during the
loan.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-source" /> Two library contracts that each look safe, and a composition
that is not.

**The deposit and withdraw orderings are chosen against a reentrant asset.** The source comments
explain that if `asset()` is an ERC-777, `transferFrom` can trigger reentrancy *before* the transfer
through the `tokensToSend` hook — so `_deposit` performs the transfer in **before** minting, and
`_withdraw` burns **before** transferring out, so that any reentrancy observes a valid
state.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-source" /><Cite src="eip-777" /> The previous lesson's subject, showing
up as an ordering constraint inside a vault implementation.

Note also what the implementation does *not* try to fix: `totalAssets()` in the reference is
`IERC20(asset()).balanceOf(address(this))`.<Cite src="oz-erc4626-source" /> A plain transfer to the
vault's address changes it. That is the whole of the next lesson, and it is in the default
implementation on purpose, because the mitigation lives somewhere else.

uint256 shares = vault.convertToShares(assetAmount);

<Answer>
**They are charged more than they asked for, and the amount is not shown to them anywhere.**
`convertToShares` MUST NOT be inclusive of fees.<Cite src="eip-4626" /> So `shares` is the count the
user's assets would buy in a fee-free world. `mint(shares, ...)` then pulls whatever assets it takes
to produce that many shares — which, with a 0.5% deposit fee, is about 0.5% more than
`assetAmount`.<Cite src="eip-4626" />

**And the excess comes out of their approval without warning.** `mint` MUST support
`approve`/`transferFrom` on the asset and MUST revert if all the required assets cannot be
deposited.<Cite src="eip-4626" /> If the user approved exactly `assetAmount`, the transaction reverts
with an allowance error that names neither the vault nor the fee. If they had an unlimited approval —
the common case — it silently takes more.

**The right call is `previewMint`, or better, the other function entirely.** `previewMint` MUST be
inclusive of deposit fees and MUST return no fewer assets than `mint` will actually
take,<Cite src="eip-4626" /> so it is the number to show the user and to bound the approval with. But
the button says "deposit exactly this many dollars", and the function whose *input* is an exact
asset amount is `deposit`, not `mint`. The integration picked the wrong side of the four-function
grid and then reached for a view function to paper over it.

**The general rule.** Use the mutator whose exact argument is the quantity the user chose, and use the
matching `preview` — never a `convert` — to display and to bound. The spec's own guidance is that
integrators should always use the preview function most relevant to their use case and never assume
they are interchangeable.<Cite src="eip-4626" />

## Where this pays off

**The next lesson** takes `totalAssets()` being a `balanceOf` call and turns it into the complete
first-depositor inflation attack, with numbers.

**Lesson eleven** gives an empty vault a defined price, which is the only structural fix, and prices
what that fix costs.

**Every lending market and yield aggregator** on the DeFi track is a consumer of this interface. The
three families of view function, and which of them is safe to price with, is the difference between
a market that survives a manipulated share rate and one that does not.
