`new Token{salt: SALT}()` compiles. It works on your local Anvil. It works on Sepolia, on Base, on
Arbitrum, on every chain you have ever used. Then a partner asks for a deployment on their app-chain,
and the same line produces nothing at all — or produces something at an address that does not match
the one you published for every other chain.

Nothing about your contract changed. What changed is that a specific contract, which you have never
mentioned in your code, is not there.

## The bootstrapping problem, stated plainly

The previous lesson left the derivation with four inputs, one of which is the **deployer**. For an
address to be identical across chains, that deployer must be identical across chains — the same
address on every one of them.

ERC-7955 states the resulting circularity better than a paraphrase can: getting the same address and
code on multiple chains "is typically done by having a known CREATE2 factory contract at a specific
address"; but then, "how do you get a CREATE2 factory contract with a specific address and
code?"<Cite src="erc-7955" />

You cannot use CREATE2 to deploy the CREATE2 factory. And you cannot use a plain CREATE from an
account you control, because a
[CREATE address depends on that account's nonce](/c/create-address-from-nonce), which diverges the
moment the account does anything on one chain and not another.

Every answer in production today is a way out of that circle. There are four, and each trades
something different away.

## Answer one: the address nobody controls

The deterministic deployment proxy lives at
`0x4e59b44847b379578588920cA78FbF26c0B4956C`.<Cite src="foundry-create2-guide" /><Cite src="erc-7955" />
Foundry routes salted creation syntax through it by default, which is why `new Token{salt: SALT}()`
has always worked for you.<Cite src="foundry-create2-guide" />

Its interface is as small as an interface gets. Send it a transaction whose "data should be the 32
byte 'salt' followed by your init code", and it deploys.<Cite src="create2-deployer-proxy-repo" />

### How it got to the same address everywhere

This is Nick's method, and it is worth walking through byte by byte, because the mechanism explains
both why it works and exactly where it fails.

**Subgoal 1 — construct a signature that could not have come from any key.** The deployment
transaction ends with `r` and `s` both set to
`0x2222222222222222222222222222222222222222222222222222222222222222`, and `v` set to
`0x1b`.<Cite src="create2-deployer-proxy-repo" /> Nobody chose those values by signing; they were
typed.

**Subgoal 2 — let ECDSA recovery invent the sender.** A signature does not have to have been produced
by a key for `ecrecover` to return an address from it. The recovered sender is
`0x3fab184622dc19b6109349b94811493bf2a45362`.<Cite src="create2-deployer-proxy-repo" /> That account
exists in the sense that anyone can send it ether; it does not exist in the sense that anyone can
spend from it. The README's phrasing for this is "a one-time-use-account", and the consequence it
draws is the whole trick: "no matter what chain the deployer is on, its address will always be the
same".<Cite src="create2-deployer-proxy-repo" />

**Subgoal 3 — accept that the gas parameters are now frozen.** Because there is no private key, the
transaction can never be re-signed. Whatever gas price and gas limit were in the signed payload are
in it forever: a gas price of 100 gwei and a gas limit of
100,000.<Cite src="create2-deployer-proxy-repo" /> The README notes the actual gas used is 68,131 and
that the excess was deliberate — "to avoid having to move the proxy to a different address, we opted
to give excess gas".<Cite src="create2-deployer-proxy-repo" />

**Subgoal 4 — replay it anywhere.** Fund `0x3fab1846…` with enough ether to cover 100,000 gas at 100
gwei, submit the raw transaction, and the proxy lands. The sender is fixed and its nonce is zero, so
the CREATE address is the same on every chain that accepts the transaction.

The README summarises the payoff exactly: between CREATE2 and the one-time-use account, "a given
contract will exist at the *exact* same address on every chain, but without having to use the same gas
pricing or limits every time".<Cite src="create2-deployer-proxy-repo" /> Your contract's deployment is
flexible; only the proxy's own deployment is frozen.

### Where it fails, and it is not "rarely"

ERC-7955 lists the downsides of Nick's method as three bullets, and each one is a class of chain, not
an edge case:<Cite src="erc-7955" />

- **It "does not work on chains that only accept EIP-155 replay-protected transactions."** The signed
  payload has no chain id. EIP-155 defines the replay-protected form as hashing nine RLP elements
  including the chain id, with `v` set from the chain id; the six-element form with `v` of 27 or 28 is
  the legacy one.<Cite src="eip-155" /> A chain that mandates the former rejects this transaction
  outright — and rejecting unprotected transactions is a perfectly reasonable policy for a new chain
  to adopt.
- **It "is sensitive to changes in gas parameters on the target chain since the gas price and limit in
  the deployment transaction is sealed, and a new one cannot be signed."**<Cite src="erc-7955" /> A
  chain whose base fee sits above 100 gwei cannot include a 100-gwei legacy transaction at all.
- **"Reverts, such as those caused by alternative gas schedules, make the CREATE2 factory no longer
  deployable."**<Cite src="erc-7955" /> This is the sharpest one. The nonce is consumed by the failed
  attempt. There is no second attempt, because there is no key to sign one with. A chain that reprices
  an opcode such that 100,000 gas is no longer enough has permanently lost the ability to host the
  proxy at that address.

### Checking, and overriding

Foundry's guide gives the one-line check, and the answer to read:<Cite src="foundry-create2-guide" />

```bash
cast code 0x4e59b44847b379578588920ca78fbf26c0b4956c --rpc-url $RPC_URL
```

`0x` means it is not there. Run this against every chain in a launch *before* you compute a single
address, not after.

When it is missing, `create2_deployer` in `foundry.toml`, or `--create2-deployer` on the command line,
points salted creation at a different factory.<Cite src="foundry-create2-guide" /><Cite src="foundry-config-overview" />
`cast create2` takes the same input as `--deployer`, defaulting to the 0x4e59 address when you omit
it.<Cite src="foundry-cast-create2" />

Two operational consequences follow, and both are things to write down rather than remember:

**The factory is part of the address.** It is the `deployer` term in
`keccak256(0xff ++ deployer ++ salt ++ keccak256(init_code))[12:]`.<Cite src="eip-1014" /> Change
factories on one chain and that chain gets a different address, for the same reason a changed
constructor argument does.

**Record which factory you used, per chain.** Six months later, "why is Base different" has exactly
one cheap answer, and it is a line in a file.

## Answers two and three: a secret key, and preinstalls

ERC-7955 names both alternatives and both of their prices.<Cite src="erc-7955" />

**A guarded secret key.** Hold a private key, sign a deployment on each new chain at whatever nonce is
canonical, and the factory lands at the same address everywhere. This is what the Safe Singleton
Factory at `0x914d7Fec6aaC8cd542e72Bca78B30650d45643d7` and CreateX at
`0xba5Ed099633D3B313e4D5F7bdc1305d3c28ba5Ed` do.<Cite src="erc-7955" /> It fixes the gas problem
completely — a key can sign a fresh transaction with today's parameters. It introduces a governance
problem instead: it "is permissioned: the party that holds the secret key has the ultimate say on
which chains will get the CREATE2 factory deployments", the key must be guarded, and "if the
transaction at the given nonce is not a successful CREATE2 factory deployment, then it is no longer
possible to have a CREATE2 factory at the canonical address" — by human error, for
example.<Cite src="erc-7955" />

**Preinstalls.** Chains ship the factories in their genesis state; OP Stack and ZKsync both do
this.<Cite src="erc-7955" /> The price is that it "is not standardized nor adopted by all chains" and
remains permissioned in a different direction, since a chain can decline. The EIP notes that attempts
to standardise it through RIP-7740 "have not been successful".<Cite src="erc-7955" />

Look at the shape of the three answers together. Nick's method removes the key and loses flexibility.
The secret key restores flexibility and reintroduces a trusted party. Preinstalls move the decision to
the chain operator. Each one moves the trust rather than eliminating it.

## Answer four: publish the key

ERC-7955's proposal is the inversion that makes the pattern click. If the problem with a secret key is
that someone holds it, do not make the key secret.

The EIP publishes it. `DEPLOYER_PRIVATE_KEY` is
`0x942ba639ec667bdded6d727ad2e483648a34b584f916e6b826fdb7b512633731`, printed in the specification,
deriving `DEPLOYER_ADDRESS` `0x962560A0333190D57009A0aAAB7Bfa088f58461C`.<Cite src="erc-7955" />

A published key alone would be useless — anyone could sign anything from that account, and the nonce
would be unpredictable. The mechanism that makes it work is
[EIP-7702 delegation](/c/delegation-designator).<Cite src="eip-7702" /> The deployment process is
three steps:<Cite src="erc-7955" />

1. Deploy a **bootstrap contract** whose code performs one `CREATE2` of a fixed
   `CREATE2_FACTORY_INIT_CODE` with a fixed `CREATE2_FACTORY_SALT` of
   `0x000000000000000000000000000000000000000000000000000000000001bec5`.
2. Sign an EIP-7702 authorization with the published key, delegating `DEPLOYER_ADDRESS` to that
   bootstrap contract.
3. Send a type `0x4` transaction carrying that authorization, calling `DEPLOYER_ADDRESS`.

The result is the factory at `0xC0DEb853af168215879d284cc8B4d0A645fA9b0E` with code hash
`0x2ad75e1e9642e6fce7d293d52fa5a8f62a79a2079abb7402256add02d6e8bc30`.<Cite src="erc-7955" />

Now walk the three failures of Nick's method against this design:

- **Replay protection.** A type `0x4` transaction is a modern, chain-id-carrying transaction. Any
  chain that accepts EIP-7702 accepts it.
- **Frozen gas.** The authorization is signed fresh, by anyone, at any time, with today's gas
  parameters. The EIP's own summary of the benefit is that the method "is secure against 'out of gas'
  and other errors".<Cite src="erc-7955" />
- **Permanent failure.** Because the final step is `CREATE2`, "no transaction executed by
  `DEPLOYER_ADDRESS` can permanently block the deployment of the CREATE2 factory contract to
  `CREATE2_FACTORY_ADDRESS`".<Cite src="erc-7955" /> A failed attempt costs gas and nothing else.

### The honest status, and the honest caveats

**It is Draft.**<Cite src="erc-7955" /> As of August 2026 the `0x4e59` proxy is what your tooling
actually uses and what exists on the chains you actually deploy to. Treat ERC-7955 as a precise
description of a real problem and a credible proposed fix — not as current practice, and not as a set
of addresses to hardcode, since draft parameters can change.

The EIP is candid about two residual issues, and both are worth knowing because they are properties of
the design rather than bugs in it:<Cite src="erc-7955" />

**Anyone can grief the deployment.** Because the key is public, anyone can sign an alternative
delegation and front-run a legitimate attempt, advancing the deployer's nonce and invalidating the
pending authorization. The EIP argues this is bounded: the attacker "can only delay the deployment of
the CREATE2 factory with a sustained attack at a gas cost to the attacker, but not permanently prevent
it", and suggests private transactions or working directly with block builders as the
circumvention.<Cite src="erc-7955" />

**A future upgrade could close the door.** If some later mechanism permanently sets an account's code,
the publicly-known key means `DEPLOYER_ADDRESS` could be set to code that does not bootstrap,
permanently preventing future deployments.<Cite src="erc-7955" /> The EIP's answer is adoption: once
mainnet has the factory, the key has no value there.

<Aside kind="note">
Notice what ERC-7955 tells you about the whole subject. Cross-chain address determinism is not a
property of `CREATE2`. It is a property of *deployment infrastructure*, which is social, per-chain, and
still being argued about in 2026. A vendor page that says "deterministic addresses on every chain"
means "on every chain where somebody already did this work".<Cite src="layerzero-uniform-address" />
</Aside>

<Answer>
**The consequence: the `0x4e59` proxy cannot be deployed there by its normal route.** Its deployment
transaction is a legacy, pre-EIP-155 transaction with no chain id and `v` of
`0x1b`.<Cite src="create2-deployer-proxy-repo" /><Cite src="eip-155" /> A chain that rejects
unprotected transactions rejects it, and there is no key to re-sign it with. ERC-7955 lists this as
the first downside of Nick's method.<Cite src="erc-7955" />

Their policy is defensible, by the way. It is not a misconfiguration to argue them out of.

**Option 1 — confirm the fact before designing around it.** `cast code 0x4e59… --rpc-url` against
their endpoint. `0x` confirms it.<Cite src="foundry-create2-guide" /> They may already have
preinstalled a factory, in which case this conversation is over — many stacks do exactly
that.<Cite src="erc-7955" />

**Option 2 — ask which factory they do have.** If they preinstalled the Safe Singleton Factory
(`0x914d7Fec…`) or CreateX (`0xba5Ed099…`), point Foundry at it with `create2_deployer` in
`foundry.toml` or `--create2-deployer`.<Cite src="erc-7955" /><Cite src="foundry-create2-guide" /> Be
explicit about what this costs: the deployer is part of the address
derivation,<Cite src="eip-1014" /> so this chain gets a different address from the other seven. That is
a product decision, not a technical one — decide whether the uniform address or the launch date
matters more.

**Option 3 — deploy your own factory there.** Any contract that does `CREATE2` on supplied
salt-plus-initcode works. It will be at an address unique to that chain, which again breaks
uniformity, but it puts you in control.

**Option 4 — change the derivation rather than the factory.** CREATE3 makes the final address depend
on deployer and salt only — but the CREATE3 deployer contract still has to be at a matching address on
every chain, so this relocates the problem rather than solving it. It helps only if you already have
that deployer everywhere.

**Option 5 — stop promising one address.** Publish a per-chain address table. This is the option
nobody proposes in the meeting and it is frequently correct: seven chains at one address plus one
outlier is a footnote, whereas delaying a launch to preserve a cosmetic property is a real cost.

**What to write down either way.** Which factory was used on which chain, in the deployment record,
next to the salt. The address is a function of both, and six months from now nobody will remember
which chain was the exception.

## Where this pays off

**Immediately next:** you now have an address and a story about how it got there. The final lesson
asks the other question a user asks — what code is actually at it, on this chain, and what does a
green "verified" badge certify about that.

**In multi-chain launches:** the factory check belongs in the pre-flight list, alongside the RPC alias
and the signer. Three lines of `cast code` before you compute anything is the cheapest step in the
runbook.

**In reading other people's deployments:** when you see a protocol at the same address on a dozen
chains and a different one on the thirteenth, you now know the shape of the answer without asking. One
of the four inputs moved, and the factory is the input people forget is an input.
