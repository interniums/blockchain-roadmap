Two failures wait for almost everyone who writes their first serious fork suite. Neither says what is
wrong.

The first arrives when you pin an interesting block — the day of an incident, eighteen months ago. The
test does not fail an assertion; it fails inside a call, with a message about a missing node or a
state that could not be found, and the natural reading is that your test is broken.

The second arrives when the suite grows. Tests that passed individually start failing in batches with
transport errors and HTTP 429s, and pass again when re-run one at a time. The natural reading is
flakiness.

Neither is a bug in your code. The first is a statement about which nodes keep which state; the second
about how a fork test is billed. This lesson covers both, then the configuration that makes a forked
suite affordable enough to keep.

## Old blocks are not universally available

The mechanism is worth stating exactly, because the vocabulary providers use is imprecise.

An execution client following the chain needs current state to validate incoming transactions.
Ethereum.org describes a full node as caching only the past few states — its example figure is the
state associated with the last 128 blocks — and states that older ones are effectively thrown
away.<Cite src="eth-archive-nodes" /> The blocks and transactions themselves are still there; what is
discarded is the *derived state* at each historical point. Pruning is a first-class client feature,
not neglect.<Cite src="geth-dbpruning" />

That does not make historical state unobtainable. Full nodes keep occasional snapshots and can
regenerate an older state by re-executing past transactions in the EVM — which ethereum.org describes
as computationally demanding, and potentially as far back as re-executing from
genesis.<Cite src="eth-archive-nodes" /> An archive node avoids that by storing every historical state
as it goes, trading disk space for immediate answers.<Cite src="eth-archive-nodes" /><Cite src="quicknode-full-vs-archive" />

So the accurate framing is not "old blocks need an archive node". It is: **reading a storage slot as
it stood at an old block is cheap on an archive node, expensive-to-impossible on a pruned one, and
your provider decides which you are talking to.**<Cite src="ethereum-org-nodes-clients" /> A free
public endpoint is usually the second kind.

What you actually see is unhelpful in a specific way. Foundry documents two fork errors, and neither
mentions retention: `Error: Failed to fetch block`, attributed to an endpoint that is unreachable or
rate-limited, and `Error: Block not found`, attributed to a block number that does not exist or has
not been indexed.<Cite src="foundry-troubleshooting" /> A pruned endpoint refusing an archive-range
query lands in that second bucket, or fails partway through your call when a state read comes back
empty — either way the message points at the block, or at nothing, rather than at the node's
retention policy.

Three practical consequences. **The block you pin constrains your provider list**, and therefore your
budget and what an outside contributor can run. **A test that works locally and fails in CI may be a
provider difference rather than an environment one** — different endpoint, different retention. And
**a recent pin is genuinely cheaper than an old one**, which is one more reason to re-date a suite
periodically instead of leaving it on a block from three years ago.

## Billed per item, not per test

The cost model follows directly from lazy fetching. A fork test does not open one connection and use
it; it issues one request per uncached account, code blob and storage slot it touches. A broad suite
therefore produces a large number of small requests — the exact traffic shape that trips
per-second rate limits and drains compute-unit budgets, which are the two ways providers meter
work.<Cite src="alchemy-compute-units" />

You do not have to take that on faith, because Foundry's own test runner carries the knobs you would
only need if this were true. From `forge test --help` on 1.7.1:<Cite src="forge-test-reference" /><Cite src="measured-forge-1-7-1" />

A test runner that ships a client-side compute-unit throttle, with a non-infinite default, is telling
you what it expects to happen. The fork-testing guide's own troubleshooting section for rate limiting
suggests `--fork-retry-backoff 1000`, describing the value as the initial retry delay in
milliseconds — or, failing that, a dedicated provider with higher
limits.<Cite src="foundry-fork-testing" /> The troubleshooting page gives the same mitigation as
configuration, `rpc_retry_delay` and `rpc_retry_backoff` under a profile, for the
`Failed to fetch block` case.<Cite src="foundry-troubleshooting" />

<Aside kind="stop">
**What not to memorise: provider pricing.** Free-tier limits, compute-unit costs per method and
per-second caps change constantly and differ between providers, and several of the figures circulating
for 2026 could not be confirmed against first-party documentation during the research for this
curriculum. Learn the *shape* — many small requests, metered per item, mitigated by the cache — and
look up the current numbers for the provider you are actually using, from that provider, on the day
you need them.
</Aside>

## Making it cheap: restoring the cache in CI

Locally the cache accumulates on its own. A CI runner is a fresh container, so every run is a first
run unless you carry the cache across.

Foundry's action does this by default. `foundry-rs/foundry-toolchain@v1` takes a `cache` input that
**defaults to `true`**, and the README states it matches Forge's behaviour by caching all RPC
responses, Etherscan queries and other data in `~/.foundry/cache` — done to speed up tests and avoid
hitting the rate limit of your RPC provider.<Cite src="foundry-toolchain-action" /> The official CI
page shows it written out explicitly.<Cite src="foundry-ci" />

```yaml
- name: Install Foundry
  uses: foundry-rs/foundry-toolchain@v1
  with:
    cache: true

- name: Run fork tests
  run: forge test --match-path 'test/fork/**' -vvv
  env:
    ETH_RPC_URL: ${{ secrets.ETH_RPC_URL }}
```

The endpoint arrives as a secret and the CI profile names it, which is what
[`[rpc_endpoints]` aliases](/c/rpc-endpoints-aliases) were for:<Cite src="foundry-ci" />

```toml
[profile.ci]
eth_rpc_url = "${ETH_RPC_URL}"
```

`eth_rpc_url` reads `FOUNDRY_ETH_RPC_URL` or `DAPP_ETH_RPC_URL` from the environment as well, so a
runner can supply it without the file naming it at all.<Cite src="foundry-testing-config" />

### The key semantics, which decide whether any of this works

The action's `cache-key` defaults to `${{ github.job }}-${{ github.sha }}` and `cache-restore-keys` to
`[${{ github.job }}-]`.<Cite src="foundry-toolchain-action" /> Read those two together, because the
behaviour is not obvious:

- The **save** key is unique per commit, so a new cache entry is written on every run.
- The **restore** key is a prefix, so a run loads the most recent cache saved under the same job.
- Consequently an exact key hit essentially never happens; every useful hit is a prefix hit. The
  README describes exactly this: always load the latest valid cache and always create a new one, so
  when fork tests do not change, the cache does not change but the key
  does.<Cite src="foundry-toolchain-action" />

This is why "did the cache work" is not answerable by reading the workflow file. Prefix restores are
best-effort, and GitHub's cache is itself evictable and scoped by branch, so a job can be configured
perfectly and still start cold.<Cite src="github-actions-cache" />

**Two failure modes worth knowing before they cost you a week.**

**Fuzzing defeats the cache.** The action's README is explicit: if you are fuzzing in your fork tests,
the caching strategy will not work unless you set a fuzz seed — and it suggests reducing RPC calls
with Multicall as a further mitigation.<Cite src="foundry-toolchain-action" /> An unseeded fuzzer
requests different state every run, so the warm cache holds answers nobody is asking for.

**Fork pull requests get no secrets.** A pull request from a forked repository cannot read your
repository secrets, so `ETH_RPC_URL` is empty and the job fails or silently skips for exactly the
contributors you most wanted to help — see [fork PR secrets](/c/fork-pr-secrets). Decide deliberately
whether the fork suite is a required check or a trusted-event-only job.

### The success criterion is a measurement, not a configuration

The step being present proves nothing. The observable evidence that CI caching works is:

1. The second run reports a **cache hit**, and
2. that run is **materially faster**, or issues materially fewer requests, than the first.

If the second run takes the same time as the first, something is wrong — most often an unseeded fuzz
test, a suite that is not pinned, or a container that moved `~/.foundry` by setting
`XDG_CONFIG_HOME`.<Cite src="foundry-config-overview" /> `forge cache ls` in a CI step, printed before
and after, turns this from a hunch into a line of log
output.<Cite src="forge-cache-reference" />

## Machine-readable results, and a constraint that shapes the job

If CI needs to parse per-test results, use `--junit`. `forge test --json` still writes non-JSON to
stdout alongside the JSON — the long-standing open issue — so it cannot be piped to a parser without
heuristics.<Cite src="foundry-issue-3001" /> `--junit` emits JUnit XML, which every CI system already
understands.<Cite src="forge-test-reference" />

There is one hard constraint that decides how the job is written. On forge 1.7.1:

```text
$ forge test --junit --gas-report
error: the argument '--junit' cannot be used with '--gas-report'
```

The argument parser rejects the combination outright.<Cite src="measured-fork-cache-1-7-1" /><Cite src="measured-forge-1-7-1" />
You cannot get machine-readable results and a gas report from one invocation.

For a local suite that is a mild annoyance: run it twice. For a **forked** suite it is a design
decision, because a second invocation is a second cold path for any state the first one did not leave
in the cache — and in CI, "run the fork suite twice" can mean doubling the traffic that made you
configure caching in the first place. The usual resolution is to keep gas measurement out of the
forked job entirely: gas numbers from a fork are not comparable to mainnet anyway (a forked test's
setup warms accounts and slots that were cold on chain — 2100 gas for a cold storage read against 100
warm, 2600 for a cold account access against 100), so the gas report belongs on the fast local
suite.<Cite src="measured-forge-1-7-1" />

## Keep the forked suite thin, deliberately

Even fully warm, forked tests are slower than local ones and depend on a network service you do not
operate. The shape that works is layered, and each layer has a different budget:

- **Local unit tests** — hundreds of them, run on every save, no network, no pins, milliseconds.
- **Stateless fuzz and invariants** — CPU-bound, no network, run on every pull request.
- **Forked integration** — deliberately small, pinned, in its own directory, selected explicitly.

```bash
$ forge test --no-match-path 'test/fork/**'   # the fast suite: the default local loop
$ forge test --match-path    'test/fork/**'   # the expensive suite: its own CI job
```

That is ordinary [test filtering](/c/test-filtering) carrying an unusual amount of weight: it is the
boundary between the tests that cost nothing and the tests that cost money.

The temptation to over-invest in the forked layer is strong, because it feels more real. Resist it on
the merits, not on the cost. **Fork tests cover integration risk — the gap between your belief about a
dependency and its deployed behaviour — and nothing else.** They are poor at exhaustiveness (each case
is a round trip), poor at edge cases (you cannot fuzz widely against real state without paying for
it), and unable to reach code paths whose state does not exist on chain. A property that holds for all
inputs is a job for a local invariant suite. What the real router does with your calldata is a job for
a fork, and there is no other way to answer it.

<Answer>
**Six, and only one of them would show up as a red build.**

**1 — `forge test --junit --gas-report` does not run at all.** forge 1.7.1 rejects the combination:
`error: the argument '--junit' cannot be used with '--gas-report'`.<Cite src="measured-fork-cache-1-7-1" />
Symptom: the step fails immediately with an argument-parser error and no tests execute. This is the one
defect that is loud. (It also means the job as written cannot be the one that is "green" — so the real
repository has one of the two flags, and the same analysis applies.)

**2 — `XDG_CONFIG_HOME` is set, which moves the Foundry root.** With `FOUNDRY_DIR` unset and
`XDG_CONFIG_HOME` set, `foundryup` installs to `$XDG_CONFIG_HOME/.foundry/` rather than
`~/.foundry/`.<Cite src="foundry-config-overview" /> The caching step is documented against
`~/.foundry/cache`.<Cite src="foundry-toolchain-action" /> Symptom: the cache step succeeds and caches
nothing useful, and the bill never falls. Fix: unset it, or set `FOUNDRY_DIR` explicitly so both the
install and the cache agree.

**3 — Nothing is pinned.** `eth_rpc_url` is configured but there is no `fork_block_number`, and no
per-test pin is visible. Symptom: every run forks at a new head, produces a new cache key, and can
essentially never hit cache regardless of how the CI cache is configured. Pinning is the precondition
for every other mitigation here.

**4 — 512 fuzz runs with no seed, in a suite that forks.** The action's README states plainly that the
cache strategy does not work for fuzzing fork tests unless a fuzz seed is
set.<Cite src="foundry-toolchain-action" /> Symptom: each run requests state the previous run never
fetched, so a warm cache coexists with a full-price bill — which is precisely the reported symptom.

**5 — The fork configuration is under `[profile.default]`, so the whole suite is forked.** Every test
in the repository now requires an endpoint. Symptom: local development needs a key; the fast unit
suite is neither fast nor local; and there is no `--match-path` separating the layers, so there is no
way to run only the cheap tests.

**6 — External pull requests cannot read `secrets.ETH_RPC_URL`.** Fork PRs are not given repository
secrets. Symptom: `ETH_RPC_URL` is empty for outside contributors, the forked environment fails to
instantiate, and their pull requests are red for reasons unrelated to their change — see
[fork PR secrets](/c/fork-pr-secrets).

**The diagnosis that ties them together.** Five of the six are invisible in the build status. The team
configured caching, saw green, and concluded it was working — but the only evidence that a cache is
working is a *cache hit plus a materially lower runtime or request count on the second run*. Print
`forge cache ls` before and after the test step,<Cite src="forge-cache-reference" /> compare two
consecutive runs on the same branch, and five of these six announce themselves in the first
comparison.

## What this unlocks, and what closes here

This is the last lesson in the module, so it is worth naming what you now have: a test on real
deployed state, a choice between run-level and per-test forks, several chains in one test without
losing your helpers, the instant before a historical transaction reconstructed and replayed, a pin
that makes a suite both reproducible and cheap, and CI whose second run is nearly offline. It composes
with the rest of the track — filtering decides which layer runs, profiles decide what CI does
differently, the cache decides what it costs.

**Straight ahead:** Track 06 uses this constantly. Reproducing an exploit is `createSelectFork` at a
transaction hash plus `vm.transact`; validating a fix is the same test asserting the opposite outcome
against a patched deployment. That track supplies the incidents and the reasoning about what to
assert.

**The thing to carry, though, is the dependency you have taken on.** Every property you verify this
way is downstream of a third party continuing to answer questions about a block you chose. Warm caches
hide that until the morning they do not — a new runner, an evicted cache, a provider that stopped
serving your archive range, a key that expired. Which of your guarantees are you willing to hold that
way, and what is the plan for the morning the answer stops arriving? The module's reflection prompt
asks exactly that, and it is worth writing down an answer before you need one.
