Three ways to keep a fuzzed value between 0 and 100. All three compile, all three do what their
author intended, and all three produce a green test.

```solidity
if (x > 100) x = 100;          // clamp
x = bound(x, 0, 100);          // forge-std
x = x % 101;                   // modulo
```

Log every value each one actually produces over 256 runs on forge 1.7.1 with `--fuzz-seed 42`, and

The clamped test spent 217 of its 256 runs on a single value and saw eight distinct inputs in total.
It is, to a very good approximation, `test_BoundaryIsHandled()` written in 256 copies. The other two
swept most of the range.

This lesson is about the general form of that: **your constraints are part of the specification the
test checks, and nothing in the output tells you what they excluded.**

## Clamping is not bounding

The arithmetic is immediate once you look at it. `if (x > 100) x = 100;` maps every value above 100
onto 100. Over a `uint256` domain that is all but 101 of 2^256 values — a share of the domain
indistinguishable from all of it. If the generator were uniform, essentially 100% of runs would test
`x == 100`.

The measured figure is 217 of 256 rather than 256 of 256 only because the generator is not
uniform: `dictionary_weight` defaults to 40 and the dictionary supplies small values, which pass
through the clamp unchanged.<Cite src="foundry-testing-config" /><Cite src="measured-forge-1-7-1" />
The bias you measure is therefore a *floor*. Change nothing about the test, refactor a constant out
of the contract so the dictionary stops scraping it, and the clamped version gets
worse.<Cite src="measured-fuzz-probe-1-7-1" />

Notice also the second column of the table. Eight runs landed on zero under clamping, against 25
under `bound`. Clamping does not only over-sample the maximum; it under-samples the *other*
boundary, because nothing in it moves values toward `min`. On a range whose lower end is where the
interesting behaviour lives — a fee that behaves differently at zero, a share calculation that
divides by a supply that can be empty — the clamp is simultaneously spending almost everything on one
end and starving the other.<Cite src="measured-fuzz-probe-1-7-1" />

`bound` avoids both by wrapping rather than saturating, and its two special-case branches are what
keep the endpoints reachable without making them dominant — `0, 1, 2, 3` map to
`min, min+1, min+2, min+3`, with the source comment stating the intent as ensuring coverage of the
min and max values.<Cite src="forge-std-stdutils-source" /><Cite src="forge-std-repo" /> Hence the 14
hits on 100 and 25 on 0: the boundaries are over-represented relative to the interior, deliberately
and by a factor of about ten, rather than by a factor of about
thirty.<Cite src="measured-fuzz-probe-1-7-1" />

Modulo measured indistinguishably from `bound` here — 88 distinct values, 13 hits on the
maximum.<Cite src="measured-fuzz-probe-1-7-1" /> It is cheaper and it is not biased in the way
clamping is. Two things keep it from being the default recommendation. It discards the boundary
structure `bound` preserves, so `x % 101` maps `type(uint256).max` to an arbitrary interior value
rather than to the maximum. And `min + x % (max - min + 1)` written by hand overflows when the range
is the full type, which `bound` handles by construction and which its `require(min <= max, ...)`
guard makes loud rather than silent.<Cite src="forge-std-stdutils-source" />

<Aside kind="note">
The same failure appears with `assume` in place of the clamp, for a different reason: a broad
`vm.assume` range collapses onto the endpoints because the dictionary is what survives the filter.
Three different mechanisms — saturating, filtering, and the dictionary — converge on the same
outcome, which is a test that reports 256 runs and examines a handful of values. Whenever a fuzz test
looks suspiciously fast or its `μ` and `~` are identical, this is the first thing to check.
</Aside>

## Every constraint is a claim about scope

Now the general problem, which survives writing all your constraints correctly.

A constraint does not make the test safer. It makes the test *smaller*. `bound(amount, 1, 1e18)` is a
statement that this property is being checked for amounts up to one token and for no larger amount.
That statement may be exactly right — perhaps the contract reverts above that, and there is a
separate test for the revert. It may also be the reason the overflow at `type(uint256).max` has been
sitting there for a year.

Three constraints that are individually reasonable and jointly hide a real class of bug:

- `amount = bound(amount, 1, 1e18)` — never sees the arithmetic edge of the type.
- `vm.assume(to != address(0))` — never sees the burn path, which in many tokens is a genuinely
  different code path with a genuinely different accounting rule.
- `vm.assume(recipient != sender)` — never sees self-transfer, which is where balance updates written
  as `balances[from] -= a; balances[to] += a;` behave differently from
  `balances[from] = balances[from] - a;` and where several real token bugs have lived.

None of those lines is wrong. Each of them is a decision about what is out of scope, taken silently,
usually to make a failing test pass. And the documentation's own framing pushes in the same
direction: `bound` is recommended because it is efficient, which is a statement about the search and
not about whether the range you chose is the right one.<Cite src="foundry-cheatcode-assume" /> The
tool has no opinion on your bounds and no way to form one.

<Aside kind="stop">
The structural problem is that **the constraints are written by the person who wrote the code**,
usually in the same sitting, and usually in response to a failure they judged uninteresting. That is
the same mind, in the same mood, drawing the boundary of the search and the boundary of the
implementation. When they disagree, the test does not tell you; it goes green.

This is not a hypothetical style complaint. It is the reason a fuzz test's constraints deserve at
least as much review attention as its assertion, and the reason "we fuzz that" is a much weaker claim
than it sounds when the person saying it also chose the domain.
</Aside>

### Reading constraints as a reviewer

Four questions, in order. They take about ninety seconds on a test you have never seen.

1. **What is the declared domain?** Read the parameter types. `uint256` claims everything;
   `uint16` has already narrowed to 65,536 values before a single constraint runs.
2. **What does each constraint remove?** Write the surviving interval down. Do it literally — the
   act of writing `[1, 1e18]` next to a `uint256` parameter is what makes the exclusion visible.
3. **Where would the bug you fear live?** If the answer is outside the surviving interval, the test
   cannot find it and the green result means nothing about it.
4. **Is the assertion still non-trivial inside the surviving interval?** Constraints and assertions
   interact. Narrow the domain enough and many assertions become tautologies.

That fourth question is the one people skip, and it is the one that catches the worst tests.

## Fixtures: enumerate instead of hoping

Everything so far has been about sampling. The fix for "the value I care about might not get tried"
is to stop sampling for that value and enumerate it.

A **fixture** is a dataset attached to a parameter *by name*. It is a storage array prefixed with
`fixture` followed by the dataset name, or a function named the same way, and the fuzzed parameter
whose name matches it draws from it.<Cite src="foundry-forge-testing" /> A test whose name begins
with `table` is then executed once per entry rather than
sampled.<Cite src="foundry-forge-testing" /><Cite src="foundry-testing-config" />

```solidity
contract TableTest is Test {
    Fees f;
    uint256[] public fixtureAmount = [0, 1, 9_999, 10_000, 10_001, type(uint128).max];

    function setUp() public { f = new Fees(); }

    function tableFeeConserved(uint256 amount) public view {
        assertEq(f.net(amount) + f.feeOn(amount), amount);
    }
}
```

The parameter is `amount`; the fixture is `fixtureAmount`. On forge 1.7.1 that runs six times, once
per entry, in order:<Cite src="measured-fuzz-probe-1-7-1" />

```text
[PASS] tableFeeConserved(uint256) (runs: 6, μ: 12200, ~: 12200)
```

`runs: 6` — the run count is now the size of your dataset, not a sampling budget. This is
deterministic enumeration. Nothing about it depends on a seed, a dictionary, or luck.

That makes it the right home for the values a domain expert knows matter: zero, one wei, the exact
threshold in a fee table, the value one below and one above a tier boundary, the maximum of the type
the contract will actually receive. Those get tried by construction.

### The part that is easy to miss

A fixture is not confined to `table` tests. On forge 1.7.1, an ordinary `testFuzz_` function whose
parameter name matches a fixture draws from it too — measured over 256 runs against
`fixtureAmount = [11, 22, 33]`, the three fixture values accounted for 72 runs, roughly 28% of the
campaign, with the remaining runs drawn normally and 146 distinct values seen
overall.<Cite src="measured-fuzz-probe-1-7-1" />

So the two mechanisms compose, and the practical pattern is:

- **`table` test** when you want exactly your list and nothing else — a specification table, a set of
  known-bad inputs from a past incident, a compatibility matrix.
- **`testFuzz_` plus a fixture** when you want the random sweep *and* a guarantee that the nasty
  values are in it.

The second is usually what you want for a property, and it directly repairs the failure mode from
lesson two: you are no longer relying on the dictionary to have scraped the constant that matters,
because you have named it yourself.

Take the reflection prompt for this module seriously for one test. Here is an ERC-4626-style vault
property from a real-looking suite:

```solidity
function testFuzz_DepositThenRedeemNeverProfits(uint256 assets) public {
    assets = bound(assets, 1e6, 1_000_000e18);
    vm.startPrank(alice);
    uint256 shares = vault.deposit(assets, alice);
    uint256 back   = vault.redeem(shares, alice, alice);
    vm.stopPrank();
    assertLe(back, assets);
}
```

Draw the domain, shade what is tested, and say what fraction of the parameter's declared domain is
left untested — and whether the bug you would most fear in this contract lives in it.

<Answer>
**Shaded region: `[1e6, 1e24]` out of `[0, 2^256 - 1]`. Untested fraction: about
1 − 10^24 / 1.16 × 10^77, which is 1 to within about fifty decimal places.** That figure is not the
useful part, though, because most of the excluded domain is amounts no token will ever have. The
useful part is *which specific excluded values matter*.

**Three regions are excluded and each one is a known bug class.**

*Below `1e6`.* You already know the ERC-4626 inflation case: the danger is a deposit that rounds down
to zero shares against a manipulated `totalAssets`. That is a small-numerator problem, and the lower
bound of `1e6` is precisely the region where it lives. Whoever wrote `1e6` almost certainly wrote it
because small amounts were producing failures — which is to say, the constraint was added to silence
the bug it was meant to find.

*Zero itself.* `deposit(0)` is a distinct code path in most vaults: it may revert, may mint zero
shares, may emit an event with a zero amount that a downstream indexer chokes on. The bound excludes
it entirely.

*Above `1e24`.* The overflow edge of `assets * totalSupply / totalAssets`. Whether it is reachable
depends on the real supply cap, but the test is not the place that decision gets recorded.

**The assertion is also weaker than it looks inside the shaded region.** `assertLe(back, assets)`
permits `back == 0`. A vault that returns nothing at all passes this property for every input. The
property that would have caught the rounding attack is not "never profits" but "loses at most one
wei", or better, "loses at most one wei and `shares > 0`" — and once you write the second clause you
have a test that fails on the excluded region and forces the bound to be justified rather than
assumed.

**What to actually change.**

1. Split into two properties. `bound(assets, 1, type(uint96).max)` for the round-trip loss bound, and
   a separate test for `deposit(0)` with an explicit expectation.
2. Tighten the assertion to `assertLe(assets - back, 1)` plus `assertGt(shares, 0)`. Now the
   constraint cannot hide the bug by making the assertion vacuous.
3. Add `uint256[] public fixtureAssets = [0, 1, 2, 1e6 - 1, 1e6, type(uint96).max];` so those points
   are tried by construction rather than by whether the dictionary scraped
   them.<Cite src="foundry-forge-testing" /><Cite src="measured-fuzz-probe-1-7-1" />
4. Write down, in a comment above the bound, *why* the lower bound is where it is. If you cannot,
   that is the finding.

**The general lesson.** The most dangerous constraint in a fuzz test is the one that was added to
make a failing test pass. It is indistinguishable in the source from one that was added because the
domain genuinely stops there, and it is the reason step four is not optional.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**Immediately, in review.** You now have a ninety-second procedure for a fuzz test you have never
seen, and a measured reason to run it: the difference between eight distinct inputs and eighty-eight
is one line of constraint, and no output distinguishes them.

**In the next lesson.** Everything so far has assumed the test either passes or fails. It does
neither reliably: without a pinned seed the same commit can go red today and green tomorrow, and both
results are correct. Lesson five is about the counterexample that appears, where forge writes it, and
how to turn a probabilistic finding into a permanent one.

**When you write the constraints on an invariant handler.** The same reasoning applies with much
higher stakes, because a handler's constraints shape not one call but every sequence the campaign can
construct. A handler that bounds a deposit to a comfortable range prevents the campaign from ever
reaching the accounting state where the bug lives, over hundreds of thousands of
calls.<Cite src="foundry-testing-config" />
