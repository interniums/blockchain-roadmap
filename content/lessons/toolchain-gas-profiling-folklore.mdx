The first time these were measured for this lesson, the results were spectacular. Caching
`array.length` outside a 32-iteration loop saved **69,434 gas**. Switching `i++` to `++i` saved
**26,387**. Both far larger than anything the previous lesson would predict for a
micro-optimisation.

Both were artefacts.<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" /> The probe called each variant once, in order, in the same test — so the
first call of each pair paid cold prices on every slot it touched and the second found them warm and
dirty.<Cite src="eip-2929" /> The measurement was of statement order, not of the code.

Warm the state first, run each variant from an identical starting point, and the numbers collapse.
Here is the same probe done properly, 32-iteration loops, measured on forge 1.7.1 with solc
0.8.36:<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" />

All figures measured on this machine, warm and dirty starting state, 32-iteration loops.<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" />

Three of the ecosystem's most-repeated tips, and none of them behaves the way the tip says.

## `++i` versus `i++`

With the optimizer off, `++i` is ahead by **116 gas across 32 iterations** — about 3.6 gas per
iteration.<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" />

Turn the optimizer on and the sign flips: `i++` comes out **44 gas cheaper**. Under via-IR, `i++` is
again ahead, by 24. The temporary that the folklore is about is eliminated by both optimising
pipelines, and what remains is noise at the level of instruction scheduling.

So the honest summary is: worth about three gas per iteration on the one configuration where it is
worth anything, and slightly negative on the two configurations a production build actually uses.
Meanwhile the rule costs a comment on every pull request, forever.

## Caching `array.length`

This one is real — under the pipeline most repositories are on — and the usual explanation for
dismissing it is wrong.

Under legacy codegen, hoisting the length read out of the loop saves **3,436 gas** with the
optimizer off and **3,183** with it on: roughly 100 gas per iteration, which is a warm `SLOAD` per
iteration for a storage array.<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" /><Cite src="measured-forge-1-7-1" /> The optimizer does **not** hoist it. It cannot in general
— the loop body could modify the array — and here it does not even in the case where it could.<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" />

Under via-IR the picture inverts: caching costs **66 gas more** than reading `arr.length` each
iteration.<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" /> The IR pipeline does the hoist, and the hand-written local is then a redundant
stack slot.

The lesson is not "cache the length" or "don't". It is that this tip's value is a **property of your
compiler configuration**, and that it is worth roughly 100 gas per iteration on a storage array and
approximately nothing on a `memory` or `calldata` array, where the length is not a storage read at
all.<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" />

## The narrow loop counter

`uint8 i` costs **456 gas more** than `uint256 i` over the same 32 iterations with the optimizer
off, and it stays more expensive under both optimising pipelines.<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" /> The EVM word is 256 bits; a
narrower type has to be masked back into range on every operation, and there is no slot being shared
to pay for it. This is the loop-counter form of the packing result from the previous lesson: the
saving comes from sharing a slot, never from the type.

Foundry's own optimisation guide makes the same point structurally, by leading its storage section
with a packed struct rather than with a narrow scalar.<Cite src="foundry-gas-optimization-guide" /> The type is the mechanism; [slot
packing](/c/slot-packing) is the saving.

There is a second cost to this one that does not show up in gas at all. A `uint8` counter overflows
at 255, and in a `for` loop whose bound is a variable it is a live infinite-loop bug waiting for
someone to raise the bound. You are paying gas for a footgun.

## `optimizer_runs`: the one setting that actually moves the needle

On forge 1.7.1 the resolved defaults are `optimizer = false` and `optimizer_runs = 200`.<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" /> The
runs value is the compiler's estimate of how many times each function will be called over the
contract's lifetime: low favours small bytecode and cheap deployment, high favours inlining and
cheap execution.

The same contract, three configurations:<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" />

| Setting | Deployment cost | Deployed size | `credit` (min) | `debit` |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| optimizer off | 312,681 | 1,232 | 34,112 | 32,251 |
| on, `runs = 200` | 190,387 | 662 | 33,488 | 31,940 |
| on, `runs = 1,000,000` | 218,236 | 795 | 33,452 | 31,904 |

Same contract, same suite, same solc 0.8.36; only the two compiler keys changed.<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" /><Cite src="foundry-solc-config" />

**Subgoal — separate the two decisions, because they are usually collapsed into one.**

*Turning the optimizer on* cut deployment cost by 39% and deployed size by 46%, and took 624 gas off
`credit` and 311 off `debit`.<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" /> There is no trade here; it is better on both axes. If you
measure gas with the optimizer off — which is what you get by default — you are not measuring your
production build, and every absolute figure in this module carries that caveat with it.

*Raising the runs from 200 to a million* is where the trade lives. It cost **27,849 more gas to
deploy** and **133 more bytes** of code, and bought **36 gas per call**.<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" /> Break-even is 27,849
÷ 36 ≈ **774 calls**, and that ignores the bytes, which are the scarcer resource near the
deployed-code limit.

For this contract, 774 calls is a low bar and a million runs would pay for itself. For a contract
deployed once per user — a wallet, a vault, a clone — deployment cost is paid every time and the
runtime saving is amortised over one user's lifetime, so a *low* runs value is correct. The number
to put in `optimizer_runs` is an estimate of calls-per-deployment, and almost nobody makes that
estimate deliberately.

<Aside kind="warn">
Whatever you choose, pin it, and [pin the compiler](/c/solc-pinning) with it. `solc` auto-detection
resolves per file from pragmas and can select a different compiler on a different machine, changing
bytecode and therefore gas.<Cite src="foundry-solc-config" /> A gas number is reproducible only alongside the compiler version,
the optimizer flag, the runs value and the pipeline.
</Aside>

## About via-IR

The via-IR column above is the best in the table on most rows, which invites an obvious conclusion.
Two cautions before you act on it.

**It is not the default.** On forge 1.7.1 `via_ir` resolves to `false`.<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" /> Legacy codegen is what
solc 0.8.36 uses unless you ask otherwise, which is why the middle column is the one describing most
production builds today.

**It does not retire "stack too deep".** The IR pipeline moves that limit rather than removing it,
and the newer codegen work in this area is not something to plan a release around. Treat via-IR as a
build configuration to adopt deliberately, measure, and pin — not as a fix that makes a class of
compiler error go away.

The practical shape most teams land on is two profiles: legacy and unoptimised for the inner loop,
because it compiles fast and produces readable traces; optimised — and, if you have measured it,
via-IR — for the profile that produces release artefacts and the gas numbers you quote. Just make
sure the snapshot is generated and checked under the same one.

<Answer>
**`i++` → `++i`: approximately zero, possibly negative.** Measured under exactly this configuration

to 20 iterations that is tens of gas, in the direction opposite to the request. Not worth doing and
not worth arguing about; measure it and move on.

**Caching `items.length`: real, roughly 100 gas per iteration, so about 2,000 gas.** This is a
storage array, so each `items.length` is a warm `SLOAD` after the first. Measured on this pipeline,
caching saved 3,183 gas over 32 iterations.<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" /> This is the one item on the list that pays, and it
would not pay under via-IR — so the reply should say which pipeline it is true for.

**`uint8` counter: costs gas, and adds a bug.** Measured, a `uint8` counter cost 456 gas *more* over
32 iterations, because narrow types are masked on every operation and no slot is being shared.<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" />
And "never longer than 200" is a property of today's data, not an invariant the type enforces: raise
that bound past 255 and the loop never terminates. Decline this one on correctness grounds before
you get to the gas.

**The reply.** "Took the length cache — about 2,000 gas here, and it's real on our legacy pipeline
though it would not be under via-IR. Skipped the increment: measured on our config, `i++` is

want to check them."

less than one avoided cold `SLOAD`. What a measured reply buys is the end of the checklist: the next
person reads the thread instead of re-litigating it, and the review budget goes back to the storage
layout, where the money actually is.

## The rule this module leaves you with

Every claim in this lesson took under an hour to check with the probe from earlier in the module.
That is the whole point. Gas folklore survives because it is cheap to repeat and nobody measures it,
and it costs review attention on every pull request forever in exchange for savings that mostly do
not survive contact with a warm state and an optimising compiler.

So: when someone hands you a tip, measure it — on your contract, with a sink so the compiler cannot
delete the operation, from a warmed starting state, under your production compiler settings, with
the isolation setting recorded. Then publish the number. One measured number retires a piece of
folklore permanently; one more repetition of the tip does not.

## Where this goes

**The practice exercises** for this module ask you to produce three numbers for one function and
explain what each includes, and to swap a storage reentrancy guard for a transient one and measure
the result rather than quoting it.

**The CI module** turns the snapshot file into a job, and makes the choice this module has been
building toward: whether gas informs the reviewer or blocks the merge.

**The security track** picks up the other half of the trade. Every optimisation on the ranking in
the previous lesson removes a check, a slot, or a bound. Knowing what a change costs is half the
decision; knowing what it removes is the other half, and that is where the incidents live.
