Every figure above was measured with a probe whose result is sunk into storage, under the pricing
EIP-2929 established.<Cite src="measured-forge-1-7-1" /><Cite src="eip-2929" />

Look at the spread. The most expensive thing on the list is two hundred times the cheapest, and
every entry above 1,000 gas is a **storage** operation. Nothing else on an EVM comes close:
arithmetic, comparisons, jumps and stack manipulation are single digits.

That is the whole economics of a contract in one table. **How many distinct [storage
slots](/c/storage-slot) does this function touch, and in what state does it find them?** Answer that
and you have predicted the cost to within a few percent. Everything else is rounding.

Notice what the table does *not* have: a column for the operation itself. There is no "cost of a
write". There is a cost of a write *to a slot in a particular state*, and the spread between the
cheapest and the most expensive version of the same `SSTORE` is 200×. Any sentence of the form "this
function costs N gas" is missing its second half.

## Packing is a first-write optimisation, and only that

The standard advice is to pack narrow fields into one slot. It is good advice and it is usually
justified with the wrong argument, so here is the measurement.

Three contracts, each with a `set(x)` that writes three fields. One uses three `uint256` in three
slots; one uses three `uint64` deliberately spaced into three slots; one uses three `uint64` that
share a single slot.<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" />

All six figures from one run of one suite, optimizer off.<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" />

Read the two columns separately, because they say opposite things.

**On the first write, packing saves 43,541 gas.** That is two avoided zero-to-non-zero writes, each
20,000 plus the 2,100 cold surcharge — 44,200 nominal<Cite src="measured-forge-1-7-1" /> — and the measurement lands 659 under it
because the packed version does more masking work in exchange. A 60% reduction. This is the real win
and it is enormous.

**On every later write, packing costs 568 gas more than three plain `uint256` slots.**<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" /> Once
all three slots are warm and already dirty in this transaction, each separate write is 100 gas,
while the packed write is a read-modify-write with masking on a single slot. The narrow types have
bought nothing and the masking is pure overhead.

And note the middle row: three `uint64` in three separate slots costs **more** than three `uint256`
in three slots, in both columns. Narrowing a type without packing it is strictly a loss.

<Aside kind="note">
Foundry's own optimisation guide leads with the same conclusion — "storage is the most expensive
operation", followed immediately by a packed-struct example.<Cite src="foundry-gas-optimization-guide" /> The refinement this measurement
adds is *when* the packing pays: on the transition into non-zero state. For a contract whose slots
are written once at initialisation and read forever after, packing is a large, permanent win. For a
hot counter written repeatedly inside single transactions, it is a small, permanent loss.
</Aside>

## The one genuinely large modern win

[Transient storage](/c/transient-storage) is the exception to "storage is expensive": `TSTORE` and
`TLOAD` cost 100 each, with no cold tier at all, because the values never reach the state trie and
are discarded at the end of the transaction.<Cite src="eip-1153" /><Cite src="measured-forge-1-7-1" />

The canonical application is the reentrancy lock, and the canonical *number* for it is wrong.
Circulating figures put the storage guard at about 7,100 gas and the transient one at about 200, a
35× ratio. Measured, the transient guard's overhead is **284** gas and the storage guard's is
**3,104** with a warm lock slot — about 11×.<Cite src="measured-forge-1-7-1" />

The two numbers disagree because the storage guard does not have a cost, it has a **range**. The
lock slot is cold on the first guarded call in a transaction and warm on every one after it, so the
overhead runs from roughly 3,100 to roughly 7,100 depending on where in the transaction the call
falls.<Cite src="measured-forge-1-7-1" /><Cite src="eip-2929" /> The transient guard is flat, because there is no cold tier to be on the wrong
side of.

Publish the range and the reason. A single number for the storage guard is how the 35× claim got
into circulation in the first place.

## Logs are cheap, and that is a design lever

Events are the one write-shaped thing that is not expensive. A topic costs 375, measured as a clean
increment: `LOG0` 784, `LOG1` 1,162, `LOG2` 1,540 in the same harness.<Cite src="measured-forge-1-7-1" /> The data body is
charged per byte, and none of it touches the state trie.

Set that against a storage slot at 20,000 for the first write,<Cite src="measured-forge-1-7-1" /> and a rule falls out that a
great deal of production Solidity gets wrong: **if the only consumer of a value is off-chain, emit
it, do not store it.** A mapping written purely so an indexer can read it back is paying tens of
thousands of gas to duplicate information an event carries for hundreds. The constraint is that
contract code cannot read logs — so the moment any on-chain path needs the value, the slot comes
back.

The `credit` function in this module's probe writes two slots and emits one indexed event. The event
is under 4% of the call's cost.

## The cheapest write is the one you skip

Two patterns, both worth more than everything in the next lesson combined.

**Accumulate in memory, write once.** A loop that does `balance += i` a hundred times performs a
hundred storage reads and a hundred storage writes; hoisting into a local and writing the result
once performs one of each. Foundry's own guide leads with exactly this example.<Cite src="foundry-gas-optimization-guide" /> With the slot
warm and dirty each iteration is only 100 gas, so the saving is thousands rather than hundreds of
thousands — but it is thousands, and it costs nothing in readability.

**Do not write a value that is already there.** An `SSTORE` of the same value a slot already holds
costs 100, but you paid a cold `SLOAD` of 2,100 to find that out unless the slot was already
warm.<Cite src="measured-forge-1-7-1" /> The guard is worth it when the no-op case is common and the alternative is a 2,900 or
20,000 write; it is a loss when writes almost always change the value. That is a workload question
with a measurable answer, which by now should be a familiar shape.

## Refunds, and the ceiling on them

Clearing a slot back to zero earns a refund. Two constants from EIP-3529 bound what that is worth:
`SSTORE_CLEARS_SCHEDULE` is set to `SSTORE_RESET_GAS + ACCESS_LIST_STORAGE_KEY_COST`, **4,800 gas**,
and the maximum refund for a whole transaction is `gas_used // MAX_REFUND_QUOTIENT` where the
quotient was raised from 2 to **5**.<Cite src="eip-3529" /> The same EIP removed the `SELFDESTRUCT` refund entirely.

You can watch the cap bite. A contract with eight pre-filled slots, measured under isolation so the
figures are transaction-level:<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" />

| Transaction | Gas |
|---|---|
| clear 1 slot | 21,962 |
| clear 8 slots | 49,953 |
| increment 8 slots (no refund) | 64,109 |

Same contract, same fixture, one argument changed.<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" />

**Subgoal — recover the refund actually paid, because the headline constant is not what you
receive.**

For the single-slot clear: the transaction consumed 26,762 before refunds, and 26,762 // 5 = 5,352,
which is above 4,800. So the full 4,800 was paid and the transaction settled at 21,962.<Cite src="eip-3529" /> The
nominal refund and the received refund agree.

For the eight-slot clear the nominal refund is 8 × 4,800 = **38,400**. But the transaction consumed
62,441, and 62,441 // 5 = **12,488**. The cap binds. You receive 12,488 — under a third of what you
earned — and the transaction settles at 49,953, which is exactly gas used minus one fifth of gas
used.

That is the shape to remember: **a transaction can never cost less than four fifths of what it
consumed.** Clearing eight slots to reclaim cost buys you a third of the reclaim you would naively
compute, and clearing eighty buys you no more, proportionally, than clearing eight.

The design consequence is blunt. Any mechanism whose economics depend on clearing state to reclaim
gas — gas tokens being the historical example, which is precisely what EIP-3529 set out to
kill<Cite src="eip-3529" /> — does not work. And a "cleanup" function that zeroes a hundred slots is not cheap; it is
expensive with a discount capped at 20%.

<Aside kind="warn">
Note also which direction the cap makes you round. `eth_estimateGas` and your gas report both see
the post-refund number, but the transaction must still be *funded* for the pre-refund number: 62,441
has to be available even though 49,953 is charged. A gas limit set from the measured 49,953 does not
run.
</Aside>

## Calldata: the model changed under you

The classic model is 4 gas per zero byte and 16 per non-zero byte, on top of the 21,000 base — the
[intrinsic charge](/c/intrinsic-gas) levied before the first opcode runs.<Cite src="eip-2028" /> The previous lesson
used it to reconcile a measurement to the gas: the 68 calldata bytes of a `credit(address,uint256)`
call, 8 non-zero and 60 zero, come to 8 × 16 + 60 × 4 = 368, and 21,000 + 368 = 21,368 exactly
matched the difference isolation introduced.<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" />

That model is now a floor away from being complete. EIP-7623 defines `tokens_in_calldata =
zero_bytes + nonzero_bytes × 4`, prices the standard path at `STANDARD_TOKEN_COST = 4` per token,
and adds a second term at `TOTAL_COST_FLOOR_PER_TOKEN = 10` per token:<Cite src="eip-7623" />

```
tx.gasUsed = 21000 + max(
    4 * tokens_in_calldata + execution_gas_used + creation_extra,
    10 * tokens_in_calldata
)
```

Two readings of the same formula:

**The standard path is unchanged.**<Cite src="eip-7623" /> 4 gas per token, and a non-zero byte is 4 tokens, which is
the familiar 16-and-4. For the `credit` call: 60 + 8 × 4 = 92 tokens, standard cost 368. Identical
to before.

**The floor is a second bid.** The same 92 tokens have a floor of 920. Execution on that call was
20,339, so the first branch wins by a mile and the floor never applies — which is why the earlier
reconciliation came out exact under the old model.

The floor bites on transactions that are **mostly data and do little computation**: a rollup posting
a batch, a contract storing a blob of bytes, a bridge relaying a proof. For those, the old model
under-prices the transaction and your local measurement will be low. Read the constants from the EIP
rather than from any lesson, including this one — that is the parameter table's whole purpose, and
these are exactly the values that move at a fork.<Cite src="eip-7623" />

<Aside kind="note">
There is a design lever hiding in `tokens_in_calldata`. A zero byte is 1 token and a non-zero byte
is 4, under both the standard price and the floor. Left-padded ABI encoding produces a great many
zero bytes — 60 of the 68 in that `credit` call — which is why ABI-encoded calldata is cheaper per
byte than its length suggests, and why a tightly packed custom encoding wins less than the byte
count implies. Count tokens, not bytes.
</Aside>

## On a rollup, there is a second bill

Everything above prices execution. On [a rollup](/c/rollup), execution is not the whole invoice.

OP Mainnet's fee is documented as the sum of an execution gas fee, an L1 data fee, and — after the
Isthmus upgrade — an operator fee.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-fees" /> The execution gas fee is described as exactly the fee you
would pay for the same transaction on Ethereum; the L1 data fee is named as the only component that
differs from an Ethereum transaction, and it arises because the transaction's data is published to
L1.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-fees" />

What that fee depends on is the interesting part: the estimated size of the transaction **in bytes
after compression**, plus the current Ethereum gas price and/or blob gas price. For blob-enabled
chains, the blob data gas price largely determines it.<Cite src="op-docs-transaction-fees" />

So on a rollup you are optimising against **two independent axes**:

- **Execution gas**, priced by the table at the top of this lesson, and visible in your gas report.
- **Compressed transaction size**, priced by an L1 market you do not control, and completely
  invisible to every instrument in this module.

<Misconception
  belief="Gas optimisation is the same job on L1 and on an L2."
  reality="An L2 transaction carries a second, separately-priced component that scales with its compressed size in bytes and is set by the L1 blob or base-fee market. A change that adds execution steps but shrinks the payload can be a net win there and a net loss on L1."
  why="Both are denominated in gas and both are reported as one fee, which hides that two different markets produced it."
  src="op-docs-transaction-fees" />

Resist the urge to attach a ratio to this. How much of an L2 fee is data depends on the L1 blob
market at the moment of posting, and blob pricing moves; the published splits disagree with each
other because they were taken at different times under different conditions. What is stable — and
enough to design with — is that **calldata size is a first-class cost axis on an L2 and a minor one
on L1**, and that your gas report measures neither.

The practical consequence is that "optimise this contract" is not a well-defined instruction until
someone names the chain. On L1, cut slots. On a rollup, cut bytes first, and be willing to spend
execution gas to do it.

<Answer>
**Both are right about their own case, and the workload picks the winner — Proposal A.**

Author B's measurement is the warm-and-dirty column: three separate slots at 100 gas each beat one
packed read-modify-write, by around 568 gas on the measurement in this lesson.<Cite src="measured-gas-profiling-1-7-1" /> That is real,
and it is the wrong column for this workload.

**Count the events, weighted by tier.** Registration is one zero-to-non-zero write per slot: 20,000
each, plus the 2,100 cold surcharge. Packing turns three of those into one, saving on the order of
44,000 gas per user, once. Reads are 2,100 cold and 100 warm; the packed layout turns three cold
reads into one, saving about 4,200 on every interaction that reads all three fields — which is every
interaction. Updates happen on one interaction in ten and are the only case where packing loses, by
hundreds.

So packing wins tens of thousands of gas at registration, thousands per read, and loses hundreds on
a tenth of interactions. It is not close.

**What would change my mind.** Two things, both measurable rather than arguable. First: if `balance`
were updated many times *inside a single transaction* — a batch settlement looping over the same
user — the dirty-write path dominates and the packed layout's masking is paid every iteration with
none of the first-write saving. Second: if the three fields were read independently rather than
together, so that a typical call touches one field and pays the masking without amortising the
shared slot.

**The habit worth taking.** "Packing is cheaper" and "packing is more expensive" are both incomplete
sentences. The complete one names the transition — zero to non-zero, non-zero to non-zero, or
already dirty — and how often each occurs in the workload. Then it is arithmetic.

## The ranking, once

Sorted by how much a change of that kind is worth, and it is not close between tiers:

1. **Remove a distinct storage slot from the hot path.** Thousands to tens of thousands.
2. **Move a transaction-scoped value from storage to transient storage.** Thousands, flat.
3. **Turn a zero-to-non-zero write into a non-zero-to-non-zero one**<Cite src="measured-forge-1-7-1" /> — the 1-and-2 trick used
   by reentrancy locks. Around 17,000 on every call that would have paid the higher tier.
4. **Shrink calldata, on an L2.** Priced separately, potentially the largest item on the invoice.
5. **Avoid a cold account access.** 2,500.
6. **Drop a log topic.** 375.
7. **Everything else.** Single digits to low hundreds, and the subject of the next lesson.
