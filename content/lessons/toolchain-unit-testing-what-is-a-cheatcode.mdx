A fuzz test on a payments contract fails after four months of green runs. The counterexample:

```
[FAIL] testFuzz_TransferTo(address) (runs: 137)
  Args: [0x7109709ECfa91a80626fF3989D68f67F5b1DD12D]
```

The engineer on call reads the trace, sees a transfer to a contract that behaves like nothing they
have ever deployed, and spends an hour looking for a reentrancy bug. There is no bug. The fuzzer
generated the one address in the entire 160-bit space that Foundry treats specially, and the test
called into it.

This lesson is about why that address exists, what happens when you call it, and the three things
that follow — including the exclusion that would have saved the hour.

## `vm` is an interface, not a keyword

Nothing about cheatcodes is compiler magic. Cheatcodes are made available through use of the
cheatcode address `0x7109709ECfa91a80626fF3989D68f67F5b1DD12D`, and you access them via `vm`,
available in the Forge Standard Library's `Test` contract.<Cite src="foundry-cheatcodes-overview" />

In forge-std that is two ordinary declarations:

```solidity
address internal constant VM_ADDRESS = 0x7109709ECfa91a80626fF3989D68f67F5b1DD12D;
Vm internal constant vm = Vm(VM_ADDRESS);
```

Both appear in forge-std v1.16.2's `Base.sol`, which `Test` inherits.<Cite src="forge-std-base-1-16-2" />
`Vm` is a Solidity interface — a long list of external function signatures with doc comments, and no
implementation anywhere.<Cite src="forge-std-vm-1-16-2" />

So when you write `vm.warp(1_000_000)`, the compiler does exactly what it does for any interface
call on any address: it ABI-encodes a call and emits a `CALL`. You already know how that encoding is
built — four bytes of selector derived from the canonical signature, then the arguments in 32-byte
words.<Cite src="solidity-abi-spec" /> Here it is, produced by `cast` on this machine:

```
$ cast sig "warp(uint256)"
0xe5d6bf02

$ cast calldata "warp(uint256)" 1000000
0xe5d6bf0200000000000000000000000000000000000000000000000000000000000f4240
```

What makes it a cheatcode is what happens on the other side: Foundry's EVM **intercepts** calls to
that address and performs the effect, instead of executing code
there.<Cite src="foundry-cheatcodes-overview" /> No contract at that address runs your calldata. The
host reads the selector, recognises `warp`, and reaches into the block environment.

### Three things that follow, and one of them will bite you

**Cheatcodes are typed, and `Vm.sol` is the list worth reading.** Because the interface is real
Solidity, every cheatcode has a signature and a doc comment sitting in one file in the library you
have already vendored.<Cite src="forge-std-vm-1-16-2" /> When the docs site and a blog post disagree,
`lib/forge-std/src/Vm.sol` in your own repo is the tiebreaker, because it is the version you are
actually compiling against.

**The list moves.** Cheatcodes ship with forge-std and with the binary, so what exists depends on
what you have pinned. A concrete example: articles still assert that Foundry cannot force a storage
slot cold. forge-std v1.16.2 declares `cool(address)` and `coolSlot(address,bytes32)`, with the doc
comment "Marks the slots of an account and the account address as
cold".<Cite src="forge-std-vm-1-16-2" /> Grep before you believe an absence claim.

**Anything can call the address.** It is a constant, not a capability handed to test contracts. This
is not a hypothetical concern — it is the mechanism behind the failure at the top of this lesson, and
it is the subject of the last section.

### `Vm` and `VmSafe`: two interfaces, one address

`Vm.sol` declares two interfaces, and `Base.sol` binds both to the same constant — `vm` and
`vmSafe`, each pointing at `0x7109…D12D`.<Cite src="forge-std-base-1-16-2" /> The relationship is
inheritance: `interface Vm is VmSafe`.<Cite src="forge-std-vm-1-16-2" />

The split is not "safe for you" versus "dangerous for you". The header comment is specific: the
`VmSafe` interface does not allow manipulation of the EVM state or other actions that may result in
Script simulations differing from on-chain execution, and it is recommended to only use those cheats
in scripts.<Cite src="forge-std-vm-1-16-2" />

That distinction matters the moment you factor a helper out of a test into something a deployment
script also imports. A script simulates, then broadcasts what it simulated. A helper that calls
`vm.warp` produces a simulation of a world that will not exist when the transaction lands. Typing the
helper against `VmSafe` makes the compiler enforce the boundary; typing it against `Vm` means you are
promising to remember.

One more line from the top of that file is worth reading: "Automatically @generated by
scripts/vm.py. Do not modify manually."<Cite src="forge-std-vm-1-16-2" /> `Vm.sol` is not a
hand-maintained wrapper that can drift from the implementation. That is why it is the tiebreaker.

## The families, and why the shape matters more than the names

There are hundreds of cheatcodes. Memorising them is the wrong goal; knowing which drawer to open is
the right one. The documented categories are: **Environment**, **Assertions**, **Fuzzer**,
**Forking**, **External**, **Signing**, **Utilities**, **Files**, **RPC** and **State
Snapshots**.<Cite src="foundry-cheatcodes-overview" />

Read them as answers to questions rather than as a taxonomy:

- **Environment** — *"what does the chain look like right now?"* `warp`, `roll`, `prank`, `deal`,
  `store`. These rewrite the world your contract observes. The next lesson is entirely about this
  family, because it is where the expensive mistakes live.
- **Assertions** — *"did the thing I expected happen?"* `expectRevert`, `expectEmit`, `expectCall`.
  Two lessons from now, and the rest of this module's title.
- **Fuzzer** — *"which generated inputs count?"* `assume`, and forge-std's `bound`. Constraining the
  search is its own skill and its own module.
- **Forking** — *"what does real deployed state look like?"* `createFork`, `selectFork`, `rollFork`.
- **External** — *"can I reach outside the EVM?"* `ffi`, `env`, JSON and TOML parsing.
- **Signing** — *"can I produce a valid signature for this key?"*
- **Utilities** — *"can I make this trace readable?"* `label`, `addr`, `makeAddr`.
- **Files**, **RPC**, **State Snapshots** — file access, direct RPC calls, and save/restore points
  for EVM state.

Two of these deserve a note now rather than later.

**Utilities are not cosmetic.** A trace full of `0x328809Bc…` is a trace you will misread. Two
cheatcodes fix that. `vm.label(address, string)` "Labels an address in call
traces".<Cite src="forge-std-vm-1-16-2" /> forge-std's `makeAddr(name)` composes it with key
derivation: it hashes the name to a private key, derives the address, and labels it — so the same
name always produces the same address across runs and machines, and the trace prints
`alice`.<Cite src="forge-std-stdcheats-1-16-2" />

The determinism is the underrated half. A hard-coded `address(0xA11CE)` is also readable, but it has
no key behind it, so the moment a test needs a signature from that account you are back to inventing
one. `makeAddrAndKey` hands you both.<Cite src="forge-std-stdcheats-1-16-2" /> Treat labelling as
part of writing the fixture, not as polish — every debugging session afterwards is cheaper.

**`ffi` is a hole in the sandbox.** The External family reaches outside the
EVM.<Cite src="foundry-cheatcodes-overview" /> `ffi` runs a command on your machine and returns its
output to the test, which is exactly as dangerous as it sounds when you have just cloned an
unfamiliar repository and typed `forge test`. Legitimate uses exist — differential testing against a
reference implementation in another language, generating fixtures from a script — and none of them
require you to run someone else's suite without reading the config first. Know the family exists so
that `ffi = true` in a `foundry.toml` reads as a fact about trust rather than as a build setting.

**State Snapshots are the escape hatch you will want twice a year.** The family lets a test capture
EVM state and return to it, which is how you compare two mutually exclusive continuations from one
expensive starting point without paying for the setup twice.<Cite src="foundry-cheatcodes-overview" />
It is the manual version of the isolation the runner already gives you between cases, and reaching
for it inside a single test is usually a sign the test is doing two jobs.

## The cheatcode address is a valid `address` value

Now the failure at the top.

Nothing in the type system distinguishes `0x7109709ECfa91a80626fF3989D68f67F5b1DD12D` from any other
`address`. A fuzzer asked to produce an `address` parameter is drawing from the full space, and that
space contains this one. When your contract then calls into the generated address, the call is
intercepted, the host tries to interpret your calldata as a cheatcode invocation, and what comes back
is not your contract's behaviour.

The documentation says so directly, and gives the fix as a line to copy: if you encounter errors for
this address when using fuzzed addresses in your tests, you may wish to exclude it from your fuzz
tests with `vm.assume(address_ != 0x7109709ECfa91a80626fF3989D68f67F5b1DD12D);`<Cite src="foundry-cheatcodes-overview" />

This is one of the cases where `vm.assume` is the right tool rather than the discouraged one.
`assume` discards the current run and starts another, so it is a poor way to express a *range*
constraint — the rejection rate eats your run budget.<Cite src="foundry-cheatcode-assume" /> But
excluding one address out of 2^160 rejects essentially nothing, which is precisely the situation
`assume` is for.

The habitual exclusion set for a fuzzed `address` parameter is three entries, and it is worth writing
all three every time rather than adding them one incident at a time:

```solidity
function testFuzz_TransferTo(address to, uint256 amount) public {
    vm.assume(to != address(0));                                          // burn hole
    vm.assume(to != address(this));                                       // the test contract
    vm.assume(to != 0x7109709ECfa91a80626fF3989D68f67F5b1DD12D);          // the cheatcode address
    amount = bound(amount, 0, token.balanceOf(alice));
    ...
}
```

<Aside kind="warn">
Note what the exclusions are *not* doing. They are narrowing the space the property is tested over,
and every narrowing is a place a bug can hide. Excluding `address(0)` because a transfer to it
reverts is fine; excluding it because the test failed there and you wanted green is how a real
defect gets fenced off. The constraints on a fuzz test are part of what a reviewer has to read, and
they are the part reviewers skip.
</Aside>

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="cheatcode-address-in-fuzzing">
A team hits the cheatcode-address failure and fixes it like this:

```solidity
contract PayoutTest is Test {
    function testFuzz_Payout(address recipient, uint96 amount) public {
        vm.assume(recipient.code.length == 0);   // "only pay EOAs, keeps it simple"
        vm.deal(address(payouts), amount);
        payouts.send(recipient, amount);
        assertEq(recipient.balance, amount);
    }
}
```

The suite is green and the cheatcode-address failure is gone. Name what this fix costs, and what it
does and does not actually exclude.

<Answer>
**It works, and for a reason the author probably did not intend.** The guard excludes any address
with code. Whether it excludes the cheatcode address depends on what `EXTCODESIZE` returns there —
which is a host implementation detail, not something the interface promises. A fix whose correctness
rests on an undocumented property of the harness is a fix that can stop working on a version bump,
silently, and reproduce the original mystery. Write the exclusion the documentation gives you:
`vm.assume(recipient != 0x7109709ECfa91a80626fF3989D68f67F5b1DD12D)`.<Cite src="foundry-cheatcodes-overview" />

**The real cost is the coverage it silently deletes.** `recipient.code.length == 0` removes every
contract recipient from the search space. That is the entire population that can reject a value
transfer, re-enter, consume unbounded gas in a `receive`, or revert on purpose. A payouts contract's
interesting failures are *exclusively* in the region this line excludes, so the fuzz test now
explores the part of the space where nothing can go wrong. It will stay green through a rewrite of
the payment loop.

**And the guard does not mean what it says.** `code.length == 0` has not identified an EOA since
EIP-7702 — a delegated EOA carries a code entry pointing at its
delegate.<Cite src="eip-7702" /> You met that as
[the broken code-length check](/c/code-length-check-broken); the comment "only pay EOAs" is
describing a check that no longer performs it.

**The shape to take away.** A constraint added to make a fuzz test stop failing needs a written
justification, because the constraint and the bug live in the same place. Two exclusions here look
identical in the diff — one is a documented harness artefact, the other quietly deletes the test's
purpose.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**Next lesson:** the Environment family in full — `prank`, `deal`, `warp`, `roll` — and the specific
way each one invites you to write a test that passes while proving nothing. You now have the frame
you need for all of them: each is a call with a moment and a lifetime, not a setting.

**Two lessons on:** the Assertions family, where the mechanism you have just learned explains the
module's nastiest trap. `expectRevert` is a call that *registers* something; the next call *consumes*
it. Once you see registration and consumption as two separate calls in a trace, the nesting bug stops
being surprising.

**And a habit for the whole track:** when a cheatcode does not behave as an article says, open
`lib/forge-std/src/Vm.sol`.<Cite src="forge-std-repo" /> The signature and the doc comment for the
version you are compiling against are twenty lines of reading, and they are authoritative in a way
that no rendered page pinned to no version can be — including for the pins this module uses, forge
1.7.1 and forge-std v1.16.2.<Cite src="measured-forge-1-7-1" /><Cite src="forge-std-vm-1-16-2" />
