The entire interface between the EVM and 128 KiB of blob data is 192 bytes wide, and the
specification lays it out to the byte:<Cite src="eip-4844" />

Send those 192 bytes to the precompile at address `0x0A` and it performs two checks and charges a
flat **50,000 gas**.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> There is no other route. A contract cannot read a byte of
blob data, cannot iterate it, cannot hash it, and cannot ask how long it is. It can call this, or it
can know nothing.

This lesson is about what those 192 bytes actually establish, what they conspicuously do not, and the
pattern — proof of equivalence — that turns one point evaluation into a claim about a rollup's entire
published batch.

## Two checks and a constant

The specification's pseudocode is short enough to read in full:<Cite src="eip-4844" />

```python
def point_evaluation_precompile(input: Bytes) -> Bytes:
    assert len(input) == 192
    versioned_hash = input[:32]
    z = input[32:64]
    y = input[64:96]
    commitment = input[96:144]
    proof = input[144:192]

    # Verify commitment matches versioned_hash
    assert kzg_to_versioned_hash(commitment) == versioned_hash

    # Verify KZG proof with z and y in big endian format
    assert verify_kzg_proof(commitment, z, y, proof)

    # Return FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_BLOB and BLS_MODULUS as padded 32 byte big endian values
    return Bytes(U256(FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_BLOB).to_be_bytes32() + U256(BLS_MODULUS).to_be_bytes32())
```

Four things in there deserve attention.

**Check one re-binds a 48-byte commitment to a 32-byte handle.** The EVM never holds a KZG
commitment. It holds a **versioned hash** — 32 bytes, produced as `0x01 || sha256(commitment)[1:]` —
and that is what `BLOBHASH` returns.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> The caller supplies the full commitment
in the input, and this check is what stops them supplying a *different* commitment that happens to
suit them. Without it the precompile would verify openings against a polynomial nobody had committed
to on-chain.

**Check two is the pairing equation from two lessons ago.** `verify_kzg_proof` is the same routine
the consensus layer uses: construct `[tau - z]_2` from the setup's second G2 point, construct
`[p(tau) - y]_1` from the commitment, and run one pairing
check.<Cite src="consensus-specs-deneb-polynomial-commitments" /> The reference string it verifies
against is the ceremony output shipped with the client, not something the caller
provides.<Cite src="consensus-specs-trusted-setup-4096" />

**The return value is two constants.** `FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_BLOB` and `BLS_MODULUS`, as two padded
32-byte big-endian words — 64 bytes total.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> It tells you nothing about your
call. It exists so a contract can learn the field parameters without hard-coding them, and it is
*not* a success flag. Success is signalled the EVM way: the call either returns or it does not.

**The gas is flat.** 50,000, regardless of anything.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Compare `BLOBHASH`, whose
cost is `HASH_OPCODE_GAS` — **3 gas**.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Four orders of magnitude separate
"which blob" from "one fact about the blob", and that gap is the pairing.

<Aside kind="warn">
**Canonicity is enforced, and it will surprise your encoder.** The precompile MUST reject
non-canonical field elements: the supplied `z` and `y` must be strictly less than
`BLS_MODULUS`.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> A 32-byte value produced by hashing something — a Fiat–Shamir
challenge, say — is a uniformly random 256-bit number, and the modulus sits just below `2^255`, so a
raw hash is out of range better than half the time. The consensus specification's own challenge
derivation reduces modulo `BLS_MODULUS` for exactly this
reason.<Cite src="consensus-specs-deneb-polynomial-commitments" /> Reduce before you send.
</Aside>

### Follow one claim from your laptop to the precompile

The round trip is short enough to hold in your head, and holding it is what makes the failure modes
obvious later. Five steps, three of them off-chain.

**Step 1 — build the blob.** Pack your bytes into 4,096 field elements, each strictly below
`BLS_MODULUS`.<Cite src="consensus-specs-deneb-polynomial-commitments" /> This is your encoding, and
it is the step nobody writes about.

**Step 2 — commit.** `blob_to_kzg_commitment` gives you 48 bytes: one linear combination of the
setup's Lagrange points against your field
elements.<Cite src="consensus-specs-deneb-polynomial-commitments" /><Cite src="c-kzg-4844" />

**Step 3 — derive the versioned hash yourself.** `0x01 || sha256(commitment)[1:]`.<Cite src="eip-4844" />
Read that slice carefully: you take the SHA-256 of the 48-byte commitment, **discard its first
byte**, and prepend `0x01`. The result is 32 bytes, not 33. Prepending without discarding is the
single most common way to get this wrong, and it produces a value that is the right shape and the
wrong number — so it fails at the precompile with no diagnostic beyond a failed call. Computing this
by hand and comparing it byte-for-byte against what your library returns is the cheapest possible
confidence check, and it is the acceptance criterion of this module's practice exercise.

**Step 4 — open at a point.** `compute_kzg_proof(blob, z)` returns the proof and the value `y`,
computing the quotient in evaluation form with the special case for `z` inside the
domain.<Cite src="consensus-specs-deneb-polynomial-commitments" />

**Step 5 — assemble and call.** Concatenate the five fields in the specified order, `staticcall`
address `0x0A`, and require the call to succeed.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Then flip one bit of `y` and
require that it fails. A test that only ever asserts the happy path has not tested a verifier.

## What the version byte is for

The `0x01` at the front of every versioned hash is not decoration. It is a scheme
identifier.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

The execution layer deliberately refers to blobs by a hash of the commitment rather than by the
commitment itself, so that the 32-byte interface every contract is written against stays fixed while
the cryptography underneath can be replaced. If a future scheme produces commitments of a different
size or a different type, it gets a new version byte, and contracts that check
`versionedHash[0] == 0x01` keep working — refusing, correctly, to interpret something they were not
written for.

Put that beside the previous lesson's closing point. The roadmap's direction of travel is away from
pairing-based schemes with structured setups, and nobody has printed a date. The version byte is what
the design put in place so that direction would not require rewriting every consumer. It is a
migration affordance built years before the migration, and it is the reason a contract that pattern-
matches on the version byte is doing something meaningful rather than defensive theatre.

## What the boolean means, exactly

A successful call establishes precisely this sentence:

> The blob whose versioned hash is `H`, read as a polynomial over the fixed evaluation domain, takes
> the value `y` at the point `z`.

Everything else is your job. Four things it specifically does not say:

**It does not say the blob is available.** The precompile never touches blob data; it checks
consistency between a commitment and an opening. Whether anyone still holds the blob, or ever served
it, is a property of the networking layer and its
incentives.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> A blob that was withheld from the moment it was published still
has a versioned hash on-chain and still passes this check for any opening the withholder chooses to
produce.

**It does not say `H` is the blob you meant.** `BLOBHASH` reads an index from the stack and returns
`tx.blob_versioned_hashes[index]` — or, if the index is out of range, **a zeroed bytes32
value**.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Not a revert. A contract that reads `BLOBHASH(3)` on a transaction
carrying two blobs gets thirty-two zero bytes and, unless it checks, treats that as a versioned hash.

**It does not say `z` was a fair question.** If the caller chose `z`, the caller chose which fact
about the polynomial gets established. That is fine when `z` encodes a position you care about; it is
catastrophic when `z` is supposed to be a random challenge, for the reason two sections below.

**It does not say anything about the other 4,095 values.** One opening, one value. The previous
lessons said this; here it becomes an on-chain gas line. Establishing the whole blob one point at a
time would cost 4,096 calls at 50,000 gas each, which is not a thing anyone does.

So how does a rollup establish something about a *whole* blob for 50,000 gas?

## Proof of equivalence

Here is the problem in its real form.

A validity rollup has two commitments to the same data, made with different machinery for different
reasons. It publishes its transaction data as a **blob**, which the protocol commits to with KZG on
BLS12-381 — that choice is not the rollup's to make.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> It proves its state
transition inside its own proof system, which commits to that same data with **its own scheme**,
chosen for proving performance and quite possibly hash-based rather than pairing-based.

Nothing so far connects them. The proof says "I correctly executed the transactions in the data
committed as `C_proof`". The chain sees a blob committed as `C_blob`. An operator who published one
batch and proved a different one satisfies both checks independently and has told the chain nothing
true.

**Proof of equivalence bridges the two, and the bridge is one random point.**

Both sides open their commitment at the *same* challenge point `z`, derived from the transcript. The
proof system proves, inside the proof, that its committed polynomial evaluates to `y` at `z`. The
blob side supplies a KZG opening for the same `z` and the same `y`, and the point-evaluation
precompile checks it on-chain for 50,000 gas.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> If both openings agree, the two
polynomials are equal — not certainly, but with the overwhelming probability you priced in the first
lesson of this module: two distinct polynomials of degree at most 4,095 agree at no more than 4,095
of the field's roughly `10^76` points.

The economics are worth stating plainly. The check costs the same 50,000 gas whether the blob holds
one transaction or ten thousand, because the pairing does not care how large the committed polynomial
was. That constant is what makes the pattern usable at all, and it is the same constant-size property
Kate, Zaverucha and Goldberg's paper claims for the scheme in
general.<Cite src="kate-zaverucha-goldberg" />

<Aside kind="note">
**Do not assume adoption.** Whether any particular production rollup uses this pattern — rather than,
say, posting and proving separately with the linkage established some other way — is something to
check per system rather than to assume from the architecture. This project's research explicitly did
not verify current adoption and does not assert it.<Cite src="chainpath-conflict-register" /> Learn
the pattern as a design tool; verify the deployment before you describe someone's system with it.
</Aside>

### Everything rests on who picks `z`

This is where the pattern is broken in practice, so it deserves its own treatment.

Suppose the prover picks `z` freely, after both polynomials are fixed. Two different degree-4,095
polynomials agree at up to 4,095 points, and a prover with free choice picks one of them. The
soundness argument does not degrade — it **evaporates**, because the argument was never about the
polynomials being close, only about the point being unpredictable.

So `z` must be derived by hashing a transcript that already includes both commitments, using the
[Fiat–Shamir](/c/non-interactivity-fiat-shamir) transform. Three failure modes follow, and all three
have produced real bugs in shipped systems:

**Incomplete transcript.** If any value the prover controls is left out of the hash, the prover can
fix `z` first and then choose that value afterwards. The hash must cover every input the prover could
still move.

**Missing domain separation.** A challenge derived for one purpose must not be reusable as a
challenge for another. The consensus specification does this explicitly, tagging its cell-batch
challenges with a dedicated domain string rather than hashing bare
inputs.<Cite src="consensus-specs-deneb-polynomial-commitments" />

**Unreduced output.** The hash gives 256 bits; the field takes values below `BLS_MODULUS`. Reduce, or
the precompile rejects your challenge as non-canonical — and a system that silently retries with a
different challenge until one is in range has just handed the prover a grinding
opportunity.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

The rule to carry: **whenever a verifier's randomness has been replaced by a hash, the only question
that matters is what went into the hash and whether the prover could still change any of it
afterwards.**

### Where the pattern generalises

Nothing about proof of equivalence is specific to rollups, and recognising the shape is worth more
than memorising the instance.

The general problem is: **the same data has been committed twice, under two schemes, by two systems
that cannot read each other's commitments.** That happens whenever a cheap, protocol-mandated
commitment (KZG on blobs) meets a commitment chosen for a different reason (a proof system's internal
scheme, a hash-based accumulator, another chain's format). The general solution is: open both at one
point that neither side could predict, and check the openings agree.

Two properties make the trick work, and both are properties of *polynomial* commitments rather than
of KZG in particular:

- The commitments bind **functions**, so "agree at a random point" is overwhelming evidence of
  "identical everywhere" — the argument from this module's first lesson.
- Openings are **constant size and constant to verify**, so the on-chain half costs the same
  regardless of how much data is being bridged.<Cite src="kate-zaverucha-goldberg" />

Swap either property out and the pattern collapses. Two Merkle roots over the same data cannot be
compared at a random point at all, because there is no such thing as a Merkle root's value at a point
— you would be reduced to comparing them leaf by leaf, which is the linear work you were avoiding.

## Cost, and the ceiling on how much you can bridge

The gas arithmetic is unusually simple, which makes it a good sanity check on any design.

One point-evaluation call is 50,000 gas, flat.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Reading the versioned hash to
feed it is 3.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> A rollup posting the maximum a single transaction may carry —
EIP-7594 caps that at **6 blobs per transaction** — and bridging each one with its own equivalence
check pays six calls, 300,000 gas, plus the 18 gas of `BLOBHASH`
reads.<Cite src="eip-7594" /><Cite src="eip-4844" />

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="point-evaluation-precompile">
A contract accepts a claim about blob data. Simplified:

```solidity
function attest(uint256 blobIndex, bytes32 z, bytes32 y, bytes calldata commitmentAndProof)
    external
{
    bytes32 vh = blobhash(blobIndex);
    bytes memory input = abi.encodePacked(vh, z, y, commitmentAndProof);
    (bool ok, ) = address(0x0a).staticcall(input);
    attested[msg.sender][z] = y;
}
```

Find every defect, and say which single one would let an attacker attest to a value of their choosing
with no valid blob at all.

<Answer>
**Four defects, and the fourth is the fatal one.**

**1. The return of `staticcall` is ignored.** `ok` is assigned and never read. The precompile signals
failure by failing the call, not by returning a flag — its 64-byte return value is
`FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_BLOB` and `BLS_MODULUS`, two constants that are identical on every successful
call.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> As written, a call that reverts is indistinguishable from one that
succeeds, and the attestation is recorded either way. This alone makes the whole function decorative:
`require(ok)` is not optional.

**2. `blobhash` on an out-of-range index returns zero, not a revert.** The opcode replaces the index
with `tx.blob_versioned_hashes[index]` if the index is in range, and **a zeroed bytes32 value**
otherwise.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> With `blobIndex = 99` on a transaction carrying no blobs, `vh` is
thirty-two zero bytes. Combined with defect 1 the call fails and nobody notices; fixed in isolation,
the call fails honestly — but the code should reject `vh == bytes32(0)` explicitly, because "no blob
here" is a distinct condition worth naming.

**3. Nothing checks the version byte or the input length.** `commitmentAndProof` is arbitrary
caller-supplied bytes, so `input` may not be 192 bytes at all, and the precompile asserts on
length.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> Encode the two 48-byte halves as fixed-size values rather than
concatenating an unbounded `bytes`, and check `vh[0] == 0x01` so that a future scheme version cannot
be silently reinterpreted by code written for this one.

**4. The fatal one: `z` is chosen by the caller.** Everything above is a correctness bug. This is a
soundness bug, and it survives fixing all three.

Suppose the contract is repaired so that `ok` is required and `vh` genuinely comes from a real blob
in this transaction. The attacker publishes a blob whose contents they control entirely, then calls
`attest` with any `z` they like and the honest `y` at that point, with an honest proof. Every check
passes, because every claim is true. What gets recorded is `attested[attacker][z] = y` — a true fact
about a polynomial the attacker authored.

If any consumer of this mapping treats `z` as "position 12 of the batch" or `y` as "the price at
block N", the attacker has attested to a value of their choosing without forging anything. The
precompile did its job perfectly; the contract asked the wrong question.

**The repair is not a check, it is a design rule.** `z` must be either a position the contract itself
determines, or a challenge derived by hashing a transcript that already contains the commitment — and
in the second case the contract must derive it, not accept it. Anything the prover can still choose
after seeing the commitment is not a challenge.

**The one-line summary worth keeping.** The precompile answers exactly the question it was asked. The
attack surface of a proof-consuming contract is almost never the proof — it is the question.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

You can now read the whole on-chain path: 192 bytes in, a versioned-hash re-binding, a pairing check
against the ceremony's reference string, 50,000 flat gas, and a boolean that means one carefully
bounded sentence. You know the pattern that turns that single sentence into a statement about an
entire published batch, and you know that the pattern's security lives entirely in how the challenge
point is derived rather than in the cryptography around it. The reference implementation's API is
where to go next if you want to build this rather than read about it.<Cite src="c-kzg-4844" />

One gap remains, and it is the one this lesson kept naming and refusing to close. Everything here
proves **consistency**: the piece you were shown matches the commitment. Nothing here proves
**availability**: that the data is out there to be fetched at all. The module's last lesson is about
the mechanism that attacks that problem — stretching each blob to twice its length before it is
distributed, so that hiding any part of it requires hiding more than half. That is not redundancy.
It is a way to make withholding detectable by someone who downloads almost nothing.
