Take the guest program from the zkVM module — the Fibonacci example, run with `n = 700,000`. Its
execution trace is hundreds of thousands of rows wide enough to hold a RISC-V machine state. Wrapped
for on-chain verification with Groth16, the proof of that execution is around **260
bytes**.<Cite src="sp1-docs-proof-types" /><Cite src="sp1-example-fibonacci-host" />

Ask the obvious question. Where did the trace go?

It was never sent. It was **committed to**, and then **opened at a handful of points the verifier
chose**. That is the whole trick, and it is not a detail of one proof system — it is the reason any
proof is short. Every succinct proof system is a way of arranging a large object so that a few
evaluations of it are enough to be convinced.

This module is about the object that makes that possible. You already know hash commitments and
Merkle trees from the fundamentals track, and you have met KZG from the blob side. This lesson is
about the thing those three are instances of, and about the single capability the third one has that
the first two structurally cannot.

## Binding and hiding are two properties, not one

A commitment scheme publishes a short value `C` standing for some data. Two properties are usually
named together and they are entirely separate:

**Binding** is a constraint on the *committer's future*. Having published `C`, you cannot later
produce a convincing story in which `C` stood for different data. Binding is what makes a commitment
an obligation.

**Hiding** is a constraint on the *verifier's present*. `C` on its own reveals nothing about the
data. Hiding is what makes a commitment a secret.

You already know these come apart, because you know that a plain hash of a low-entropy value is
binding but not hiding — `sha256("yes")` is binding perfectly well and reveals the answer to anyone
who tries both options, which is why a [hash commitment](/c/hash-commitment) needs a salt.

What is less obvious, and more useful, is that **a scheme can be entirely useful with only binding**,
and that Ethereum's most visible deployment of a polynomial commitment is exactly that case. A blob's
KZG commitment binds. It does not need to hide, because the blob is broadcast to the network on
purpose — publishing it is the entire point of the mechanism.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

Carry that separation into the rest of the track, because the two properties have different failure
modes. A break in hiding leaks a secret. A break in binding lets someone prove a false statement —
and in a proof system, that is the end of the system. The trusted setup lesson later in this module
is about a binding failure, not a confidentiality one.

## A commitment scheme is defined by the questions it answers

Here is the reframing that makes the rest of the module easy. Do not think of a commitment scheme as
"a short value standing for data". Think of it as a **query interface** with three operations:

```text
commit(data)                  -> C
open(query)                   -> proof
verify(C, query, answer, proof) -> bool
```

Every scheme you know fits that shape. What separates them is not the size of `C` — all three are
short — but the **query language**: what you are allowed to ask, and what the proof costs.

**A plain hash supports one query: "all of it."** The only opening is the preimage. Proof size is the
data. There is no way to establish anything about part of it without revealing the whole.

**A Merkle tree supports "element at index `i`."** The opening is a sibling path, and its size grows
with the data — logarithmically, which is genuinely good, but it grows. Crucially, the query language
is limited to *positions that exist*. You may ask about leaf 3,001 of 4,096. There is no leaf 9,000,
and there is no question you can ask that is not about a specific leaf.

**A polynomial commitment supports "what is `p(z)`?" — for any `z` you name.** Commit to a polynomial
`p`; later prove a claim of the form `p(z) = y` at a point of the verifier's choosing, with an
opening the verifier checks against the commitment alone. Kate, Zaverucha and Goldberg's original
result states the sizes: commitments are constant size, the overhead of opening one is constant, and
even opening at multiple evaluation points costs only a constant amount of
communication.<Cite src="kate-zaverucha-goldberg" />

Three query languages, increasing in expressiveness. The jump from the first to the second is what
made Merkle trees useful. The jump from the second to the third is what makes proof systems possible,
and it is worth being precise about why.

Put concrete sizes against the three for one job — establishing one value inside a 128 KiB Ethereum
blob, which is exactly 4,096 field elements of 32 bytes:<Cite src="eip-4844" />

The KZG row is not an optimisation of the Merkle row. The commitment and the opening are each a
single elliptic-curve point whose size is independent of the polynomial's degree, which is the
property Kate, Zaverucha and Goldberg's paper claims and the reason the scheme was interesting when
it was published.<Cite src="kate-zaverucha-goldberg" /><Cite src="eip-4844" />

## The clause that matters: `z` need not be an index

Re-read the third query language and notice what it does not say. It does not say "`z` must be one of
the positions where you put data". A polynomial is defined everywhere in the field. You can ask for
its value at a point that corresponds to no byte anyone ever wrote down, and the commitment already
determines the answer.

That single clause is the difference, and it produces two capabilities that a Merkle root cannot
imitate at any price.

**Capability one: the verifier can choose the question.** This is what makes proofs small. Suppose a
prover claims two large objects are equal — say, that the polynomial encoding the execution trace
satisfies a constraint polynomial. Reading both would cost linear work, which is exactly what the
verifier refuses to do. Instead the verifier picks a random point `z` and demands both sides'
evaluations there.

The soundness of that move is a fact about polynomials rather than about cryptography. Two distinct
polynomials of degree at most `d` agree at no more than `d` points, because their difference is a
nonzero polynomial of degree at most `d` and therefore has at most `d` roots. So if the prover is
lying — if the two objects differ at all — a uniformly random `z` catches it unless `z` happened to
land on one of at most `d` coincidences.

Put Ethereum's numbers on it. A blob is 4,096 field elements, so `d` is at most 4,095, and the field
modulus is the BLS12-381 scalar field:<Cite src="eip-4844" />

```text
BLS_MODULUS = 52435875175126190479447740508185965837690552500527637822603658699938581184513
d           = 4095
```

The probability that a random `z` misses a discrepancy is `d / BLS_MODULUS` — roughly one in `10^73`.
The verifier read one value and is as convinced as it is possible to be.

**Capability two: the commitment binds values nobody wrote down.** The commitment is to a *function*,
not to a list. Once `p` is fixed, its value at every point in the field is fixed — including the
4,096 extra evaluations that PeerDAS uses as erasure-coded extension data. A cell proof attests that
a KZG commitment opens to a cell at a given index, and this works for extension cells exactly as it
does for original ones.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> The last lesson in this module is about what that
buys.

Now try to do either one with a Merkle root, and watch it fail on the same missing clause.

**Random-point checking has no Merkle analogue.** You can sample random *leaves*, but the leaves are
the data, and an adversary who corrupted one leaf out of 4,096 is caught by a random sample with
probability `1/4096` — not `4095 / 10^76`. To reach comparable confidence you must sample until you
have effectively read everything, which is the linear work you were trying to avoid.

**A Merkle root binds only what was put in the tree.** There is no leaf 9,000 unless someone put a
value there, and if someone did, the root says nothing about whether that value is consistent with
the other 4,096. A root over extended data commits to whatever the publisher chose to extend it with.

## What it costs, stated up front

Nothing about this is free, and the module's remaining lessons are largely about the bill.

**A pairing-friendly curve.** The verification of a KZG opening is a pairing equation, so the scheme
does not work over an arbitrary elliptic curve. Ethereum uses BLS12-381 for blob
commitments.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> The pairing itself is the subject of the module after this one.

**A structured reference string, produced by a trusted setup.** The public parameters are not
arbitrary: they are encrypted powers of a secret scalar, and anyone who knows that scalar can open a
commitment to values the polynomial does not take — which destroys binding entirely. The ceremony's
own FAQ puts the consequence in operational terms: an attacker who extracted the secret could make
arbitrary claims about the data.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" /> Two lessons from here is about how
that secret is arranged never to exist.

**A degree bound.** Unlike a hash, the parameters are sized. Ethereum's shipped mainnet setup carries
4,096 G1 points in the Lagrange basis, 4,096 in the monomial basis, and 65 G2
points.<Cite src="consensus-specs-trusted-setup-4096" /> You cannot commit to a polynomial of higher
degree than the string supports, and extending the string means another ceremony.

<Aside kind="note">
**Where to look when you want the operation list rather than the theory.** The reference
implementation of the Polynomial Commitments API is `c-kzg-4844`, and its function names are the
cleanest available map of what the scheme actually does in production: turning a blob into a
commitment, computing an opening, verifying a batch of cell proofs, and recovering cells from a
partial set.<Cite src="c-kzg-4844" /> When a paper's notation stops helping, the API surface usually
starts.
</Aside>

## The third property nobody gets

Binding and hiding are the two properties a commitment scheme offers. There is a third that people
routinely assume they are also buying, and no commitment scheme has ever provided it: **availability**.

A commitment establishes *consistency*. Given a commitment `C`, an opening proves that a claimed
piece really is the piece `C` determines at that point. It says nothing whatsoever about whether
anybody currently holds the data, is willing to serve it, or will still have it next week. Those are
facts about a network and about incentives, and no amount of algebra produces them.

This distinction is easy to lose because the two arrive together in every explanation. Blobs are
introduced with their KZG commitments and their sampling scheme in the same breath, so the commitment
appears to be what makes the data available. It is not. Availability comes from the sampling and
networking layer, and the erasure coding that makes sampling meaningful — which is why PeerDAS is a
*networking* protocol specification rather than a cryptographic one.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

<Misconception
  belief="A valid opening proof shows that the data is available."
  reality="It shows the claimed piece is consistent with the commitment. Whether anyone can obtain the rest of the data is a property of the network and the incentives around it, supplied by sampling and distribution, not by the commitment scheme."
  why="Commitments and data-availability sampling are always taught together, and the proof arrives at the moment you successfully fetched something — which feels like evidence that fetching works."
  src="eip-7594"
/>

The practical form of this is a question worth asking about any system that publishes a commitment:
**which mechanism supplies consistency, and which separate mechanism supplies availability?** If a
design has an answer only to the first, it has an operator who can publish a commitment, satisfy
every cryptographic check, and simply decline to hand over the data. The commitment will still verify
against everything that was served. The data will still be gone.

Keep the two columns separate on paper. The module's closing lesson is entirely about the second one,
and the reflection prompt for this module asks you to fill both columns in for a real system.

<Check type="explain" concept="polynomial-commitment">
A team is designing an off-chain data layer. Their plan: split each 128 KiB chunk into 4,096 pieces,
build a Merkle tree over the pieces, publish the root on-chain, and have light nodes sample random
pieces with Merkle proofs to gain confidence the data exists. They add: "We looked at KZG, but the
proofs are 48 bytes versus a few hundred for a Merkle path, and we don't need that saving."

Explain why the proof size was the least important difference, and name the specific attack their
design admits.

<Answer>
**The proof-size comparison is real and almost irrelevant.** A twelve-level Merkle path for 4,096
leaves is a few hundred bytes; a constant-size opening is 48. If that were the whole difference, they
would be right to skip the pairing curve, the ceremony and the degree bound.

**The difference that matters is what the commitment binds.** Their root binds a *list of 4,096
pieces*. It says nothing about any relationship among those pieces, because a Merkle tree has no
opinion about the contents of its leaves. A polynomial commitment binds a *function*, and once the
function is fixed its value at every point in the field is fixed with
it.<Cite src="kate-zaverucha-goldberg" />

**The attack that opens up.** Their sampling scheme needs erasure coding to be meaningful — otherwise
a publisher can withhold one piece out of 4,096 and be caught with probability `1/4096` per sample.
So suppose they add it: extend the data to 8,192 pieces and build the tree over all of them. Now a
malicious publisher builds the tree over a *deliberately inconsistent* extension — the first 4,096
pieces are real, the second 4,096 are noise. Every individual sample verifies against the root,
because every piece really is in the tree. Light nodes sample, every check passes, and they conclude
the data is recoverable. It is not: no set of 4,096 pieces reconstructs anything, because the values
are not evaluations of one polynomial.

With a polynomial commitment there is nothing to attack here, because there is nothing to choose. The
extension values are evaluations of the committed polynomial, so a cell proof against the commitment
*is* a proof that the cell is the correct extension.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

**Repairing the Merkle design costs more than adopting KZG.** They would have to prove that the
extension was computed correctly from the original — a statement about a computation over all 4,096
inputs, proved cheaply enough to run every block. That puts them in the succinct-proof business with
a heavier dependency than the one they avoided.

**The rule to take away.** When comparing commitment schemes, do not compare proof sizes. Compare
the query language: what may you ask, and what does the answer bind? A scheme that can only be asked
about entries in a list cannot make claims about the relationship between those entries — and the
whole value of erasure coding, and of random-point checking, lives in exactly that relationship.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

You now have the frame the rest of this module fills in. A commitment scheme is a query interface;
binding and hiding are separate properties and only one of them is load-bearing here; and the
polynomial commitment's ability to answer at points nobody wrote down is what makes both succinct
proofs and data-availability sampling mechanically possible.

The next lesson makes it concrete. It reads a blob as a polynomial rather than as bytes, shows how
48 bytes stand in for 128 KiB regardless of content, and works through the opening proof — which
turns out to be a commitment to a quotient, checked with a single equation. That equation is the
first place in this track where the pairing does visible work, and the reason it is only one equation
is the reason verification is constant.
