Start with the attack, because the defence only makes sense once you have watched the obvious
approach fail.

A publisher posts a blob transaction. The versioned hash is on-chain, the commitment is valid, and
everything the previous lesson described works perfectly. Then the publisher serves 4,095 of the
blob's 4,096 field elements to anyone who asks, and withholds **one**.

Now try to catch them by sampling. Each random element you request has a `1/4096` chance of being the
withheld one. To reach 99% confidence you need `n` draws with `(1 - 1/4096)^n <= 0.01`:

```text
n  >=  ln(0.01) / ln(1 - 1/4096)  ~=  18,861 draws
```

Eighteen thousand draws to find one missing element out of four thousand. Sampling has not saved you
bandwidth — it has cost you four and a half times the blob. Naive sampling is not merely weak here;
it is strictly worse than downloading everything.

And withholding one element is enough to matter. That element might be the one transaction a user
needs to prove their withdrawal, and the whole security argument for a rollup rests on the data being
recoverable by anyone who wants it.

So the fix cannot be "sample harder". It has to change what the adversary is forced to hide.

## Stretch the data until hiding is expensive

Before distribution, each blob is Reed–Solomon extended to **twice its length**. The consensus preset
states it as a definition: `FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_EXT_BLOB` is `2 * FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_BLOB`, so 4,096
becomes 8,192.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-preset" /><Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-polynomial-commitments-sampling" />

You already know why this is free rather than clever. The 4,096 numbers are evaluations of a
polynomial of degree at most 4,095, and such a polynomial is determined by **any** 4,096 of its
evaluations. Evaluate at 4,096 additional points and you have 8,192 values, any 4,096 of which
reconstruct the whole thing. EIP-7594 states the consequence for a node directly: it can reconstruct
the entire data matrix if it acquires at least **50%** of all the columns, and can request the rest
from peers if it has less.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

Now redo the adversary's arithmetic. To make any part of the original data unrecoverable, they must
prevent anyone from assembling 4,096 of the 8,192 extended values — which means withholding **more
than half** of the extended data. Hiding one byte now costs the same as hiding the entire blob.

And that is what makes sampling work:

```text
adversary withholds > 50%  ->  each random sample misses with probability < 1/2
n independent samples      ->  all miss with probability < 2^-n
n = 20                     ->  under one in a million
```

Twenty draws instead of eighteen thousand. The change was not to the sampler. It was to the shape of
the lie the adversary is forced to tell.

That distinction has a practical edge. A backup scheme is judged on how much loss it survives. This
scheme is judged on how *cheaply a lie is detected* — and the interesting parameter is not how much
redundancy you added but how large a fraction the adversary is now forced to suppress.

### The commitment is doing the other half of the work

Extension alone would not be enough, and this is where the previous three lessons pay off in one
sentence.

A malicious publisher could extend the data *incorrectly* — real values in the first half, noise in
the second — so that every individual sample verifies while no set of 4,096 values reconstructs
anything. Against a Merkle root over the extended data, that attack works: the root commits to
whatever was put in the tree, and says nothing about the relationship between entries.

Against a polynomial commitment there is nothing to choose. The extension values *are* evaluations of
the committed polynomial, so a proof that a cell opens correctly against the commitment is
simultaneously a proof that the cell is the correct extension. EIP-7594 says exactly this: cells are
the smallest units that can be authenticated with their respective blob's KZG commitments, and a cell
proof shows that a commitment opens to a cell at a given index.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

Erasure coding makes withholding expensive. The commitment makes mis-extension impossible. Neither
works without the other.

## Cells and columns: the units a node actually handles

Two definitions, both exact, and both worth reading from the specification rather than from a
summary.

**A cell** is `FIELD_ELEMENTS_PER_CELL = 64` field elements, so `BYTES_PER_CELL` is 64 × 32 = **2,048
bytes**, and `CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB` is 8,192 / 64 = **128** cells per extended
blob.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-polynomial-commitments-sampling" />

A cell is not an arbitrary 2 KiB slice. It is the polynomial's evaluations over a **coset** of the
order-64 subgroup of the roots of unity, and the coset for cell `i` is the slice of the *bit-reversed*
roots of unity from `64 * i` to `64 * (i +
1)`.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-polynomial-commitments-sampling" /> That bit-reversal permutation
is the one you met two lessons ago in `blob_to_kzg_commitment`. A reordering chosen for the original
blob format is what makes "a contiguous 2 KiB range" and "an algebraically coherent evaluation
domain" the same thing — which is why a single 48-byte proof can open all 64 evaluations of a cell at
once.

**A column** is the same cell index taken across **all blobs in the block**. `NUMBER_OF_COLUMNS` is
128, matching the cell count, and each column is associated with a specific gossip
subnet.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" /><Cite src="eip-7594" />

That second definition is the one that changes the economics, so state its consequence plainly: **a
sampling node downloads columns, not blobs.** Its bandwidth is a fraction of the block's data
regardless of how many blobs the block carries. EIP-7594's rationale puts a number on the current
parameters — a node downloads **1/8** of the total data to satisfy its local availability check, and
this can in future be reduced to 1/16 or even 1/32 by making samples smaller and increasing the
number of columns.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

Read that as the scaling argument it is. Blob capacity can rise without raising any individual node's
bandwidth, because the fraction each node handles is a protocol parameter rather than a consequence
of block size. That is the whole point of the design, and it is why PeerDAS is specified as a
*networking* protocol built on a cryptographic property rather than as cryptography.

## The consumer-facing consequence: the wrapper changed

Here is the part that reaches ordinary application engineers, and it broke real libraries.

Before PeerDAS, a blob transaction's network wrapper carried blobs, commitments and proofs as a
1:1:1 triple — one proof per blob. EIP-7594 replaces that. The wrapper is
now:<Cite src="eip-7594" />

<Figure
  caption="One version field and a proof per cell — the two members that broke every library written against the EIP-4844 wrapper."
  alt="The PeerDAS blob transaction network wrapper as an RLP list: the transaction payload body, a wrapper version, the blobs, the commitments, and cell_proofs holding CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB proofs for every blob."
>

      { label: 'wrapper_version', variable: true, tone: 'accent', note: 'set to 1' },
      { label: 'blobs', variable: true, note: 'sent unextended' },
      { label: 'commitments', variable: true },
      { label: 'cell_proofs', variable: true, tone: 'accent', note: 'CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB per blob' },
    ]}
  />
</Figure>

with `wrapper_version` set to `1`, and `cell_proofs` containing a proof for **every cell of every
blob, including the extension cells** — exactly `CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB * len(blobs)` of
them.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

Two details in that design are worth extracting.

**The blobs are sent unextended.** Only `CELLS_PER_EXT_BLOB / 2` cells' worth of blob data travels;
the receiving node computes the extension itself, because recomputing is cheaper than transmitting
redundant bytes.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> So the wrapper carries proofs for data it does not carry —
which is only coherent because the commitment binds values nobody transmitted.

**The sender computes the proofs, and the EIP says why.** Computing cell proofs for a blob is
expensive, and a block producer would have to repeat it for many blobs; proof verification is much
cheaper than proof computation, and proof size is negligible compared to cell size. So the obligation
is pushed onto the transaction sender, who includes the proofs in the
wrapper.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> That is a cost being moved to the party with the incentive to pay it —
the same reasoning that runs through the whole proving track, applied to a mempool.

Put numbers on "negligible": a 48-byte proof per 2,048-byte cell is about 2.3% overhead. For a
transaction carrying the maximum of 6 blobs — EIP-7594 introduces that per-transaction
limit — that is 6 × 128 = 768 cell proofs, roughly 36 KiB of proofs alongside 768 KiB of blob
data.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

Note carefully what did **not** change: the execution-layer surface. `BLOBHASH` still returns a
versioned hash, the point-evaluation precompile still takes 192 bytes, and a contract still cannot
read a blob.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> The break was entirely in tooling that parsed the transaction
wrapper. If a library of yours constructs blob transactions, that is where to look.

## Consistency, sampling, retention: three different guarantees

The module closes by separating three things that arrive together and fail separately.

**Consistency** comes from the commitment. A cell you were handed matches the polynomial the
commitment fixed. Nothing about who holds the rest.

**Availability** comes from erasure coding plus sampling. Enough of the extended data is being served
right now that random draws succeed, which implies the original is reconstructible by someone willing
to collect columns.<Cite src="eip-7594" />

**Retention** is a bounded window, and it is neither of the above. Blob data is kept for a defined
number of epochs and then dropped: EIP-4844 sets `MIN_EPOCHS_FOR_BLOB_SIDECARS_REQUESTS` to
**4,096 epochs**, which at 32 slots of 12 seconds each is roughly 18
days.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> After that the commitment remains on-chain forever and the data does
not. A proof of consistency against a commitment whose data nobody retains is a true statement about
something that can no longer be produced.

That is the honest end state, and it is the shape of the reflection prompt for this module: for any
system publishing a commitment, name which mechanism supplies each of the three, and what an operator
would have to do to satisfy one while failing another.

<Check type="predict" concept="cell-and-column">
A block's blob data is spread across 128 columns. A malicious builder withholds 65 of them — one more
than half — so that no node can reconstruct. A sampling node draws 8 distinct columns at random each
slot.

Estimate the chance the withholding survives one slot's sampling by that node, then say what
actually makes this attack hopeless in practice, and what it would still achieve.

<Answer>
**The arithmetic first.** With 65 of 128 columns withheld, 63 remain available, so a single random
draw lands on an available column with probability 63/128 ≈ 0.492. Treating eight draws as
independent (they are without replacement, which makes detection slightly *more* likely, so this is
the conservative estimate):

```text
P(all 8 samples succeed)  ~=  0.492^8  ~=  0.0034
```

So roughly a **0.34%** chance of surviving one slot against one node — about 99.66% detection.
Eight samples per slot is the specification's `SAMPLES_PER_SLOT`, so this is the real
parameter.<Cite src="consensus-specs-fulu-das-core" />

**Why that number understates it enormously.** The attacker is not facing one node. Every sampling
node in the network draws independently, and the probabilities multiply. Against ten nodes the
survival chance is roughly `0.0034^10`, a number with no practical meaning. Against the network it is
zero in every sense that matters.

**And the attacker must sustain it.** Withholding is not a one-shot event — the data has to stay
hidden. Meanwhile nodes that hold columns serve them, a node with at least 50% of the columns
reconstructs the rest, and reconstruction is contagious: once anyone rebuilds the matrix they can
serve any column.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> The attack has to beat every sampler in every slot while the
number of parties able to defeat it grows.

**What the attacker cannot do at all.** They cannot withhold *less*. Because the data is
Reed–Solomon extended, hiding one field element and hiding the whole blob require the same act —
suppressing more than half the extended data.<Cite src="eip-7594" /> There is no cheap, surgical
version of this attack, and that is the entire design goal.

**What it would still achieve, and this is the honest part.** Detection is not repair. Sampling tells
the network the data is unavailable; it does not produce the data. What follows is a protocol-level
consequence — the block is not treated as available, and does not get the attestations it needs.
That is a liveness event, and the attacker has spent a block slot to cause one. The thing sampling
buys is that nobody is *fooled*: no node accepts a block as available when it is not. Distinguishing
"we detected it and rejected the block" from "we recovered the data" is the difference between a
safety property and a durability one, and only the first is on offer here.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**This module ends here**, and the ladder it built is worth restating in one pass. A commitment is a
query interface, and binding and hiding are separate properties of it. A polynomial commitment
answers at points nobody wrote down, which is what makes both random-point checking and erasure
extension possible. KZG realises that in 48 bytes as the polynomial evaluated at a secret, with an
opening that is a commitment to a quotient which exists only when the claim is true. That secret was
manufactured by thousands of people so as to exist nowhere, under a 1-of-N assumption with a public
transcript. On-chain, one precompile call turns all of it into a single flat-priced boolean about
data the EVM cannot see. And the data is stretched to twice its length before distribution so that a
lie about it becomes too large to hide.

Two threads run out of here. The **reflection prompt** asks you to separate consistency from
availability for a system you have actually read about, and the practice exercise is the round trip:
compute a commitment, derive the versioned hash by hand, and call the precompile — then flip a bit
and watch it fail.<Cite src="c-kzg-4844" />

The **next module** goes underneath all of it. Every lesson here leaned on a pairing without saying
what one is, on BLS12-381 without saying why that curve, and on "48 bytes because a compressed G1
point" without justifying any of those words. That is the next descent: what a pairing buys, why it
buys exactly one multiplication, and how to choose between the two curves the EVM can actually
compute with — a choice that turns out to be about security bits and encodings, and to have a live
consequence for anything targeting Ethereum's L1 proving requirements.
