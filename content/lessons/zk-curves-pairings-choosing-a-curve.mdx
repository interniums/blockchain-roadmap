Two pairing-friendly curves are available to an Ethereum contract today. Every summary you will read
compresses the choice into one sentence: *BN254 is cheaper, BLS12-381 is safer.* Half of that is
wrong, and the half that is right is right for a reason nobody states.

This lesson makes the trade concrete: what each curve's security estimate actually is (a range, from
sources that disagree), where the proving cost difference comes from (field width, not folklore),
where the on-chain cost difference comes from (point sizes and scalar multiplication — **not** the
pairing check, which is cheaper on BLS12-381), and what is forcing the choice regardless of any of
that.

in the table to make a point that matters for the rest of the lesson: **a curve is chosen for a job,
and "stronger" is not a job.**

Two things are true at once and both matter.

**The spread is wide.** More than twenty bits on each curve. Estimating the cost of a number field
sieve computation requires choices about optimisations, about converting asymptotic complexity into
concrete operation counts, and about pricing those operations — and there is dedicated literature on
how hard those choices are to defend.<Cite src="iacr-nfs-assessment-challenges" /> Anyone quoting a
single number for either curve has picked one estimator without saying so.

**The ordering is not in dispute.** Every source, including the two that disagree most, puts BLS12-381
comfortably above BN254 and BN254 comfortably below 128. So the defensible statements are:

- **BN254: around 100 bits, and definitely not 128.**
- **BLS12-381: targeting roughly 128 and commonly assessed slightly under.**

That is enough to decide with. A decision does not need a point estimate; it needs to know which side
of a threshold it is on, and for the 128-bit threshold the answer is unambiguous.

<Aside kind="note">
The NCC Group assessment is worth reading for its posture rather than its number. Having put
BLS12-381 at 117 to 120 bits, it treats that as a perfectly adequate level of security and describes
reaching "128-bit" as mostly psychological.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" /><Cite src="ncc-zcash-2018-report" />
That is a defensible engineering view and it does not help you if your requirement is written as a
number, which is exactly what the standards pressure later in this lesson does.
</Aside>

## Why one estimate fell and the other held

Both curves have embedding degree 12, so both are in the family the extended tower number field sieve
attacks — the improvement applies when the embedding degree factors as `k = i·j` with both factors
greater than one and coprime, which covers every embedding degree divisible by
six.<Cite src="iacr-extnfs" /><Cite src="ietf-pairing-friendly-curves" /> So why did only one estimate
move?

**Timing.** BN254's parameters were fixed before the attack existed, and Ethereum adopted the curve in
EIP-196 and EIP-197 in February 2017, with the stated rationale that alt_bn128 is well suited to
zkSNARK verification and that choosing it allowed reuse of Zcash's components and
artifacts.<Cite src="eip-196" /><Cite src="eip-197" /> BLS12-381 was designed in early 2017,
specifically as a response to the exTNFS results, and was adopted by Zcash for exactly that
reason.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" /><Cite src="ietf-pairing-friendly-curves" /> One curve was
parameterised in ignorance of an attack; the other was parameterised against it.

**Field width is the lever.** The IETF draft records the follow-on estimate: to reach 128-bit security
after exTNFS, a BN or BLS12 curve needs a base field modulus of at least **461 bits**, which is why
its "prudent" recommendation for the 128-bit level is BN462 — assessed at 134 bits — while BLS12-381
is recommended as the efficient option with the explicit caveat that its level is below
128.<Cite src="ietf-pairing-friendly-curves" />

Read that carefully, because it is the most useful sentence in the standard. **Even BLS12-381 is
below the bar the same document sets for 128-bit security.** It is recommended anyway, as the
efficient half of a deliberate pair. The standard is not pretending that 381 bits is enough; it is
saying that 381 bits is what the ecosystem uses and that the honest alternative has a 462-bit base
field and is, in the draft's own words, significantly slower than
BLS12-381.<Cite src="ietf-pairing-friendly-curves" />

## The cost side, without a benchmark

Almost every published comparison of proving speed on these two curves is a benchmark on unstated
hardware with an unstated workload. You do not need one to understand the direction, because the
direction is arithmetic.

**Subgoal: count the machine words.** BN254's base field modulus is 254 bits and BLS12-381's is
381.<Cite src="eip-197" /><Cite src="eip-2537" /> On a 64-bit machine that is four limbs against six.

**Subgoal: apply the cost model of field multiplication.** Schoolbook multiplication of multi-limb
integers is quadratic in the limb count: 16 word-multiplications against 36. Real implementations use
Karatsuba and Montgomery arithmetic and do better than the schoolbook bound, so treat 2.25× as an
upper end rather than a measurement — but the sign of the difference is fixed by the limb count and
no implementation effort reverses it.

**Subgoal: notice where that cost lands.** A prover performs field multiplications in enormous
numbers, dominated by multi-scalar multiplication and by the fast Fourier transforms over the scalar
field. So the wider field is a tax on every proof produced, paid off-chain, in wall-clock and in GPU
hours.

**Subgoal: notice where it does not land.** A verifier performs a fixed, small number of operations.
The wider field costs it too, but the multiplier applies to a constant, and on-chain that constant is
set by a gas schedule rather than by field arithmetic — which is where the folklore breaks.

<Aside kind="warn">
Do not convert the limb-count argument into a proving-cost figure. Proving costs in this ecosystem are
quoted in incompatible units — dollars per proof and cents per transaction are not the same quantity —
and latency claims span from seconds to over an hour across different years, hardware and
workloads.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" /> A number without its unit, its workload, its
hardware and its date is not a number. That discipline was established on the scaling track and it
applies with more force here, where the curve is only one term in the product.
</Aside>

**The BLS12-381 pairing check is cheaper than BN254's at every k**, in both the per-pair coefficient
and the fixed base.<Cite src="eip-1108" /><Cite src="eip-2537" /> The stronger curve is priced below
the weaker one for the single most expensive operation a verifier performs.

That is not a mistake in either schedule. EIP-1108's prices come from 2019 benchmarks of the
then-current BN254 implementations, derived by measuring `ecrecover` at 116 microseconds, treating its
3,000 gas as fair, and pricing at 25.86 gas per microsecond of runtime.<Cite src="eip-1108" />
EIP-2537's prices were set later, against a curve whose reference implementations had the benefit of
several more years of work, using the same `ecrecover` baseline.<Cite src="eip-2537" /> Two schedules,
set years apart, each faithful to the implementations in front of it.

So where does BLS12-381 actually cost more on-chain? **Point arithmetic and bytes.** Scalar
multiplication is 2× and addition 2.5×, and every point is twice the calldata. A verifier that
performs one pairing check and a handful of scalar multiplications for its public inputs will find
the pairing saving partly or wholly eaten by the rest — and which way the total falls depends on how
many public inputs you have, which is a property of your circuit, not of the curve.

### What a port actually involves

"Switch the curve" sounds like a configuration change. It is six changes, and they are in different
places owned by different people.

**Subgoal: change the field constants.** The scalar field modulus your circuit works in changes, so
every hardcoded `r`, every range check derived from it, and every hash-to-field reduction in the
contract and in the witness generator changes with it. On BN254 the scalar field is 254 bits; on
BLS12-381 it is 255.<Cite src="eip-197" /><Cite src="eip-2537" />

**Subgoal: change the point sizes and the encoding rules.** `G₁` goes from two 32-byte coordinates to
two 64-byte ones, `G₂` from 128 bytes to 256, and the base field elements acquire a mandatory
top-16-bytes-zero check.<Cite src="eip-197" /><Cite src="eip-2537" /> Every struct, every ABI
signature, every calldata offset in the verifier moves.

**Subgoal: change the precompile addresses and the call shapes.** `0x06`/`0x07`/`0x08` become
`0x0b`–`0x11`, and there is no direct replacement for `ECMUL`: EIP-2537 deliberately omits a dedicated
multiplication call, because multi-scalar multiplication with `k` equal to one has exactly the same
ABI.<Cite src="eip-2537" />

**Subgoal: re-budget the gas.** Not by scaling the old figure. The pairing check gets cheaper and the
scalar multiplications get more expensive, so the total moves in a direction determined by your
public-input count.

**Subgoal: re-examine the input validation.** On BN254 the pairing precompile requires an order check
on `G_2` inputs and treats a curve-equation check as sufficient for `G_1`.<Cite src="eip-197" /> On
BLS12-381 subgroup checks are required for the multi-scalar multiplications and the pairing check, and
explicitly *not* performed by the addition precompiles.<Cite src="eip-2537" /> If your contract was
relying on the precompile to reject a malformed point, confirm the new one does too.

**Subgoal: retarget the prover.** The proving stack must produce proofs over the new curve at all,
which for some toolchains is a supported flag and for others is not a supported configuration. This is
usually the item that decides the timeline, and it is the one furthest from the contract.

## What is actually forcing the choice

If the gas comparison is ambiguous and the security ordering is clear, why is BN254 still the default?

**Because it has had precompiles since 2017, and cheap ones since EIP-1108 repriced
them.**<Cite src="eip-196" /><Cite src="eip-197" /><Cite src="eip-1108" />
Every proving stack, every verifier template, every audited library and every tutorial targets it.
BLS12-381 became available to the EVM only with EIP-2537, activated in the Prague execution upgrade on
7 May 2025.<Cite src="eip-2537" /><Cite src="ethereum-org-history" /> That is a fifteen-month-old
capability set against an ecosystem that has been targeting BN254 since 2017.

<Aside kind="warn">
This module has **not** measured how many BLS12-381 verifier contracts are actually deployed on L1
since Pectra, and no figure for that should be inferred from anything on this page. The capability
exists and is priced; the adoption is unquantified here.
</Aside>

**And the thing pushing the other way is a standards requirement, not a gas price.** The Ethereum
Foundation's second L1 zkEVM post resets its milestones around *provable* security: milestone M2 at
100-bit provable security with proof size relaxed, milestone M3 at 128 bits with a formal soundness
argument for the recursion architecture.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" /> The bluntness of
the stated reason is the useful part — foundational conjectures underpinning some hash-based
constructions were mathematically disproven, and what had been advertised as 100 bits might actually
have been 80.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" />

Apply that same standard to a curve rather than to a proof system and the conclusion writes itself. A
succinct [argument of knowledge](/c/argument-of-knowledge) is sound only against a computationally
bounded prover, and the bound is set by the hardest assumption in the stack. An argument whose
soundness reduces to discrete-log-style assumptions on BN254 cannot claim more than BN254's assessed
level, whatever the proof system's own analysis says. **You cannot build a 128-bit-sound argument on a
100-bit curve.**

That is a structural constraint on anything L1-facing that has to meet a written security bar, and it
disfavours BN254 regardless of what the gas schedule says. It is direction rather than deadline:
nothing has been deprecated, this module found no EIP proposing a repricing or removal of the BN254
precompiles, and the pressure applies to systems with a formal requirement rather than to every
contract.

<Check type="explain" concept="curve-security-vs-speed">
You are the engineer on a protocol that will hold custody of user funds and must still be secure in
fifteen years. A vendor proposes a Groth16 verifier on BN254 with a measured on-chain cost, and
argues:

> BN254 is 100-bit secure, which is 2¹⁰⁰ operations. Bitcoin's whole network does about 2⁹⁰ hashes a
> year. Nobody is doing 2¹⁰⁰ anything. The gas saving is real and today.

Take the argument seriously — the arithmetic in it is not wrong — and then say what you would put in
the decision record.

<Answer>
**Grant the arithmetic and reject the framing.** 2¹⁰⁰ operations is genuinely far beyond current
capability, and if the requirement were "secure this year" the vendor would be right. The requirement
is fifteen years, and the number in the argument is not a constant. It is an estimate of the best
*publicly known* attack, produced by one group of estimators, and the same estimate for the same curve
was 128 until 2016.<Cite src="iacr-pairing-key-sizes" /><Cite src="ietf-pairing-friendly-curves" />
The relevant history is not "has anyone done 2¹⁰⁰" but "has the exponent moved", and it has, once,
downward, within the lifetime of the curve.

**The spread is itself evidence.** Published figures for the same curve run from 80 to 103 bits
depending on who is estimating.<Cite src="eip-2537" /><Cite src="ietf-pairing-friendly-curves" /> An
argument built on the top of that range is choosing the most favourable estimator without saying so.

**Then check whether the gas saving is even there.** Put both schedules against a Groth16-shaped
verifier — one pairing check at four pairs, plus one scalar multiplication and one addition per public
input:

```text
BN254        181,000 + n × 6,150      (34,000·4 + 45,000; ECMUL 6,000 + ECADD 150)
BLS12-381    168,100 + n × 12,375     (32,600·4 + 37,700; G1MSM k=1 12,000 + G1ADD 375)
```

<Cite src="eip-1108" /><Cite src="eip-2537" /> At `n = 2` those come to **193,300** and **192,850** —
a difference of 450 gas, in BLS12-381's favour. The two curves cross at just over two public inputs,
after which BN254 pulls ahead by about 6,225 gas per additional input. So the vendor's saving is not a
property of the curve; it is a property of *your circuit's public-input count*, it is zero or negative
for a small one, and it is measurable on your actual verifier before you decide. Calldata is the term
that still favours BN254 unconditionally, since every point doubles in
size.<Cite src="eip-197" /><Cite src="eip-2537" />

**Then write the record.** It should contain: the horizon (fifteen years, custody of funds); the
assessed level with date and estimator, stated as a range; the two candidate curves with their
measured verifier gas on your actual circuit, not a template's; the third option the standard actually
recommends for a 128-bit requirement, BN462, along with the fact that it has no
precompile and is therefore not viable on L1 today;<Cite src="ietf-pairing-friendly-curves" /> and the
migration path with a named owner.

**The migration path is the part that decides it.** If the answer to "what happens when the estimate
moves again" is "we redeploy", then someone must be entitled to point users at the new deployment,
and that entitlement is a trust assumption you have just added to a system whose selling point was not
requiring one. A verifier address behind an upgrade mechanism has an admin; a verifier address that is
immutable has a fifteen-year bet on a number that has already moved once. Choosing the stronger curve
now is the cheapest way to avoid having to choose between those two later.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson makes all of this executable.** Addresses, input encodings, the multi-scalar
multiplication discount table, and the specific way each schedule prices what it prices — including
why a Groth16 verifier pays for four pairings when the paper says three.

**In the aggregation lesson**, BLS12-381's choice stops being about verifiers at all: consensus picked
it for a property BN254 has too but at a security level the beacon chain would not accept, and the
sizes in the table above are the reason validator public keys are 48 bytes and not 96.

**And in your own decision records**, the transferable move is the one this lesson used twice: when
sources disagree on a number, check whether the *decision* depends on the number or on which side of a
threshold it falls. Most of the time it is the threshold, and the disagreement stops mattering the
moment you say so out loud.
