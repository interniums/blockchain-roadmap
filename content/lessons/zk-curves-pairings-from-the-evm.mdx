Three curves, three eras, three sets of encoding conventions that do not agree with each other. This
lesson is the practical one: what each call takes, what it costs, and the specific places where a
correct-looking integration fails. Two of those places have burned real teams, and one of them burns
all your gas rather than reverting politely.

## The BN254 precompiles, and the repricing that made them matter

EIP-196 defined addition at `0x06` and scalar multiplication at `0x07` with a plain encoding: field
elements are 32-byte big-endian numbers, a curve point is the pair `(x, y)`, and the point at infinity
is encoded as `(0, 0)`.<Cite src="eip-196" /> EIP-197 added the pairing check at `0x08`, taking
`192·k` bytes — a 64-byte `G₁` point and a 128-byte `G₂` point per pair — and returning 32 bytes that
are one or zero.<Cite src="eip-197" />

The original prices made them close to unusable: 500 gas for addition, 40,000 for multiplication, and
`80,000·k + 100,000` for the pairing check.<Cite src="eip-196" /><Cite src="eip-197" /> EIP-1108 cut
them to **150**, **6,000**, and **34,000·k + 45,000**.<Cite src="eip-1108" />

The reason is a good corrective to the idea that gas prices encode intrinsic difficulty. Nothing about
the mathematics changed. The Go reference implementation moved to a faster bn256 library and Parity's
field arithmetic and pairing code were optimised, so the same operation started taking roughly an
order of magnitude less time, and the schedule was brought into line.<Cite src="eip-1108" />

The EIP shows its working, which is rare enough to be worth reading:

```text
ecrecover measured at 116 µs, priced at 3,000 gas
  → 25.86 gas per microsecond of precompile runtime

pairing precompile:  1 pairing  = 3,037 µs
                    10 pairings = 14,663 µs
  → fixed cost 1,745 µs, plus 1,292 µs per pairing
  → ~35,000·k + 45,000 gas
```

<Cite src="eip-1108" />

**Gas is a price for wall-clock on the slowest client of the day, calibrated against one reference
operation.** That is the whole model. It explains why two curves priced years apart are not
comparable as a statement about mathematics, and it explains why a repricing can arrive without any
protocol change of substance.

What the repricing bought is stated in the EIP itself as projections: an AZTEC confidential
transaction's cryptography from 820,000 gas to 197,000, and a ZEther transaction from roughly
6,000,000 to roughly 1,000,000.<Cite src="eip-1108" /> Those are the EIP's own forward-looking figures
rather than post-deployment measurements, and they are the reason on-chain SNARK verification became
economically ordinary.

## What a Groth16 verifier actually pays, derived rather than recalled

Groth16's proof is three group elements and its verification is a single pairing product equation
using **three** pairings.<Cite src="iacr-groth16" /> On-chain, it pays for four. Working out why is
the most useful five minutes in this lesson.

**Subgoal: recall what the precompile refuses to accept.** EIP-197 specifies a *check*, not an
evaluation, precisely because the pairing's codomain is a complex field that would raise encoding
problems.<Cite src="eip-197" /> So there is no way to hand the precompile a target-group value.

**Subgoal: see which term that strands.** One of Groth16's three pairings is over two fixed elements
of the verification key. In the paper that term is a constant, computed once. On-chain it cannot be
stored as a constant, because a target-group element has no encoding the precompile accepts — so the
verifier passes the two source-group points and lets the precompile re-derive it inside the same call.
Three pairings in the equation, four pairs in the calldata.

**Subgoal: price it from the schedule.**

```text
pairing check, k = 4      34,000 × 4 + 45,000  =  181,000 gas
per public input           6,000 (ECMUL) + 150 (ECADD)  =  6,150 gas
```

<Cite src="eip-1108" />

So a two-input Groth16 verifier's precompile cost is 181,000 + 12,300 = **193,300 gas**, before a
single line of the surrounding Solidity.

**Subgoal: compare that with the figure you will see quoted.** A widely repeated model gives Groth16
verification as roughly 207,000 gas plus about 7,160 per public input. It traces to a single community
analysis rather than to a specification.<Cite src="orbiter-verifier-gas" /> Notice that it is close to
the derivation above and slightly higher — consistent with the extra being calldata, memory expansion,
input validation and dispatch in the verifier contract itself.

The reason to derive rather than quote is not that the quoted number is wrong. It is that the derived
one comes apart into terms you can act on: the 181,000 is fixed by the proof system, the 6,150 scales
with a design decision you control, and the remainder is your contract's, which means it is the only
part a gas-profiling pass can touch. A single number tells you none of that. Measure your own verifier
and compare it against the floor above; the gap is yours.

**The [pairing dominates](/c/pairings-dominate-gas), and it is a constant.** It does not shrink with a
smaller circuit, a faster prover, or better Solidity — the proof is three group elements whether it
attests to ten constraints or ten billion.<Cite src="iacr-groth16" /> That is succinctness working as
advertised, and it is also a floor you cannot optimise below while verifying one proof per
transaction.

Which is why the two real levers are structural rather than local. **Reduce public inputs**: each one
costs 6,150 gas, and a common move is to replace several with a single hash commitment to them,
trading circuit constraints for calldata and gas. **Or verify fewer times**: fold many proofs into one
before touching L1, so that a single pairing check settles a batch. The
[cost of a verifier](/c/groth16-verifier-cost) and the existence of aggregation layers are the same
fact seen from two directions — when 94% of the bill is a constant, the only way to shrink it per unit
of work is to increase the work per call.

## The operation BN254 does not give you

EIP-196 exposes addition and multiplication for points encoded as *two* field elements — that is
`G₁`.<Cite src="eip-196" /> There is no `G₂` addition and no `G₂` multiplication precompile on BN254 at
all. The pairing check at `0x08` accepts `G₂` points as inputs, but nothing lets a contract *compute*
with them.<Cite src="eip-197" /><Cite src="zellic-bls-signatures" />

For a Groth16 verifier that is fine, because its `G₂` elements are fixed verification-key constants.
For anything that aggregates on-chain it is a wall, and the workaround that grew up around it is
instructive.

EigenLayer's AVS contracts verify BLS signatures on BN254, which needs an aggregate public key. If
public keys lived in `G₂`, aggregating them on-chain would need `G₂` additions that do not exist. So
the aggregator submits the signers' individual public keys in `G₁` *and* a claimed aggregate key in
`G₂`, and the contract checks in one pairing call both that the signature verifies and that the
claimed `G₂` key is consistent with the `G₁` list — combining the two checks with a factor derived by
hashing every public value, Fiat–Shamir style, so a malicious aggregator cannot forge the
pairing.<Cite src="zellic-bls-signatures" />

Read that as an architecture consequence of a missing precompile. A gap in the exposed operation set
did not merely raise a gas cost; it changed who submits what, and added a soundness argument that
would otherwise not exist. Zellic's own note is that with EIP-2537, which does expose `G₂` arithmetic,
the trick is no longer needed on BLS12-381.<Cite src="zellic-bls-signatures" /><Cite src="eip-2537" />

## EIP-2537, in the form you will call it

Seven precompiles, shipped in the Prague execution upgrade — dated 7 May 2025 on ethereum.org's own

`prague/`.<Cite src="eip-2537" /><Cite src="ethereum-org-history" /><Cite src="execution-spec-tests-eip2537" />

<Cite src="eip-2537" />

The encoding rules underneath those lengths:<Cite src="eip-2537" />

- An `Fp` element is **64 bytes**, big-endian. The modulus is 381 bits, so the top 16 bytes are always
  zero — and the EIP requires that this be checked, not assumed, even when only a sub-slice of the
  input is decoded.
- An `Fp2` element is **128 bytes**: the two coefficients concatenated.
- A `G₁` point is **128 bytes**, a `G₂` point **256**. A scalar is **32**.
- The point at infinity is a run of 128 or 256 zero bytes, by convention — because `(0, 0)` is not
  actually on the curve for BLS12 curves and so the naive reading was free to be repurposed.

<Misconception
  belief="A BLS12-381 G₁ point is 48 bytes."
  reality="48 bytes is the compressed serialisation used by KZG commitments and consensus signatures. The EIP-2537 precompiles take 128-byte zero-padded uncompressed points, and reject the compressed form."
  why="The same curve appears with two different encodings in two different Ethereum subsystems, and both are correct in their own context. The consensus one is the one most readers meet first."
  src="eip-2537"
/>

That misconception is not academic. A validator public key read from the beacon state is 48
bytes;<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" /> handing it to `0x0b` is a length error. The EIP notes why
the padded uncompressed form was chosen: 64 bytes keeps the ABI 32-byte-aligned, representable as
`bytes32[2]`, and decompression would cost more gas than supplying the decompressed point in
calldata.<Cite src="eip-2537" />

### Three behaviours that differ from what you would guess

**Errors burn all the gas.** Following the convention of the other precompiles, an error in any
EIP-2537 call burns all the gas supplied with the `CALL` or
`STATICCALL`.<Cite src="eip-2537" /> This is not an ordinary revert that returns the remaining gas to
the caller. A wrong encoding in a batch operation does not cost you an error path; it costs you the
transaction's whole budget.

**Empty input is an error — the opposite of BN254.** EIP-197 states that empty input to the pairing
check is valid and returns one.<Cite src="eip-197" /> EIP-2537 discourages that use case explicitly:
variable-length operations must return an error on empty input.<Cite src="eip-2537" /> Two pairing
checks, two curves, opposite behaviour on the same degenerate input — and "returns one" versus "burns
all gas" is a meaningful difference to a contract that builds its input list dynamically.

**Subgroup checks are performed on five of the seven.** The multi-scalar multiplications and the
pairing check must reject inputs that are on the curve but outside the correct subgroup. The two
addition precompiles explicitly do not perform that check.<Cite src="eip-2537" /> Addition is safe
without it, but a contract that treats "the precompile validated my point" as a uniform guarantee is
generalising from five cases to seven.

### Success is not the answer

A precompile call has two independent results and they mean different things. `staticcall` returns a
boolean saying whether the call itself succeeded. The pairing check *also* returns a value saying
whether the pairing equation held.

Both pairing precompiles return exactly 32 bytes: EIP-197's output is one or zero as a 32-byte
big-endian number, and EIP-2537's is 31 zero bytes followed by `0x00` for false or `0x01` for
true.<Cite src="eip-197" /><Cite src="eip-2537" /> So a call with a perfectly well-formed input over
points that do not satisfy the equation is a **successful call returning false**.

```solidity
(bool ok, bytes memory out) = address(0x0f).staticcall(input);
require(ok, "pairing call failed");          // says nothing about the proof
require(abi.decode(out, (uint256)) == 1);    // this is the verification
```

Dropping the second line produces a verifier that accepts every proof whose calldata is
well-encoded — which is every proof an attacker submits, because encoding correctly is free. It is the
same shape of defect as the passkey precompile at `0x100`, which returns zero-length output on an
invalid signature rather than reverting, so a bare `require(success)` there reads an invalid signature
as a valid one.<Cite src="eip-7951" /> Two different precompiles, two different failure encodings, one
identical mistake.

<Aside kind="note">
The fixed-price outlier on the list is `0x0a`, the KZG point-evaluation precompile. It takes exactly
192 bytes, costs a flat 50,000 gas with no `k` term, and on success returns the number of field
elements per blob and the BLS modulus as two padded 32-byte
values.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> It is the one entry on the number line that is not a general-purpose
curve operation but a single protocol-specific check —
[the opening verification](/c/point-evaluation-precompile) for a blob commitment — which is why it
prices as a constant rather than as a formula.
</Aside>

## Multi-scalar multiplication, and the table you must not interpolate

EIP-2537 exposes no dedicated multiplication call at all. The rationale is that a dedicated
multiplication would have exactly the same ABI as multi-scalar multiplication with `k` equal to one,
so implementations are told to recognise that case and dispatch to a single-point
routine.<Cite src="eip-2537" />

**Multi-scalar multiplication** computes the sum of `[aᵢ]Pᵢ` over many pairs in one call. It is the
dominant cost inside almost every prover, which is why prover performance work concentrates there and
why [GPU acceleration pays off](/c/cpu-vs-gpu-proving). The reason it earns a discount is Pippenger's
algorithm: batching the pairs shares work across them so that cost per pair falls as the batch
grows.<Cite src="eip-2537" />

The price is `(k · multiplication_cost · discount(k)) / 1000` with integer division, where the
multiplication cost is 12,000 for `G₁` and 22,500 for `G₂`, and `discount(k)` is read from an explicit
128-entry table per group, with a cap for `k` above 128 — 519 for `G₁`, 524 for
`G₂`.<Cite src="eip-2537" />

Two readings. First, **the per-pair cost flattens rather than falling linearly** — from `k = 1` to
`k = 64` in `G₁` it drops by about 42%, and from 64 to 128 by only a further 10%. Second, the `G₂`
column stays close to 1.9× the `G₁` column throughout, which is the size asymmetry showing up as
money rather than as bytes.

<Aside kind="warn">
**Do not fit a curve through two of those rows.** The discount is a lookup table, not a formula. It is
not monotonic in its increments — successive entries repeat, for instance 92 and 93 both discount at
547 in `G₁` — so an interpolation will look plausible and be wrong at points you did not sample. The
practice exercise for this module measures `k` at 1, 8 and 64 and asks you to compare each against the
table rather than against a fitted line, for exactly this reason.
</Aside>

There is also a gas-accounting subtlety with a security flavour. The EIP specifies that the gas
function computes `k` by *floor division* of the input length and performs no length validation, so an
input that is not a multiple of the per-pair length still produces a gas charge — and then the main
routine returns an error. For `k = 0` the formal gas cost is zero, which the EIP notes is safe because
the `CALL` itself is not free and a zero-cost precompile already exists in
`blake2f`.<Cite src="eip-2537" />

<Misconception
  belief="Multi-scalar multiplication is a loop over scalar multiplications, so its cost is k times the single cost."
  reality="Batching shares work across the pairs, and the schedule prices that with an explicit per-k discount: 64 pairs in G₁ cost 442,368 gas rather than 768,000, and the per-pair cost falls by about 42%."
  why="The name describes the result, not the algorithm, and the naive implementation genuinely is a loop."
  src="eip-2537"
/>

The EIP also puts a number on the other saving, which has nothing to do with cryptography: performing
an MSM of 100 points by calling a multiplication precompile 100 times and an addition precompile 99
times would waste roughly **138,600 gas** in `CALL` overhead
alone.<Cite src="eip-2537" /> A batched interface is partly a cryptographic optimisation and partly a
way of not paying the EVM's per-call toll two hundred times.

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="bls-precompiles">
A contract verifies an aggregated BLS signature using the new precompiles. The relevant fragment:

```solidity
// pubkeys[i] is a 48-byte compressed G1 point read from a registry.
bytes memory input;
for (uint256 i = 0; i < pubkeys.length; i++) {
    input = bytes.concat(input, pubkeys[i], bytes32(weights[i]));
}
(bool ok, bytes memory apk) = address(0x0c).staticcall(input);
require(ok, "aggregation failed");
```

Three separate defects. Name them, and say what the caller observes for each.

<Answer>
**Defect one: the points are in the wrong encoding.** `0x0c` is `G1MSM`, and it expects each pair as a
128-byte uncompressed padded `G₁` point followed by a 32-byte scalar — 160 bytes per
pair.<Cite src="eip-2537" /> The registry's 48-byte compressed form is the consensus and KZG
serialisation, and the precompile does not accept it.<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" /> The
contract must decompress before the call, off-chain or in Solidity; the EIP's own rationale notes that
supplying decompressed points in calldata is deliberately cheaper than making the precompile
decompress.<Cite src="eip-2537" />

**Defect two: what the caller observes is not a revert.** Each pair is 80 bytes instead of 160, so
`k = floor(len / 160)` is half the intended count, the gas function charges for that smaller `k`
without validating the length, and the main routine then errors — at which point **all gas supplied
with the `STATICCALL` is burned**.<Cite src="eip-2537" /> The `require` never runs with a useful
budget behind it. In a batch transaction this is the difference between one failed operation and a
consumed block-gas allowance.

**Defect three: the loop's cost model is wrong in the caller's favour and its correctness model is
wrong in the caller's disfavour.** `bytes.concat` in a loop reallocates and copies the whole buffer on
every iteration, so building the input is quadratic in the number of signers — for a large committee
that alone can dominate the precompile cost the code is trying to optimise. Build the buffer once at
the known size and write into it.

**A fourth thing worth flagging even though it is not a bug here.** `G1MSM` performs a subgroup check
on every input point, so a point that is on the curve but outside the prime-order subgroup is
rejected.<Cite src="eip-2537" /> That is a real guarantee and it is worth knowing you have it — but
had this code used `G1ADD` at `0x0b` in a loop instead, it would have had no such check, because the
addition precompiles deliberately omit it.<Cite src="eip-2537" /> Whether your points are validated
depends on which precompile you called, not on the curve.

**The reviewable rule:** for every precompile call, write down the per-pair input length, whether the
inputs are validated, and what happens to your gas on error. Three lines in a comment above the
`staticcall`. All three differ between `0x08` and `0x0f`, on the same conceptual operation.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**The module's practice exercise is this lesson with a compiler attached.** It asks you to call `0x0b`
and `0x0f` with vectors from the execution-spec tests, then break the call twice — once by sending an
`Fp` element as 48 bytes instead of 64, once by sending a compressed `G₁` point — and to measure
`G1MSM` at three values of `k` against the EIP's own
formula.<Cite src="execution-spec-tests-eip2537" /><Cite src="eip-2537" /> Taking the vectors from the
spec tests rather than generating your own keeps "is this a valid point" and "is this the right
encoding" as two problems instead of one.

**The next lesson uses `0x10` and `0x11`.** Those two mapping precompiles exist because turning bytes
into a curve point is a cryptographic operation with its own standard, and the alternative — everyone
improvising — breaks the security proofs of the signature schemes built on top.

**And in gas work generally**, the transferable habit is the derivation. When a cost arrives as a
single quoted number, take it apart into the terms whose owners differ: the schedule's, the proof
system's, and yours. Only the last one is negotiable, and you cannot find it without the first two.
