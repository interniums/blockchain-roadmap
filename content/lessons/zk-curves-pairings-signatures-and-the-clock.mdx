A number from the beacon chain's design notes: with 500,000 active validators, the network carries
over **1,300 attestations per second** as a sustained average, with much higher
bursts.<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" /> Every one of them is a signed message. Every one has to
be verified by every node, and stored.

Now price it with the signature scheme the reader already knows. ECDSA verification is fast — faster,
per signature, than the scheme this lesson is about, by more than an order of
magnitude.<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" /> And it does not matter, because 1,300 fast
verifications per second is still 1,300 verifications per second, and 1,300 signatures per second is
still that many objects to gossip and store.

The property that makes a validator set this large tractable is not speed. It is that many signatures
compress into one, and the compressed signature verifies with a **constant** number of pairings
regardless of how many signers it represents.<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" /> That property is
only available on a pairing-friendly curve, and it is the reason Ethereum consensus runs on
BLS12-381.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" />

This lesson does three things: the scheme and why aggregation works; the step that looks trivial and
is not, which is turning a message into a curve point; and the clock — the structural reason a 2026
curriculum should present all of this as load-bearing today and transitional over a decade, without
attaching a date to the transition.

## The scheme, in four lines

Using Ethereum's assignment of groups — public keys in `G₁`, signatures in
`G₂`:<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" />

```text
secret key   sk        a scalar, uniform in [1, r-1]
public key   pk        = [sk]g₁                       48 bytes compressed
message pt   H(m)      a point in G₂                  from a hash-to-curve map
signature    σ         = [sk]H(m)                     96 bytes compressed
verify                 e(g₁, σ) == e(pk, H(m))
```

The verification identity is bilinearity applied once and then read backwards:

```text
e(pk, H(m)) = e([sk]g₁, H(m)) = e(g₁, H(m))^sk = e(g₁, [sk]H(m)) = e(g₁, σ)
```

<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" />

Notice what is *not* in that list. There is no nonce. ECDSA needs a fresh random value per signature,
and a repeated one leaks the private key. BLS signing is deterministic: the same key over the same
message always produces the same point. That removes an entire class of implementation failure, and it
is what lets aggregation be non-interactive — signers never have to coordinate, and aggregation can
happen incrementally, by anyone, after the fact.<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" />

## Aggregation, and the two numbers that make it worth it

Signatures are points in `G₂`, so they can be added. Public keys are points in `G₁`, so they can be
added. Push both sums through the verification identity and it survives:

```text
e(pk₁ + … + pk_n, H(m)) = e(g₁, σ₁ + … + σ_n)
```

<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" />

An aggregate signature is mathematically indistinguishable from an individual one and is the same 96
bytes.<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" /> So:

- **Verification work.** N signatures naively cost 2N pairings. Aggregated, they cost **two pairings**,
  plus N−1 additions in `G₁` and N−1 in `G₂` — and a point addition is very much cheaper than a
  pairing.<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" />
- **Space.** N signatures become 96 bytes, plus one bit per validator to record who signed, since the
  verifier must reconstruct the matching aggregate public key and must not count anyone
  twice.<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" />

That one-bit-per-signer detail is where an [attestation](/c/attestation)'s `aggregation_bits` field
comes from, and why aggregates can only be merged when their bit lists are
disjoint.<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" /> It is also why aggregation preserves individual
accountability: the protocol still knows exactly who signed, which is what makes
[slashing](/c/slashing) possible on an aggregated vote.

<Aside kind="note">
The standard names two aggregation regimes and they have different security requirements.
Aggregating over **the same message** — Ethereum's case — collapses to two pairings regardless of N.
Aggregating over **distinct messages** costs N+1 pairings rather than
two.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" /><Cite src="ietf-bls-signature" /> Both are useful; only the first
gives the constant-cost headline, and a design that assumes the headline while aggregating distinct
messages has mispriced its verifier by a factor of N.
</Aside>

## The rogue-key attack, and why deposits sign themselves

Adding public keys together is exactly as easy for an attacker as for the protocol, and that is a
problem.

Suppose your public key is `pk₁`. I hold a secret key `sk₂`, but instead of publishing `[sk₂]g₁` I
publish

```text
pk₂' = [sk₂]g₁ - pk₁
```

I then sign a message alone, producing `σ = [sk₂]H(m)`, and claim it is an aggregate that you and I
both signed. Verification against the sum of our two published keys succeeds, because
`pk₁ + pk₂' = [sk₂]g₁`.<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" /> You have been recorded as signing
something you never saw.

Nothing about the pairing prevents this. The scheme is doing exactly what it promises; the flaw is
that a "public key" was accepted without evidence that anyone holds the matching secret. The standard
names this the **rogue key attack** and specifies defences at the scheme level rather than the curve
level.<Cite src="ietf-bls-signature" />

Ethereum's defence is **proof of possession**: a validator must sign its own public key when it
deposits, and the attacker cannot produce such a signature for `pk₂'` because it does not know and
cannot compute the corresponding secret.<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" /><Cite src="ietf-bls-signature" />
The alternative defence, used where registration is not a natural step, is to require that aggregated
messages be *distinct*, which removes the algebraic freedom the attack needs — at the cost of the N+1
pairing count above.<Cite src="ietf-bls-signature" />

### Domain separation, one layer up

The same key signs ten different kinds of protocol message. The beacon chain therefore never signs a
message directly: it signs a **signing root** that mixes in a domain type, the fork version, and the
genesis validators root.<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" />

Each ingredient buys one property. The domain type stops a signature made for one purpose being
replayed as another — necessary because objects of different types are not guaranteed to have distinct
hash tree roots. The genesis validators root distinguishes this chain from any testnet or fork of it.
The fork version invalidates messages from validators that have not
upgraded.<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" />

With one deliberate exception: **deposit signatures do not mix in the fork version**, so a deposit
remains valid however the chain is later upgraded.<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" /> That is the
proof-of-possession signature from the previous section, and it has to outlive every fork for the
defence to hold at any point in the future.

This is [signature replay](/c/signature-replay) prevention as a protocol-level design, and it is worth
carrying into application work: a signature is only ever a claim about the exact bytes signed, and
"the exact bytes" must include everything that distinguishes one context from another.

## Hashing to a curve is not hashing

Look back at the scheme and find `H(m)`, the map from a message to a point in `G₂`. It reads like a
formality. It is a standard of its own, and the reasons are worth knowing because the naive approach
appears to work.

**The naive approach.** Hash the message, interpret the digest as an x-coordinate, check whether a
matching y exists on the curve, and if not, increment and retry. It produces points. It passes tests.

**Why it is not acceptable.** RFC 9380 declines to specify such probabilistic rejection methods —
"try-and-increment" or "hunt-and-peck" — at all, on the grounds that the goal is algorithms that can
plausibly run in constant time, and states that their use is **NOT RECOMMENDED** because they have
been a perennial cause of side-channel vulnerabilities, citing Dragonblood as an example in
practice.<Cite src="rfc-9380" /> The number of retries depends on the message, so the running time
leaks information about it.

**And the property that is actually required.** The schemes built on top are proved secure in the
random-oracle model, so the map must be **indifferentiable from a random oracle** — a strong property
the specified constructions are proved to have, with one variant producing a uniformly random point
and another a non-uniform one.<Cite src="rfc-9380" /> An improvised map does not merely feel
unprincipled; it invalidates the proof that the signature scheme is unforgeable.

**Then there is the subgroup problem.** The map lands somewhere on the curve, and the curve is larger
than the prime-order subgroup. BLS12-381's `G₁` and `G₂` cofactors contain small prime
factors,<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" /> so the result must be multiplied by the cofactor to clear it
into the right group before it can be used at all.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" /> RFC 9380 defines
complete suites that include this step — the BLS12-381 `G₁` and `G₂` suites are named in its list of
recommended ciphersuites.<Cite src="rfc-9380" />

Ethereum exposes the field-to-curve halves of that process as precompiles: `MAP_FP_TO_G1` at `0x10`
for 5,500 gas and `MAP_FP2_TO_G2` at `0x11` for 23,800.<Cite src="eip-2537" />

<Misconception
  belief="Hashing to a curve point is hash-then-interpret-as-x, with a retry when the x has no matching y."
  reality="Retry-based maps leak timing information about the message and are explicitly NOT RECOMMENDED by the standard, which specifies constant-time constructions proved indifferentiable from a random oracle instead — and adds cofactor clearing, which the naive method omits entirely."
  why="The naive method produces valid-looking points and passes every functional test, so nothing in a normal development loop signals a problem."
  src="rfc-9380"
/>

<Aside kind="warn">
Read EIP-2537's own description of what those two precompiles do, because it is narrower than the
name suggests. The mapping function specified there does **not** map a byte string into a field
element — that part can be implemented in many ways and is left to the caller — and only performs the
field arithmetic that takes a field element to a curve point.<Cite src="eip-2537" /> So `0x10` is one
of the two halves of hash-to-curve. Getting the other half wrong is entirely possible while calling
the precompile correctly, and the [two-fields](/c/scalar-field-vs-base-field) lesson is exactly about
the mistake you would make there.
</Aside>

### The same scheme, priced from a contract

Since EIP-2537 the aggregation above is available to the EVM, which makes an on-chain light client for
consensus signatures a gas question rather than an impossible one. Working the price out surfaces a
choice the precompile set makes easy to get wrong.

**Subgoal: price the pairing.** BLS verification is two pairings, so one call to `PAIRING_CHECK` at
`0x0f` with `k = 2`: `32,600 × 2 + 37,700` = **102,900 gas**, whatever the number of
signers.<Cite src="eip-2537" /> That is the constant the whole scheme exists to deliver.

**Subgoal: price rebuilding the aggregate public key, which is not constant.** The verifier has to sum
the `G₁` keys of exactly the validators who signed. Two precompiles can do it, and the obvious choice
is the wrong one.

</Figure>

<Cite src="eip-2537" />

**Subgoal: see why.** Aggregation is a sum of points with every scalar equal to one — it is addition,
not multiplication. `G1MSM` charges the multiplication cost per pair even when the scalar is one,
because the discount table prices batched *multiplication*, and the best discount available caps at
519.<Cite src="eip-2537" /> Repeated addition is roughly **17×** cheaper here, before counting the
per-call overhead of issuing 511 separate calls, which is real but far smaller than the gap.

**Subgoal: state the rule rather than the number.** Use `G1ADD` when the scalars are all one, and
`G1MSM` when they are genuinely varied — a weighted committee, a random linear combination, a
polynomial commitment. Multi-scalar multiplication is the cheapest way to do a job that involves
multiplying; it is an expensive way to do a job that does not.

<Aside kind="warn">
This module has not measured how many BLS12-381 contracts are actually deployed on L1 since Pectra,
and nothing above should be read as a claim about adoption. The arithmetic is from the two gas
schedules;<Cite src="eip-2537" /> the question of who is using it is unquantified here. Measure your
own path against the EIP's formula rather than assuming a template got this right — the same
substitution appears in production code on BN254, where a contract without `G₂` arithmetic has to
route around a missing precompile entirely.<Cite src="zellic-bls-signatures" />
</Aside>

## The clock

Everything above rests on the difficulty of a discrete logarithm. A sufficiently large quantum
computer solves discrete logarithms efficiently, which breaks pairing-based cryptography as a whole:
the signatures, the KZG commitments, the pairing-based proof systems, all of
it.<Cite src="ethereum-org-quantum-resistance" />

Hash-based constructions do not fall to the same attack. A hash function's security rests on the
absence of exploitable structure rather than on a hard algebraic problem, which is why hash-based
signatures, STARKs and FRI-style commitments survive an attack that removes every construction in this
module.<Cite src="ethereum-org-quantum-resistance" /> That asymmetry — first raised in this module's
opening lesson as a difference between two kinds of assumption — is the structural reason Ethereum's
long-horizon direction moves toward hash-only assumptions, and it is the same reason a
[transparent setup](/c/transparent-setup) is attractive independently of quantum computers.

Four disciplines are needed to state this responsibly.

**Nothing has been removed and no removal is scheduled.** The BN254 precompiles are
live.<Cite src="eip-197" /> EIP-2537's seven BLS12-381 precompiles are
live.<Cite src="eip-2537" /> Consensus signatures are BLS. Ethereum's next upgrade — Amsterdam on the
execution side, Gloas on the consensus side — is listed on ethereum.org's own upgrade table with its
date as **TBD**, and it
changes no curve.<Cite src="ethereum-org-history" /> Treat the direction as a direction. Print no date,
because there is none to print.

**The state-tree question shows the asymmetry deciding a design.** Verkle trees were proposed as
Ethereum's next state commitment, and they use polynomial commitments built on elliptic curves — which
puts them on the wrong side of this lesson's divide, and also makes them expensive to verify inside a
proof system. The protocol track carries [the current status of that proposal](/c/verkle-is-off-the-roadmap)
and its caveats: the direction moved toward an explicitly hash-based tree, the stated reasons are the
two just named, and there is no single announcement post to point at. What is stated here is
what was proposed, what the direction is, and that there is **no date** attached to any of it — a
distinction worth keeping, because "proposed", "preferred" and "shipped" are three different
statuses and only the third has a block number.

**The hash-based side has had its own numbers revised — downward.** The Ethereum Foundation's second
L1 zkEVM post is blunt about it: foundational conjectures were mathematically disproven, and what had
been advertised as 100 bits of security might actually have been 80, which is why its milestones were
restated around *provable* security at 100 bits and then 128.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" />
"Post-quantum" is not a synonym for "settled". Both families are moving targets; they are moving for
different reasons.

**And the horizon is long enough that the design question is real.** A verifier deployed today is a
fifteen-year artifact if the product it serves lasts fifteen years. The question the module's
reflection asks is not which curve is best but what in your contract design makes a migration possible
at all — because "we would redeploy" is an answer only if somebody is entitled to point users at the
new deployment, and naming that somebody is the moment a trustless system acquires a trusted party.

**"We should build on STARKs."** Possibly, but not for the reason given, and not without the caveat.
Hash-based systems genuinely resist the attack that breaks pairings.<Cite src="ethereum-org-quantum-resistance" />

tooling maturity and stated provable security with its date, on both sides.

**"BLS in consensus is a liability needing urgent replacement."** Urgency is the wrong axis. A signature
scheme's exposure is not symmetric with a commitment scheme's: a broken signature scheme is
catastrophic the day it breaks, but the migration is a fork away and consensus already versions its
signatures by fork version.<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" /> What is genuinely hard to migrate is
data already committed to and contracts already deployed with immutable verifiers. **Sort the
inventory by how hard each item is to change, not by how frightening it sounds.**

target to be changed, name the party that may exercise it, publish that as a trust assumption rather
than hiding it, and re-read the assessed security level of our curve annually with the date and
estimator recorded."

That statement contains no prediction, no date, and one concrete action — which is the most a honest
planning document can contain about a decade-scale cryptographic transition.

## What this unlocks

**Circuits, next.** You now know what the curve underneath a proving system is, which two fields you
are working in, what a pairing buys, what it costs from the EVM, and where the scheme layer adds
requirements the curve does not. Writing a circuit is the layer above all of that, and the errors it
produces will now decompose into layers you can name.

**On-chain verification** becomes an exercise in the arithmetic from the previous lesson: a fixed
pairing cost, a per-public-input cost, and your own contract's overhead — with aggregation layers
appearing as the answer to a constant you cannot shrink.

**And in the security work you already did**, the pattern in this lesson generalises past
cryptography. The rogue-key attack broke nothing in the curve; the naive hash-to-curve map broke
nothing in the hash. Both defeated a system by attacking the joint between two correct components.
That joint is where the security track told you to look, and it is where the cryptography turns out to
live too.
