Different tracks introduced them, different problems motivated them, and nothing so far has said out
loud that all four are the *same object*: a point on an elliptic curve, produced by multiplying one
fixed public point by one secret number, and published in the confident belief that nobody can run
the multiplication backwards.

That one step — easy forwards, believed infeasible backwards — is the entire load-bearing assumption
under commitments, signatures, and every pairing-based proof system in this track. This lesson makes
it precise, then does the thing curricula usually skip: it shows that the strength of the assumption
is an *estimate*, that the estimate for one of the curves Ethereum actually deployed fell by roughly
a quarter after the curve was chosen, and that published figures for how far it fell still disagree.

## The group, in the only three lines you need

An elliptic curve over a finite field is a set of coordinate pairs `(x, y)` satisfying an equation.
The curves in play here are unusually plain. BN254 — the curve behind Ethereum's original pairing
precompiles — is `y² = x³ + 3` over the prime field of order
`21888242871839275222246405745257275088696311157297823662689037894645226208583`, with the generator
sitting at the almost comically small coordinates `(1, 2)`.<Cite src="eip-197" /> BLS12-381 is
`y² = x³ + 4` over a 381-bit prime.<Cite src="eip-2537" /><Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" />

On top of that set there is an addition rule. Two points add to a third point; there is an identity
element, called the point at infinity; every point has an inverse. That is what "group" means, and
it is the whole of the structure cryptography uses.

Adding a point to itself `a` times is **scalar multiplication**, written `[a]G`. The square brackets
are not decoration — they mark the asymmetry this lesson is about. `a` is an ordinary integer. `G`
is a curve point. `[a]G` is a curve point. There is no operation that takes two curve points and
"multiplies" them; the group offers addition and nothing else.

<Aside kind="note">
The additive notation is a convention, not a fact about the mathematics. Older cryptographic writing
uses multiplicative notation for exactly the same objects, so `[a]G` becomes `g^a` and the discrete
logarithm looks like a logarithm. The elliptic-curve literature settled on additive notation because
the group operation is drawn as a chord-and-tangent construction that looks like addition. When you
read a paper that writes `g^a` for a curve point, nothing has changed but the typography.
</Aside>

**Everything in this track is built by putting a secret into a scalar and publishing the point.** A
[private key](/c/keypair) is a scalar and a public key is the point.<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" />
A polynomial's coefficients are scalars and a [KZG commitment](/c/kzg-commitment) is a weighted sum of
points. A circuit's
witness values are scalars and a proof is a handful of points. There is no fifth trick. Once you see
that the API surface of the whole subject is "choose scalars, publish points", the individual
constructions stop looking like separate inventions.

## The one-way step, stated as an assumption rather than a fact

The **elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem** is: given the generator `G` and the point `[a]G`,
recover `a`.

Two things about that sentence deserve attention, and both get skipped.

**First, forwards is cheap and backwards is not, but neither is impossible.** Computing `[a]G` for a
254-bit `a` takes a few hundred point operations by repeated doubling. Recovering `a` takes, by the
best generic method — Pollard's rho — work on the order of the square root of the subgroup order.
That is not a wall. It is a very large number, and "very large" is a claim about hardware and
algorithms, both of which move.

**Second, nobody has proved it is hard.** The security of every construction in this track is
conditional on an assumption for which no lower bound exists. That is not a scandal; it is the normal
state of applied cryptography. But it changes what a "security level" is, which is the next section.

That misconception is worth holding onto, because the last lesson of this module turns on it.
[Hash functions](/c/hash-function) and discrete logs do not fail together, do not fail for the same
reasons, and — under a sufficiently large quantum computer — do not fail to the same
degree.<Cite src="ethereum-org-quantum-resistance" />

## What "128-bit security" actually claims

Security is quoted in bits, and the informal reading is right: *n*-bit security means breaking it
should take about 2ⁿ operations.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" />

For an ordinary signature curve there is one target and one bound. To reach *n*-bit security against
Pollard's rho, the prime subgroup order `r` must be at least 2*n* bits, because rho's cost scales
with the square root of `r`.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" /> BLS12-381's subgroup order is 255 bits,
which buys roughly 127 bits against that attack alone.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" />

For a **pairing-friendly** curve there are three targets, and this is where the arithmetic stops
being reassuring. A pairing maps a pair of curve points into a third group, `GT`, which lives inside
an extension of the base field. The discrete logarithm must be hard in all three groups — the two
curve groups *and* the extension field.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" /> Discrete logarithms in a
finite field are attacked by the number field sieve, an entirely different family of algorithm from
Pollard's rho, and the number field sieve has kept improving.

You will meet the mechanics of `GT` and embedding degrees in the pairing lesson. What matters now is
the consequence: **a pairing-friendly curve has an attack surface an ordinary signature curve does
not have, and it is the one that moved.**

## The reassessment, and why the numbers still disagree

In 2016, Kim and Barbulescu published the **extended tower number field sieve**, which improves
polynomial selection in the number field sieve for discrete logarithms in extension
fields.<Cite src="iacr-extnfs" /> The improvement applies when the embedding degree is composite —
specifically when it factors as `k = i·j` with `i` and `j` both greater than one and coprime — which
is exactly the shape of the BN and BLS families, whose embedding degrees are divisible by
six.<Cite src="ietf-pairing-friendly-curves" />

The effect on BN254 was not marginal. The IETF's pairing-friendly-curves draft states it plainly: a
BN curve with a 254-bit characteristic was adopted by many libraries as a 128-bit-security parameter,
and "ensures no more than the 100-bit security level" once the attack is
accounted for.<Cite src="ietf-pairing-friendly-curves" /> The underlying re-estimation is
Barbulescu and Duquesne's, which put the BN curves previously believed to give 128 bits at
approximately 100.<Cite src="iacr-pairing-key-sizes" /><Cite src="ietf-pairing-friendly-curves" />

Now the part a careful reader has to sit with. **Published figures for BN254's post-attack level do
not agree, and neither do the figures for BLS12-381.**

Two curves, six figures, a spread of more than twenty bits on each. This is not sloppiness by any
single author. Estimating the cost of a number field sieve computation involves choices about which
optimisations an attacker will find, how to convert asymptotic complexity into concrete operation
counts, and how to price operations — and a dedicated paper exists specifically about how hard those
choices are to make defensibly.<Cite src="iacr-nfs-assessment-challenges" />

So the defensible statements, and the ones this module will use throughout, are:

- **BN254 is around 100 bits, down from an original 128-bit claim.** Not 128. Do not publish a point
  estimate for the current figure.
- **BLS12-381 targets roughly 128 bits and is commonly assessed slightly under.** Do not publish a
  point estimate for that either.

<Aside kind="warn">
The practical form of this is a review question, not a maths question. When a document quotes a
security level, ask **as of when, against which attack, and estimated by whom**. A figure without
those three fields is a figure that was true about a different year. The same discipline applies to
proving costs and latencies later in this track, where the units are worse and the disagreements are
larger.
</Aside>

## Not every point on the curve is a point you can use

One more structural fact, because it is the source of a class of bug you will meet at the precompile
boundary two lessons from now.

The set of all points satisfying the curve equation is larger than the prime-order subgroup the
cryptography actually uses. The ratio between them is the **cofactor**. Ordinary elliptic-curve
practice wants that cofactor to be one or very small, to avoid small-subgroup attacks. Pairing-based
curves cannot have that: BLS12-381's `G1` and `G2` cofactors are enormous, and both contain small
prime factors — 3, 11 and 10177 among others in `G1`; 13, 23 and 2713 in
`G2`.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" />

So on this curve, **"is on the curve" and "is in the group" are two different checks**, and an
implementation that performs only the first can be handed a point that lives in a small subgroup and
leaks information about the scalar it gets multiplied by.

The specifications take this seriously and they are not uniform about it, which is worth noticing
before you write a line of integration code:

- EIP-197 says that to confirm an input is in `G_1`, checking the encoding and the curve equation is
  sufficient; for `G_2`, the element's order must additionally be checked against the group
  order.<Cite src="eip-197" />
- EIP-2537 requires a subgroup check on every input to the multi-scalar multiplications and to the
  pairing check, and states explicitly that **there is no subgroup check for the G1 and G2 addition
  precompiles**.<Cite src="eip-2537" />

Read that second bullet twice. Two precompiles on the same curve, specified in the same document,
with different validation guarantees. Neither is a bug; addition is safe without the check and the
check is expensive. But an integration that assumes "the precompile validates my input" is right
about five of the seven and wrong about two.

A colleague proposes shipping a verifier on BN254 for a product that must remain secure for fifteen
years, and defends the choice like this:

> BN254 is the standard curve for on-chain verification, it has been in the EVM since 2017, and
> nobody has broken it. The precompiles are cheap. If a break ever happens we will redeploy.

Three of those four sentences are true. Say what is wrong with the argument anyway, in terms of what
a security level is.

<Answer>
**The unbroken-so-far claim is not the claim that matters.** Nobody has broken BN254, and nobody
needs to for the argument to fail. The relevant fact is that the *estimated cost* of breaking it fell
from 128 bits to around 100 in 2016 without any attack being executed and without any parameter
changing.<Cite src="iacr-pairing-key-sizes" /><Cite src="ietf-pairing-friendly-curves" /> The
security level is a forecast of attacker cost, and forecasts get revised downwards more often than
upwards, because progress in the number field sieve is cumulative.

**Fifteen years is longer than the interval that already produced one revision.** The extended tower
number field sieve is from 2016;<Cite src="iacr-extnfs" /> the curve was chosen before it. Betting a
fifteen-year horizon on "no further improvement" is betting against the only trend the field has.

**"It has been in the EVM since 2017" is an argument about availability, not strength.** BN254 is the
default because EIP-196 and EIP-197 gave it precompiles in 2017 and EIP-1108 made them cheap in
2019.<Cite src="eip-196" /><Cite src="eip-197" /><Cite src="eip-1108" /> That is a real and
substantial advantage — it is why most deployed verifiers use it — but it is an advantage in cost and
tooling, and it is being weighed here against a security property it says nothing about.

**"We will redeploy" is the sentence to interrogate.** It is not wrong, but it is not free either. A
verifier address is embedded in other contracts, referenced by off-chain infrastructure, and trusted
by users. The migration question is who is entitled to point users at the replacement, and what that
authority does to the trust story of the system that was supposed to need no trusted party. That is
the module's closing reflection, and it is worth writing the answer down before you deploy rather
than after.

**What a defensible version of the argument looks like:** name the horizon, name the assessed level
with its date and source, state that BLS12-381 is available on L1 through EIP-2537 at a known gas
cost,<Cite src="eip-2537" /> and either choose the stronger curve or record explicitly that you chose
the cheaper one and what event would trigger migration.
</Answer>
</Check>

## Where this pays off

**Immediately, in the next lesson.** A curve carries two different finite fields, and the reason the
distinction is the most common ZK integration bug is visible already in the numbers on this page:
BN254's base field modulus and its subgroup order are both 254 bits and agree on their first 38
decimal digits. They are different numbers, they are not interchangeable, and nothing in a type
system will tell you which one you just used.

**In the pairing lesson**, the extension field that made the security estimate fall becomes the thing
that makes pairings possible at all. The attack surface and the capability are the same structure
seen from two sides — which is why pairing-friendliness is a deliberate construction rather than a
property you find lying around.

**In the precompile lesson**, "on the curve" versus "in the group" stops being a footnote and becomes
an encoding rule with a gas cost, enforced on five of seven precompiles and deliberately not on the
other two.<Cite src="eip-2537" />

**And in the last lesson**, the difference between a discrete-log assumption and a hash assumption
becomes the reason Ethereum's long-horizon direction moves away from pairings
entirely.<Cite src="ethereum-org-quantum-resistance" /> That is a direction, not a date, and the
lesson is careful about the difference.
