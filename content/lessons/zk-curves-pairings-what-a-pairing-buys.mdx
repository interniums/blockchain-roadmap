You have already watched a verifier accept a KZG opening. One equation, two pairings, a handful of
points, and a claim about a polynomial the verifier never saw:

```text
e(C - [y]G₁,  G₂)  ==  e(π,  [s]G₂ - [z]G₂)
```

The blobs lesson treated that as a black box, which was the right call at the time. Now open it. The
question that matters is not how a pairing is computed — you will never implement one — but what the
verifier is doing on that line that it could not have done with point addition alone.

The answer is exact and it is small. **A pairing buys one multiplication between values the verifier
cannot see.** Not two. Not arbitrarily many. One. Everything pairing-based cryptography can do
follows from that single extra degree, and everything it cannot do follows from the fact that there
is no second one.

## What a verifier can already do without a pairing

Start with what the [group](/c/ec-group) alone gives you, because it is more than people expect and
the boundary is the point of the lesson.

Suppose a prover has committed to three secret scalars by publishing `A = [a]G`, `B = [b]G` and
`C = [c]G`. A verifier holding only those three points can check, with certainty and cheaply:

- **`a + b = c`.** Compute `A + B` and compare it to `C`. Two point additions.
- **`5a - 3b = c`.** Compute `[5]A - [3]B` and compare. The coefficients 5 and 3 are public, so the
  verifier can apply them itself.
- **any linear combination with public coefficients**, including "these thousand committed values sum
  to zero" and "this committed vector is the sum of those two committed vectors".

That is a real capability, and it is what makes commitments composable at all. Points add; therefore
commitments add; therefore the hidden scalars underneath add. The verifier learns nothing about `a`,
`b` or `c` and still gets a hard yes or no.

Now the thing it cannot do.

**`a · b = c` is out of reach.** The verifier has `A`, `B` and `C`. To multiply `a` by `b` it would
have to *get at* `a` and `b`, which is the discrete logarithm problem, which is the assumption the
whole scheme rests on being infeasible.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" /> Multiplying two hidden
scalars is not a missing feature of the group. It is precisely the thing the group is built to make
impossible.

<Aside kind="note">
This is worth restating because it is the hinge of the lesson. The verifier is not blocked by an API
limitation that a cleverer library could route around. It is blocked by the security assumption
itself: any operation that would let it multiply two hidden scalars would also let it recover them.
The interesting question is therefore not "how do we multiply" but "how do we get a *checkable
statement about* a product without recovering either factor".
</Aside>

## The pairing, and the one identity you need

A **pairing** is an efficiently computable map

```text
e : G₁ × G₂ → G_T
```

taking a point from one group and a point from a second group to an element of a third, called the
target group. It has two defining properties, and EIP-2537 lists them as required behaviour that any
implementation must satisfy:<Cite src="eip-2537" />

- **Bilinearity.** `e(a·P, b·Q) = e(a·b·P, Q) = e(P, a·b·Q)`. Scalars slide across the map and
  multiply. Written with the target group's multiplicative notation, `e([a]P, [b]Q) = e(P, Q)^(ab)`.
- **Non-degeneracy.** `e(P, Q) ≠ 1` for non-trivial inputs, so the map does not collapse everything
  to the identity and say "true" to any question.

EIP-2537 also pins the two identities integrators forget: `e(P, 0·Q) = e(0·P, Q) = 1`, and
`e(P, -Q) = e(-P, Q)`.<Cite src="eip-2537" /> The second is why on-chain verifiers negate one input
and check a *product of pairings equals one* rather than checking an equality of two pairings — a
shape EIP-197 builds directly into the precompile's contract.<Cite src="eip-197" />

Here is the sentence that makes a pairing feel less like magic. You could *define* a bilinear map
trivially: given `P = [p]G` and `Q = [q]G`, take their discrete logarithms `p` and `q`, multiply, and
return `[pq]G`. That is bilinear. It is also useless, because taking those discrete logarithms is the
thing nobody can do.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" /> A pairing computes the *same answer* — a value
determined by the product `p·q` — without ever recovering `p` or `q`. That is the entire trick, and
"efficiently computable" is the property doing all the work.

## The target group is real, and you never get to see it

`G_T` is not a formality. On BLS12-381 it is a subgroup of the multiplicative group of `F_{p¹²}`, the
twelfth-degree extension of the base field.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" /> An element of it is
twelve base field coefficients. At 48 bytes each, that is 576 bytes for a single value — an order of
magnitude larger than either input.

Which is why **no Ethereum precompile ever returns one.** EIP-197 says so in its own rationale: the
contract specifies a *check* rather than an evaluation of the pairing function, because the codomain
is a complex field that would raise encoding problems, and every known use in zkSNARKs needs only the
check.<Cite src="eip-197" /> EIP-2537 follows the same shape: `BLS12_PAIRING_CHECK` returns 32 bytes
whose last byte is `0x00` or `0x01`.<Cite src="eip-2537" />

That decision, made once in a February 2017 EIP for encoding convenience,<Cite src="eip-197" />
propagates into the cost of every verifier
on Ethereum, and you will price it in the precompile lesson. A verification equation that would
naturally carry a *precomputed* target-group constant cannot pass one in; it has to re-derive the
constant from its two source-group preimages inside the same call, which costs an extra pairing.

<Aside kind="note">
EIP-197 has one more piece of specification style worth pausing on. It defines the precompile's
semantics **in terms of discrete logarithms** — the output is one exactly when
`log_P1(a1)·log_P2(b1) + … + log_Pk(ak)·log_P2(bk) = 0` in the scalar field — and then remarks that
the discrete logarithm is of course assumed hard to compute, before giving an equivalent
specification via pairings that can actually be evaluated.<Cite src="eip-197" />

A specification written in terms of a quantity nobody can compute, made implementable by an identity
that computes the same answer another way. That is not a quirk of drafting; it is what a pairing *is*,
stated by a spec author who wanted the meaning to be unambiguous before the mechanism arrived.
</Aside>

## Exactly one multiplication, and why there is no second

Count degrees. Group operations let a verifier check statements of degree one in the hidden values:
sums and public multiples. A pairing raises that to degree two: it lets a verifier check statements
containing one product of hidden values.

Why does it stop there? Because the output lands in `G_T`, and `G_T` is not a source group for
another pairing. To multiply a third hidden value in, you would need a map from `G_T × G₁` into
somewhere useful, and no efficiently computable non-degenerate map of that shape is available on
these curves. Pairings do not chain.

So the ladder has three rungs and the third is a different technology:

| Degree in the hidden values | Machinery required |
|---|---|
| Linear — sums, public multiples | Group operations. No pairing. |
| Quadratic — one product of secrets | One pairing. |
| Higher | Not reachable by adding pairings. Either [recurse](/c/recursion) — verify a proof inside another circuit — or drop algebraic structure entirely for a hash-based commitment.<Cite src="ietf-pairing-friendly-curves" /> |

### Why degree two happens to be exactly enough

Two of the most consequential constructions in this ecosystem need degree two and no more. That is
not a coincidence — they were designed against the tool.

**KZG openings.** The scheme rests on the identity that if `p(z) = y`, then `p(X) - y` is divisible
by `X - z`, so there exists a quotient polynomial `q` with `p(X) - y = q(X)·(X - z)`. The prover
publishes a commitment `C` to `p` and a commitment `π` to `q`. The verifier must confirm that
identity *at the setup's secret point* `s`, which it does not know — so it must check

```text
p(s) - y  ==  q(s) · (s - z)
```

where `q(s)` is hidden inside `π` and `s` is hidden inside the setup element `[s]G₂`. **One product
of two quantities the verifier cannot see.** Exactly one pairing's worth of capability, which is why
the [opening check](/c/kzg-opening-proof) is one equation and not a protocol. The blob machinery
depends on it: the point-evaluation precompile at `0x0a` is that check, priced at 50,000
gas.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

Push the bilinearity through the equation at the top of this lesson and watch the multiplication
appear. Write `g = e(G₁, G₂)`, the target-group element you get from the two generators. Then, since
`C = [p(s)]G₁` and `π = [q(s)]G₁`:

```text
left   e(C - [y]G₁, G₂)            = g^(p(s) - y)
right  e(π, [s]G₂ - [z]G₂)         = g^(q(s) · (s - z))
```

Both sides are the same target-group element raised to some power, so the equation holds exactly when
the two exponents agree — which is the polynomial identity, evaluated at the secret point. The left
exponent is linear in hidden values. The right one is a product: `q(s)` times `(s - z)`, neither
factor known to the verifier. **The pairing's entire contribution to this protocol is turning that
one product into something comparable.** Strip it out and the check is not merely harder; it is not
expressible.

<Aside kind="note">
Notice what the verifier still has to be given. It never learns `s`, but it must hold `[s]G₂`, a
point produced by whoever ran the setup. That is where the trusted setup enters — not as a general
feature of pairings, but as the specific requirement that somebody once knew `s` and is trusted to
have discarded it. A [transparent setup](/c/transparent-setup) avoids that requirement by giving up
the algebraic structure this equation depends on, which is the trade the last lesson returns to.
</Aside>

**Quadratic constraint systems.** A rank-1 constraint system expresses a computation as constraints
of the form "(linear combination of the witness) times (linear combination of the witness) equals
(linear combination of the witness)". Every constraint is degree two, by construction, because
degree two is what the verification technology can check. Groth16 compiles such a system down to a
proof of **three group elements**, verified by checking **a single pairing product equation using
three pairings**.<Cite src="iacr-groth16" />

Read those two paragraphs together and the shape of the field becomes visible. Circuit languages make
you decompose everything into multiplications of two things not because multiplication is
fundamental, but because the verifier's one available non-linear check is a product of two hidden
values.

### The comparison in the title: what a hash cannot do here

A [hash commitment](/c/hash-commitment) is binding and, with a salt, hiding — the same two properties
a curve-point commitment has. The difference is not strength. It is that a hash is **opaque**: the
only operation it supports is opening. Given `H(a)` and `H(b)` there is nothing you can compute that
tells you anything about `a + b`, because a hash function is engineered so that its output carries no
usable structure. That is the property that makes it a good hash.

A curve-point commitment is **homomorphic**: the group operation on the commitments mirrors addition
on the committed values. That is what let the auditor in the first section check a sum without seeing
a salary. The pairing extends the mirroring by one degree, to a single product.

So the honest statement of the trade is: hash-based commitments give you no arithmetic and demand no
structural assumption; algebraic commitments give you arithmetic on hidden values and, in exchange,
carry a discrete-log assumption on a curve whose extension field is small enough to
pair.<Cite src="ietf-pairing-friendly-curves" /> Hash-based proof systems recover the missing
arithmetic by a different route — proving statements *about* opened values rather than checking
relations on unopened ones — which is why their proofs are larger and their assumptions
narrower.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" />

## Pairing-friendliness is a construction, not a discovery

If pairings are this useful, why is the curve underneath Ethereum accounts not used for them?

The **embedding degree** `k` of a curve is the smallest positive integer such that the subgroup order
`r` divides `pᵏ - 1`, where `p` is the base field modulus.<Cite src="ietf-pairing-friendly-curves" />
It is the degree of field extension you must climb to before the target group exists at all: `G_T`
lives inside `F_{pᵏ}`.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" />

For a curve chosen at random, `k` is astronomically large — comparable to `r` itself. `F_{pᵏ}` would
then be a field whose elements cannot be written down, let alone multiplied. **The pairing exists
mathematically and is not computable.** Pairing-friendly curves are curves whose parameters have been
deliberately constructed to make `k` small: chiefly the Barreto–Naehrig and Barreto–Lynn–Scott
families.<Cite src="ietf-pairing-friendly-curves" /> As eth2book puts it, create an elliptic curve at
random and it has a minuscule chance of being pairing-friendly.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" />

BLS12-381's embedding degree is 12 — that is what the 12 in the name
means.<Cite src="ietf-pairing-friendly-curves" /><Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" /> BN254's is also
12.<Cite src="ietf-pairing-friendly-curves" />

<Misconception
  belief="You could do pairings on secp256k1 if someone wrote the library."
  reality="secp256k1's embedding degree is enormous, so the target group lives in a field far too large to compute in. No implementation effort changes that; pairing-friendliness is a property built into a curve's parameters at design time."
  why="From an API surface every curve looks alike — a modulus, a generator, add and multiply — so the difference reads as a feature gap rather than a structural one."
  src="eth2book-bls12-381"
/>

And now the uncomfortable symmetry. The embedding degree is also called the **security
multiplier**: a larger `k` makes the discrete logarithm in `G_T` harder, while a smaller `k` puts
`G_T` in a field small enough for the number field sieve to
attack.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" /><Cite src="ietf-pairing-friendly-curves" /> A curve intended
purely for [ECDSA signatures](/c/ecdsa-secp256k1) therefore *wants* a huge embedding degree, because
a small one would hand an attacker a second, easier way in. Pairing-friendly curves accept that
second attack surface on purpose in exchange for the capability — and it is exactly that surface the
extended tower number field sieve improved against in 2016, taking BN254's assessed security down
with it.<Cite src="iacr-extnfs" /><Cite src="ietf-pairing-friendly-curves" />

The capability and the weakness are the same structure viewed from two sides. Twelve is described as
a sweet spot: high enough that `F_{p¹²}` resists the sieve, low enough that arithmetic in it remains
tractable.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" />

## G₁ and G₂ are not two names for the same thing

A pairing needs two *distinct* source groups of the same prime order. A plain curve over `Fp` has
only one subgroup of order `r`, so the second one has to be found somewhere else: in the curve over
the extension field. On BLS12-381, `G₁` sits in `E(Fp)` and `G₂` sits in `E'(Fp²)`, the sextic twist,
which is a coordinate transformation that lets `G₂` be represented over a quadratic extension instead
of the twelfth-degree one it formally inhabits.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" />

The practical consequence is that a `G₂` point's coordinates are *pairs* of base field elements
where a `G₁` point's are single ones, and every downstream cost roughly doubles or worse.

Because the two groups cost differently, **where you put each object is a design decision with
consequences**, and the BLS signature standard makes it an explicit parameter: *minimal-signature-size*
puts signatures in `G₁` and public keys in `G₂`, while *minimal-pubkey-size* does the
reverse.<Cite src="ietf-bls-signature" />

Ethereum consensus chose minimal-pubkey-size: validator public keys are `G₁` points at 48 bytes,
signatures are `G₂` points at 96.<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" /> The reasoning is workload,
not aesthetics — public keys are aggregated far more often than signatures, and every validator's
public key is stored in the beacon state, so the small representation is the one that must be
small.<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" /> Zcash, designing for a different workload, chose the
opposite assignment.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" />

Neither is wrong. The lesson is that "which group" is a question you have to answer, and the answer
follows from what your system transmits and stores most often.

For each of the following, say whether it needs a pairing, and if it does, say what the single
multiplication of hidden values is. Where a pairing is involved, also say which object you would put
in `G₁` and why.

1. A payroll contract holds Pedersen commitments to each employee's salary and must prove to an
   auditor that the salaries sum to a published total, without revealing any of them.
2. A rollup must prove that a batch's output state root is the result of executing 5,000
   transactions against its input state root.
3. A sync-committee light client must check that 400 named validators signed the same block root.

<Answer>
**1 — no pairing.** "These committed values sum to a published total" is a linear statement. The
auditor adds the commitment points and compares against a commitment to the published total. Point
addition, no pairing, no pairing-friendly curve required. This is the case that most often gets
over-engineered: a team reaches for a proof system because the words "prove" and "without revealing"
appear in the requirement, when the group operation alone already settles it.

**2 — a pairing, if the proof system is pairing-based.** The execution is compiled into a quadratic
constraint system, and the single multiplication is the one inside each constraint: a linear
combination of the witness times another linear combination of the witness. Groth16 turns the whole
batch into three group elements checked by one pairing product equation with three
pairings.<Cite src="iacr-groth16" /> Note what does *not* scale: the number of pairings is fixed by
the proof system, not by the 5,000 transactions. **`G₁` should hold the proof elements**, because
the proof is transmitted and stored for every batch, forever, while the verification key's `G₂`
elements are written once at deployment and read from contract storage. That is the same reasoning
Ethereum applied to public keys, applied to a different object.

**3 — a pairing, and this is the aggregation case.** The single multiplication is the one inside the
BLS verification identity: the signature is `[sk]H(m)`, and the check confirms that the same `sk`
relates the public key to the generator and the signature to the message point, which is a product of
`sk` with a value derived from the message.<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" /> **Public keys go in
`G₁`** — 48 bytes rather than 96, and a light client aggregates 400 of them on every check while
handling exactly one signature.<Cite src="eth2book-bls-signatures" /><Cite src="ietf-bls-signature" />
Getting this backwards would triple the bytes the client must hold and put the expensive group
arithmetic on the side of the equation you do 400 times instead of once.

**The transferable move:** before asking which curve or which proof system, ask what degree your
statement is in the values the verifier cannot see. Degree one needs no pairing at all. Degree two
needs one. Anything higher is a signal that you are about to pay for recursion or change commitment
scheme, and it is much cheaper to discover that on a whiteboard than in a prover.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**The next lesson picks the curve.** You now know that pairing-friendliness is a constructed property
and that the embedding degree is both the capability and the attack surface. That is the whole frame
for choosing between BN254 and BLS12-381: the same design choice that makes a pairing computable is
the one the number field sieve attacks, and the two curves made it in different years with different
knowledge.

**The precompile lesson turns this into bytes and gas.** Every asymmetry in the table above shows up
as an address, an input length and a price — and the two curves expose *different sets* of
operations, which is a constraint on protocol design and not merely on cost.

**And the last lesson takes the ladder seriously.** A system that needs degree three or a system that
wants no algebraic assumption at all has to leave pairings behind, and that exit is the same one the
post-quantum roadmap is heading for. The rung you cannot climb is the reason the ladder eventually
gets replaced.
