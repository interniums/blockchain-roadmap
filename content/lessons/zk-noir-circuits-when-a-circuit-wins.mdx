Two teams are given the same requirement. A user holds a credential issued off-chain; they must prove
to a contract that their credential is in a published set, and that they have not used it before.
Merkle membership against a public root, plus a nullifier. Nothing else.

The first team writes it in Rust, compiles it for a zkVM, and has a proof verifying on a testnet
before lunch. The second team writes it as a circuit, spends two days learning why their loop bounds
have to be constants, and ships on Thursday.

Six months later the first team is paying a proving bill that scales with a user base that grew, and
the second team is not. Neither outcome was obvious on day one, and — this is the part most write-ups
skip — **neither team can tell you the ratio between their two costs without measuring it.**

This lesson is about that decision: what generality actually costs inside a proof system, why the
multiplier everyone quotes is not a fact you may repeat, and what the shape of a problem has to look
like before descending to a hand-written circuit is the right call rather than an aesthetic one.

## The unit of cost is the constraint, and you already have the intuition

You have spent this curriculum's core tracks learning to read a contract's cost in gas: a number
fixed by the code and the state it touches, knowable before the transaction runs, and the thing every
optimisation argument ultimately resolves to. **A circuit's gas is its constraint count.**

The analogy is not decorative — the Noir documentation makes it explicitly, naming the circuit
equivalent of Solidity's "gas golfing" as "gate golfing": finding the lowest gate representation of
the compiled code.<Cite src="noir-docs-thinking-in-circuits" /> In its own words, gate count is the
significant cost, and depending on the proving system it is linearly proportionate to proof size and
proving time.<Cite src="noir-docs-thinking-in-circuits" />

Carry three consequences of that framing through the whole module:

- Cost is a property of the **code**, not of the input. There is no cheap path through a circuit the
  way there is a cheap path through an `if` in the EVM: a loop's gates are duplicated for every
  iteration, and an `if` is flattened so that gates exist for each path even though execution takes
  one.<Cite src="noir-docs-thinking-in-circuits" />
- The number is available **before you prove anything**, the way a gas estimate is available before
  you send. `nargo info` reports it from a compiled artifact; its documented output per circuit is
  the number of ACIR opcodes and the final number of gates a backend
  uses.<Cite src="noir-docs-nargo-commands" />
- Optimisation is arguing about that number, and any argument that does not move it did not happen.

Every remaining lesson in this module is either a way that number goes up, a way it comes down, or a
way you were about to read the wrong one.

## What generality costs

Recall from [zkVM versus circuit](/c/zkvm-vs-circuit) what a zkVM is doing. SP1's own description:
you define a function in Rust, compile it to a RISC-V ELF, and the system proves the execution of
that program.<Cite src="sp1-docs-what-is-a-zkvm" /> Behind that, a STARK encodes the computation as
algebraic constraints, FRI commits to them, long computations are split into chunks and proved
recursively, and a STARK-to-SNARK wrapping layer compresses the result into something small enough to
verify on an EVM chain.<Cite src="sp1-docs-what-is-a-zkvm" />

Look at what has to be true for that to work for *any* program. The constraint system cannot know
that your program is a Merkle membership check. It has to encode the semantics of a general-purpose
instruction set — every register, every memory access, every branch — because the next program it is
handed might use all of them. Your Merkle check is expressed as a sequence of RISC-V instructions,
and each instruction's correctness is what gets constrained. SP1 operates over the Baby Bear field
and supports recursive STARKs precisely so that long instruction traces can be split into chunks and
proved piecewise.<Cite src="sp1-docs-what-is-a-zkvm" />

A hand-written circuit skips the intermediary. There is no instruction to constrain, because there
are no instructions. The relation itself — *this leaf, hashed up this path, equals this root* — is
written directly as arithmetic over field elements — a Noir program compiles to a structure whose
leaves are `Field` inputs and whose nodes are the arithmetic gates combining
them<Cite src="noir-docs-thinking-in-circuits" /> — and the constraints are that arithmetic. You pay
for the hashes. You do not pay for a machine that could have been running something else.

That is the whole argument, and it is genuinely a large effect. The problem is what comes next.

## The multiplier you were about to quote

Circuit-versus-zkVM write-ups almost always carry a number: 10×, 100×, sometimes "up to 1000×". Do
not repeat it, including from this curriculum's own research notes, which flag it as unsupported.

The reason is not scepticism about the direction. It is that the quantity is not well defined until
you name four things: **which relation**, **which zkVM at which version**, **which backend and
proving scheme**, and **whether you are counting constraints, wall-clock, memory or dollars** — four
axes on which the answer moves independently. A ratio measured on SHA-256 does not transfer to a
Poseidon-based Merkle tree; a ratio measured in gates does not predict a ratio in seconds, because a
backend's gates are not uniformly priced; and a ratio measured against a zkVM without a precompile
for your hot operation reverses when the precompile lands.

What to do instead is the same move you would make for two candidate database indexes: build both at
toy scale and measure. Write the relation twice, once as a Noir circuit and once as a zkVM guest
program, and record four columns for each — backend gate count, proving wall-clock on named hardware,
peak memory, and on-chain verification gas. The circuit half of that table is cheap to produce,
because the gate count comes out of a compile, not a proving run,<Cite src="noir-docs-nargo-commands" />
and because the backend exposes it directly: `bb gates` constructs the circuit from bytecode, expands
black-box functions, and returns gate-count information without proving
anything.<Cite src="bb-docs-cli-reference" />
Two afternoons of measurement retires an argument that otherwise runs for a quarter.

## The zkVM's counter-move: precompiles

Before costing anything out, check whether the comparison has already been made for you.

zkVMs answer the circuit's structural advantage by importing it. SP1 ships built-in precompiles for
cryptographic operations, described in its own documentation as **specialised, highly optimised
circuits that execute directly within the zkVM**, reached through patched versions of popular Rust
crates so that ordinary-looking code routes through them.<Cite src="sp1-docs-precompiles" /> The
patched list read on 26 August 2026 covers `sha2`, `sha3`, `tiny-keccak`, `crypto-bigint`,
`curve25519-dalek`, `k256`, `p256`, `secp256k1`, `substrate-bn`, `bls12_381` and
`rsa`.<Cite src="sp1-docs-precompiles" />

Read that list against your relation. If the expensive part of your statement is a SHA-256 chain, a
secp256k1 signature check, or BN254 arithmetic, then the zkVM is not paying VM prices for it either —
it is running a hand-written circuit under a Rust-shaped API, and the gap you were counting on has
collapsed to the glue around it. If the expensive part is something not on the list — an unusual
hash, a domain-specific predicate, an algebraic structure nobody has patched — then the gap is real
and probably large.

<Aside kind="note">
**The general form of this check.** Any "specialised beats general" argument is really an argument
about whether the general system has already specialised the specific thing you care about. It is
the same question as asking whether your hot loop hits a SIMD intrinsic or a vendor BLAS call before
deciding to write assembly. The honest version of "should I hand-write this circuit" starts with
"has someone already hand-written the expensive 90% of it inside the VM I would otherwise use".
</Aside>

## The test: one page, one quarter

Strip away the cryptography and the decision has the shape of any build-versus-generalise call.
Two conditions, and both have to hold.

**Would the relation fit on one page?** Not the application — the *relation*. Merkle membership
against a root. One signature verification. A range proof on a committed value. A single
application-specific predicate: this bid is below this cap, this credential carries this attribute,
this state transition is one of four legal shapes. If describing what must be true takes more than a
page, every extra paragraph is constraints you will hand-write and hand-audit.

**Will it still be that relation next quarter?** A circuit is closer to a deployed contract than to a
service: changing it changes the verification key, which changes the deployed verifier, which
changes every artifact pinned to it. A relation still under product discussion is a relation you will
pay to re-derive.

If both hold and the thing is proved constantly — per user action, per transaction, per login — the
circuit is worth costing out. If either fails, the zkVM's overhead is almost certainly cheaper than
your engineering time, and this is the mainstream 2026 path for general computation, not a
compromise.<Cite src="succinct-sp1-docs" />

## What you are buying the saving with

Two costs, and the second is the one that ends projects.

**Expertise, priced honestly.** The next lesson is about a language whose syntax you already read
fluently and whose execution model you do not. That gap does not close in an afternoon, and it closes
by writing circuits and reading gate counts, not by reading about them.

**Blast radius.** This is the sharper cost. A soundness mistake in a bespoke circuit has no
VM-level constraint system standing behind it. In a zkVM, the thing being constrained is the
execution of a well-specified instruction set that many teams have attacked; your bug is a bug in
your Rust, and it produces a proof of the wrong-but-honestly-computed answer. In a hand-written
circuit, a missing constraint means the prover can choose the value, and the proof is of a statement
you did not intend to make. The verifier still returns true. Nothing anywhere reports an error.

That failure mode is not hypothetical at the ecosystem's own tooling layer. On 7 August 2026 Aztec
disclosed a critical vulnerability in the V5 Alpha proving system, identified on 27 July 2026 through
internal AI-assisted auditing: an attacker might construct a proof that passes verification for a
transaction the network should reject, producing a state transition outside the rules the system
intends to enforce.<Cite src="aztec-v5-proving-vulnerability" /> Read the sentence that follows it in
the disclosure, because it is the part that generalises: contributors could not determine whether
anyone had exploited the flaw, because the affected system lacks the information to distinguish
ordinary accepted transactions from transactions accepted through the flawed proving
path.<Cite src="aztec-v5-proving-vulnerability" />

That is a protocol-level proving system, not a small application circuit, and it was found by people
who build this tooling for a living during a phase explicitly designated for finding such things —
the disclosure says so in as many words.<Cite src="aztec-v5-proving-vulnerability" /> Take from it
the shape rather than the verdict: **soundness failures are silent, and they are silent backwards.**
An unsound circuit does not tell you it was exploited, and the history does not tell you either.
Budget for review accordingly, and treat the mandatory reflex — every value the prover supplies gets
a constraint — as the module's actual subject, which it is.

<Check type="explain" concept="when-circuit-wins">
Four features arrive in the same planning cycle. For each, say whether you would cost out a
hand-written circuit or reach for a zkVM, and name the single fact that decided it.

```text
A  Prove a user's off-chain KYC attestation contains "jurisdiction != X",
   without revealing the attestation. Runs on every withdrawal. Spec frozen.

B  Prove that an existing 4,000-line Rust risk engine produced a given
   margin number for a given position set. Engine ships changes weekly.

C  Prove a secp256k1 signature over a message, inside a larger statement,
   at high volume.

D  Prove the correct execution of a rollup's state transition function.
```

<Answer>
**A — circuit.** The deciding fact is that the spec is frozen and it runs per withdrawal. The
relation is one predicate over one attestation; it fits on a page, it is not moving, and the volume
is per-user-action. Both conditions hold, so the gate count is worth measuring against a zkVM guest
doing the same thing before committing.

**B — zkVM.** The deciding fact is *weekly*. Four thousand lines already exist in a general-purpose
language and change on a cadence faster than you could re-derive and re-audit a constraint system,
and every change would rotate a verification key. Program size alone would be an argument; the change
rate settles it.

**C — measure, and expect the zkVM to be closer than you think.** The deciding fact is that
`secp256k1` and `k256` are on SP1's patched-crate list, so the signature check inside the zkVM is
already running through a specialised circuit rather than through interpreted instruction
semantics.<Cite src="sp1-docs-precompiles" /> The structural advantage you were counting on has
mostly been imported. The residual question is what the surrounding statement costs, which is a
measurement, not an argument.

**D — zkVM, decisively.** The deciding fact is that the relation is an entire execution engine.
Nothing about it fits on a page, and proving general execution is the exact problem a zkVM
exists for.<Cite src="sp1-docs-what-is-a-zkvm" />

**The cross-cutting note.** In every one of the four, the honest first move is the same: get a
backend gate count from a compiled circuit and a proving measurement from a guest program on named
hardware, and put them in one table. The circuit half is cheap — the count falls out of a compile
rather than a proving run.<Cite src="noir-docs-nargo-commands" />
</Answer>
</Check>

## The state of the ground you are stepping onto

One framing correction before the next lesson, because it changes how you read everything else.

Noir is not "the ZK language". Its centre of gravity is application-specific circuits — identity and
attestation predicates, membership proofs, private voting, contracts on Aztec — while general
computation moved to zkVMs. Noir compiles to a backend-agnostic intermediate representation and is
proved by a backend — the documentation states the design goal as proving-backend agnosticism, with
Barretenberg given as the worked example<Cite src="noir-docs-quickstart" /> — and Barretenberg
describes itself as the cryptographic backend for Noir, taking ACIR, converting it to a circuit
representation, computing a witness and producing the proof.<Cite src="barretenberg-docs" />

And it has not shipped 1.0. Read on **26 August 2026**, the documentation's current versioned release
is `v1.0.0-beta.26`, with `v1.0.0-beta.25`, `.24` and `.23` still selectable alongside a `dev`
build,<Cite src="noir-docs-quickstart" /> while the GitHub release feed is dominated by dated
nightlies.<Cite src="noir-releases" /> No stable 1.0 date is committed anywhere this module could
find. That is not a reason to avoid it; it is a reason that every example you ship pins both the
compiler and the backend, which version independently.<Cite src="bb-docs-cli-reference" />

## What this unlocks

**Next lesson** puts you inside the language. Noir's syntax is Rust's on purpose, and the execution
model underneath is not imperative at all — an `if` costs both branches, a loop bounded at 1000 costs
1000 iterations regardless of the data, and the default numeric type wraps at a field modulus rather
than at a power of two. Every one of those is a sentence you now know how to price, because you know
what the unit is.

**In the decision you will actually face**, the transferable move is refusing the multiplier. When a
comparison arrives as a bare ratio, ask which relation, which version, which backend, which unit —
and if the answer is missing any of the four, the ratio is not a measurement of anything. That
discipline is what the rest of this module is built on: constraint counts you produce yourself,
against a circuit you wrote, on a version you pinned.
