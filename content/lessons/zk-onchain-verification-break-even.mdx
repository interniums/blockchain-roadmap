A proposal lands: replace the loop that checks a multisig's signatures with a single SNARK. One
proof instead of `n` `ecrecover` calls, constant verification instead of linear. The slide has the
usual pair of lines on it, one flat and one rising, crossing somewhere pleasing.

The lines are real. The crossing point is a number, and nobody on the slide has computed it. This
lesson computes it, then names the five things the crossing point leaves out — one of which turns
out to matter more than the gas.

## Two curves, both derivable

**Re-execution** costs gas proportional to the work. Every operation is metered; double the work and
you double the bill. There is no fixed overhead worth naming.

**Verification** costs a near-constant amount regardless of what was proved. You derived the
dominant part of that constant in the second lesson: `k = 768 / 192 = 4` pairs at EIP-1108's
`34,000 · k + 45,000` gives **181,000 gas** for the pairing check of any Groth16-on-BN254
verifier.<Cite src="eip-197" /><Cite src="eip-1108" /> The published community model puts the full
fixed cost at about **207,700**, with a per-public-input slope on top.<Cite src="orbiter-verifier-gas" />

That is the whole of the theory. A flat line at roughly 207,700 plus whatever your public inputs
cost, against a line through the origin with your workload's slope. Proofs win to the right of the
crossing, re-execution wins to the left, and the crossing point is arithmetic.

## Working the crossover: signature verification

**Subgoal one: price one unit of the work on-chain.** `ecrecover` is priced at 3,000 gas —
EIP-1108's own rationale uses exactly that figure as its fairness baseline, deriving the alt_bn128
schedule from it after measuring `ecrecover` at 116 microseconds per
invocation.<Cite src="eip-1108" />

The signature also has to arrive. A packed 65-byte signature at EIP-2028's 16 gas per non-zero
calldata byte is 1,040 gas.<Cite src="eip-2028" />

```text
per signature, on-chain  =  3,000 (ecrecover)  +  1,040 (calldata)  =  4,040 gas
```

**Subgoal two: price the proof side, floor included.** The verifier's fixed cost plus the proof's own
calldata — a Groth16 proof is 256 bytes, `A` and `C` in `G1` at 64 bytes each and `B` in `G2` at
128<Cite src="orbiter-verifier-gas" /> — plus one public input to bind whatever the signatures were
over:

```text
verifier fixed          ~207,700
proof calldata  256 × 16   4,096
one public input           7,160
                        --------
                        ~218,956 gas, flat in n
```

**Subgoal three: solve.**

```text
n* = 218,956 / 4,040 ≈ 54 signatures
```

Below about 54 signatures, the loop is cheaper. Above it, the proof is. That is the number the slide
was missing, and it is a large number — most multisigs never come close.

<Aside kind="note">
**Every input to that division is an assumption you should restate.** The 4,040 assumes packed
calldata with no zero bytes and no per-signature bookkeeping; real code does more. The 218,956
assumes one public input and the published Groth16 model, which is one 2024 community analysis of
snarkjs output rather than a specification.<Cite src="orbiter-verifier-gas" /> And the calldata terms
assume EIP-2028 per-byte pricing, which EIP-7623 has since put a floor
under for data-heavy transactions.<Cite src="eip-2028" /><Cite src="eip-7623" /> The *shape* — a flat
line crossing a rising one somewhere in the dozens — is robust. The 54 is an estimate whose job is
to be replaced by your own measurement.
</Aside>

### Generalising the division

The signature example is not special. Any workload made of repeated units gives you a crossover by
the same division: **fixed verification cost, divided by the gas of one unit.** Four units with
prices you can source, against the same ~219,000 floor:

Read down the last column. **The more expensive the unit, the sooner proving wins** — which is
obvious as arithmetic and startling as a design principle, because it means the workloads best
suited to proofs are the ones you would least expect to be doing in bulk. Ten cold storage writes is
a small function. A hundred cold reads is an ordinary view helper. Neither feels like "heavy
computation", and both sit near a crossover.

It also explains why the classic examples are what they are. Signature checking, curve arithmetic,
hashing in bulk: expensive per unit, done many times, no storage. The crossover for pure arithmetic
— an `ADD` at 3 gas — would be somewhere around 73,000 operations, which is why nobody proposes
proving addition.

<Aside kind="note">
**Where the asymmetry comes from.** The floor is not an implementation wart to be optimised away. It
is the shape of [prover-verifier asymmetry](/c/prover-verifier-asymmetry) showing up in a gas
schedule: the verifier's work is bounded below by the cryptographic operations that make succinctness
possible at all, and those operations do not get cheaper because your statement got smaller. A
constant-time verifier has a constant, and the constant is the price of admission.
</Aside>

## The five things the crossover leaves out

The gas crossover is necessary and nowhere near sufficient. Five corrections, roughly in order of how
often they change the decision.

### One: the prover's bill is not on the chart

Both lines are gas. Only one of the two designs also incurs a cost that never appears in a block
explorer: producing the proof. Proving is orders of magnitude more expensive than executing the
same computation, and it is paid in machine time, energy and hardware.

This is why the Ethereum Foundation had to express its real-time proving requirement as a **capital
and power budget** — a hardware capex ceiling and a kilowatt ceiling — rather than as anything
denominated in gas.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" /> There is no gas meter for a GPU cluster.

Academic cost modelling of proving infrastructure exists precisely because the gas number is not the
P&L.<Cite src="arxiv-cost-effective-zk-rollups" /> For a break-even decision the practical form of
the correction is: **the crossover you computed is a lower bound on the workload that justifies a
proof.** Adding prover cost moves it right, never left.

### Two: proving latency versus a loop that finishes now

The `ecrecover` loop completes inside the transaction. The proof has to exist first, which means a
prover ran, which takes wall-clock time you must design around: a pending state, a retry path, an
answer to what happens when the prover is down.

Published latencies for real proving workloads span from seconds to tens of minutes across different
systems, hardware and years, and the figures are not comparable to one
another.<Cite src="arxiv-zk-rollup-bottlenecks" /> The honest planning input is not a number, it is
a **structural fact**: a proof introduces an asynchronous step into a flow that did not have one.
Below the crossover you are paying gas *and* accepting that step. Above it you are buying the gas
saving with it.

### Three: complexity is a permanent cost

The loop is five lines of Solidity that a reviewer understands completely. The proof route adds a
circuit, a toolchain, a trusted setup or a universal one, a generated verifier, a binding layer, a
replay guard, and a proving service — each of which is a thing that can be wrong, and the first
lesson of this module was that the binding layer is where the money is lost.

There is no gas figure for this and it is frequently decisive. A 200,000-gas saving per transaction
at, say, a few dozen transactions a day is not obviously worth a new subsystem in the trust
boundary.

### Four: the floor is a policy variable

The 181,000 is a spec constant, and spec constants move. EIP-197's original schedule priced the same
four-pair check at `80,000 × 4 + 100,000 = 420,000` gas; EIP-1108 cut it to
181,000.<Cite src="eip-197" /><Cite src="eip-1108" /> One fork moved the floor by a factor of 2.3,
which moved every crossover in the ecosystem by the same factor, retroactively, with no application
changing a line of code.

So a break-even is not a property of your workload. It is a property of your workload **and the
current gas schedule**, and the schedule tracks how fast client implementations happen to
be.<Cite src="eip-1108" />

### Five: the data has not gone anywhere

The last one is the easiest to miss because it looks like a solved problem. A proof asserts that a
computation was performed correctly on some inputs. It says nothing about whether anyone can *see*
those inputs.

If the point of proving your 4,000 signatures was to avoid putting 4,000 signatures on-chain, ask
who else needs them. If nobody does — the signatures were only ever an internal check — you have
genuinely removed the data. If somebody does — a user reconstructing their position, an indexer,
a challenger, a regulator — then the data must still be published somewhere, and you have moved
cost from the execution meter to a data meter rather than eliminating it.

That distinction is the entire difference between a rollup and a validium, and you met it in the
scaling track as a [data availability](/c/data-availability) question rather than a cost one. Here
it shows up as a line item: the crossover arithmetic above priced 65 bytes of calldata per signature
on the re-execution side and nothing on the proof side, which is correct **only if the signatures
truly disappear**. If they have to be published anyway, add their cost back to both columns and the
crossover moves right again — possibly past any batch size you will ever have.

The general form: **before crediting a proof with a data saving, name the party who no longer needs
the data.** If you cannot name one, there is no saving to credit.

## Three regimes, not two

Framing this as a single crossover undersells what happens at the far end. There are three regimes,
and the third is why validity rollups exist.

**Below the floor.** The proved computation costs less gas than the verifier's fixed cost. Run it
on-chain. A proof here is strictly worse on every axis: more gas, more latency, more complexity, and
a prover bill on top. This regime is larger than people expect — anything under roughly 200,000 gas
of work, which is a great deal of ordinary contract logic.

**Between the floor and the transaction cap.** Both options are possible and the comparison is a
measurement. This is where the crossover arithmetic lives, and where the four corrections decide it.

**Above the transaction cap.** EIP-7825 enforces a protocol-level maximum of **16,777,216 gas — 2 to
the 24th — for any single transaction**, regardless of the block gas
limit.<Cite src="eip-7825" /> Work that exceeds it cannot be re-executed on L1 in one transaction at
any price. And an L2 batch representing thousands of transactions is far beyond it.

In the third regime the two curves have stopped competing, because one of them no longer exists.
Verifying a proof is not cheaper than re-executing a rollup batch on L1; it is the only way to
convince L1 of the batch's result at all. Ethereum's own roadmap material frames validity proving in
those terms — as a way to make verification tractable, not as a discount on
execution.<Cite src="ethereum-org-zkevm-roadmap" />

**Which means the honest sentence about proofs is neither "cheaper" nor "more expensive". It is
"constant".** Constant is a disaster below the floor, a judgement call in the middle, and a
superpower above the cap.

<Check type="explain" concept="verification-floor">
Two proposals arrive in the same week.

**Proposal A.** "Our NFT mint checks an allowlist with a Merkle proof — about 12 hashes, roughly
1,200 gas. Let's replace it with a ZK proof so the allowlist stays private."

**Proposal B.** "Our game settles a tournament round: 4,000 player moves, each a state update we
currently replay on-chain at about 6,000 gas. Let's prove the round instead."

Evaluate each with the framework, and say what you would need to measure before committing to
either.

<Answer>
**Proposal A is below the floor by two orders of magnitude, and the stated goal is not a gas goal.**

The Merkle check is ~1,200 gas. The verifier's fixed cost is around 207,700 before public
inputs.<Cite src="orbiter-verifier-gas" /> Replacing 1,200 gas of work with 200,000+ gas of
verification makes every mint about 170× more expensive on that line, adds a prover to the critical
path of a mint, and adds a circuit to the audit surface.

But notice they did not ask for a gas saving. They asked for **privacy** — the allowlist should not
be public. That is a real requirement and the floor does not refute it; it prices it. The answer is
"this costs roughly 200,000 gas per mint plus proving; is allowlist privacy worth that", which is a
product question with a number attached rather than an engineering objection. Cheaper alternatives
should be tried first: publishing only the root and distributing proofs off-chain already hides the
list from casual observers, though not from anyone who can guess-and-check candidate addresses
against the root.

**Proposal B is above the transaction cap, so the comparison is not close.**

```text
4,000 moves × 6,000 gas = 24,000,000 gas
```

That exceeds EIP-7825's per-transaction ceiling of 16,777,216 gas outright — the round **cannot** be
settled on-chain in one transaction at any gas price.<Cite src="eip-7825" /> Splitting it across
transactions is possible but reintroduces partial-settlement states and their failure modes.
Verification, meanwhile, is flat at roughly 208,000 gas plus public inputs, so the settlement cost
drops by something like two orders of magnitude and, more importantly, becomes *possible in one
transaction*.

**What to measure before committing to B.** Four things, none of them the gas.

1. *Proving time for a 4,000-move round on hardware you can actually rent*, and what the round's
   settlement promise becomes as a result. This is the term that decides whether the product still
   works.
2. *Proving cost per round in currency*, stated with its hardware and date, because a figure without
   those is not comparable to anything.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" />
3. *Where the move data lives.* The proof asserts a transition; anyone who needs to reconstruct the
   round still needs the moves. If they must be published, you have moved cost from execution to
   data rather than removing it.
4. *What happens when the prover is unavailable.* Re-execution has no liveness dependency; proving
   does. Name the fallback before shipping, not during the incident.

**The general rule the pair illustrates.** Ask which regime you are in before asking which design is
better. Below the floor, do not prove — unless you are buying a property other than cost, in which
case say which. Above the cap, the choice was never a choice. The interesting middle is exactly
where a measurement is worth doing, and it is narrower than the discourse suggests.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**Next lesson** takes the correction that kept recurring here — that the prover's bill is not on the
chart — and gives it its own accounting. A proving system has at least three cost lines, each
metered by a completely different mechanism, and optimising the one that shows up in a block
explorer while the other two move against you is a common and expensive mistake.

**In product decisions**, the transferable move is regime-first. Compute the fixed floor, compute
your workload, and see which of the three regimes you are in before opening the design argument. Two
of the three regimes have no argument in them.

**And when someone shows you the two-line chart**, ask for the crossing point in the units of your
workload. If they can produce it, the conversation is now about the four corrections, which is a good
conversation. If they cannot, the chart was a picture of a definition rather than an analysis of your
system.
