Ask what it costs to verify twenty Groth16 proofs on Ethereum and there are two correct answers,
separated by a factor of several. Which one you get depends on a property of the pairing precompile
that most people read past.

EIP-197 does not define a pairing evaluation. It defines a check over `k` pairs at once, where `k`
is the input length divided by 192, returning one when the combined product condition
holds.<Cite src="eip-197" /> And EIP-1108 prices it `34,000 · k + 45,000` — a **per-pair** term on
top of a **fixed base**.<Cite src="eip-1108" />

A per-pair price with a fixed base is an invitation. The repricing EIP said so at the time: among
its stated motivations was that cheaper elliptic-curve operations would help **batch multiple
zk-SNARK proofs together**, and would make it practical to split one monolithic circuit into several
smaller ones.<Cite src="eip-1108" /> Everything in this lesson is a way of accepting that
invitation.

## The naive cost, and the shape of the saving

Twenty separate `verifyProof` calls, each making its own four-pair check:

```text
20 × (34,000 × 4 + 45,000)  =  20 × 181,000  =  3,620,000 gas
```

Note where that goes. The `45,000` base is paid twenty times — 900,000 gas of pure repetition. And
of the eighty pairs, sixty are the *same three pairs* over and over, because `α`, `β`, `γ` and `δ`
are constants of the verification key and every proof in the batch was made against the same
circuit.

That is the redundancy batching removes.

### Deriving the batched count

**Subgoal: write the equation the verifier is checking.** For each proof `i`, Groth16 verification
is the four-pair product:

```text
e(-A_i, B_i) · e(α, β) · e(vk_x_i, γ) · e(C_i, δ)  =  1
```

where `vk_x_i` is the folding of proof `i`'s public inputs, and `α, β, γ, δ` come from the
verification key and are identical across every proof.<Cite src="orbiter-verifier-gas" />

**Subgoal: combine them with random coefficients.** Pick a random scalar `r_i` per proof and check
the product of all `N` equations, each raised to `r_i`. Bilinearity lets each exponent move inside a
pairing argument, so:

- `e(-A_i, B_i)^{r_i}` becomes `e(-r_i·A_i, B_i)`. The `B_i` differ per proof, so these **cannot**
  merge. **N pairs.**
- `e(α, β)^{Σ r_i}` becomes `e((Σ r_i)·α, β)`. **1 pair.**
- `e(vk_x_i, γ)^{r_i}` becomes `e(Σ r_i·vk_x_i, γ)`, because `γ` is shared. **1 pair.**
- `e(C_i, δ)^{r_i}` becomes `e(Σ r_i·C_i, δ)`, same reason. **1 pair.**

```text
pairs(N)  =  N + 3
gas       =  34,000 × (N + 3) + 45,000
```

At `N = 20`: `34,000 × 23 + 45,000 = 827,000` gas, against 3,620,000 unbatched. **About 41,000 gas
per proof of pairing cost instead of 181,000.**

<Aside kind="warn">
**That derivation has assumptions, and a different count circulates.** It assumes one shared
verification key, a pairing-based system with this specific equation shape, and the four-pair
snarkjs formulation.<Cite src="orbiter-verifier-gas" /> Figures of the form `N + 2` also circulate
in the ecosystem, presumably from a formulation that absorbs one term differently. The concept to
carry is not a constant: **the pairing count grows by roughly one per proof plus a small shared
remainder**, and the exact remainder is a property of your proof system. Re-derive it against the
precompile's own pricing rather than adopting anybody's per-proof figure — including this one.
</Aside>

### What the saving costs, in the same units

The pairing term is not the whole bill. Producing `r_i·A_i`, `r_i·C_i` and `r_i·vk_x_i` means three
`G1` scalar multiplications per proof plus accumulating additions. At EIP-1108 prices that is
`3 × 6,000` gas of `ECMUL` plus `ECADD` at 150 each — call it **around 18,500 gas per proof of extra
curve work** before any public-input folding.<Cite src="eip-1108" /><Cite src="eip-196" />

```text
per proof, batched   ≈  41,000 (pairing share)  +  ~18,500 (randomisation)  ≈  ~60,000
per proof, separate  ≈ 181,000 (pairing)        +  ~26,700 (fixed remainder) ≈ ~208,000
```

Roughly a 3.5× improvement rather than the 4.4× the pairing arithmetic alone suggests. That gap
between "the headline ratio" and "the ratio after the work you added" is exactly the gap vendor
material tends to omit, and it is why any published per-proof batch figure deserves re-derivation
before it enters a model.

Two readings. **Most of the benefit arrives early** — going from 1 to 10 cuts the per-proof pairing
cost by 73%, going from 10 to 100 cuts it by another 27%. And **the curve has an asymptote you can
name**: 34,000 gas, the per-pair price. No batch size ever gets below it, because every proof
contributes its own irreducible `e(-r_i·A_i, B_i)` pair. Add the randomisation floor and the real
asymptote is somewhere around 52,500.

That asymptote is the honest answer to "how cheap does batching get". Not zero. Roughly a quarter of
an unbatched verification, approached quickly and then never improved on.

### The soundness condition, and how to break it

Why is a random linear combination safe? Because a set of equations that does not all hold survives
a random combination only with negligible probability: a forged proof would have to be forged
*against the specific coefficients*, and it cannot be, if it was produced first.

Read that last clause again, because it is a deployable bug. **The coefficients must be
unpredictable to whoever produced the proofs.** If the submitter chooses `r_i`, or if they are
derived from a value the submitter controls, a batch of one valid and one invalid proof can be
crafted whose combination checks out. The standard fix is to derive the coefficients by hashing the
proofs and public inputs themselves — Fiat–Shamir over the whole batch — so the prover would have to
find their forgery before knowing what it must satisfy.

A batch verifier that takes the coefficients as calldata is the same class of mistake as a verifier
whose public inputs come from calldata. Same question, one lesson later: who chose this value?

The consequence of that misconception is operational. Batch failure is all-or-nothing: one bad proof
in a batch of a hundred reverts the batch and you learn nothing about which one. Production systems
either re-verify individually on failure — expensive, but only on the failure path — or make it the
submitter's problem by bonding them.

### Where batching stops working

Three requirements, and real systems break all three:

- **Same verification key.** Proofs of different circuits do not share `α, β, γ, δ`, so the three
  merged pairs stop merging and you are back to roughly `4N`.
- **Same curve.** A BN254 proof and a BLS12-381 proof cannot enter the same product; they are checked
  by different precompiles at different addresses, with different pricing
  formulas.<Cite src="eip-197" /><Cite src="eip-1108" />
- **Same proving system.** A Groth16 proof and a FRI-based STARK have no shared pairing equation to
  combine.

A single application proving one statement over and over is the ideal case. A general-purpose
service receiving proofs from unrelated systems is the worst one, and it is exactly the case the
aggregation industry exists to serve — by a different mechanism.

## Recursion: verify off-chain, settle once

The other route does not batch the pairing check; it removes the multiplicity before the chain sees
it. Verify `N` proofs *inside a circuit*, and produce one proof that the verification succeeded. The
chain verifies that one proof.

```text
on-chain gas  =  one verifier call  ≈  ~208,000 gas, whatever N is
per proof     =  ~208,000 / N  →  0 as N grows
```

You already met [recursion](/c/recursion) and [proof aggregation](/c/proof-aggregation) as
constructions. Here is what changes when they meet a settlement contract.

**Heterogeneity stops mattering.** The aggregating circuit can verify proofs of different circuits,
because a verifier is just a program and a circuit can express any program. That is precisely what
batching could not do, and it is why services that accept proofs from unrelated systems are built on
recursion rather than on a batched pairing
check.<Cite src="aligned-whitepaper" />

**Gas per proof genuinely approaches zero.** Not asymptotically-in-theory: the on-chain figure is a
constant divided by `N`.

**And two costs move off the gas sheet entirely.** The recursive verification work is real proving —
often the most expensive kind, since verifying a proof inside a circuit is a heavy statement. And a
proof must **wait for its batch**: whatever the cadence, the last transaction in a window sits until
the window closes.

| | Batched pairing check | Recursive aggregation |
|---|---|---|
| Where the work happens | On-chain, one precompile call | Off-chain, in a proving circuit |
| Gas per proof | Falls toward the per-pair price (~34,000) plus randomisation | Falls toward zero |
| Heterogeneous proofs | No — one verification key, one curve | Yes |
| Extra proving cost | None | Substantial, and invisible in gas |
| Latency added | None beyond assembling the batch | Aggregation cadence plus recursive proving |
| Failure granularity | All-or-nothing | All-or-nothing at the aggregate |

Neither is strictly better. A single application with one circuit and steady volume gets most of the
benefit from batching with none of the latency. A service handling unrelated proofs has no choice
but recursion.

<Aside kind="note">
**A cap you can hit.** EIP-7825 enforces a protocol-level maximum of 16,777,216 gas — 2 to the 24th
— for any single transaction, regardless of the block gas limit.<Cite src="eip-7825" /> At roughly
208,000 gas per separately-verified Groth16 proof that is about 80 proofs in one transaction before
the cap bites, and a batched check reaches far higher — but "far higher" is not "unbounded", and a
system that grows its batch size until something breaks will find this edge rather than the block
limit it was watching.
</Aside>

## Somebody else's batch: aggregation as a service

Once aggregation is the answer, a market appears between application provers and L1. Several
services collect proofs from different systems and settle them together, and choosing one is a
**security decision before it is a pricing decision**.

Aligned's own material distinguishes two modes, and the distinction is the one that matters. One
mode produces a proof that Ethereum itself verifies, so the result inherits Ethereum's security. The
other attests through a restaked validator set — a different and weaker assumption, and a faster,
cheaper one.<Cite src="aligned-whitepaper" /><Cite src="aligned-verification-vs-aggregation" /> Their
guidance is explicitly that the two modes suit different
situations.<Cite src="aligned-verification-vs-aggregation" /> These are shipped products described
by their own operators rather than research proposals; what this lesson does not do is repeat any
vendor's cost multiple, because those compare a chosen batch size against a chosen baseline and are
re-derivable from the precompile schedule in one line.<Cite src="eip-1108" />

Three questions to ask any such service, in this order:

1. **What does my finality rest on?** A proof Ethereum verifies, or a quorum of stakers? If the
   second, name the slashing condition and who can trigger it. This changes your product's trust
   story, not your gas bill — and it is the distinction the vendors themselves draw between their
   own two modes.<Cite src="aligned-whitepaper" /><Cite src="aligned-verification-vs-aggregation" />
2. **What latency does the cadence add?** Batching buys gas with waiting. The number that matters is
   the interval between settlements, not the prover's speed.
3. **What is the cost multiple, measured how?** Vendor multiples compare a chosen batch size against
   a chosen baseline. Re-derive from the precompile: at `34,000 · (N + 3) + 45,000` you can compute
   your own curve for your own `N` in one line, and any figure that disagrees with it by a lot is
   measuring something else.

### Aggregation redistributes cost as much as it reduces it

One thing gets lost when the discussion is framed as a gas multiple: the three costs land on
different people, and aggregation moves them.

**Gas** is paid by whoever sends the settling transaction. In an aggregated design that is the
aggregator, once, for everybody — so the individual user's marginal gas cost falls to a fee the
aggregator sets, which is a price, not a cost. Prices have margins.

**Latency** is paid by users, unevenly. The proof that arrives first in a window waits the longest.
If your product's promise is a p99 rather than an average, the batching cadence is now inside your
p99 and the worst case is the full window.

**Proving** is paid by whoever runs the recursion, and it is the term that grows: verifying proofs
inside a circuit is heavy work, and it scales with the batch it is collapsing. Academic work on
community proving treats prover provision and its cost as a first-class design problem rather than
an implementation detail — who supplies the machines, how they are paid, and what happens to
liveness when they stop.<Cite src="arxiv-crowdprove" /> Those questions do not appear anywhere in a
gas comparison, and for an aggregated system they are the operational
risk.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" />

The practical consequence: when someone shows you a 10× gas reduction, the correct follow-up is not
"is that number right". It is **"whose number is it now"** — because a cost that moved from your
transaction to a service's fee schedule has not necessarily gone down.

### The same idea, one layer down

The most interesting version of this is not a service at all. L2BEAT's native-rollups page, read on
**26 August 2026**, describes a design in which raw proofs travel in ephemeral sidecars alongside a
new L1 transaction type, Ethereum clients validate them through a *program-agnostic proof engine*,
and **recursive aggregation keeps verification efficient at scale** — inside the protocol, not on
top of it.<Cite src="l2beat-native-rollups" /> The same page frames native rollups as one use of a
broader primitive: letting any contract consume proofs already verified by Ethereum's consensus
infrastructure, with protocol-level multi-proof as a property that falls
out.<Cite src="l2beat-native-rollups" />

That is a proposal, described on a project page, and the last lesson of this module gives it the
scepticism it deserves. It is here because it tells you where the argument goes: if aggregation is
the answer to verification cost, the question is eventually whether it belongs in a contract, a
service, or the protocol.

A team runs an identity attestation service. Every user action submits one Groth16 proof against a
single circuit on BN254. They currently verify each separately and are choosing between batching
in their own contract and buying an aggregation service.

Their volume is about 40 proofs an hour, and their product promise is that an action is settled

Work the numbers and make the call.

<Answer>
**Step one: price the status quo.** Per proof, roughly 181,000 gas of pairing check derived from
EIP-197's `k = bytes / 192` and EIP-1108's `34,000·k + 45,000`, plus the rest of the
verifier.<Cite src="eip-197" /><Cite src="eip-1108" /> Call it ~208,000 gas per user action.

**Step two: price batching at a batch size the promise allows.** Two minutes is the constraint, so
at 40 proofs an hour a two-minute window holds about `40 / 30 ≈ 1.3` proofs. **The batch is
empty.** `34,000 × (1 + 3) + 45,000 = 181,000` — identical to verifying it alone, plus the
randomisation work you added. At this volume and this latency promise, batching is a net loss.

**Step three: notice which variable is binding.** Not the gas model, and not the vendor. The
product promise. To make a batch of 20 they would need to wait half an hour, which breaks the
promise; to keep the promise they can never fill a batch.

**Step four: the options that actually exist.**

- *Relax the promise for a subset.* If some actions tolerate an hourly settlement, batch those and
  verify the latency-sensitive ones alone. Two paths, two cost profiles, one circuit — batching
  works fine here because the verification key is shared.
- *Grow first.* At 40 proofs a minute rather than an hour, a two-minute window holds 80 and the
  arithmetic inverts completely: `34,000 × 83 + 45,000 = 2,867,000` for 80 proofs, about 36,000 each
  before randomisation. The optimisation is waiting for volume, not for engineering.
- *Buy a service only if it changes the shape.* An aggregation service does not help either: its
  batch has the same latency problem, and if it fills faster by pooling other customers' proofs, ask
  question one — whether settlement rests on a proof Ethereum verifies or on a restaked validator
  set, because at this size that is a bigger change to their product than the gas
  is.<Cite src="aligned-verification-vs-aggregation" />

**The transferable point.** Batch economics are a function of arrival rate against latency budget,
not of the gas model. The gas model tells you the shape of the curve; your traffic tells you where
on it you are sitting, and a team at 1.3 proofs per window is buying complexity for nothing.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**Next lesson** asks the question this one has been carefully not asking: whether to verify a proof
at all. Aggregation drives the per-proof cost down, but the *first* proof still costs a floor that
does not shrink, and below a certain amount of proved work paying that floor is simply worse than
doing the computation on-chain. You will find the crossover with arithmetic drawn from the same two
specifications.

**In vendor conversations**, you now have a one-line audit of any batching claim: `34,000 · (N + 3)
+ 45,000`, plus the curve work the randomisation costs, against `N` separate calls. A figure that
matches is a real measurement; a figure that beats it is measuring a different thing, and the
useful next question is which.

**And the security habit carries.** Batch soundness rests on coefficients the prover could not
anticipate, which is the same question as public-input binding wearing different clothes. In both
cases the exploit is not against the cryptography. It is against a value somebody let the wrong
party choose.
