A proving system does not have a cost. It has three, and they are measured by three different
instruments that do not talk to each other.

Each is denominated differently, each moves for its own reasons, and — the part that catches teams —
they are frequently paid by different parties. A change that improves one and worsens another is not
an optimisation until you have converted them into a common unit, and for the third line no such
conversion exists on-chain.

This lesson is about the accounting. What each meter measures, why the second one is separate on
purpose, and why the third had to be expressed in kilowatts.

## Line one: execution gas, and why it is the small one

You have now derived this line twice. A Groth16-on-BN254 verifier's pairing check is
`34,000 × 4 + 45,000 = 181,000` gas from EIP-197's `k = bytes / 192` and EIP-1108's
schedule,<Cite src="eip-197" /><Cite src="eip-1108" /> with the full fixed verifier put at around
207,700 by the published community model.<Cite src="orbiter-verifier-gas" />

Two properties matter for the P&L rather than for the design.

**It is amortised over the whole batch.** A rollup pays it once per settlement, not once per
transaction. Divide 207,700 gas across a few thousand L2 transactions and the per-transaction
verification cost is tens of gas — a rounding error next to what those transactions cost their users
on L2.

Make that concrete, because the amortisation is the whole reason this line is boring:

```text
207,700 gas  /  1,000 L2 transactions  =  ~208 gas per transaction
207,700 gas  /  5,000 L2 transactions  =   ~42 gas per transaction
```

Roughly the cost of two cold storage reads, spread across a user's entire experience of the chain.
Whatever is making L2 fees what they are, it is not this — and note that the amortisation improves
with traffic, so the argument for optimising it gets *weaker* exactly as a chain succeeds.

**And it is the only one of the three the EVM can see.** Which is exactly why it dominates the
discourse: it is the line a block explorer displays. Lines two and three are equally real and neither
appears in a transaction trace — one lives in a parallel fee market the EVM cannot read, the other in
an invoice from a cloud provider.

## Line two: blob gas is a separate market by design

EIP-4844 did not make data cheaper by discounting calldata. It introduced a **new resource with its
own accounting** — blob gas, with its own price, its own target and maximum per block, and its own
update rule — deliberately decoupled from execution gas so that the two do not compete for the same
meter.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

You know the mechanism from the protocol side: [blobs](/c/blob) carry data the EVM cannot read, and
only a versioned hash of the commitment is visible to contracts.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> The point
here is the *economic* consequence of that separation, which is easy to state and easy to forget:

**Your verification gas and your data cost can move in opposite directions on the same day, for
unrelated reasons.** An execution-gas spike driven by an NFT mint does not raise your data bill. A
blob-market squeeze driven by other rollups does not raise your verification bill. Two markets, two
demand curves, one invoice.

A rollup's L1 spend therefore splits between publishing data and verifying proofs, and since blob
transactions arrived it is the data side that has been the term that
moves.<Cite src="eip-4844" /><Cite src="arxiv-zk-rollup-bottlenecks" /> Verification gas has been a
comparatively minor line for most rollups since 2024.

**Do not take a ratio from that misconception and quote it.** The exact split varies per rollup and
per week with blob pricing, and this module deliberately publishes no proportion. The claim that
survives is directional: data moves, verification mostly does not. If you need a number, pull one
rollup's proof-submission address for a month and compute it — and label it with the month, because
next month's is different.

<Aside kind="warn">
**The word "cheap" hides which market you mean.** "Blobs made rollups cheap" is three claims stacked:
that the data resource is priced lower than calldata was, that it is priced *independently*, and that
the resulting total fell. The first two are structural properties of
EIP-4844.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> The third is an empirical claim about a particular period, and
periods end. Blob capacity and pricing have been adjusted repeatedly since, which means the third
claim needs a date attached every time it is made.
</Aside>

## Line three: the one with no meter

The third cost has no on-chain representation at all. No opcode, no precompile, no fee market. It is
GPU-hours, electricity, and the capital cost of the machines — and that absence is not an oversight,
it is a structural fact about where proving happens.

The clearest evidence is how the Ethereum Foundation had to specify its own requirement. Asked to
define what "real-time proving" means, it could not state a gas figure, because none exists. So the
target was written as a **capital ceiling and a power ceiling** — a bounded hardware spend and a
bounded kilowatt draw for the proving cluster — alongside a latency
target.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" /> A requirement expressed in dollars and watts, for a system
whose every other requirement is expressed in gas.

The Ethproofs board, built by the Ethereum Foundation, publishes cost and latency per proven block
with the cluster attached to each row,<Cite src="ethproofs" /> and its metrics page charts a **GPU
price index** alongside proof cost on the explicit reasoning that proving economics depend on GPU
rental costs, with the gap between the two lines being efficiency gained beyond cheaper
hardware.<Cite src="ethproofs-metrics" />

Sit with what that implies. **Your proving cost changes when a rental market moves, with no
change to your software.** A figure from eighteen months ago is not a stale version of today's; it is
a different quantity, against a different hardware market, on different silicon. There is no
equivalent phenomenon on lines one and two, where a price change requires a fork or a fee-market
update.

### Stating a cost so that it means something

Because the three lines are denominated differently, a bare figure is unusable. The [proving
cost](/c/proving-cost) discussion in the scaling track works through the published claims in detail;
the compressed rule is that **any cost claim about a proving system must name four things**:

| Field | Why it is load-bearing |
|---|---|
| Which line | Verification gas, data, or proving. Three different invoices |
| The unit and its denominator | Per proof, per transaction, per million gas — these are not convertible without knowing the workload |
| The workload | One L1 block is not one L2 batch is not one application proof |
| Hardware and date | Line three moves with a rental market; lines one and two move with forks |

Figures missing any of these circulate constantly and are not comparable to each other. A
per-proof dollar figure and a per-transaction cent figure describe different denominators; a latency
of "seconds" for one L1 block and a latency of "minutes" for a large batch describe different work.
Neither pair can be averaged, ranked or reconciled, and attempting it produces a confident number
with no referent. Ethproofs is unusual precisely because it ships cost with its cluster and date
already attached.<Cite src="ethproofs" /><Cite src="ethproofs-learn" />

### Three lines, three clocks

The reason a cost model for a proving system goes stale so fast is that its three terms are updated
by three different processes running at three different speeds.

| Line | Changes when | Cadence | Who decides |
|---|---|---|---|
| Verification gas | A fork reprices a precompile or opcode | Years | Ethereum's upgrade process |
| Data cost | The blob fee market clears | Every block | Aggregate demand across all rollups |
| Proving cost | Hardware generations and rental rates move | Continuous, no announcements | A market outside the ecosystem entirely |

The first is discontinuous and well-signposted: EIP-1108 cut the pairing check by a factor of 2.3 in
a single fork, and everybody had months of notice.<Cite src="eip-1108" /> The second is continuous
and observable — it is on-chain, with its own accounting, by
construction.<Cite src="eip-4844" /> The third is continuous and *unobservable from inside the
system*, which is why the Ethereum Foundation's own board plots a hardware price index next to it
rather than treating the cost as a property of the
software.<Cite src="ethproofs-metrics" /><Cite src="ethproofs" />

The practical consequence for anyone maintaining a model: **the three terms need re-checking on
different schedules.** Re-derive the verification term when a fork changes a relevant price, and
otherwise leave it alone. Re-read the data term whenever you quote it, because it was different last
week. And treat any proving figure older than a hardware generation as a historical record rather
than an estimate — including your own from last year.

## Why optimising one line misreads the P&L

Now the failure mode this lesson exists to prevent.

**Subgoal: trace a plausible optimisation through all three lines.** Take the public-input folding
from the second lesson — hash 32 signals into 1, saving roughly 222,000 gas of verifier cost at the
published slope.<Cite src="orbiter-verifier-gas" />

If the hash is cheap in your proving system, this is a straightforward win. If it is expensive —
a hash function your arithmetisation handles badly, multiplied by every proof you will ever
generate — the third line can swallow the first several times over, and **nothing in your gas
tooling will tell you.** You would have to measure proving time before and after, in seconds, and
convert at your cluster's hourly rate.

The general statement: **an optimisation that halves verification gas while tripling prover hardware
is a loss**, and the only way to see it is to price all three lines in the same currency yourself.
Academic cost modelling of proving infrastructure exists because the gas number is not the
P&L.<Cite src="arxiv-cost-effective-zk-rollups" />

### The three lines are paid by different people

The final complication, and the one that turns an accounting problem into a governance problem.

- **Verification gas** is paid by whoever sends the settling transaction — typically the operator,
  from sequencer revenue.
- **Data cost** is paid by the same party, out of the same revenue, but priced by a market they do
  not control.
- **Proving cost** is paid by whoever runs the prover, which may be the operator, a contracted
  service, or in some designs a permissionless set of participants. Work on community proving treats
  exactly this as a design problem: who supplies the machines, how they are compensated, and what
  happens to liveness when they stop.<Cite src="arxiv-crowdprove" />

When the three payers are the same entity, the optimisation question is arithmetic. When they are
not, it becomes an incentive question — an operator optimising the line *they* pay may push cost onto
a prover set that then under-provisions, and the failure shows up as latency or as a stalled chain
rather than as a bigger invoice.<Cite src="arxiv-crowdprove" />

Q3 win: reduced L1 verification gas by 38% via verifier optimisation.
        Estimated annual saving: significant.

Write the three questions that turn it into a claim about the business, and say what you expect each

<Answer>
**Question one: what fraction of our total L1 spend was verification before the change?**

Expected answer: a small one. For most rollups since blobs, data publication is the term that moves
and verification gas is comparatively minor.<Cite src="eip-4844" /><Cite src="arxiv-zk-rollup-bottlenecks" />
A 38% cut to a small line is a small saving, and the sentence "reduced verification gas by 38%" is
constructed so that this does not have to be said. Ask for the two lines side by side, in the same
currency, for the same month. Do not accept a proportion from a slide — the split varies per rollup
and per week with blob pricing, so it has to be computed from our own settlement transactions.

**Question two: what did it do to proving time?**

Expected answer: nobody measured, because there is no meter. Verifier optimisations very often work
by moving computation into the circuit, where it is paid by the prover in seconds and GPU-hours on
every proof rather than in gas once per settlement.<Cite src="arxiv-cost-effective-zk-rollups" /> The
concrete ask is a before-and-after proving time on the same cluster for the same workload. If it went
up, convert it at the cluster's hourly rate and subtract; the answer may be negative.

**Question three: is the saving denominated in gas or in currency, and at what gas price?**

Expected answer: gas. A 38% gas reduction is a real technical result, but the annual *saving* depends
on base fee over the year, which is not ours to predict. Meanwhile the third cost line moves with a
GPU rental market — Ethproofs charts a GPU price index beside proof cost for exactly that
reason<Cite src="ethproofs-metrics" /> — so a currency figure for one line and a gas figure for
another cannot be added.

**What the rewritten line should say.** Something with all four fields attached: verification gas per
settlement, before and after, at a stated public-input count; the resulting share of L1 spend for a
named month; proving time before and after on named hardware; and the net, in one currency, with the
gas price and GPU rate it assumed. That version is three sentences longer and is the first version
anyone could act on.

## What this unlocks

**The final lesson** leaves cost behind entirely and asks what the verifier actually guarantees. You
now know what it costs to run, what it costs to batch, when it is worth running at all, and how it
sits inside a three-line P&L. What remains is the question none of that touches: who is allowed to
replace it, and what the proof is worth to a user once you have answered that.

**In planning**, carry the three-column table. Any proposal that touches proving should be evaluated
by writing its effect on all three lines, with "unknown" written explicitly where nobody measured.
The blanks are the finding.

**And the habit generalises past this topic.** Whenever a system's cost is metered by more than one
mechanism — compute against storage, bandwidth against requests, gas against a fee market against a
hardware bill — the optimisation that looks best is usually the one on the meter you can read. That
is a property of the instrument, not of the system.
