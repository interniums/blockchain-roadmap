The clearest statement of this lesson's problem is in the Motivation section of an EIP, written by
an L2BEAT researcher and an Ethereum Foundation researcher. Their argument for changing the
protocol is that today's arrangement — every EVM-equivalent rollup implementing and maintaining its
own proof system to replicate what Ethereum already does — significantly increases the probability
of bugs, and **prevents projects from getting rid of security councils and from moving to Stage
2**.<Cite src="eip-8079" />

Read that clause again. It is a specification document conceding that the verifier contract, the
whole cryptographic apparatus of this module, does not by itself get a rollup out of needing a
committee with an override key.

This lesson is about why, and about what is being proposed to change it. It is deliberately short on
the ladder itself — you met the [stage framework](/c/l2beat-stage-0) and the
[key that overrides the proof](/c/upgrade-key-risk) in the scaling track. What is new here is the
precise logical boundary, and the fact that the most credible proposal to move it is, as of today, a
research programme with no fork attached.

## The precedence relation, in one line

A verifier is a deployed contract. The consuming contract reaches it through an address. That
address is either immutable in bytecode or it is a variable — in storage, behind a proxy, or both.

If it is a variable, then whoever can write it can substitute a contract that returns `true`
unconditionally. Nothing about the substitution is detectable by the proof: the proof was never
about the verifier, only about the statement. So:

The intersection is bounded by its weakest term, and the third term is not cryptography. It is a
multisig, a timelock, a council, a governance process — a set of people and the delay before they
can act.

**A verifier contract is necessary for trustlessness and nowhere near sufficient.** That sentence
does the work, and it is worth being precise about the "necessary" half too: without a verifier, the
proof establishes nothing on-chain at all. The apparatus is not decorative. It is simply not
self-sufficient.

<Aside kind="warn">
**And the override capability is load-bearing for safety.** This is what makes the situation a
dilemma rather than a scandal. Emergency verifier replacement is how a live system survives a bug in
its prover or its circuit — the capability gets used, correctly, by competent teams responding to
real defects. The same key that can rescue the chain from a soundness bug can steal from it. You
cannot have the first without the second, and no amount of process design makes that go away; it only
adds delay.
</Aside>

## What the stage framework does and does not measure

The independent framework that grades this exists and you should read the live version rather than a
remembered snapshot, because projects move. Two things about it are worth stating precisely, because
both are commonly mis-cited.

**It grades control, not safety.** The framework's own explainer, last updated 23 July 2025, says
outright that it discusses maturity "in terms of decentralization, and not in terms of security", and
that it is very easy for a team to deploy a **very unsafe but highly-staged project** — because the
proof system might be experimental, or the contracts unaudited.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" /> Stage 0
means fully controlled by a few entities; Stage 2 means fully controlled by
code.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" /> A high stage is not a safety certificate, and a low stage is not an
accusation.

**The two risks trade off against each other.** The same explainer notes that given some perceived
probability of bugs and some perceived probability of actors being compromised, you can compute which
stage gives the best security — and that decentralising is right precisely when permissioned-operator
risk exceeds bug risk.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" /> That is a genuinely useful framing for a product
decision, and it explains why a young system with a new prover *should* keep an override, and why
keeping one indefinitely stops being justifiable.

The practical instruction: **check the live classification when you need it.** Stage assignments are
a moving target maintained against changing contracts, and any lesson that printed a table of who
sits where would be teaching a snapshot.<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" />

### The thing to actually look up

For any proof-consuming system you are trusting with money, four questions, all answerable from a
block explorer in twenty minutes:

1. **Is the verifier address fixed or replaceable?** Read the consuming contract's storage layout.
2. **Who can replace it?** Name the owner: an EOA, a multisig with a threshold, a governance
   contract. Get the threshold.
3. **After what delay?** A timelock in seconds. Zero is a real and common answer.
4. **What can a user do inside that delay?** This is the exit window, and it is the only thing
   standing between a hostile upgrade and your funds.

Question four is where EIP-8079 makes an observation that is easy to miss and hard to unsee. Because
rollups run bespoke governance that cannot be forced to follow Ethereum's decisions, the best an
EVM-equivalent rollup can do is offer a long exit window — and **no finite, reasonable exit window
protects all use cases**, specifically those relying on timelocks: governance contracts, staking
contracts, vesting contracts.<Cite src="eip-8079" />

That is a structural limit, not an engineering shortfall. If your contract's whole purpose is to
lock funds for two years, no seven-day or thirty-day window helps you. Longer windows protect more
use cases at the cost of taking longer to ship any change at all.<Cite src="eip-8079" />

## The structural proposal: stop building bespoke verifiers

If the problem is that every rollup maintains its own proof system and its own verifier, one answer
is to stop needing them. That is the native-rollup idea, and reading it properly means reading two
artefacts that currently say different things — which turns out to be the most useful fact about it.

### What the EIP specifies

EIP-8079, *Native rollups*, is a **Draft** Standards Track Core EIP created 13 November 2025, authored
by Luca Donno of L2BEAT and Justin Drake of the Ethereum
Foundation.<Cite src="eip-8079" /> Its proposal: expose Ethereum's state transition function to the
execution layer through a new `EXECUTE` precompile that verifies a state transition against provided
inputs.<Cite src="eip-8079" />

The specified logic is short. Take a chain, a block and an anchor; reject any blob-carrying
transaction inside the block; perform anchoring by processing a system transaction to a fixed anchor
address, which is how L1-to-L2 messaging works; then run the state
transition.<Cite src="eip-8079" /> Alongside it, the block header gains a field for fees burned, so a
rollup can choose to collect its base fee rather than burn it.<Cite src="eip-8079" />

The design keeps rollups programmable where it matters. The rationale spells out that a rollup can
restrict sequencing to a permissioned entity for fast preconfirmations, or leave it open, or restrict
it to a staked network; can fix the coinbase to a DAO treasury; can tweak gas limits — all through
its own smart contract, around L1's shared execution
rules.<Cite src="eip-8079" /><Cite src="l2beat-native-rollups" /> What it gives up is customisation
*inside* the exposed state transition: no custom opcodes, no custom precompiles, no custom
transaction types.<Cite src="eip-8079" />

**Now read the document's unfinished sections, because they are the honest status.** `PROOF_TX_TYPE`,
the precompile address and the anchor address are all TBD. "Proof-carrying transactions" reads: TBD,
heavily dependent on the ZK L1 design. Test Cases, Reference Implementation and Security
Considerations are TBD. And a rationale subsection titled **"Re-execution vs ZK proofs" contains the
single word TBD**.<Cite src="eip-8079" />

That last one matters for a specific reason. Summaries of this proposal disagree about whether
`EXECUTE` means literal L1 re-execution or an enshrined interface eventually discharged by proofs.
The disagreement is not a failure of the summarisers. **The EIP has a section for exactly that
question and has not filled it in.**

### What the research has done since

The project's own tracking page, read on **26 August 2026**, resolves the puzzle by showing that both
readings were correct at different times. Its timeline records: the founding research post in January
2025 introducing the `EXECUTE` precompile; EIP-8079 in November 2025 formalising **the original
re-execution path**; a March 2026 proof-of-concept from the ethrex and LambdaClass teams demonstrating
EIP-8079 **via L1 re-execution**, explicitly described as validating the contract and messaging model
while being a prototype rather than the target architecture; and then, in May 2026, a
program-agnostic design that **replaces `EXECUTE` in the ZK path** with proof-carrying transactions
and consensus-layer verification usable by any ZK
application.<Cite src="l2beat-native-rollups" />

So the answer to "is it re-execution or proofs" is: it was re-execution, it was prototyped as
re-execution, and the research has since moved to proofs, while the draft EIP still describes the
earlier design. Two artefacts, one moving target, and the correct citation depends on which you mean.

The page also states the status in a sentence that should end every conversation about timing:
**native rollups are not part of any scheduled Ethereum hard fork** — EIP-8079 remains a draft, the
consensus-layer proof feature it would build on is experimental, and proof-carrying transactions are
still a research proposal.<Cite src="l2beat-native-rollups" /> Its own future milestones are labelled
targets rather than fork commitments.<Cite src="l2beat-native-rollups" />

Cross-check that against what *is* scheduled. The next major upgrade after Fusaka is Glamsterdam,
anchored on enshrined proposer-builder separation and block-level access
lists;<Cite src="eipsinsight-upgrades" /><Cite src="eip-7732" /><Cite src="eip-7928" /> the
censorship-resistance work under EIP-7805 sits in the upgrade *after*
that.<Cite src="eipsinsight-upgrades" /><Cite src="eip-7805" /> Glamsterdam has not
activated,<Cite src="eipsinsight-upgrades" /> and this lesson prints no date for it, because the
dates that circulate for unshipped upgrades are projections that have already moved more than once.
Nothing in either scope is native rollups.

### The list of things that do not exist yet

One more artefact on that page deserves reading in full, because it is the antidote to every
"native rollups are almost here" summary. Under the heading of what Ethereum still needs, the project
lists the unfinished L1 components and unresolved protocol choices the design depends
on:<Cite src="l2beat-native-rollups" />

- a **new L1 transaction type** carrying program and backend identities plus a commitment to public
  values, exposed through dedicated EVM opcodes;
- a **program-agnostic proof engine** in the clients, generalising the experimental consensus-layer
  proof feature so arbitrary guest programs can be verified rather than only L1 execution proofs;
- **L1 proof recursion and aggregation**, so that folding proof sidecars into a block proof avoids one
  extra validator-side verification per rollup update;
- **block data availability** binding L2 transactions and block access lists to data Ethereum makes
  available;
- **proof economics and resource limits** — pricing, size bounds, backend diversity rules, and
  protection against excessive builder and client workload;
- **stable program identity**, so custom guest programs keep an identifier across zkVM patches.

Six items, each of which is an unfinished protocol design rather than an implementation task, and
one of which — proof aggregation and its pricing — is the subject of the third lesson of this module
relocated into the consensus layer.<Cite src="l2beat-native-rollups" /> That is not a critique of the
programme. It is what a research programme looks like when it is being described honestly, and the
contrast with how the same programme is summarised elsewhere is the lesson.

### What would still need trusting

Suppose it all lands. Ethereum verifies the state transition against its own execution rules; the
rollup no longer deploys a verifier, a proof router, or circuit upgrades of its
own.<Cite src="l2beat-native-rollups" /> What is left in the trust inventory?

- **The settlement contract**, which is still the rollup's own code and still decides what it accepts.
  Native rollups keep governance programmable by design — that is a feature, and a feature is a
  surface.<Cite src="eip-8079" />
- **The sequencer**, unchanged. Ordering, censorship and preconfirmation trust are orthogonal to how
  the state root is checked.
- **Data availability**, unchanged. A verified transition over data nobody can retrieve is still
  useless, and the project's own list of unfinished components includes binding L2 transaction data
  to something Ethereum makes available.<Cite src="l2beat-native-rollups" />
- **Whatever governs upgrades to the rollup contract itself.** Removing the bespoke verifier removes
  one upgrade surface. It does not remove the concept.

The improvement is real and it is narrower than the headline: it deletes an entire category of
per-project cryptographic infrastructure and the governance that surrounds it, which is precisely the
category the EIP's Motivation blames for keeping projects on security
councils.<Cite src="eip-8079" /> It does not delete governance.

<Check type="explain" concept="verifier-upgradeability-risk">
A partner sends you a one-paragraph security summary for a chain they want you to integrate:

```text
Our L2 is secured by zero-knowledge validity proofs verified by an
immutable smart contract on Ethereum mainnet. Every state root is
cryptographically proven. There is no trusted operator.
```

Say what you would verify, in what order, and what each possible answer would mean.

<Answer>
**Start with the word "immutable", because it is the only falsifiable claim in the paragraph.**

*Check one: is the verifier reachable through a proxy?* An immutable verifier that is called through
a mutable address is not immutable in any sense that protects a user. Read the consuming contract:
is the verifier a constant in bytecode, or a storage variable? If it is a variable, ask questions two
and three. If it is genuinely a constant, ask whether the *consuming* contract is behind a proxy,
which achieves the same thing one level up.

*Check two: who holds the upgrade authority, and after what delay?* Name it — an EOA, an n-of-m with
its threshold, a governance contract — and get the timelock in seconds. Zero delay means the
guarantee is "these keys are honest right now". A long delay means "these keys are honest, or I have
this long to leave".

*Check three: can I actually leave inside the delay?* This is the exit window, and it depends on
forced-inclusion paths and on the bridge working while the upgrade is pending. And note the limit
EIP-8079 identifies: no finite, reasonable exit window protects use cases that rely on timelocks —
governance, staking and vesting contracts cannot exit in
seven days.<Cite src="eip-8079" /> If our integration locks funds, the exit window may not be
available to us at all.

*Check four: what does the independent classification say, today?* The stage framework grades exactly
this and is maintained live.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" /><Cite src="l2beat-glossary" /> Two cautions
when reading it: it measures decentralisation rather than security, and a project can be unsafe and
highly staged or careful and Stage 0.<Cite src="l2beat-stages" />

**On "there is no trusted operator".** Even with a genuinely immutable verifier, the sequencer still
orders transactions, the bridge still holds escrow, and the data still has to be published and
retrievable. None of those is inside the statement the proof establishes.

**What I would send back.** Not a rejection — most live systems answer these questions
imperfectly and are still worth integrating with. A rewritten paragraph: the verifier's address and
whether it is replaceable, the upgrade authority and its threshold, the delay in seconds, the exit
path, and the current independent stage with the date I read it. That version is checkable. Theirs
is a claim about cryptography being used to describe a governance arrangement.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**The next module** changes the subject entirely. Everything in these six lessons used *succinctness*
— proofs as a way to make verification cheap, with the hiding property incidental or absent. The
privacy module is the one place the zero-knowledge property itself is what the system is for, and
almost every lesson in it has the same shape as this one: a cryptographic mechanism that works
perfectly, wrapped in a system property that decides whether it delivers anything.

**In diligence**, the four questions are now a checklist you can run in twenty minutes on any
proof-consuming system, and the answers are public. The reason people do not run it is not difficulty.
It is that the proof is interesting and the address list is not.

**And the reading discipline generalises.** When a specification and a project page disagree, that is
not noise to be averaged — it is usually a design that moved, and the gap between the two documents
is the most informative thing available. Date both, cite both, and say which one describes the thing
you are actually relying on.
