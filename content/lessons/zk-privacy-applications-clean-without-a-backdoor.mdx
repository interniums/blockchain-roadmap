A counterparty asks where the money came from.

The two obvious answers are both bad. **Surrender your history** — hand over a key, let them read
everything, accept that they now hold a permanent record of every payment you have ever made,
including all the ones they had no business seeing. Or **refuse**, and lose the counterparty, because
they have obligations and "trust me" does not discharge them.

The interesting claim of this area is that there is a third answer: prove the specific thing they
need to know, and nothing else. This lesson works through what that looks like in deployed systems,
what it genuinely delivers, and where the argument for it stops being cryptography and becomes a
claim about incentives that is not yet settled.

The material here is contested and carries real legal weight. The approach throughout is to describe
mechanisms and name trade-offs, and to be explicit where a claim is a vendor's, a researcher's
argument, or nobody's.

## Viewing keys: read without spend

The simplest form of disclosure separates two capabilities that ordinary blockchain keys bundle
together. A **spending key** authorises action. A **viewing key** authorises reading. Hand someone
the second and they can decrypt your history; they gain no ability to move anything.

Railgun implements this concretely and the engineering detail explains itself. Spending keys are
generated on the Baby Jubjub curve — chosen because it is SNARK-friendly, since verifying proofs
on-chain requires embedding a curve inside the circuit to keep the work small enough for a block.
Viewing keys use Ed25519 instead, because no circuit is involved in viewing and a different scheme is
more efficient there. Both public halves are encoded in the user's private address, which is why
those addresses are longer than ordinary ones. A viewing key holder can decrypt the encoded
interaction information and scan contract events to see what was sent and received — and cannot send
anything.<Cite src="railgun-docs-wallets-keys" />

Aztec separates the same capabilities as part of a broader key architecture: nullifier keys for
spending private state, incoming viewing keys for decrypting received notes, and an
application-defined signing key for authorisation. The documentation lists **selective disclosure** —
sharing viewing access without compromising spending ability — as an explicit design goal of the
separation, alongside damage limitation when a key is
compromised.<Cite src="aztec-docs-keys" /> The protocol-managed keys, including the viewing key,
**cannot be changed once an account is created**; only the signing key is
rotatable.<Cite src="aztec-docs-keys" />

### Two properties that make viewing keys a poor compliance answer

**They are all-or-nothing.** A viewing key reveals everything it covers. There is no way to say "show
this counterparty the three payments that concern them" — you show them the account. That is a
different disclosure from the one you were asked for, and it is strictly larger.

**And they may not be revocable.** This is where the documentation deserves careful reading rather
than summarising. Railgun's page describes viewing keys in one paragraph as something a user can
define a beginning and ending block for, in a scoped way — a tax year, for example. A note further
down the same page states that once a viewing key is generated it is **irrevocable**, meaning whoever
holds it can see private interactions forever, and that an **upcoming update** will allow viewing
keys to be scoped by block number.<Cite src="railgun-docs-wallets-keys" />

Those two statements describe different products. The page carries a last-updated stamp roughly a
year old, so the most likely explanation is documentation that ran ahead of, or behind, an
implementation. **The safe reading is the one that costs you least if wrong: assume a viewing key you
hand over is permanent and unscoped, and verify block-scoping against the current implementation
before designing a product around it.**

That is not a criticism of the project — self-contradictory documentation is ordinary, and the note
disclosing the limitation is more than many products provide. It is an illustration of the discipline
this whole area demands: a compliance design built on a feature you read about in one paragraph, and
that another paragraph calls unreleased, is a design built on nothing.

## Selective disclosure: prove the predicate

The alternative inverts the transaction. Rather than surrendering a history so the other party can
evaluate it, **you evaluate it yourself and prove the conclusion.**

`P` can be any statement a circuit can express: that funds did not originate from a listed source,
that a threshold was not exceeded, that tax owed was paid, that the holder is over an age. Nobody
holds a master key, the disclosure is initiated by the user, and its scope is exactly the predicate.

That inversion is what makes the usual framing of this debate wrong. The debate is normally posed as
privacy *versus* compliance, with a backdoor as the only bridge. Selective disclosure is a third
option that is neither.<Cite src="privacy-pools-paper" />

Two deployed shapes exist, and they are duals of each other.

## Shape one: prove membership in a curated good set

The Privacy Pools design has a user prove membership not in the full deposit set but in a chosen
**subset** of deposits, curated by an Association Set Provider. The proof still hides which deposit is
theirs within that subset, while establishing that their funds came from inside
it.<Cite src="privacy-pools-paper" />

The deployed implementation makes the shape unusually legible, because the circuit's signals are
published. Recall the withdrawal circuit's public inputs: `withdrawnValue`, `stateRoot`,
`stateTreeDepth`, **`ASPRoot`**, **`ASPTreeDepth`**, `context`. And its private inputs include both
`stateSiblings` with `stateIndex` and **`ASPSiblings` with `ASPIndex`**. Two of its six constraints
are "validates existing commitment in the state tree" and "verifies label inclusion in the ASP
tree".<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-withdrawal-circuit" />

**Subgoal: read what that dual membership buys.** One proof asserts two facts simultaneously —
*I own an unspent note in the pool* and *my deposit's label is in the approved set* — while revealing
the position in neither tree. The compliance claim and the ownership claim are established by the
same proof, and neither leaks an index.

The infrastructure around it is a layer with its own responsibilities: it manages the list of
approved deposit labels, provides the inclusion proofs withdrawals need, enables label revocation,
and determines withdrawal eligibility.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-asp-layer" /> Root updates are
performed only by authorised accounts, each update carrying a Merkle root of approved labels, a hash
referencing off-chain data, and a timestamp; a history of recent roots is kept in a circular buffer
for proof verification, with the latest root used for
validation.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-asp-layer" /> A failed validation triggers the ragequit
option — the public exit path from the previous
lesson.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-asp-layer" /><Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-core-concepts" />

## Shape two: prove non-membership in a curated bad set

Railgun's partner project takes the complement. Rather than proving you are in an approved set, you
prove your funds are **not** part of a preset list of interactions and wallets deemed illegitimate.
The documentation describes the mechanism: on a shield — value entering the private system — a proof
is automatically created establishing that the tokens are not part of that list, in a process
described as end-to-end encrypted, using only publicly available on-chain data and requiring no
personal information from the user.<Cite src="railgun-docs-ppoi" />

Three details make this concrete rather than aspirational.

**The lists have named providers.** The documentation names Elliptic, ScamSniffer, PureFi, SlowMist
and a Chainalysis sanctions oracle as list providers.<Cite src="railgun-docs-ppoi" /> Naming them is
useful in both directions: it tells you the compliance data has real commercial provenance, and it
tells you exactly which private companies now sit inside the trust boundary.

**Users select lists by jurisdiction.** The stated design lets input data be adjusted to a user's
risk profile and local jurisdiction — European users choosing EU-relevant lists, US users choosing US
ones.<Cite src="railgun-docs-ppoi" /> That is a sensible product decision and it has an awkward
consequence discussed below: if "clean" is list-dependent, it is not one property.

**It sits alongside the protocol rather than inside it.** The documentation states the system is
separate from the core smart contracts, is open-source infrastructure other protocols can use, does
not change how the underlying spending system works, and gives no third party access into the privacy
system.<Cite src="railgun-docs-ppoi" /> Those are the vendor's characterisations of a partner
project, and they are the claims a diligence process would want to verify independently rather than
adopt.

The row that matters is the last. **Both shapes need somebody to decide what goes on the list**, and
nothing in the cryptography constrains that decision. The proof is honest about exactly what it
proves: membership, or non-membership, in a set *somebody else assembled*. That is the subject of the
next lesson, and it is the single most under-stated fact in this area.

### What the proof is actually proving

It is worth being exact here, because the phrase "prove your funds are clean" invites a
misunderstanding that the mechanism cannot support.

A circuit cannot determine that funds are legitimate. Legitimacy is not a computable property of a
transaction graph; it is a judgement about people and intent. What a circuit *can* do is check a
Merkle path — establishing that a label sits inside a tree whose root somebody
published.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-withdrawal-circuit" />

So the honest reading of an association proof is:

```text
not:  "these funds are clean"
but:  "a party with a name published a root at a timestamp,
       and my deposit's label is inside the tree it commits to"
```

Everything load-bearing about the claim happened before the circuit ran, off-chain, in whatever
process produced that root. The cryptography guarantees the *faithful transmission* of somebody's
judgement, with a very strong integrity property and zero epistemic content of its own.

That is not a weakness of the design — it is the correct division of labour, and it is exactly why
the architecture publishes a root plus a reference to off-chain data rather than trying to encode
policy in constraints.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-asp-layer" /> But it does determine where you
should look when assessing such a system. **The interesting questions are all upstream of the
proof:** who assembled the set, on what data, with what appeals process, and how recently.

The same reading applies to the complement. A proof of non-membership establishes that your funds do
not appear in lists assembled by named commercial providers — Elliptic, ScamSniffer, PureFi, SlowMist
and a Chainalysis sanctions oracle among
them.<Cite src="railgun-docs-ppoi" /> That is a meaningful, checkable, falsifiable statement about
those lists. It is not a statement about the world.

### The compliance root is a liveness dependency

One operational consequence follows from the architecture and is easy to miss when reading the
cryptography.

A withdrawal in this design requires a **current** compliance root: updates arrive from authorised
accounts, each carrying a root, a reference to off-chain data and a timestamp; a history of recent
roots is retained in a circular buffer for proof verification, and the latest root is used for
validation.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-asp-layer" /> The Entrypoint contract exposes the update
function and stores the sequence.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-entrypoint" />

Trace the dependencies that creates. To withdraw privately, a user needs their label included in a
recently-published root, needs the off-chain data behind it to be retrievable so their client can
build the inclusion proof, and needs the update pipeline to have run recently enough that the root on
chain is current.

**That is an off-chain data pipeline sitting on the critical path of a user's access to their own
money.** If the pipeline stalls, the roots go stale; if the off-chain data becomes unretrievable,
proofs cannot be constructed even for labels that are approved. The circular buffer softens the first
problem by keeping recent roots valid for a while, which is the same root-window trade you met with
the state tree — deep enough for liveness, shallow enough that revocation means something.

And when a label is removed rather than merely missing, the documented outcome is the public exit:
removal restricts withdrawal, and the original depositor
ragequits.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-asp-layer" /><Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-core-concepts" />
The compliance layer therefore has a specific power that is worth naming plainly: **it cannot take
your money, and it can take your privacy**, by leaving you with the public exit as your only route
out.

## The equilibrium argument, and its limits

Why would anyone opt into being screened when the whole point was privacy? The answer is not
cryptographic. It is an argument about incentives, and it deserves to be stated precisely so it can
be assessed.

The argument: if excluding yourself from tainted deposits is cheap, and the resulting proof is what
counterparties accept, then honest users will do it voluntarily — no compulsion, no key holder.
Illicit funds then concentrate in whatever remains, which is both small and conspicuous. Privacy and
compliance are obtained simultaneously with no backdoor.<Cite src="privacy-pools-paper" />

It is an elegant argument and its empirical support is thin. Name the conditions it depends on,
because each is a place it can fail:

**Counterparties must accept the proof.** If exchanges and banks do not treat an association proof as
satisfying anything, opting in costs users effort for no benefit and the equilibrium never forms.
This is the load-bearing condition and it is not a technical one.

**Opting in must be genuinely cheap.** Extra proving work, a curation delay, a fee, or a complicated
wallet flow all reduce participation. If only sophisticated users opt in, the "clean" set describes
sophistication rather than honesty.

**The curated set must not exclude honest users.** Over-restrictive curation pushes lawful users into
the residual set or into ragequitting, which both weakens their privacy and pollutes the signal the
argument relies on.

**"Clean" must mean one thing.** With multiple providers, multiple jurisdictions and user-selected
lists, a proof against list A says nothing to a counterparty who cares about list
B.<Cite src="railgun-docs-ppoi" /> Fragmentation is the practical enemy of the equilibrium, and the
same feature that makes the design jurisdictionally sensible causes it.

**And the residual set must actually be small.** If a large fraction of ordinary users never opt in
for reasons of inertia, the remainder is not conspicuous — it is just everyone who did not read the
documentation.

<Answer>
**1. The accountant: a viewing key, and understand what you handed over.**

The accountant needs the whole year's activity, so a predicate is the wrong tool — this is genuinely
a disclosure. A viewing key grants exactly read access and no spend
authority.<Cite src="railgun-docs-wallets-keys" /><Cite src="aztec-docs-keys" />

*What it reveals:* everything the key covers. If block-scoping is not actually available in the

*Residual risk:* the key is likely permanent and possibly not rotatable at the protocol
level.<Cite src="aztec-docs-keys" /> You have given one firm perpetual read access to a user's
financial life. Mitigations are procedural rather than cryptographic: a fresh account per period, so
that the key covers only that period's activity by construction. Design for the scoping you can
verify, not the scoping you read about.

**2. The supplier: a predicate proof, not a key.**

They do not need your history; they need one fact — that the funds came from an acceptable source.
This is exactly the association-set or proof-of-innocence shape: a proof of membership in a curated
approved set, or of non-membership in a curated flagged
list.<Cite src="privacy-pools-paper" /><Cite src="railgun-docs-ppoi" />

*What it reveals:* the truth of that predicate, and the fact that you generated it. The withdrawal
circuit's dual-membership structure shows the shape working — the proof establishes note ownership
and label inclusion at once, revealing a position in neither
tree.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-withdrawal-circuit" />

*Residual risk, and it is the real one:* **their bank has to accept it.** Nothing establishes that any
bank does, and no jurisdiction has been shown to have blessed the mechanism. Before building the flow,
find out what that bank will accept — the answer may be "a viewing key" or "a letter", and if so, the
elegant mechanism is not the product. Second risk: which list. A proof against a list the supplier's
bank does not recognise proves something nobody asked about.<Cite src="railgun-docs-ppoi" />

**3. The regulator auditing the operator: neither, mostly.**

They are asking about your controls, not a user's balance. What they want is your policy, your
screening process, your provider relationships and your records — which is a governance answer, not a
cryptographic one.

*What you can offer cryptographically:* aggregate proofs about the operator's own behaviour, and a
precise account of what you can and cannot see. That second part is unusually important here, because
"we cannot see it" is a real answer only if it is true, and you must be able to demonstrate the
architecture that makes it true.

*Residual risk:* you are naming the parties in your trust boundary — the curator, the list providers
behind it, whoever holds upgrade authority over the pool
contracts.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-entrypoint" /><Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-asp-layer" /> That
inventory is the next lesson, and it is also the document a regulator will actually want.

**The framing to carry into all three.** Ask what the requester needs to *conclude*, not what they
have asked to *see*. Those differ far more often than the request implies, and the gap between them
is exactly the space selective disclosure operates in. Then check separately whether they will accept
a conclusion without the underlying data — which is a question about them, not about your system.

## What this unlocks

**The final lesson** finishes the trust inventory these mechanisms keep gesturing at. Every design
here moved the trust problem rather than removing it: from a key holder to a list curator, from a
custodian to an upgrade authority, from an authority to a set of private data vendors. Naming every
party and what each could do if hostile is the deliverable, and alongside it sits the legal exposure
of shipping any of this — which is two separate stories that are routinely and consequentially
collapsed into one.

**In product design**, the useful discipline is the one the check ends on. Separate what a party
needs to conclude from what they have asked to see, then separate again whether they are *able* to
accept a proof in place of data. Many cannot, for reasons that have nothing to do with cryptography.

**And the general pattern is worth naming.** Selective disclosure is a mechanism for turning a
request for data into a request for a conclusion. That pattern applies to identity, credentials,
credit, insurance and audit far beyond this domain — and everywhere it applies, the hard part is the
same: not building the proof, but getting the other party to accept one.
