A zero-knowledge proof hides which member of a set you are. It does that perfectly, in the strong
sense the word carries in cryptography: no adversary, however powerful, learns more than the
statement.

So state the statement precisely, because it is not the one people think they are getting:

```text
∃ a leaf in this tree, and secrets for it, such that ...
```

**"A leaf in this tree."** The proof's guarantee is exactly as strong as the tree is large and as
uniform as its leaves are. If the tree has one leaf, a perfect proof identifies you completely.
Nothing has gone wrong with the cryptography; you asked it to hide you among one person.

Anonymity is therefore not a property of the proof system. It is a **liquidity property** — a
function of how many other people are using the thing, with what amounts, at what times. Protocols
can encourage it. None can manufacture it.

## The funnel

Privacy does not fail all at once. It is filtered away, in stages, by facts an observer already has.
Work a hypothetical pool with round numbers — **the numbers below are illustrative, not a
measurement of any real system**, and the shape is the lesson:

Each line is a join an observer performs on public data. None of them touches the proof.

- **Denomination** filters because amounts are visible when they are not standardised.
- **Ordering** filters because a withdrawal cannot precede its deposit.
- **Already-spent** filters because published nullifiers are public and cumulative — every prior
  withdrawal permanently removes a candidate.
- **Timing** filters hardest of all, and is the one users control least.

Ten thousand became thirty-nine. A one-in-39 guess is not a cryptographic failure; it is what a
perfect proof over a badly-shaped set yields. And an observer who runs this against thousands of
withdrawals does not need to be right every time — they need to be right often enough to build a
graph.

<Aside kind="note">
**The third filter has a property worth pausing on.** The anonymity set of an early user *shrinks
over time*, because every subsequent withdrawal publishes a nullifier that eliminates a candidate
deposit. Your privacy on the day you withdraw is not the privacy you have two years later. Systems
where deposits and withdrawals eventually balance out converge toward a fully-resolved matching, and
the only thing preventing it is continuous new activity. **Anonymity is not a state you reach. It is
a flow you consume.**
</Aside>

## Amounts: the filter you can design away

The second line of the funnel is the one protocol design attacks directly, because unique amounts are
catastrophic. A deposit of 3.7182 ETH and a withdrawal of 3.7182 ETH need no analysis at all.

**Fixed denominations** were the earliest answer: everyone deposits the same size, so the amount
carries no information and the second filter does nothing. The cost is paid in user experience —
depositing 5 ETH means five separate 1-ETH deposits, five notes, five withdrawals, and no way to pay
someone 0.3.

**Hiding the amounts** is the harder and better answer, and its shape is specified rather than
folkloric. A shielded protocol commits to each value with a **homomorphic** commitment, then proves
that inputs balance outputs by doing arithmetic on the commitments themselves — so the balance
property is checked without any amount being revealed.<Cite src="zcash-protocol-spec" /> The
commitments add up; the values stay hidden. That is strictly more construction for strictly better
usability, and it is why mature private systems support arbitrary amounts.

A live hybrid is worth seeing because it shows the trade being made explicitly rather than
theoretically. Privacy Pools exposes `withdrawnValue` as a **public** input to its withdrawal
circuit, while the remaining balance goes into a new commitment the circuit
computes.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-withdrawal-circuit" /> So the amount leaving is public — it
has to be; the contract is transferring it — and the amount staying behind is not. Partial
withdrawals are supported, which means the amount you withdraw need not equal the amount you
deposited, which weakens the denomination filter without eliminating it.

**The design instruction that follows:** if amounts are visible at the boundary, treat round numbers
as your friend and unique numbers as an identifier. Withdrawing 1.0 ETH when the pool is full of
1.0 ETH withdrawals is a different act from withdrawing 0.9973 ETH, and no proof system distinguishes
them for you.

## Metadata: where deanonymisation actually happens

Now the part that dominates in practice, and where the published research is.

The core finding is not subtle. Academic work on Ethereum profiled and deanonymised users through
**quasi-identifiers** — patterns that characterise only a small set of users — and applied them
directly to link mixing parties, using time-of-day activity profiles, transaction-fee behaviour,
address reuse and graph-representation techniques, with Ethereum Name Service identifiers as ground
truth.<Cite src="arxiv-ethereum-deanonymization" /> The proofs were never involved. The attack ran
entirely on the shape of ordinary behaviour around them.

### The gas leak

A withdrawal to a brand-new address has a problem: that address holds no ETH and cannot pay for its
own transaction. Fund it from an address you control and you have drawn, in public, the exact edge
the pool was supposed to erase. This is the single most common self-inflicted deanonymisation, and it
happens *after* the cryptography has done its job perfectly.

### Timing

Deposit at 14:03, withdraw at 14:11. The window filter in the funnel above collapses to whoever else
deposited in those eight minutes, which in most pools is nobody. Waiting is the cheapest privacy
purchase available and the one users least want to make, because the reason they are withdrawing is
usually that they want the money now.

Worse, timing is a *habit*. The research's use of time-of-day activity as a profiling signal works
precisely because people act at the same hours as themselves and not as
each other.<Cite src="arxiv-ethereum-deanonymization" />

### Fee and wallet fingerprints

Transaction-fee behaviour is named in the same work as a profiling
signal.<Cite src="arxiv-ethereum-deanonymization" /> A wallet with an unusual tip policy, a client
that sets an idiosyncratic gas limit, a relayer fee configured to an unusual basis-point value —
each narrows the candidate set. Privacy Pools' relayed withdrawals carry a `relayFeeBPS` in their
fee data,<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-withdrawal-flow" /> and a fee nobody else uses is a label.

### The node you are talking to

This one is invisible from on-chain data and is admitted candidly in vendor documentation. Aztec's
own docs note that when the client-side execution environment queries a node for world state — for
instance to check whether a nullifier exists — **the node learns which data the user is interested
in**, and record it as a known trade-off users can mitigate by running their own
node.<Cite src="aztec-docs-pxe" />

Generalise that. Every privacy system has a client that must read chain state to function, and
whoever serves that read sees the pattern. An RPC provider watching a wallet fetch exactly the
Merkle path for leaf 8,412 does not need to break anything. Add the IP address the request came from
and the anonymity set is one.

### The destination

Withdraw to an address that later deposits into an exchange with your identity documents attached and
the entire chain of reasoning completes at the exchange's database. Privacy on-chain ends where
identified custody begins, and most funds eventually go somewhere identified.

### The gift you did not ask for

The most elegant attack in the literature deserves its own paragraph, because it is entirely outside
the cryptography. The same Ethereum privacy analysis describes a **malicious value-fingerprinting
attack** — a variant of the Danaan-gift attack — applicable to confidential transaction overlays, and
estimates its success probability using real user activity
statistics.<Cite src="arxiv-ethereum-deanonymization" />

The shape: an attacker sends a victim a payment of a strange, precisely chosen value. When that value
later moves — mixed into a spend, combined with other funds, withdrawn — the distinctive amount acts
as a tracer. The victim did nothing wrong and the proof did nothing wrong. **An adversary who can
choose what you receive can mark you.**

The defensive consequence generalises well past this domain: in a system where amounts are visible
anywhere, *incoming* value from strangers is a vector, not just a gift.

The practical statement is uncomfortable and true: **operational discipline matters more to a user's
privacy than the choice of proof system.** A perfect protocol used carelessly leaks; a mediocre one
used carefully often does not. And almost none of the discipline is something a protocol designer can
enforce.

## The relayer: fixing one leak by adding a party

The gas leak has a standard fix. A **relayer** submits the withdrawal transaction on the user's
behalf and takes its fee out of the withdrawn amount, so the fresh destination address never needs
funding from anywhere.

Privacy Pools implements this as a first-class mode. A relayed withdrawal names the Entrypoint
contract as the processor rather than the recipient, and carries fee data specifying the final
recipient, the relayer's fee address, and the fee in basis points. The relayer verifies the proof
locally, submits the transaction, pays the gas, and the Entrypoint processes the withdrawal and
distributes the funds.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-withdrawal-flow" /><Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-entrypoint" />
Their own comparison of the two modes puts it plainly: the direct withdrawal offers basic privacy and
the relayed one enhanced privacy, precisely because the withdrawal address
differs.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-withdrawal-flow" /> Railgun calls the equivalent role a
Broadcaster — a wallet that submits gas on a user's behalf, so interactions appear to originate from
the Broadcaster and cannot be linked to a shielding
address.<Cite src="railgun-docs-privacy-system" />

**Subgoal: enumerate what the relayer gains.** Every fix that adds a party should be evaluated by
what that party now knows and can do.

That last row is the one thing the cryptography does defend, and it is worth naming as a success. The
withdrawal proof binds a `context` covering the whole withdrawal struct including the fee data, so a
relayer who edits the recipient invalidates the proof.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-withdrawal-circuit" /><Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-withdrawal-flow" />
**The relayer can refuse to help you. It cannot rob you.** That is a meaningfully better trust
relationship than "custodian", and meaningfully worse than "nobody".

You have met the same problem in a different costume: a [paymaster](/c/paymaster) sponsoring gas so a
user need not hold ETH. Same mechanism, same benefit, and the same overlooked consequence — the
sponsor sees the operation. Account abstraction did not solve this for privacy; it re-solved gas
sponsorship with a different trust shape and inherited the visibility.

<Aside kind="warn">
**A relayer set is only as private as its diversity.** If one relayer handles most withdrawals, the
gas-funding link has not been removed; it has been re-pointed at a single party who can reconstruct
it for everybody at once. Ask two questions of any relayer network: how many are actually active,
and can a user choose freely between them? A permissioned list of three is a different product from
an open market, whatever the documentation calls it.
</Aside>

## Three heuristics an analyst runs first

The filters in the funnel are mechanical. The heuristics on top of them are what turn a candidate set
into an accusation, and they are worth knowing by name because each one has a countermeasure.

**The multi-note heuristic.** A user moving 5 ETH through a fixed-denomination pool makes five
deposits and, later, five withdrawals. Those ten events cluster: five deposits in one burst, five
withdrawals of the same denomination in another burst, same count on both sides. An analyst does not
need to match any individual pair — matching the *bursts* is enough, and the count is a strong
signal. Countermeasure: split across time and across pools, or use a system with hidden amounts so
one note suffices.<Cite src="zcash-protocol-spec" />

**The one-shot heuristic.** Addresses that appear once, do exactly one thing and go quiet are
distinguishable from addresses with a history. A destination whose entire lifetime is "received a
pool withdrawal, forwarded it, never used again" is a labelled node in every analytics product, and
the fact that it is labelled "probably a mixer output" is itself information about its owner's
intent. Countermeasure: destinations with ordinary lives, which requires planning before the
withdrawal rather than after.

**The behavioural-profile heuristic.** The published work's contribution is precisely this: profiling
users by patterns that characterise only a small set of them, then applying those quasi-identifiers
to deanonymisation tasks — including linking mixing parties — with time-of-day activity and
fee behaviour among the signals.<Cite src="arxiv-ethereum-deanonymization" /> Countermeasure: almost
none available to an individual, because you cannot easily stop being yourself. This is the heuristic
that survives careful users.

### What the protocol can do that the user cannot

Given that most of the failure modes are behavioural, protocol design is not powerless — it is just
aiming at defaults rather than at cryptography.

- **Standardise what is visible.** Fixed denominations, or hidden amounts, remove the denomination
  filter for everybody at once, including users who never think about it.
- **Make internal activity cheap.** Every transfer that stays inside the boundary adds an unknown hop
  to the graph, which is why a system supporting private sends and swaps is structurally harder to
  analyse than one that only takes deposits and pays withdrawals.<Cite src="railgun-docs-privacy-system" />
- **Ship a relayer path as the default**, not as an advanced option, because the gas leak is the most
  common single failure and the users who most need protecting are the ones who will not find a
  setting.
- **Default the client to a privacy-preserving read path**, or make running your own node
  realistic, since the node serving your state queries learns what you are interested
  in.<Cite src="aztec-docs-pxe" />
- **Delay by default** — a withdrawal that waits by construction is a withdrawal whose owner did not
  have to be disciplined.

Each of those is a product decision with a user-experience cost, and each is worth more to aggregate
privacy than any improvement to the proof system. **The lever with the largest effect on real
anonymity is the default, not the cryptography.**

## The compounding rule

Assemble the lesson into one operational statement.

Privacy is an **intersection**, not a sum. A user is private only if every one of the following holds
simultaneously: the proof is sound, the nullifier is unlinkable, the anonymity set is large after
filtering, the amount is unremarkable, the timing is unremarkable, the gas came from somewhere
unlinked, the RPC endpoint did not identify them, and the destination is not already tied to their
name.

Eight conditions. The system provides the first two. **The user, the wallet and the operational
environment provide the other six**, and any one of them failing reduces the whole to zero,
retroactively and permanently, because the chain does not forget.

Vendor documentation that acknowledges this is telling you the truth. Railgun's own privacy page
states outright that the level of privacy is a function of the number of unique shield interactions
and users, the value locked, and the volume of private
activity.<Cite src="railgun-docs-privacy-system" /> The same page also carries a competitive claim —
that its privacy at a given amount of value locked is always greater than alternatives, because of
the additional noise from internal activity.<Cite src="railgun-docs-privacy-system" /> Treat the
first as a concession worth trusting and the second as a vendor's argument for its own product:
the structural reasoning behind it is sound, the "always" is not established, and no independent
measurement was found for it.

<Answer>
**Start with what went right,** because two things did. Waiting a week defeats the tight timing
window, which is the filter that usually decides these cases. Generating the destination offline
avoids leaking it to a service. Both are real and both are more than most users do.

**Now the questions, in the order I would ask them.**

*1. How did the new address pay for gas?* If they funded it from the main wallet — or from any
address traceable to them — the link is drawn in public and everything else was wasted effort. This
is the most common single failure, and it happens after the cryptography has worked
perfectly.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-withdrawal-flow" /> If they used a relayer or broadcaster
instead, good; then ask *which* one, and whether that relayer handles enough traffic for their
withdrawal not to stand out.<Cite src="railgun-docs-privacy-system" />

*2. Was the amount exactly 10?* A 10 ETH deposit and a 10 ETH withdrawal are joined by the
denomination filter unless the pool is full of 10 ETH movements. If the withdrawal was 9.9987 after
a relayer fee, that residue is a fingerprint of a specific fee configuration — fee behaviour is a
named profiling signal in the published
work.<Cite src="arxiv-ethereum-deanonymization" />

*3. What is the anonymity set after filtering, not before?* "Thousands of deposits" is the number
before any filter. The relevant number is deposits of the same size, made before this withdrawal,
not yet spent, in a plausible window. That is computable from public data by anyone, and it is
routinely one or two orders of magnitude smaller.

*4. What RPC did both transactions go through?* Same provider, same IP, one week apart, one funding
the pool and one draining it. That provider has the join without touching the chain — and this is a
known trade-off that privacy systems document about themselves rather than a hypothetical
one.<Cite src="aztec-docs-pxe" />

*5. Where does the money go next?* If the new address forwards to an identified exchange account, the
chain of reasoning finishes at the exchange, and the week of waiting bought nothing.

*6. Had this address ever appeared anywhere?* Address reuse is a named quasi-identifier; "brand-new"
must mean never used, in any context, before or after.<Cite src="arxiv-ethereum-deanonymization" />

**What I would tell them about scaling it up.** A larger amount makes every filter sharper, because
large deposits are rarer and therefore more distinguishable. The anonymity set for a 10 ETH
movement and for a 1,000 ETH movement are not the same set with different sizes — the second is
usually a handful of participants who all know about each other. If the plan is to move
significantly more, the honest answer is that the funnel gets narrower exactly as the stakes rise,
and no protocol choice fixes that.

**And the framing.** "Untraceable" is not a property their transaction has. It is a claim about an
adversary — how much data they hold, how much effort they will spend, and whether they only need to
be right often enough rather than every time. Ask which adversary they mean, and the conversation
becomes answerable.

## What this unlocks

**Next lesson** steps back to compare the systems this module keeps citing, because they are not
competing implementations of one idea. Hiding balances while the contract logic stays public is a
different product from hiding which code ran on which private state, and the second one changes how
you write contracts rather than how you store data.

**In product work**, the funnel is a design tool, not just an attack. Ask what filters an observer
of *your* system can apply, and each one is a feature request: standardise amounts, add internal
activity, provide a relayer set, default to a private RPC path, delay by default. Every filter you
remove is a multiplier on everyone's anonymity, including users who will never think about any of
this.

**And the transferable idea is the intersection rule.** Any property that requires eight conditions
to hold simultaneously will be delivered by whichever one is weakest, and it is never the one the
architecture diagram is about. That is true of privacy, and it is true of security, availability and
correctness in exactly the same way.
