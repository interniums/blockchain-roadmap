Five lessons of mechanism. This one is the invoice.

Two things come with shipping a privacy system, and neither is cryptographic. The first is a list of
parties your users are now trusting, most of whom were introduced by the compliance layer that was
supposed to reduce trust. The second is legal exposure that is real, jurisdiction-specific, and
routinely described wrongly in both directions.

The approach here is descriptive throughout. This lesson names mechanisms and documented facts, marks
what is unresolved as unresolved, and takes no position on what anyone should build or how any of it
should be regulated. It is not legal advice and is not a substitute for counsel in a specific
jurisdiction.

## The trust inventory

Association sets and proofs of innocence do something precise: they **move** the trust problem from a
key holder to a list curator. That is a real improvement — a curator cannot read your history and a
key holder can — and it is not removal. Write the inventory out.

The deployed association-set architecture publishes enough of its own structure to make this
concrete, so use it rather than a generic diagram.

### One: the curator

The compliance layer maintains the list of approved deposit labels, provides the inclusion proofs
withdrawals depend on, enables label revocation, and determines withdrawal
eligibility.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-asp-layer" /> Root updates come only from authorised
accounts, each update carrying a root, a reference to off-chain data and a
timestamp.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-asp-layer" /><Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-entrypoint" />

*If hostile or mistaken:* a lawful user is omitted, and their private withdrawal path closes. Nothing
in the cryptography constrains the decision — the circuit checks a Merkle path, and a Merkle path
carries no opinion about whether the set was assembled fairly.

*The user's remedy, stated fairly:* removal from the set triggers withdrawal restrictions, and the
original depositor can ragequit — recovering their full remaining value by exiting
publicly.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-asp-layer" /><Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-core-concepts" />
So the curator cannot confiscate. **The curator's power is over your privacy, not your principal**,
and a design that provides that exit is meaningfully better than one that does not.

### Two: whoever supplies the curator's data

A curator does not generate its own intelligence. The complementary design names its providers
outright — Elliptic, ScamSniffer, PureFi, SlowMist and a Chainalysis sanctions oracle among
them<Cite src="railgun-docs-ppoi" /> — and naming them is the useful practice, because it makes
visible that several private analytics companies now sit inside the trust boundary of a system
marketed on trust minimisation.

*If hostile or mistaken:* a false positive at a provider propagates downstream into every system
consuming that list. The user has no relationship with the provider, no notice, and typically no
appeal. Whatever dispute process exists is a commercial one between companies.

### Three: the upgrade authority

The coordinating contract follows the UUPS upgradeable-proxy pattern with role-based access control.
An owner role registers and removes pools, updates configurations, winds pools down and withdraws
accumulated fees; a separate role updates the compliance roots that validate
withdrawals.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-entrypoint" />

*If hostile:* everything. This is the same conclusion the on-chain verification module reached from
the other direction — a cryptographic guarantee is bounded by the governance around the contract
implementing it — and it applies here with the additional sting that the contract in question holds
everyone's money in one balance.

### Four: the relayer

The party that submits withdrawals so a fresh address never needs funding. It receives the withdrawal
before the chain does, chooses whether and when to submit, and takes a fee in basis
points.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-withdrawal-flow" />

*If hostile:* silent censorship, timing manipulation into a worse anonymity window, and a commercial
record of destinations and amounts. It cannot redirect funds, because the proof binds the withdrawal
parameters.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-withdrawal-flow" />

### Five: whoever hosts the off-chain data

Each root update references off-chain data by hash.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-asp-layer" /> A
client needs that data to build an inclusion proof.

*If unavailable:* a liveness failure that is **indistinguishable, from the user's side, from
exclusion.** They cannot prove membership, so they cannot withdraw privately, and no on-chain
evidence distinguishes "the data is gone" from "you were removed". Availability failures that
masquerade as policy decisions are a nasty category, and this architecture has one.

### Six: the client you use

The wallet or web front-end holds your secrets, constructs your proofs, and chooses which node to
query. It is the party best placed to deanonymise you and the one least often listed.

*If hostile:* total compromise, silently. A privacy system's threat model that names the contract,
the curator and the relayer but not the front-end is missing the easiest attack in the set.

## Legal exposure: two stories that are not one story

Publishing privacy software has produced real criminal exposure in the United States. Two distinct
threads run through the best-known case, and collapsing them produces confident statements that are
wrong in opposite directions.

### Thread one: sanctions, and the reversal

The United States Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control published a **Cyber-related Designation
Removal dated 21 March 2025**, recording deletions from the Specially Designated Nationals list that
include Tornado Cash, its website, and a long list of associated Ethereum
addresses.<Cite src="ofac-tornado-delisting" /> That is the primary record: a government publication
listing what was removed, on a stated date.

Legal analysis of the removal places it after an appellate decision holding that immutable smart
contracts are not property of a sanctioned person — reasoning that undercut the original designation
rather than merely superseding it as a policy matter.<Cite src="venable-tornado-delisting" />

**A curriculum that still says "Tornado Cash is OFAC-sanctioned" is wrong**, and this is a common
error: the 2022 designation received heavy coverage and the 2025 reversal received very little.

### Thread two: criminal prosecution, and what is unresolved

Separately — different law, different forum, different question — a criminal prosecution of a
developer produced a **split verdict in August 2025**: conviction on conspiracy to operate an
unlicensed money transmitting business, with the jury hung on the remaining
counts.<Cite src="mayer-brown-tornado-verdict" />

The subsequent procedural history is **not stated in this lesson**, because this module's research
could not confirm it. What happened after the split verdict — retrial, dismissal, sentencing, appeal
— is a fact you must check against a current tracker rather than take from any teaching
material.<Cite src="defi-education-fund-storm-2026" /> Note when consulting that tracker that it is
maintained by an advocacy organisation, which does not make it inaccurate and does mean it is not
neutral.

### Why the two must stay separate

Sanctions law asks whether a person or property is designated. Money-transmission law asks whether an
activity requires a licence. **They are different exposures with different elements, and a favourable
outcome in one says nothing about the other.**

The practical consequence is a sentence worth carrying exactly as written: the delisting did not make
writing this software legally safe, and the conviction did not make it illegal to write privacy
software. Both statements are over-readings, and both circulate.

**Scope limits on everything above.** These are United States matters. Other jurisdictions have their

the above. Nothing here is legal advice; the engineering-relevant point is narrower and more useful
than a legal conclusion, and it is the subject of the next section.

## What an engineering team actually decides

The reason a legal thread belongs in a technical module is that **architecture choices map onto legal
categories**, and the mapping is made at design time by engineers, usually without anyone noticing a
choice was made.

Six questions, each of which is an architectural decision with a different profile:

| Decision | Why it is not just an implementation detail |
|---|---|
| Do you take custody at any point? | Custody is the axis most regulatory categories are built around |
| Do you take a fee on transfers? | Fee-taking on value movement is a fact about your activity, and this architecture has both deposit vetting fees and relayer fees<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-entrypoint" /> |
| Do you operate the relayer? | Operating the submission path is a different posture from publishing software others run |
| Do you curate, or consume someone else's list? | Curation is a judgement you are making about users |
| Do you publish a hosted front-end? | Publishing an interface is a different act from publishing a library |
| Where are your users? | Determines whose rules apply, and you may not know |

Write those answers down before you talk to a lawyer, not after. The answers *are* the description of
what your product does, and a counsel conversation that begins with "we use zero-knowledge proofs" is
a worse conversation than one that begins with a six-row table.

Two further design notes that follow from the inventory rather than from the law:

**Fewer parties is a feature you can ship.** Every party in the inventory is an architectural choice.
Consuming a third-party list rather than curating adds a party and removes a judgement; running your
own relayer removes a party and adds a posture. Neither is right in general. Both should be
deliberate.

**Document what you cannot see.** "We cannot decrypt user activity" is only credible if you can point
at the architecture that makes it true. That document is useful to a regulator, to a security
reviewer, and to your own team the first time someone proposes a feature that would quietly break it.

Your company is about to ship confidential payroll on a shielded pool with an association-set
compliance layer. The security review asks one question:

```text
List every party a user must trust, and what each could do if hostile.
```

Produce the list, then say which single change would most reduce it.

<Answer>
**The parties, and their hostile capability.**

1. **The pool contract's upgrade authority.** Can replace the implementation and reach every user's
   funds. The coordinating contract is an upgradeable proxy with an owner role that registers,
   reconfigures and winds down pools and withdraws fees.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-entrypoint" />
   This is the largest single exposure and it is not cryptographic.
2. **The compliance curator.** Can exclude a lawful employee from private withdrawal by omitting or
   revoking their label.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-asp-layer" /> Cannot take their money — the
   documented remedy is a public exit that returns the full remaining
   value.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-core-concepts" /> So: power over privacy, not principal.
3. **The upstream list providers.** A false positive at a commercial analytics vendor propagates into
   the curator's set, with no user-facing appeal.<Cite src="railgun-docs-ppoi" />
4. **The relayer.** Sees each withdrawal's destination, amount and timing first; can censor silently
   or delay into a worse anonymity window; cannot redirect, because the proof binds the withdrawal
   parameters.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-withdrawal-flow" />
5. **The off-chain data host.** If the data behind a published root becomes unretrievable, employees
   cannot construct inclusion proofs — a liveness failure indistinguishable from
   exclusion.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs-asp-layer" />
6. **The client software and its RPC endpoint.** Holds the secrets, builds the proofs, chooses whom
   to ask. Total compromise if hostile, and invisible.
7. **Us.** We know every employee's salary and address by construction — we are the payer. No
   protocol removes this, and for a payroll product the internal threat model is the one users would
   care about most if asked.

**The single change with the largest effect: shorten the upgrade path.** A timelock long enough for
employees to exit, with the queued upgrade monitored and alerted on, converts party one from
"unbounded" to "bounded by a delay". Nothing else on the list can lose principal, and no cryptographic
improvement touches it.

**Two cheaper changes worth making at the same time.** Ship the relayer path as the default rather
than an option, so employees do not fund fresh addresses from identified ones. And pin the off-chain
compliance data ourselves in addition to the curator, so a hosting failure does not present to an
employee as exclusion.

**And the honest sentence for the review document.** This design does not remove trust; it relocates
it from a custodian to a curator and an upgrade key, and it buys confidentiality of salaries against
outside observers rather than against us. Both halves belong in the document. A privacy claim that
does not say *from whom* is not a claim.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**You have finished the elective.** Both of its modules taught the same structural lesson from
opposite ends. On the verification side, a proof that is mathematically airtight is bounded by the
contract that consumes it and the key that can replace it. On the privacy side, a proof that hides
perfectly is bounded by the anonymity set, the metadata around it, and the curator who decides who
belongs. **In both cases the cryptography is the part that works, and the system property is the part
that decides.**

**In practice**, the two deliverables from this module are short and neither is code: the public-input
binding table from the first lesson, and the trust inventory from this one. A team that produces both
before shipping has done more for its users than any choice of proof system will.

**And the reading habit is the thing to keep.** Everything in these two modules that could be pinned
to a specification, a deployed contract's own documentation, or a government publication has been.
Everything that could not — cost figures without hardware, latency figures without workloads, launch
dates for unshipped upgrades, the current status of a live legal matter — has been named as
unresolved instead. That distinction is not caution for its own sake. In a field where the confident
numbers are mostly incomparable and the confident legal claims are mostly one story mistaken for
another, knowing which of your beliefs are sourced is the difference between expertise and fluency.
