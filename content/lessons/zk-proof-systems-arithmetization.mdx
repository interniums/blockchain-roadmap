Here is a line of Rust from a guest program that has been proved thousands of times.

```rust
if balance >= amount {
    balance -= amount;
}
```

Two operations, both of which your CPU does in a single cycle each. Inside a proof system, the
subtraction is nearly free and the comparison is one of the more expensive things on the page. The
reason is not an implementation weakness. It is that `>=` does not exist where the proof lives.

This lesson is about the translation layer: the step that turns a computation into algebra, and the
step where, by broad agreement, most of the engineering effort and most of the bugs in the whole
stack are concentrated.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" /><Cite src="zkevm-constraint-design-arxiv" />

## The only thing a proof system can talk about

Everything downstream — commitments, low-degree tests, pairings — operates on elements of a finite
field: the integers modulo a large prime, with addition and multiplication defined modulo that
prime.<Cite src="sok-zk-snarks-arxiv" /> That is the entire vocabulary. Two operations.

Consider what is missing.

**There is no ordering.** Field elements wrap. "Is `a` less than `b`" has no field-arithmetic
answer, because subtracting one from the other lands somewhere in the ring with no sign attached. To
compare, you first prove that both values fit in a known bit width — decompose each into bits,
constrain every bit to be 0 or 1, constrain the bits to recompose to the original value — and only
then can a comparison mean anything. That is why the `>=` above costs so much more than the `-=`:
one is a field subtraction, the other is a range argument.

**There is no branching.** A constraint system is a fixed set of equations. It cannot execute one
branch and skip the other; it evaluates the algebra for the whole shape of the computation every
time. Conditionals become arithmetic: with a selector `s` constrained to be 0 or 1,
`out = s * a + (1 - s) * b` computes the choice without branching, and both `a` and `b` are
evaluated whether or not they are used.

**There are no data-dependent loop bounds.** The number of constraints is fixed before the witness
exists. A loop whose trip count depends on an input must be unrolled to its maximum, with a selector
disabling the iterations that do not happen.

**There are no bytes, strings or objects.** Everything is field elements, and every structure you
care about is an encoding you constrain into existence.

## Three ways the industry writes the equations down

There are three families in wide use. You will meet all three by name and the differences are real,
but the mental model underneath is shared: **fill in a table of values such that a fixed set of
polynomial identities holds.**

### R1CS — rank-1 constraint systems

The oldest of the three and the one behind Groth16-era tooling. The witness is a vector `z`, and the
system is a list of constraints, each of the form

```text
(A_i · z) * (B_i · z) = (C_i · z)
```

Each constraint permits exactly one multiplication of two linear combinations. Additions are free —
they are absorbed into the linear combinations — and every multiplication costs a
row.<Cite src="sok-zk-snarks-arxiv" /> Circom is the canonical compiler targeting this
shape.<Cite src="circom-repo" /> Groth16 is a preprocessing argument for arithmetic circuit
satisfiability, which is the same statement in different clothes.<Cite src="groth16-paper" />

The consequence for a working engineer: **in R1CS your cost model is "count the multiplications".**
A hash function that is cheap on a CPU because it is all XOR and rotation is catastrophic here,
because bitwise operations must be re-expressed as arithmetic over bits.

### PLONKish — a table with custom gates and lookups

The halo2 formulation describes a matrix of cells divided into three kinds of column: *fixed*
columns whose values are part of the circuit, *advice* columns holding the prover's witness, and
*instance* columns carrying the public inputs.<Cite src="halo2-book-arithmetization" /> Over that
matrix sit custom gates — polynomial identities of bounded degree applied at each row, usually
enabled by a selector — plus copy constraints that force two cells anywhere in the table to be
equal, and lookup arguments that force a tuple of cells to appear in a
table.<Cite src="halo2-book-arithmetization" /><Cite src="plonk-paper" />

Two things here are worth carrying.

First, **the public inputs have their own column.** The line drawn in the first lesson between `x`
and `w` is not a convention in the calling code; it is structurally visible in the circuit, in which
columns a value lives in. There is no ambiguity to be lazy about.

Second, **lookups change the cost model.** An operation that is horrible as arithmetic — a byte-wise
XOR, a range check, an S-box — can instead be proved by asserting that its input/output tuple
appears in a precomputed table. That single technique is why modern circuits for hash functions and
bit manipulation are tractable at all.

### AIR — an execution trace with transition constraints

The STARK-side formulation, and the one to hold in your head for zkVMs. The witness is a **trace
table**: each row is the full state of a machine at one step, each column is a register or an
auxiliary value, and the constraints come in two kinds — *boundary* constraints pinning specific
cells (the initial state, the final output) and *transition* constraints, which are polynomial
identities relating each row to the next.<Cite src="ethstark-documentation" /><Cite src="stark-paper" />
Cairo's paper is the clearest statement of this as a CPU architecture: a small instruction set
designed so that one set of transition constraints covers every
instruction.<Cite src="cairo-paper" /> Plonky3 is a current toolkit in this
lineage.<Cite src="plonky3-repo" />

The mental picture is worth the paragraph: imagine a spreadsheet where every row is a clock cycle.
The prover fills it in by actually running the program. The verifier never sees the sheet — it sees
commitments to the columns and checks, at random points, that "row `i+1` follows from row `i` by the
rules" holds as a polynomial identity across the whole table.<Cite src="iop-paper" />

<Aside kind="note">
The three families differ less than their vocabularies suggest, and current systems mix them
freely — PLONKish tables with lookup arguments and AIR-style transition constraints turn up in the
same stack. What genuinely differs, and what you should ask about, is the *cost model*: what is free,
what costs a row, and what has a lookup table available. That determines which algorithms are
affordable, and it is the thing you cannot infer from the name of the proof system.
</Aside>

### A worked arithmetization: one Merkle path step

Take something you already know cold — verifying an inclusion path — and watch it become algebra.
The claim: given a leaf, an index bit, and a sibling hash, produce the parent hash.

**Subgoal: eliminate the branch, because there is no branch.** In imperative code you write
`if bit == 0 { hash(node, sibling) } else { hash(sibling, node) }`. In a constraint system both
orderings must be computed as arithmetic. With `bit` constrained boolean, set
`left = node + bit * (sibling - node)` and `right = sibling + bit * (node - sibling)`. When `bit` is
0 those reduce to `(node, sibling)`; when it is 1, to `(sibling, node)`. Two multiplications, no
control flow, and — crucially — the boolean constraint on `bit` is what makes the reduction valid at
all.

**Subgoal: price the hash, because it dominates everything else.** The two swap lines cost a handful
of constraints. The hash costs thousands, and its cost depends entirely on which hash it is. A hash
built from field multiplications is comparatively cheap here; a hash built from XOR, rotation and
byte-level mixing must have every one of those operations re-expressed over bits or looked up in a
table.

**Subgoal: repeat, and notice what scales.** A path of depth 32 is 32 copies of the above. The
constraint count is fixed by the depth, not by the tree's contents, because the system must be laid
out before any witness exists. If your tree depth varies, you pad to the maximum and disable the
unused levels with selectors — and you pay for the disabled levels anyway.

**Subgoal: mark the boundary values.** The leaf's authenticity and the final root have to connect to
the outside world. The root is a public input, so it lives in an instance
column;<Cite src="halo2-book-arithmetization" /> the siblings and the index bits are advice. Move
the root to advice and you have rebuilt the first lesson's worst bug at the level of columns.

**Subgoal: count what you just decided.** Roughly: 32 boolean constraints, 64 multiplications for the
swaps, and 32 hash invocations whose cost is between two and four orders of magnitude larger than
everything else combined. Every optimisation worth doing is in the last term. That is a general
pattern, not a property of this example.

### What is expensive, and why it reshapes your algorithm

The cost inversion relative to ordinary code is severe enough to change designs, not just constants.

**Cheap:** field addition and multiplication, linear combinations, anything expressible as a
low-degree polynomial identity, and — where the system supports them — operations you can push into
a lookup table.<Cite src="plonk-paper" /><Cite src="halo2-book-arithmetization" />

**Expensive:** comparisons and range checks, bitwise operations, division, and any hash function
designed around bit manipulation rather than field arithmetic.<Cite src="sok-zk-snarks-arxiv" />

**Structural, and often decisive:** memory. A general-purpose machine needs random-access memory,
and arguing that a read returned what was last written to that address is a whole sub-protocol with
its own constraint cost. This is one of the main axes on which zkEVM designs differ from one
another.<Cite src="zkevm-constraint-design-arxiv" />

<Aside kind="warn">
One piece of received wisdom here has expired. From roughly 2021 to 2024, the default answer to
"which hash function in a circuit" was an arithmetic-friendly one designed for exactly this cost
model, and most tutorials still say so. For Ethereum L1 the direction has since moved toward
conservative SHA and BLAKE-family primitives, as part of a broader drift toward resting on hash
assumptions alone.<Cite src="ethereum-org-quantum-resistance" /> The specific decision to move away
from the arithmetic-friendly default for L1 is *reported* rather than confirmed by a located
Foundation decision record, so treat the direction as real and the details as unsettled. Inside L2
and application circuits the arithmetic-friendly hashes remain widely used.
</Aside>

## The gap between your program and your constraints

Now the part that matters more than any of the above.

The proof does not say your program is correct. It says: **there is an assignment to this constraint
system's variables that satisfies every constraint and matches these public inputs.** Your program
was the *intention*. The constraint system is the *claim*. When they diverge, the proof faithfully
attests to the claim, and your intention has nothing to do with it.

Divergence has a dominant direction. Circuits are rarely over-constrained in production — an
over-constrained circuit rejects honest witnesses, so it fails your tests on day one and you fix it.
The dangerous direction is **under-constrained**: the constraint system admits assignments the
author never imagined, and the only party who explores those assignments is an attacker.

### Three under-constraining bugs, concretely

**A selector that was never forced to be boolean.** You wrote `out = s * a + (1 - s) * b` and meant
`s` to be a choice bit. Unless you also constrain `s * (s - 1) == 0`, a malicious prover may set
`s = 5` and get `out = 5a - 4b`, an arbitrary affine blend of the two branches. The honest prover
always sets 0 or 1, so every test passes.

**A hint that was checked loosely.** Division is not a field operation you get for free; the normal
pattern is to supply the inverse as a witness and constrain `a * a_inv == 1`. That is correct — and
it is a proof that `a` is non-zero, which may or may not be what the surrounding logic assumed.
Write the same pattern where `a` is legitimately allowed to be zero and the circuit silently becomes
unsatisfiable for a valid input, which is the benign failure. Write a *conditional* version that
skips the check when a flag is set, forget to constrain the flag, and you have handed the prover a
switch that turns off a soundness check.

**An unconstrained computation whose result was trusted.** Noir makes this explicit as a language
feature: `unconstrained` functions run outside the circuit and their results enter it as ordinary
witness values, which the caller is then responsible for
constraining.<Cite src="noir-docs-unconstrained" /><Cite src="aztec-unconstrained-blog" /> This is a
genuinely good design — computing a value out of circuit and verifying it in circuit is very often
far cheaper than computing it in circuit — and it is a loaded gun. The value coming back is *chosen
by the prover*. If you compute a square root out of circuit and then forget to constrain that
squaring it returns the input, the prover returns whatever number is convenient.

<Aside kind="warn">
The reviewing discipline this implies is different from ordinary code review. For imperative code you
ask "what does this do on each input?" For a constraint system you ask, cell by cell, **"what is the
full set of values this can take, and what forces it?"** A variable with no answer to the second half
of that question is prover-controlled, and prover-controlled is the definition of the attack surface.
</Aside>

### Why this bug class resists ordinary tooling

Two structural reasons, both worth knowing before you plan a review budget.

**Coverage means nothing here.** Line coverage over circuit code tells you which constraints were
emitted, not which assignments were forbidden. A circuit can have every line executed by the test
suite and still admit an enormous space of malicious witnesses, because the tests only ever supply
witnesses produced by the honest witness generator. The honest generator is, in effect, a second
implementation of the intended semantics — and the bug is precisely the gap between it and the
constraints.

**The right question is a solver's question, not a tester's.** "Does there exist an assignment
satisfying these constraints where the output differs from the intended function?" is a
satisfiability query, and the tools that help are the ones that search that space rather than sample
it: symbolic analysis of the constraint system, differential comparison of the witness generator
against the constraints, and formal verification of the arithmetization itself. That last one is
exactly what the Foundation's zkEVM programme is investing in for the recursion and circuit layers
rather than treating the security-bit count as the whole story.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" />

## zkVM or hand-written circuit

The same material now answers the architectural question, and it answers it as a trade rather than a
ranking.

**A zkVM arithmetizes an instruction set once.** Someone writes the constraints for a general-purpose
machine — RISC-V in the case of both SP1 and RISC
Zero<Cite src="succinct-sp1-docs" /><Cite src="risc0-zkvm-docs" /> — and thereafter any program that
compiles for that machine becomes provable with no circuit work at all. Cairo makes the same move
with a purpose-built architecture designed so that its transition constraints stay
small.<Cite src="cairo-paper" />

**A hand-written circuit arithmetizes exactly one relation.** No instruction decoding, no general
memory argument, no registers you are not using. Only the algebra your specific claim needs. That is
why an application-specific circuit can be dramatically smaller than the same logic run inside a
zkVM.

The trade has four axes, and the third is the one usually left out of the comparison.

The third row is the argument that decides most real projects. A zkVM's constraint system is a
single artefact that many teams depend on, so it attracts audits, formal verification effort and
adversarial attention proportional to the whole ecosystem's stake in it. A bespoke circuit is
reviewed by however many people your budget stretches to. Constraint count is a cost; blast radius
is a risk; they are not measured in the same units and the smaller circuit is not automatically the
better decision.

### The same EVM, arithmetized differently

One consequence worth naming, because it explains a piece of terminology you will meet constantly.

Different zkEVMs encode identical EVM semantics in materially different constraint architectures —
different decompositions into sub-circuits, different memory arguments, different lookup
strategies.<Cite src="zkevm-constraint-design-arxiv" /> The proof each one produces attests to *its*
constraint system, so "this proof says the EVM ran correctly" is only as true as the fidelity of
that particular arithmetization to the actual EVM.

That is what the Type 1 through Type 4 classification is measuring. Type 1 is fully
Ethereum-equivalent, with no modifications to the EVM, so any Ethereum block can be proven exactly
as-is; Types 2 through 4 modify EVM behaviour in various ways to make proving
easier.<Cite src="ethereum-org-zkevm-roadmap" /> For L1 block verification, Type 1 is essential,
because any deviation from Ethereum's exact behaviour would create consensus
issues.<Cite src="ethereum-org-zkevm-roadmap" /> "Type" is not a performance grade. It is a statement
about how much of your original semantics survived the translation in this lesson.

<Check type="spot-bug" concept="arithmetization">
A circuit is meant to prove: "I know a value `v` and a bit-decomposition of it into 8 bits `b0..b7`,
and `v` is at most 200."

The author writes these constraints:

```text
(1)  v == b0 + 2*b1 + 4*b2 + 8*b3 + 16*b4 + 32*b5 + 64*b6 + 128*b7
(2)  (200 - v) * inv == 1          // inv supplied as a witness
```

Both constraints are satisfied by every honest witness the author tested. Name every way a malicious
prover can exploit this, and give the corrected constraint set.

<Answer>
**Bug one: the bits are not constrained to be bits.** Nothing forces `b0..b7` into `{0, 1}`. The
prover may set `b0 = 300` and every other bit to 0, and constraint (1) is satisfied with `v = 300`.
The decomposition is therefore not a decomposition and it proves nothing about `v`'s magnitude. Fix:
add `bi * (bi - 1) == 0` for each of the eight bits. This is the single most common missing
constraint in circuit code, and it is invisible in testing because honest witnesses always supply
real bits.

**Bug two: constraint (2) proves the wrong thing.** `(200 - v) * inv == 1` proves only that
`200 - v` is *invertible*, that is, non-zero. It does not prove `v <= 200`; it proves
`v != 200`, which is close to the opposite of the intent. Any `v` at all, including `v = 10^18`,
satisfies it as long as the prover supplies the correct inverse.

**Bug three, the one that survives fixing bug two naively.** Suppose the author replaces (2) with a
comparison of field elements. There is no such thing. Ordering does not exist in the field, so
"`v <= 200`" must be built out of a bit-decomposition and arithmetic on the bits. The range argument
*is* the mechanism, not a preliminary to it.

**Corrected constraint set.**

```text
(1)  bi * (bi - 1) == 0                  for i in 0..7
(2)  v == b0 + 2*b1 + ... + 128*b7       // now genuinely bounds v to [0, 255]
(3)  d == 200 - v
(4)  d == c0 + 2*c1 + ... + 128*c7       // decompose the difference
(5)  ci * (ci - 1) == 0                  for i in 0..7
```

Constraints (1) and (2) together bound `v` to the range 0 to 255. Constraints (3) through (5) prove
that `200 - v` also fits in 8 bits without wrapping, which — given that `v` is already known to be
small — is the statement `v <= 200`. In a PLONKish system you would instead push both range checks
into a lookup argument against a precomputed table, which is cheaper and much harder to get
wrong.<Cite src="halo2-book-arithmetization" />

**The transferable lesson.** Two of the three bugs are the same bug: a value the prover supplies with
nothing forcing its shape. Ask of every witness cell, "what forces this?" — and treat "the honest
prover would never" as a non-answer.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**Next lesson**: Fiat–Shamir, the step that removes the interactive verifier from the protocol.
Arithmetization gave the prover a table to fill; the interactive protocol has the verifier throw
random challenges at it; Fiat–Shamir replaces those challenges with hashes. Getting the hashed
transcript wrong is a soundness bug of exactly the same family as the missing boolean constraint —
a value the prover was left free to choose.<Cite src="iop-paper" />

**In the Noir module**: you will write constraints directly, read a gate count, and find an
unconstrained hint that lets a prover prove something false. The vocabulary from this lesson is what
makes that exercise diagnosable rather than magic.<Cite src="noir-docs-quickstart" />

**And in every conversation about zkEVM security**: you now know why the answer to "is this proof
system safe" splits into two questions that are usually merged. Is the proof system sound at the
claimed bits? And does the constraint system say what its authors meant? The first is cryptography.
The second is engineering, it is where the surface area is, and it is why the Foundation's
requirements ask teams to document their architectures and subject them to formal
verification.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" />
