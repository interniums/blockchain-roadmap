Two rows from a rollup's contract call log, a year apart.

```text
2025-08 : verifyBatch(proof, publicInputs)    calldata 256 B   covers    9,412 txs
2026-08 : verifyBatch(proof, publicInputs)    calldata 256 B   covers  418,730 txs
```

Same function, same calldata size, same gas. Forty-four times the work behind it. Nothing about the
verifier changed, and nothing about the proof system's succinctness was improved. What changed is
that the proof now stands for other proofs.

This lesson is about the single move that makes that possible, the two things it is used for that
get conflated, and the reason it is the part of a modern proving stack that security reviewers worry
about most.

## The move: a verifier is a program

Recall two facts from earlier in this module and put them next to each other.

**Fact one.** Any computation can be arithmetized: expressed as a constraint system such that a
satisfying assignment exists exactly when the computation ran correctly.

**Fact two.** A verification algorithm is a computation. For Groth16 it is a specific, small
sequence of group operations and one pairing product equation.<Cite src="groth16-paper" /> For a
hash-based system it is a large number of hash evaluations and Merkle path checks over a
transcript.<Cite src="ethstark-documentation" />

Therefore a verifier can be written as a circuit, and a proof can be produced whose statement is:
*"I know a proof `π` such that Verify(vk, x, π) accepts."* That proof attests to the acceptance of
another proof. That is the entirety of recursion. Everything else is engineering around making it
affordable.<Cite src="sok-zk-snarks-arxiv" />

The immediate consequence is the log above. If one proof can stand for two, it can stand for two
proofs each of which stood for two, and so on. Proof size stops depending on the amount of work
covered, because at every level the statement being proved is the same fixed-size verification
algorithm.

<Aside kind="note">
Notice how directly this depends on succinctness. If verification cost grew with the computation
being verified, the verifier-in-a-circuit would grow too, and each recursive layer would be more
expensive than the last — the recursion would diverge instead of converging. Succinctness is not
merely useful here; it is the precondition that makes the whole construction terminate.
</Aside>

## Why it is hard, in one specific way

The obstacle is arithmetic, and it is worth knowing because it explains a large amount of otherwise
opaque design vocabulary.

A pairing-based verifier operates on elliptic curve points whose coordinates live in one field —
call it the base field — while the circuit doing the verifying computes in a different field, the
curve's scalar field.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" /> Simulating base-field arithmetic inside a
scalar-field circuit means representing each element as several limbs and implementing carries and
reductions by hand, and the constraint count explodes. Naive recursion over a pairing-based system
is enormously expensive for this reason alone.

Two families of answer are in wide use.

**Curve cycles.** Pick two curves where each one's scalar field is the other's base field, and
alternate between them as you recurse, so the arithmetic is always native. Halo's contribution was
to show recursive proof composition *without a trusted setup*, by amortising the expensive part of
verification across a chain of proofs rather than performing it at every
step.<Cite src="halo-paper" /> The technique's descendants are the reason "no trusted setup and
recursive" stopped being a contradiction.

**Hash-based recursion.** If your verifier's work is dominated by hash evaluations rather than curve
arithmetic, and you choose a hash that is cheap in your constraint system, the verifier circuit
becomes tractable directly.<Cite src="ethstark-documentation" /><Cite src="cairo-paper" /> This is
the path most zkVM stacks take internally, and it is why the choice of hash function inside a
proving system is an architectural decision rather than a detail.

**Folding, a third answer that changes the question.** Nova's observation is that you do not always
need to *verify* a proof inside a circuit; you can instead *fold* two instances of the same relation
into a single instance, so that satisfying the folded instance implies satisfying both, and defer
the actual verification to one final step.<Cite src="nova-paper" /> Folding steps are far cheaper
than in-circuit verification, which is what makes incremental proving over long computations
practical.

## Three things recursion is used for

They are genuinely different, and conflating them is where most confusion starts.

### 1. Unbounded computation, constant proof

Prove a step, then prove "I verified the proof of the previous step and correctly executed one more
step". Repeat. The proof at step `n` is the same size as the proof at step 1, and it covers `n`
steps of work. This is incremental verifiable computation, and it is what Nova's folding scheme
targets.<Cite src="nova-paper" />

The product consequence: a chain, a machine, or a long-running computation can have a single
constant-size attestation to its entire history, rather than one proof per epoch that a verifier has
to accumulate.

### 2. Distributed proving

Split the trace into shards, prove each shard on a different machine in parallel, then recursively
combine the shard proofs into one. The wall-clock latency of proving a large computation becomes
roughly the latency of proving one shard plus the combination tree, instead of the latency of proving
the whole thing serially.

This is what makes the Foundation's latency envelope reachable at all: a 10-second budget for a
mainnet block is not a claim about a single fast machine, it is a claim about a
cluster.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" /> It also opens a genuinely different operating model —
research on community proving explores distributing rollup proving work across many independent,
heterogeneous machines rather than one operator's data
centre.<Cite src="arxiv-crowdprove" /><Cite src="arxiv-cost-effective-zk-rollups" />

### 3. Compression, which is the previous lesson's wrapper

Prove transparently, then produce a small pairing-based proof whose statement is "the transparent
proof verified". The recursion here is one layer deep and its purpose is purely to change the shape
of the final artefact for cheap on-chain
verification.<Cite src="groth16-paper" /><Cite src="eip-1108" />

### A recursion tree, counted

Work the second use concretely, because the numbers make the shape obvious in a way the prose does
not.

**Subgoal: pick the leaf size.** Say a block's execution trace is split into 64 shards, each small
enough to prove on one machine within your latency budget. Sixty-four independent proofs, produced
in parallel on 64 workers, in the time of one shard.

**Subgoal: combine pairwise.** A combining circuit takes two shard proofs and emits one proof
asserting that both verified. Sixty-four leaves fold to 32, then 16, 8, 4, 2, 1 — six levels, each
level's work parallel across its own nodes.

**Subgoal: count the wall clock, not the total work.** Total prover work went *up*: 64 shard proofs
plus 63 combining proofs. Wall-clock latency went down to roughly one shard proof plus six
sequential combining steps. That inversion — more total work, far less elapsed time — is the entire
reason distributed proving exists, and it is why proving-cost figures and proving-latency figures
move in opposite directions when a team adds machines.<Cite src="arxiv-cost-effective-zk-rollups" />

**Subgoal: notice where the shards have to agree.** The shards are not independent claims. Shard
`i+1` begins in the state shard `i` ended in, so the combining circuit must check that the boundary
values match — and if it does not, a prover can submit shards from different executions and have
them accepted as one. That boundary check is exactly the public-input discipline of the first lesson,
applied at every internal node of the tree rather than at the contract.

**Subgoal: name the glue.** Everything above is a design: how many shards, which circuits combine
which, what the boundary conditions are, and how the final wrapper attaches. That design is what the
Foundation means by teams composing circuits with recursion in custom ways, with lots of glue in
between.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" />

## Aggregation is a use, not a synonym

Now the distinction the concept exists to make.

**Recursion** is the general capability: a verifier expressed as a circuit, so a proof can attest to
proofs.

**Aggregation** is the specific application: take `N` independent proofs, of possibly unrelated
statements, and arrange for a verifier to check once instead of `N` times, amortising the on-chain
verification cost across the batch. Aggregation-layer designs make exactly this their product —
collecting proofs from many systems and producing a single verification on
Ethereum.<Cite src="aligned-whitepaper" /><Cite src="aligned-verification-vs-aggregation" />

The two are not the same thing, in both directions.

**Recursion without aggregation** is the incremental case above: one continuing computation, not
many independent claims.

**Aggregation without full recursion** also exists, which is the half people miss. Batching
techniques can amortise verification without a verifier-in-a-circuit at all. For pairing-based
proofs sharing a verification key, a random linear combination lets one batched pairing check stand
for many individual ones — EIP-1108's own rationale notes that cheaper precompiles aid in batching
together multiple zk-SNARK proofs.<Cite src="eip-1108" /> On the hash-based side, several low-degree
tests can be batched into one rather than each being verified separately. Neither of those is
recursion; both are aggregation.

### What aggregation always costs

One trade is unavoidable and it is not cryptographic: **aggregation buys gas with latency.**

The batch must fill before it can be proved. If your aggregation window is five minutes, then a
proof submitted at second one waits five minutes before its batch is even assembled, plus the
proving time of the aggregate, plus inclusion. Every participant's tail latency is set by the batch
policy, not by their own submission.

That produces a design dial rather than an optimisation: larger batches mean lower per-proof gas and
worse tail latency, and where you set it is a product decision about what your users are waiting
for.<Cite src="aligned-verification-vs-aggregation" /> A withdrawal flow and a game have opposite
answers.

<Misconception
  belief="A constant-size proof means constant proving cost."
  reality="Proof size stays flat while the work behind it grows. The proving cost does not — it still scales with the total work, plus the overhead of every recursive layer. Recursion moves cost from the verifier to the prover; it does not remove it."
  why="'Constant-size proof for unbounded computation' is stated as the headline property, and it is a claim about one of the two costs."
  src="nova-paper"

## Why reviewers worry about this layer

Recursion is the part of a modern proving stack where soundness is hardest to argue, for three
compounding reasons.

**Soundness errors accumulate.** Each layer has a soundness error, and a composition's error is at
best the sum. A tower deep enough to be useful is a tower with enough layers for that sum to matter,
which is one of the reasons the security-bit question from the first lesson is answered by a tool
that models the whole system rather than by reading a single parameter off one
protocol.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" />

**A bug in the verifier circuit is a total break.** The recursive step's circuit *is* a verifier, and
an under-constrained verifier circuit accepts proofs it should reject — with the result that
everything above it in the tower inherits a false statement as though it were checked. This is the
third lesson's bug class applied to the most consequential circuit in the stack.

**Every layer re-applies Fiat–Shamir.** Each recursive verification has its own transcript, hashed
inside a circuit. Everything from the fourth lesson applies at every level, and the transcripts have
to be complete at every level.<Cite src="iop-paper" />

The Foundation's write-up states the operational reality without softening it: modern zkEVMs involve
many circuits composed with recursion in custom ways, with lots of glue in between, and each team
does it differently — which is why documenting the architecture and its soundness is described as
essential for the security of the entire system.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" /> The
milestones follow from that: a compact description of the recursion architecture with a sketch of
its soundness by the end of May 2026, then a formal security argument for the soundness of the
recursion architecture by the end of 2026.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" />

<Aside kind="warn">
Read that "each team does it differently" as the operational warning it is. There is no standard
recursion topology to review against, no shared specification, and — unlike Fiat–Shamir, which at
least has a stateable rule of thumb — no one-line correctness criterion. When you assess a proving
stack, the recursion architecture is the component least likely to have an artefact you can check
and most likely to be described in a diagram rather than a specification.
</Aside>

<Check type="explain" concept="recursion">
A rollup team proposes this change: instead of posting one validity proof per batch, they will
aggregate one hour of batch proofs into a single proof and post that.

Set out what improves, what gets worse, and what does not change at all. Then name the two questions
you would ask before approving it.

<Answer>
**What improves.** On-chain verification cost per batch. One aggregate verification replaces roughly
sixty individual ones, and since the verification cost per proof is roughly fixed, the saving is close
to proportional.<Cite src="groth16-paper" /><Cite src="eip-1108" /> Calldata for proofs drops by the
same factor.

**What gets worse.**

*Latency to L1 finality, for everyone.* A batch proved at minute two now waits until the hour closes
before its proof is even assembled, then waits for the aggregate proving time. Withdrawals that
depend on a proven state root inherit the whole delay.<Cite src="aligned-verification-vs-aggregation" />

*Failure blast radius.* One aggregate proof failing to produce — a prover crash, an
out-of-memory, a bug in one shard — now blocks an hour of batches rather than one. The aggregation
step is a new single point of failure with an hour of work behind it.

*Soundness surface.* An aggregation circuit is a verifier circuit, and it is now in the trust path
for every batch.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" />

**What does not change.**

*Data availability.* Batch data still has to be posted for the chain to be a rollup; aggregating
proofs changes nothing about that.

*Soft confirmation latency.* Users' sub-second confirmations come from the sequencer, not from the
proof. This proposal does not touch them — which is worth saying explicitly, because "we made
proving slower" is often heard as "the chain got slower".

*Total proving cost.* The work behind the proofs is unchanged; recursion adds the aggregation
overhead on top. The prover's bill goes slightly up, not down.<Cite src="nova-paper" />

**The two questions.**

*"What is the withdrawal-facing latency at the 99th percentile after this change, and who has agreed
to it?"* The gas saving accrues to the operator and the latency cost is paid by users. If nobody has
priced that trade explicitly, the proposal is an unexamined transfer.

*"What is the recursion architecture, and what is its soundness argument?"* Not "which library" —
the architecture. The Foundation's programme asks for exactly this artefact because each team's glue
is bespoke, and there is no standard to check against.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" />
If the answer is a slide, the change is not ready.

**One thing worth proposing instead.** An adaptive window: aggregate up to a size or time bound,
whichever comes first, with an escape hatch that flushes early when a withdrawal is pending. That
keeps most of the gas saving and removes the worst of the tail. It is the same shape as every other
batching decision you have made — the cryptography did not change what kind of problem it is.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

**This closes the module.** You now have the layer underneath the fraud-versus-validity comparison
you met in Scaling: what the proof is claiming (a relation, with a line drawn between statement and
witness), which of the two properties it actually uses, how the computation became algebra, how the
verifier was removed from the protocol, where the parameters came from, and why production runs two
proof systems in series.<Cite src="ethereum-org-zkevm-roadmap" />

**The commitments module** goes one layer further down, to the polynomial commitment schemes that
every one of these systems is built on. The powers of tau, the KZG opening, and the blob commitments
you already know from the protocol side all turn out to be the same object seen from different
angles.<Cite src="plonk-paper" />

**The on-chain verification module** turns the gas arithmetic in this module into measurement on a
real deployed verifier, and asks the question this module deliberately left open: what a valid proof
does *not* authorise.<Cite src="eip-1108" />

**And the reflection prompt for this module** is worth doing properly. Take a system marketed as
zero-knowledge, work out which of the two properties it uses, and write down what an observer of its
on-chain data can learn about an individual user. If the answer is "everything except the execution
trace", you have found the gap between the name and the
mechanism — and you can now explain exactly where it came from.<Cite src="succinct-sp1-docs" /><Cite src="sok-zk-snarks-arxiv" />
