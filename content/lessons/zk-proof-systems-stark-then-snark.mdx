Two proof systems, in series, for one claim. If either family were simply better, nobody would run
both. This lesson is about why the pipeline has that shape, and about the discipline required to
talk about its costs without saying something false.

## Two costs that are not the same cost

Separate them before anything else, because most confused arguments about proof systems are two
people using "cost" for different quantities.

**Verification cost** is paid on-chain, by every verifier, on every proof. It is public, it is
denominated in gas, and it is small.

**Proving cost** is paid off-chain, once per proof, by whoever runs the prover. It is denominated in
machine-hours and electricity, and it is very large.

The Ethereum Foundation's definition of realtime proving is the clearest possible demonstration
that these are different animals, because of what it chooses to measure. Its working definition
lists: latency of 10 seconds or less for the 99th percentile of mainnet blocks, on-premises capital
expenditure of 100,000 USD or less, on-premises power of 10 kW or less, fully open-source code,
security of 128 bits or more, and proof size of 300 KiB or less with no trusted
setups.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" /> Two of those six are a hardware budget and an electricity
budget.

The reasoning behind the power figure is worth repeating because it makes the physicality concrete:
the target is home proving by solo stakers, and most residential homes have at least 10 kW entering
from the street, with some circuits intended for electric appliances or vehicle charging at that
capacity — so realtime proving must be possible at 10 kW or less.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" />
The latency figure comes from the same kind of reasoning: a 12-second slot minus roughly 1.5 seconds
of maximum network propagation leaves 10 seconds.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" />

Nobody writes a specification in kilowatts for something that is merely a fee.

## The asymmetry is the entire economic argument

State it plainly. The prover pays a large multiple of the cost of simply running the computation.
Every verifier pays a small, roughly constant amount that does not grow with the computation. That
gap is not an inefficiency to be optimised away; it *is* the product. You are buying the right to
have many parties be convinced of something none of them ran.

The multiple has an unhelpful history. Figures in the folklore range put prover overhead at five to
six orders of magnitude relative to native execution, and modern zkVMs are far below that — but no
uniform 2026 benchmark measuring the same workload across systems on the same hardware could be
located. Treat the overhead as an order-of-magnitude intuition, never as a number you quote. What
does not vary is the direction: proving is much more expensive than executing, and verification is
much cheaper.<Cite src="sok-zk-snarks-arxiv" />

Research on proving infrastructure treats the consequence as an optimisation problem in its own
right — how to provision and schedule prover hardware for a rollup, and where the bottlenecks in a
production proving pipeline actually
sit.<Cite src="arxiv-cost-effective-zk-rollups" /><Cite src="arxiv-zk-rollup-bottlenecks" /> That is
the shape of a real operational discipline, not a footnote.

### Where the prover's money actually goes

Worth knowing in outline, because it explains why the answer to "make it faster" is so often
"buy different hardware" rather than "write better code".

**Witness generation.** The prover runs the computation and fills in the trace table from the third
lesson. For a zkVM this means executing the guest program and recording every cycle's machine state.
Cheap relative to what follows, but it sets the table's height, and the table's height sets
everything downstream.<Cite src="succinct-sp1-docs" />

**Committing to the trace.** Every column becomes a polynomial that must be committed to. In
hash-based systems that is a very large number of hash evaluations over a table with millions of
rows; in pairing-based systems it is a multi-scalar multiplication over as many curve points. Either
way this is the dominant term, and it is dominated in turn by memory bandwidth rather than by
arithmetic cleverness.

**The interactive rounds, collapsed.** Evaluations, quotient computation, low-degree
testing — polynomial arithmetic at scale, with large transforms that are bandwidth-bound for the
same reason.<Cite src="ethstark-documentation" />

That profile is why hardware acceleration is a first-class part of the tooling rather than an
optimisation afterthought: the work is wide, uniform and parallel, which is precisely what GPUs
are for.<Cite src="sp1-docs-hardware-acceleration" /><Cite src="risc0-zkvm-docs" /> It is also why
the Foundation wrote a power ceiling into its definition — a wide parallel workload run at home has
a wall socket as its binding constraint.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" />

**And it is why the wrapper is affordable.** The recursive step proves a *verifier*, and a verifier
is small — the whole point of succinctness is that verification does not scale with the computation
it checks.<Cite src="groth16-paper" /> So wrapping adds a proving job whose size is set by the
verification algorithm, not by the original workload. That asymmetry is what makes the pipeline
practical rather than merely possible.

## Why the on-chain end wants a pairing

Now the mechanical reason the pipeline ends the way it does.

A Groth16 proof is three group elements, and verification is one pairing product equation using
three pairings.<Cite src="groth16-paper" /> On Ethereum, that maps onto a precompile: the pairing
check at address `0x08` costs `34,000 * k + 45,000` gas for `k` pairs under EIP-1108's
schedule,<Cite src="eip-1108" /> consuming 192 bytes per pair — 64 of G1 and 128 of
G2.<Cite src="eip-197" /> Three pairings is a 147,000-gas precompile floor, plus 6,000 for an
`ECMUL` and 150 for an `ECADD` per public input.<Cite src="eip-1108" /> Calldata: 256 bytes.

A transparent hash-based proof verified directly on-chain has an entirely different profile. Its
verifier's work is dominated by hash evaluations and Merkle path checks over a proof that the
Foundation's own milestones cap at 600 KiB, tightening to 300 KiB — sizes chosen so proofs can
propagate across Ethereum's peer-to-peer network reliably and in
time.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" /> Every one of those kilobytes must arrive as
calldata and every hash must be executed by the EVM. There is no precompile that collapses a
low-degree test into one instruction the way `0x08` collapses a pairing.

So the two families sit at opposite ends of a single trade:

| | Pairing-based (Groth16, PLONK) | Transparent (STARK, FRI-based) |
|---|---|---|
| Setup | Required; a ceremony<Cite src="gnark-schemes-curves" /> | None<Cite src="stark-paper" /> |
| Proof size | Hundreds of bytes<Cite src="groth16-paper" /> | Hundreds of kilobytes<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" /> |
| On-chain verification | A precompile call<Cite src="eip-1108" /> | Many hashes plus large calldata |
| Post-quantum posture | Broken by a quantum adversary | Rests on hash security only<Cite src="stark-paper" /> |
| Proving speed at scale | Slower for very large statements | The reason it is used for the bulk |

Neither column dominates. So production takes the left column's verification cost and the right
column's setup story, and pays for the combination with a recursion step: prove the bulk of the work
transparently, then run a circuit that *verifies that proof* and emit a small pairing-based proof of
that verification. The next lesson builds the mechanism; here, the point is that the wrapper exists
for one reason, and that reason is L1 gas.

<Aside kind="warn">
A precise claim to keep straight: a system that proves with a STARK and wraps in Groth16 **has a
trusted setup**. The bulk proving step does not, and the thing an L1 contract verifies does. "We use
STARKs, so no trusted setup" is a true statement about one stage of a pipeline being presented as a
statement about the pipeline.
</Aside>

One nuance about "constant" before moving on, because it is load-bearing in design reviews. The
pairing count for a Groth16 check is fixed, but the linear combination over public inputs is
not — it grows with how many public inputs the circuit
declares.<Cite src="eip-1108" /><Cite src="groth16-paper" /> So verification cost is constant in the
size of the *computation* and linear in the size of the *interface*. Teams respond by hashing many
logical public values into one field element, which works, and then owe the contract an explicit
check that the preimage of that hash is what they think it is. The saving is real; the obligation it
creates is the first lesson's public-input discipline arriving as a gas optimisation.

## The collision that is redirecting the L1 path

Two facts you now hold separately, put together.

**Fact one.** The Foundation's realtime-proving definition requires proof size under 300 KiB **with
no trusted setups**, and states that proof systems must not rely on recursive wrappers that use
trusted setups.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" /> That is a direct prohibition of the pipeline
above, for this use case.

**Fact two.** BN254, the curve behind Ethereum's alt_bn128 precompiles and therefore behind the
cheapest available on-chain pairing check, offers roughly 100 bits of security rather than the 128
it was once thought to have; BLS12-381 was designed for around 128 and later analysis places it
lower, with one report cited by the eth2 book giving roughly 117 to 120 bits and the IETF draft
citing 126.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" /> Sources disagree on the precise figures. Meanwhile the
Foundation's end-of-2026 milestone asks for 128-bit *provable*
security.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" />

Together those explain a direction of travel that otherwise looks arbitrary. The cheapest wrapper
available on Ethereum today is both prohibited by the L1 zkEVM definition and, on the numbers, below
the security level that definition targets. So the L1 path cannot take the shortcut that L2s take,
and has to make transparent proofs small enough to verify directly — which is precisely why the
Foundation's roadmap post talks about compact polynomial commitment schemes, jagged commitment
techniques, grinding and recursion topology as the tractable path to those
targets.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" />

None of that says L2 wrapping is wrong. L2s verify their own proofs in their own contracts under
their own risk tolerance, and a 147,000-gas pairing check is an extremely good deal. It says the two
settings have different constraints, and a claim proved for one does not transfer.

### The trade in one sentence per party

Three parties, three different bills, and confusion between them is what makes proof-system
arguments circular.

**The prover** pays machine-hours and electricity, unconditionally, for every proof, scaling with
the work proved. Their optimisation is throughput per watt, and their lever is hardware and
arithmetization.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" />

**The chain** pays verification gas, per proof posted, roughly independent of the work
proved.<Cite src="groth16-paper" /> Their lever is the shape of the final proof — which is exactly
what the wrapper controls.

**The user** pays latency: how long between submitting something and the proof covering it landing.
Their lever is nobody's priority by default, which is why it is worth naming. Aggregating more work
into one proof lowers the per-item gas and raises the wait, because the batch has to fill before it
can be proved. The next lesson makes that trade explicit.

A design conversation that names which of those three it is optimising will converge. One that says
"cheaper" without saying "for whom" will not.

## Reading a proving number without being misled

This is the most portable skill in the lesson, so give it its own procedure.

**Every proving claim needs four attachments: the workload, the hardware, the unit, and the date.**
Strip any of the four and the number becomes unusable — and, worse, comparable to numbers it has no
relationship with.

Here is what a fully attached claim looks like. In December 2025, the Foundation summarised nine
months of ecosystem progress as proving latency dropping from 16 minutes to 16 seconds, costs
collapsing 45-fold, and zkVMs proving 99% of all Ethereum blocks in under 10 seconds on target
hardware.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" /> Workload: Ethereum L1 blocks. Hardware: the
target hardware defined by the realtime-proving envelope.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" /> Unit:
wall-clock seconds per block, and a relative cost multiple. Date: December 2025.

Now three traps, all of which are live.

**Trap one: the same vendor's posts do not agree.** Succinct's reporting on SP1 Hypercube has been
published more than once with different figures — a percentage of blocks proved under 12 seconds in
one post, different percentages under 12 and under 10 seconds in
another.<Cite src="succinct-sp1-hypercube" /><Cite src="succinct-realtime-proving" /> These come from
different posts and hardware configurations and have not been reconciled. Do not quote a single
percentage; quote the post and the configuration, or quote neither.

**Trap two: L1 block proving is not L2 batch proving.** They are different workloads by an enormous
factor, and the seconds-scale figures above are for the first. Published latency claims for
production L2 batch proving run into tens of minutes and beyond, across different systems, years and
hardware, and the figures in circulation have not been reconciled into anything
comparable.<Cite src="arxiv-zk-rollup-bottlenecks" /> Quoting an L1 block-proving latency as though
it described an L2's batch cadence is the single most common error in this area.

**Trap three: cost claims mix units.** Figures quoted "per proof" and figures quoted "per
transaction" are not the same quantity and cannot be compared, and both circulate for the same
systems. No cost number in this track appears without its unit, because the ones in circulation do
not resolve into a single comparable
scale.<Cite src="arxiv-cost-effective-zk-rollups" />

<Aside kind="note">
The same discipline applies to the verification side, where it is easier to check and therefore less
excusable. The widely repeated Groth16 verifier gas formula — a base of roughly 207,000 gas plus
about 7,160 per public input, with a comparable pair quoted for FFLONK — traces to a single 2024
community analysis rather than a specification, and has not been independently
confirmed.<Cite src="orbiter-verifier-gas" /> The precompile floor computed above is derived from
EIP-1108's schedule and is solid;<Cite src="eip-1108" /> the full-verifier figures are folklore.
The on-chain verification module measures a deployed verifier rather than repeating either.
</Aside>

**Question 1: "Under 10 seconds on what hardware, over what sample, and on what date?"**

Testing whether the latency claim has its attachments. The Foundation's own realtime definition
pairs a 10-second figure with a 99th-percentile target, a 100,000 USD capital expenditure ceiling
and a 10 kW power ceiling, because the number is meaningless without the
machine.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" /> A vendor quoting the latency but not the hardware is
quoting half a specification. Ask also for the percentile: a median under 10 seconds and a 99th
percentile under 10 seconds are different products, and the tail is what breaks a slot.

**Question 2: "Is that cost per proof or per transaction, and how many transactions were in the
block you measured?"**

Testing the unit. Per-proof and per-transaction figures are routinely quoted for the same systems
and are not comparable quantities; the conversion between them is the block's transaction count,
which the claim omits.<Cite src="arxiv-cost-effective-zk-rollups" /> Ask what is included, too:
amortised hardware, electricity, spot-instance pricing, or marginal compute only. Those differ by
more than the number itself.

**Question 3: "What verifies your proof on L1, and does that component have a trusted setup?"**

Testing the setup claim, which as written is almost certainly incomplete. If the final on-chain
verification is a pairing check, there is a pairing-based wrapper and therefore a ceremony
somewhere.<Cite src="gnark-schemes-curves" /> If they genuinely verify a transparent proof directly
on L1, ask for the gas and the calldata size, because the proof sizes at stake are measured in
hundreds of kilobytes.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" />

**What you would flag regardless of the answers.** Three items.

*The latency claim's workload.* If the 10 seconds is for L1 block proving and your system needs L2
batch proving, the number does not transfer at all — different workload by a large
factor.<Cite src="arxiv-zk-rollup-bottlenecks" />

*The security bits, provable or conjectured.* Ask which, and ask whether the estimate was produced
by the Foundation's `soundcalc`, which exists precisely so this number is computed against current
bounds and known attacks rather than asserted.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" />

*The curve, if there is a wrapper.* A BN254 wrapper sits at roughly 100 bits, which caps the whole
pipeline's security regardless of what the transparent stage
achieves.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" /> A chain is as strong as its weakest composed stage, and
the wrapper is a stage.

## What this unlocks

**Next lesson**: recursion, which is the mechanism the wrapper uses and much more besides. Once a
verifier can be expressed as a circuit, the same move gives you constant-size proofs over unbounded
work, distributed proving across many machines, and batch aggregation — and it also multiplies the
places a soundness argument has to hold, which is why the Foundation asks for the recursion
architecture to be documented and formally
argued.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" /><Cite src="halo-paper" />

**The on-chain verification module** turns the gas discussion here into measurement: a real deployed
verifier, its actual gas, and where it goes.<Cite src="eip-197" />

**And whenever you read a proving benchmark**, including one from a source you trust: workload,
hardware, unit, date. Four attachments. A number missing any of them is not a weak number, it is not
a number at all — and the reason to be strict about it is that the field's own vendors and its own
foundation publish figures that cannot be reconciled with each
other.<Cite src="succinct-sp1-docs" /><Cite src="l2beat-glossary" /><Cite src="ethereum-org-zkevm-roadmap" />
