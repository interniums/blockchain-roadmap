Open two block explorers side by side.

The first belongs to a chain that the industry, its own documentation, and most of the press call a
zero-knowledge rollup. Pick any block. You can read every transaction in it: sender, recipient,
calldata, amounts, the lot. Sort by value and find the largest transfer of the day. Click through to
the sender and read their entire history.

The second belongs to a shielded pool. Pick any block. You get a list of commitments and nullifiers
— opaque 32-byte values — and no sender, no recipient, no amount.<Cite src="zcash-protocol-spec" />

Both systems use the same underlying technology. One of them is named after a property it does not
use. This lesson is about why that happened and what it costs you when you reason about either.

## Two properties, and they are independent

The previous lesson listed three properties of a proof system: completeness, soundness, knowledge
soundness. Here are the two that get confused, and they are both *additional* to those three.

**Succinctness** is a statement about the verifier's bill. A proof system is succinct when the proof
size and the verification time grow far more slowly than the computation being proved — in practice
constant or polylogarithmic in the size of that
computation.<Cite src="sok-zk-snarks-arxiv" /><Cite src="groth16-paper" /> That is the property
that makes an L1 contract able to accept the outcome of a computation it could never afford to run.

**Zero-knowledge** is a statement about what leaks. A proof is zero-knowledge when the verifier
learns nothing from it beyond the fact that the statement is true. The formal test is simulation: if
an algorithm that does *not* have the witness can produce transcripts indistinguishable from real
proofs, then real proofs cannot be carrying any information about the witness, because the simulator
had none to
carry.<Cite src="sok-zk-snarks-arxiv" />

Those two definitions do not mention each other. Nothing in "the verifier's work is
polylogarithmic" implies anything about leakage, and nothing in "the transcript is simulatable"
implies anything about size. They are orthogonal axes, and every combination is occupied.

<Aside kind="note">
Be careful with the top-right cell rather than clever about it. RFC 8235's non-interactive Schnorr
proof is zero-knowledge and its proof is two field values, which sounds succinct.<Cite src="rfc-8235" />
It is not an instance of succinctness, because succinctness is a claim about *scaling with the size
of a computation*, and there is no computation here to scale with — the statement is a single
discrete-log relation of fixed size. Calling it succinct is a category error in the same family as
calling a constant-time function "O(1) in the input". A protocol for a fixed one-line statement
neither exhibits nor refutes the property.
</Aside>

### What succinctness costs and buys, in bytes

Make it concrete, because "sublinear" is easy to nod along to.

A Groth16 proof is three group elements, and verification is one pairing product equation using
three pairings in total.<Cite src="groth16-paper" /> On Ethereum's alt_bn128 curve, the pairing
precompile consumes its input in 192-byte chunks — 64 bytes of G1 and 128 bytes of G2 per
pair<Cite src="eip-197" /> — so those three elements, two in G1 and one in G2, are 256 bytes. That
is the `uint256[8]` from the previous lesson's verifier signature: eight 32-byte coordinates,
nothing more.

The verification cost has the same shape. Under EIP-1108's schedule, the pairing check at address
`0x08` costs `34,000 * k + 45,000` gas for `k` pairs,<Cite src="eip-1108" /> so a three-pairing
Groth16 check has a precompile floor of 147,000 gas, plus one `ECMUL` at 6,000 and one `ECADD` at
150 per public input for the linear combination.<Cite src="eip-1108" /> That floor is the same
whether the proof covers ten thousand constraints or ten billion.

<Aside kind="warn">
That 147,000 is a precompile floor, not a verifier's gas cost. Circulating full-verifier formulas —
the widely repeated `~207k + 7.16k` per public input for Groth16, and a comparable pair for
FFLONK — trace back to a single 2024 community analysis rather than to a specification, and have not
been independently confirmed.<Cite src="orbiter-verifier-gas" /> Treat them as folklore with a
plausible shape. The on-chain verification module measures a real deployed verifier instead.
</Aside>

Transparent hash-based proofs sit at the other end. Rather than quote a size that would be
meaningless without its parameters, take the constraint the Ethereum Foundation wrote into its own
L1 zkEVM programme: final proof size of 600 KiB or less by its May 2026 milestone, tightening to
300 KiB or less by its end-of-2026 milestone.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" /> Those are
ceilings a proof must fit under to be considered acceptable, and they are three to four orders of
magnitude above 256 bytes. Both families are succinct in the formal sense — verification does not
scale with the computation — and they differ by a factor of a thousand on the constant. The sixth
lesson is about what production does with that gap.

### Three things succinctness does not say

The definition is narrow, and each thing it excludes is something people read into it.

**It does not say proving is cheap.** Succinctness constrains the verifier's side of the ledger and
says nothing about the prover's. The prover's cost is a large multiple of running the computation
natively — the Foundation's own realtime-proving definition budgets for it in capital expenditure
and kilowatts rather than in gas, because it is a physical process with a power
draw.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" /> The sixth lesson of this module treats that asymmetry as the
economic point rather than an implementation detail.

**It does not say the verifier holds nothing large.** Most practical systems are *preprocessing*
arguments: a one-off setup step digests the constraint system into a verification key that the
verifier keeps. Verification is succinct given that key; obtaining it is
not.<Cite src="sok-zk-snarks-arxiv" /><Cite src="groth16-paper" /> On-chain, this shows up as
kilobytes of curve points baked into the deployed verifier contract. The fifth lesson is about where
those bytes came from and who could have poisoned them.

**It does not say the verification cost is constant in everything.** For Groth16, the pairing count
is fixed but the linear combination over public inputs grows with the number of public
inputs<Cite src="eip-1108" /> — which is a real design pressure: teams hash many logical public
values into a single field element to keep that term down, and then have to remember that the
contract must check the preimage of that hash rather than trust it.

## Zero-knowledge is a deliberate, separable addition

Here is the mechanical reason the two properties come apart.

In most systems, hiding the witness is achieved by *blinding*: the prover adds randomness to the
committed polynomials in a way that leaves every checked identity satisfied but makes the openings
the verifier sees carry no information about the underlying values. PLONK, for instance, is
presented with the blinding scalars that give zero-knowledge as an explicit part of the
construction.<Cite src="plonk-paper" /> Groth's construction states zero-knowledge as a property of
the scheme alongside its size result.<Cite src="groth16-paper" />

The important engineering consequence: **blinding is a feature you can leave switched off, and
switching it off does not weaken soundness.** A prover who does not blind produces a proof that is
just as convincing and possibly leakier. If nothing in your statement is secret, there is nothing to
leak, and the question never arises — which is the situation almost every deployed Ethereum system
is in.

## Why production validity rollups use exactly one of them

Now the payoff, and it follows from something you already know from the Scaling track rather than
from any cryptography.

A rollup is a rollup because its data is published where anyone can reconstruct its state
independently of the operator. That is the whole definition — the property that separates a rollup
from a validium is where the data goes, not what the proof
proves.<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" /> Since EIP-4844 that publication usually happens in
blobs.<Cite src="eip-4844" />

So a validity rollup publishes every transaction, by construction, and then proves that its state
transition followed the rules. The proof buys **compression of verification**: the L1 contract
checks one succinct argument instead of re-executing a batch. It buys nothing about privacy, because
the inputs were published in full before the proof was ever posted. Zero-knowledge is not merely
unused; it would be pointless, since the witness is public.

The accurate name for such a system is a **validity rollup**. L2BEAT's glossary uses exactly that
framing when classifying proof systems.<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" /> The name "ZK rollup" survives
because it was there first and it markets better.

### The same confusion, one layer down: the L1 zkEVM

The confusion is not confined to L2 marketing. Ethereum's own roadmap page for using proofs to
verify L1 block execution opens with a disambiguation note telling readers not to confuse it with ZK
rollups.<Cite src="ethereum-org-zkevm-roadmap" /> The page is dated 24 June
2026.<Cite src="ethereum-org-zkevm-roadmap" />

The L1 zkEVM is the purest possible example of succinctness without zero-knowledge. Its purpose is
to move Ethereum from a verification model where every validator re-executes every transaction —
the page calls this N-of-N — to one where a prover executes and everyone else verifies a proof, a
1-of-N model.<Cite src="ethereum-org-zkevm-roadmap" /> Every input is a public Ethereum block that
the whole network already has. Nothing is hidden from anyone. The word "zk" in "zkEVM" is doing no
work whatsoever except historical continuity, and the benefit claimed is throughput and lower
validator hardware requirements, not privacy.<Cite src="ethereum-org-zkevm-roadmap" />

The Foundation's own definition of the target reinforces this: the requirements it lists are
latency, capital expenditure, power draw, open source, security bits and proof
size.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" /> Not one of them mentions confidentiality.

## What privacy actually requires

If succinctness is not privacy, what is? The answer is a design change, not a component swap, and it
is worth knowing the shape now because the last module of this track builds it.

A system that hides state cannot keep an account-and-balance model, because a balance that updates
is a balance that can be watched. Shielded designs replace it: value lives in **notes**, each
represented on-chain only by a commitment; spending a note publishes a **nullifier** derived from it,
which prevents double-spending without revealing which note was
spent.<Cite src="zcash-protocol-spec" /> The proof's job is to assert that the spender knows a note
in the commitment set, that the nullifier is correctly derived from it, and that inputs balance
outputs — all with the note itself as witness. Aztec applies the same shape to a general-purpose L2
with private contract state,<Cite src="aztec-docs" /> and Railgun applies it as shielded balances
on top of existing chains.<Cite src="railgun-docs" />

That is a different state model, a different fee model and a different user experience. You do not
get there by adding a SNARK to an account-based system.

### What to call things, and why it is worth the pedantry

Three renamings survive contact with an engineering conversation, and using them changes what people
notice.

| Common name | Accurate name | What the rename surfaces |
|---|---|---|
| ZK rollup | Validity rollup<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" /> | The data is public. Compression of verification, not of information. |
| zk-SNARK, used publicly | Succinct argument | It is an argument with a security level in bits, not an absolute proof.<Cite src="sok-zk-snarks-arxiv" /> |
| "we added zero-knowledge" | "we made a public claim cheap to check" | Forces the question of which value was ever secret. |

The pedantry earns its keep in two specific rooms. In a design review, "validity rollup" stops
somebody assuming user data is protected and skipping a privacy analysis. In a compliance
conversation, calling a validity rollup a privacy technology is a factual error about a system whose
entire transaction history is publicly readable — and the error is much harder to retract after it
has been written into a policy document.

### And privacy of the witness is not anonymity

One more separation, because it catches teams that got everything above right.

Zero-knowledge hides the witness from the verifier. It says nothing about who submitted the
transaction, from which address, paying which gas, at what time, from which IP, with what deposit
history. Those are metadata, and metadata is exactly what deanonymisation research on Ethereum works
from — profiling and linking users from on-chain behaviour patterns rather than from any
cryptographic break.<Cite src="arxiv-ethereum-deanonymization" /> A shielded withdrawal to an
address that has never held anything else, funded minutes earlier from an exchange, has a very small
effective anonymity set no matter how good the proof is. Privacy-pool designs make the
association-set question explicit precisely because the proof alone does not settle
it.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs" />

1. A validity rollup running a general-purpose EVM, posting batches to blobs.
2. Ethereum L1 verifying block execution with a zkEVM proof instead of re-executing.
3. A shielded pool where users deposit ETH and later withdraw to a fresh address.
4. A contract that accepts a proof that "this address holds a credential from an approved issuer",
   with the credential as witness.

<Answer>
**1 — succinctness only.** The observer learns everything: every transaction, its sender, its
calldata, its value, and the full history of any address. The data is published in blobs because
publishing it is what makes the system a rollup rather than a validium.<Cite src="l2beat-glossary" /><Cite src="eip-4844" />
The proof compresses *verification*, not information. Calling it a "ZK rollup" is a naming
convention, and treating it as a privacy control is a compliance mistake with real consequences.

**2 — succinctness only, and the extreme case of it.** The observer learns exactly what they learned
before: Ethereum blocks are public. Nothing is hidden from anyone, and nothing is meant to be. The
purpose is to replace N-of-N re-execution with 1-of-N proving so the gas limit stops being pinned to
validator hardware.<Cite src="ethereum-org-zkevm-roadmap" />

**3 — both.** Succinctness because the membership proof over a large commitment set must be cheap to
verify on-chain; zero-knowledge because which note is being spent is the witness. The observer
learns: that a deposit of some denomination occurred, that a withdrawal occurred, the nullifier
(which links to no note anyone can identify), and all of the timing, gas payment and address-reuse
metadata.<Cite src="zcash-protocol-spec" /> Whether those last items collapse the anonymity set is
an operational question the cryptography does not answer.<Cite src="arxiv-ethereum-deanonymization" />

**4 — both, and this is the case where the second property is doing real work at small scale.** The
observer learns that this address holds *some* credential from the approved set, plus everything in
the public statement. That residue is not nothing: the very fact of holding an approved credential
may be sensitive, and if the approved set has one member, the proof has revealed the issuer. Zero-
knowledge means the verifier learns nothing *beyond the truth of the statement* — so the leak
budget is set by how you wrote the statement, which is the previous lesson's whole point.

**The cross-cutting observation.** Only two of the four use zero-knowledge, and both of those had to
change their data model to make it meaningful. The two that do not are the two that carry "zk" in
their names.
</Answer>
</Check>

## The question to ask about your own feature

Reduce all of this to something you can run in a design meeting.

**Subgoal: name the expensive verification, if there is one.** Is there a computation that some party
must currently trust rather than check, because checking it would cost more than the thing is worth?
If yes, succinctness is on the table. If no — if everyone who cares can already afford to re-run it
— a proof system adds a prover, a verifier contract, a setup story and a new bug class in exchange
for nothing.

**Subgoal: name the value that must stay secret, if there is one.** Not "sensitive data" in the
privacy-policy sense. A specific value that the verifier must not learn while still being convinced
of a claim that depends on it. If there is no such value, zero-knowledge is not part of your design,
and any effort spent on blinding is wasted.

**Subgoal: check whether the secret value can survive being a witness.** This is where most privacy
features die. If the secret has to be readable by a second party later, or updated by a contract, or
enumerated for accounting, then it cannot live purely in the witness and you are back to a state
model change.<Cite src="aztec-docs" />

**Subgoal: write the leak budget explicitly.** Zero-knowledge means the verifier learns nothing
beyond the truth of the statement. So write the statement down in English and read it as an
adversary: everything it asserts is disclosed. "This address holds a credential from the approved
set" discloses that the address holds a credential from the approved set, forever, to
everyone.<Cite src="privacy-pools-docs" />

Two of those four subgoals are about succinctness and two are about zero-knowledge, and a feature
can need either, both or neither. Most need the first pair only.

marketing. Does this system's verifier cost grow with the computation? Is any input to the statement
secret from the verifier? Two independent answers. Any explanation that gives you one answer for
both questions is describing a product, not a system.<Cite src="ethstark-documentation" />
