Open any deployed Groth16 verifier on a block explorer and scroll to the constructor arguments or
the hardcoded constants. You will find something like this, repeated a dozen times:

```solidity
vk.alpha1 = Pairing.G1Point(
    0x2d4d9aa7e302d9df41749d5507949d05dbea33fbb16c643b22f599a2be6df2e2,
    0x14bedd503c37ceb061d8ec60209fe345ce89830a19230301f076caff004d1926
);
```

Those are not the output of a compiler. Somebody generated them, on a specific machine, on a
specific day, from randomness that had to be destroyed afterwards — and if it was not destroyed, the
holder can produce proofs of false statements that this contract will accept, forever, with no
on-chain evidence that anything is wrong.

That is the trusted setup problem. This lesson is about what the secret actually is, how the
industry reduced the trust to something defensible, what the alternative costs, and how Ethereum's
own roadmap has been drifting on the question.

## The secret, and what knowing it buys

Pairing-based systems need public parameters — a **structured reference string** — derived from
secret randomness. Ethereum's own ceremony documentation names it precisely: the powers of tau are
the consecutive powers of a secret `τ`, the sequence `[1, τ, τ², τ³, …]`, encoded as elliptic curve
points so the raw numbers are hidden.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" />

You already know the shape from the protocol side. A KZG commitment commits to a polynomial by
evaluating it at that secret value, which is why the scheme needs the
ceremony at all.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" /> Ethereum's blob commitments rest on exactly this
string.<Cite src="eip-4844" /><Cite src="c-kzg-4844" />

The consequence of knowing `τ` is not "you learn something you should not". It is that **binding
breaks**. With `τ` in hand you can construct a commitment and then open it to whatever value you
like, because the algebra that normally pins one polynomial to one commitment collapses when you can
evaluate at the secret point yourself. Ethereum's ceremony FAQ states the blob-side consequence
plainly: if the secret were somehow extracted, an attacker would be able to make arbitrary claims
about the data in EIP-4844, breaking every service and application that depends on blob
data.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" /> On the proving side the equivalent is: forge a proof for a false
statement that every honest verifier accepts.

<Aside kind="warn">
Sit with the detection question, because it is what makes this risk different in kind from the
others in this track. A forged proof produced with knowledge of `τ` is *the same shape* as an honest
proof. There is no anomaly to alert on, no dispute to watch, no divergence between two clients. The
first evidence would be a balance that does not add up. Compare this with an optimistic system,
where an invalid claim produces a visible challenge, and you can see why "who ran the ceremony" is a
question worth asking out loud rather than assuming.
</Aside>

### What is actually in those constants

Worth being concrete, because "parameters" is vague enough to hide the structure.

A Groth16 verifying key holds a small fixed set of group elements plus **one group element per public
input**, used to build the linear combination the verifier checks against the
proof.<Cite src="groth16-paper" /> That is why the deployed contract at the top of this lesson is a
few kilobytes rather than a few hundred bytes: the proof is three group
elements,<Cite src="groth16-paper" /> but the key that checks it is sized by the circuit's interface.
The proving key is far larger again — it is sized by the constraint system — which is why it lives
next to the prover and never on-chain.

Two things follow that are easy to get wrong in conversation.

**The reference string is public, and is meant to be.** Nothing about it is secret. The secret is the
value it was *derived from*, which is supposed to no longer exist anywhere. "Toxic waste" names the
discarded input, not the published output.

**Publishing the key does not let anyone check the ceremony.** The constants are only curve points;
they look identical whether or not somebody kept `τ`. Verifying the ceremony means checking the
transcript of contributions and their witnesses, which is a separate artefact from the verifying key
the contract holds.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-specs" />

## The mitigation: make the trust one-of-N

The fix is not to trust one party harder. It is to run the generation as a multi-party computation
in which each participant contributes their own secret and destroys it.

The mechanism as Ethereum's documentation describes it: a participant chooses a random number and
runs a computation on it, the output is passed to the next participant who mixes in their own secret,
and this repeats until there are enough participants, at which point the last output becomes the
final result.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" /> The final secret is the product of everyone's
contributions, and nobody ever holds it.

The resulting trust assumption is the important part, and it is genuinely different from what the
phrase "trusted setup" suggests. The FAQ states it as a **1-of-N trust assumption: only a single
participant in the entire ceremony needs to have not revealed their secret input for everything to
be secure.**<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" /> Breaking it requires every participant to have extracted
their own secret from their software and then colluded with every other
participant.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" />

### What actually has to go right

The honest reading of one-of-N is not "so it is fine". It is "so the realistic risk moved somewhere
else", and Ethereum's own FAQ says where: **a more realistic failure mode is a common bug which
leaks the randomness**, which is why the contribution software was audited and why alternative
implementations built on entirely different software stacks were
offered.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" />

That reframes the diligence question. One-of-N protects you against dishonest participants. It does
not protect you against a *correlated* failure — one buggy library that every participant used, one
compromised build, one bad entropy source shared across implementations. Independence of
participants is the assumption, and independence is an engineering property, not a headcount.

Two other properties are load-bearing and easy to miss:

**The contributions must be verifiable.** Each participant's output must come with a proof that it
really is the previous output raised to a new secret exponent, rather than an arbitrary substitution.
Ethereum's ceremony publishes a transcript containing the witnesses — the proofs of correct
contribution — and the FAQ explicitly frames verification as checking both that people you trust
appear in the transcript and that the transcript was constructed correctly with all its
witnesses.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" /><Cite src="kzg-ceremony-specs" /> Without that check, a
malicious coordinator could simply discard everyone's work and substitute a string it generated
itself.

**The coordinator is not trusted for correctness.** The Sequencer serves data and verifies each
contribution before passing it on, but the FAQ is explicit that you do not have to trust it to
produce a biased or invalid output, because the transcript is a verifiable
record.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" />

### The history the vocabulary comes from

A short chronology, because it explains why the words are shaped the way they are.

The earliest deployed ceremonies were per-circuit and run by small groups; Ethereum's documentation
notes that trusted setups were most famously used by Zcash to bootstrap its privacy
features.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" /> A small, closed group generating parameters for one circuit
is exactly what "trusted setup" sounds like, and for that generation of systems the name was
accurate.

Two things then changed. Setups became **universal**, so one string could serve many circuits,
and **updatable**, so the participant set could keep growing rather than closing at a
date.<Cite src="plonk-paper" /><Cite src="gnark-schemes-curves" /> And ceremonies became mass
events: Ethereum's Summoning ceremony was deliberately designed so that thousands of participants
would contribute using different pieces of software on different operating systems, precisely to
avoid single points of failure in both people and
software.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" /> Contributing took under three minutes on a standard laptop
once your turn arrived.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" />

The vocabulary did not keep up with either change, which is why "trusted setup" still lands, to most
readers, as "there is a party who could betray you" rather than "there is a published transcript of
a large multi-party computation you can verify".

## Circuit-specific versus universal

Now the operational axis, which decides how much of the above you have to live through and how often.

**Circuit-specific.** Groth16 is a preprocessing argument whose parameters are tied to one
constraint system. gnark's documentation states it directly: Groth16 is a circuit-specific
preprocessing construction, and needs a circuit-specific trusted setup for its preprocessing
phase.<Cite src="gnark-schemes-curves" /><Cite src="groth16-paper" /> Change one constraint, and the
old parameters no longer describe your circuit.

**Universal and updatable.** PLONK is a universal preprocessing construction with a preprocessing
phase that can be updated, and a short and constant verification time; the cost is that its proofs
are bigger and slower to generate than Groth16's.<Cite src="gnark-schemes-curves" /><Cite src="plonk-paper" />
One string serves any circuit up to a size bound, and *updatable* means new participants can extend
it later, which preserves the one-of-N argument over time rather than freezing it at the moment the
ceremony ended.

gnark's own guidance draws the practical line: Groth16 suits an application generating many proofs
for the same circuit where performance is critical, PLONK suits handling many different circuits with
reasonably fast performance.<Cite src="gnark-schemes-curves" />

<Aside kind="note">
In practice implementations layer the work: a large reusable phase that depends only on a degree
bound, and then a smaller phase bound to a particular constraint system. That layering makes the
per-circuit step much cheaper to run, and it does not change the conclusion — the per-circuit step is
still a ceremony whose output is bound to one constraint system, so a circuit change still means
running it again and re-establishing that somebody honest participated.<Cite src="gnark-schemes-curves" /><Cite src="circom-repo" />
</Aside>

**The product consequence is a release-process consequence, and it is the one teams underestimate.**
With a circuit-specific setup, "we fixed a bug in the circuit" is not a patch release. It is a patch
release plus a ceremony plus a fresh argument to your users about who participated in it and why they
should believe someone deleted something. Teams that did not price that in end up either shipping
fewer fixes than they should, or quietly running a one-person "ceremony" and hoping nobody reads the
release notes.

### The uncomfortable part: almost nobody verifies

One honest observation before leaving ceremonies, because it changes how much comfort the one-of-N
argument should give you in practice.

The security argument requires that *someone* checked the transcript — that the contributions are
genuine exponentiations and that the claimed participants really appear. Ethereum's documentation
frames verification as a community responsibility with two parts: confirming that you, or enough
people you trust, actually participated, and confirming the transcript was constructed correctly
including all its witnesses.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" /> Both parts are things a person has to
choose to do, after the excitement has passed.

For a ceremony at Ethereum's scale that is a reasonable bet: enough independent parties care, and the
artefacts are published for exactly this reason.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-specs" /> For a mid-sized
project's per-circuit ceremony it very often is not. The transcript exists, nobody outside the team
has read it, and the security claim rests on a document with no readers. That is not a cryptographic
weakness; it is a social one, and it is the reason "we ran a ceremony" and "our parameters are
trustworthy" are separate claims that deserve separate evidence.

## Transparent setups: no secret at all

The third option removes the question rather than answering it.

A transparent system has no setup secret. Every parameter is publicly derivable — in practice, from
a hash — so there is no toxic waste, nothing to delete, and no ceremony to organise. The
transparency claim is in the title of the founding work: scalable, transparent, and post-quantum
secure computational integrity.<Cite src="stark-paper" /> The randomness the verifier would have
supplied is produced by the Fiat–Shamir transform from the previous lesson, which is why that
lesson's discipline matters so much more in a transparent
system.<Cite src="ethstark-documentation" />

Two consequences follow, and only one of them is usually stated.

**The stated one: plausible post-quantum security.** Because such systems rest on the security of a
hash function rather than on a discrete-log or pairing assumption, they are not broken by the quantum
algorithm that breaks elliptic-curve cryptography.<Cite src="stark-paper" /> This is the structural
reason Ethereum's long-run roadmap drifts toward hash-only assumptions.<Cite src="ethereum-org-quantum-resistance" />

**The understated one: proofs get much bigger.** Transparency is not free; it is paid for in proof
size and verification cost, which is why nearly every production system that has to touch L1 wraps
its transparent proof in a small pairing-based one. That is the whole of the next lesson.

## Where Ethereum's own policy has landed

Three current positions, each stated with its uncertainty intact.

**For the L1 zkEVM, trusted-setup wrappers are ruled out.** The Foundation's realtime-proving
definition requires proof size under 300 KiB **with no trusted setups**, and adds that proof systems
must not rely on recursive wrappers that use trusted
setups.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" /> That is a deliberate rejection of the standard production
trick, and it is why the security-and-size milestones — 600 KiB with 100-bit provable security, then
300 KiB with 128-bit — are as hard as they are.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" /> Note the
scope: this is a rule for L1 block verification, not a judgement on L2s, which continue to wrap.

**For blobs, the ceremony already happened and the parameters are in use.** The KZG scheme was
chosen for proto-danksharding because it is fast to prove and verify, including inside SNARKs, while
having a small commitment size.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" /> The commitments module of this track
takes that apart properly.

**For the state trie, the roadmap is genuinely unsettled.** You may have absorbed at some point that
Ethereum's state format was moving to a polynomial-commitment-based tree. What is proposed now is a
unified binary tree built on hash-based commitments — EIP-7864 — with the choice of hash function
still open.<Cite src="eip-7864" /> The commitment-based design's status is the part to be careful
about: protocol research indicates it may be off the roadmap entirely, with the stated reasons being
that its commitments are elliptic-curve based rather than post-quantum, and unfriendly to proving
inside a SNARK — which is consistent with the same drift toward hash-only
assumptions.<Cite src="ethereum-org-quantum-resistance" /> No authoritative announcement of that
change was located, and no activation date exists for anything here. State what is proposed; print
no date.

<Aside kind="note">
The recurring logic across all three positions is worth extracting, because it predicts future
decisions better than any individual fact. Elliptic-curve assumptions buy small proofs and cheap
verification today, and carry a long-dated risk. Hash assumptions buy longevity and a
simpler trust story, and cost bytes. Ethereum's protocol layer keeps choosing longevity; application
and L2 layers keep choosing bytes. Both are rational, because they have different time horizons and
different failure consequences.
</Aside>

### The failure that is not the ceremony's fault

One more category, because it is the one that has actually cost money.

Everything above concerns the *generation* of parameters. A separate and more common failure is the
**binding of a verifier to the wrong parameters** afterwards: a contract deployed with a verifying
key for a different circuit revision than the one the prover is running, an upgradeable verifier
whose key can be replaced by an admin, or a system where the key and the circuit are versioned
independently and drift.

This has nothing to do with `τ` and everything to do with release engineering, and it produces the
same outcome — a verifier that accepts proofs of something other than what you believe it accepts.
The Foundation's insistence on documented architectures and specifications that match deployed code
is aimed at the same class of problem one layer up.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" />

The practical rule: treat the verifying key as part of the circuit's build artefact, hash it, pin it,
and make a mismatch a deployment failure rather than a runtime surprise. A ceremony you can defend
and a key nobody can trace to a circuit revision is not a coherent security story.

## Reading a system's setup story

A short procedure you can apply to any proof-consuming system, in the order that finds problems
fastest.

**Subgoal: identify the family before asking about the ceremony.** Pairing-based means there are
parameters with a secret behind them. Hash-based means there are not. If the documentation does not
make this obvious, the curve name usually does — BN254 or BLS12-381 in the verifier is a strong
signal of a pairing-based final step.<Cite src="eth2book-bls12-381" />

**Subgoal: if there is a ceremony, find the transcript, not the blog post.** You are looking for a
verifiable record with contribution witnesses, not a claim that a ceremony
occurred.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-specs" />

**Subgoal: find one participant you would personally believe.** One-of-N is only as good as your
belief that at least one specific person or organisation both participated and deleted. "Thousands of
participants" is a good sign; "at least one of these three organisations, each of whom published
their own attestation" is an argument.

**Subgoal: ask what a circuit change costs.** Circuit-specific setups turn every circuit fix into a
ceremony.<Cite src="gnark-schemes-curves" /> Ask the team when they last changed the circuit and what
they did about parameters. The answer is unusually informative about the whole operation.

**Subgoal: check independence, not headcount.** Did participants use different implementations on
different stacks, or did everyone run the same binary?<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" />

parameters.<Cite src="gnark-schemes-curves" /> Four ceremonies in year one, each of which needs
participants you can point at, a published transcript, and a public argument that the one-of-N

event with a lead time, so your circuit-fix latency is now bounded by it. This option is excellent
for a frozen circuit and hostile to an evolving one.

**Option 2 — universal setup.** One string, reusable across circuit revisions up to its size bound,

to Groth16.<Cite src="gnark-schemes-curves" /> You still carry a setup assumption — it did not
vanish, it became reusable and extendable.

step,<Cite src="stark-paper" /> and the circuit-change story is a rebuild with no ceremony at all.

from the part that changes quarterly to the part that changes rarely, which is a real improvement
and not an elimination. Worth stating precisely, because "we use STARKs, so no trusted setup" is a
claim that this architecture does not support.

then an ordinary gas-versus-latency question rather than a trust question.

and it is the reason Ethereum's own protocol layer is drifting toward hash-based
assumptions,<Cite src="ethereum-org-quantum-resistance" /> but for a feature shipping this year with
a quarterly release cadence, the setup's *operational* cost will dominate the decision long before
the cryptographic horizon does. Note also that if this feature were an L1 zkEVM component rather than
an application, the answer would be forced: the Foundation's definition forbids recursive wrappers
that use trusted setups.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" />

## What this unlocks

**Next lesson** takes the tension this one set up — small proofs need a setup, transparent proofs are
large — and shows what production actually does with it. The answer is neither: it is a pipeline that
uses both, and understanding why requires the prover-versus-verifier asymmetry as a first-class
economic fact rather than a footnote.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" />

**The commitments module** goes underneath everything in this lesson. The powers of tau exist to
support a polynomial commitment scheme; once you have built KZG openings by hand, "the secret breaks
binding" stops being a slogan and becomes an equation you can write
down.<Cite src="kzg-ceremony-faq" /><Cite src="sok-zk-snarks-arxiv" />

**And in diligence**, you now have a question that separates careful teams from careless ones in
about ninety seconds: *where did the verifying key come from, and what happens to it when you change
the circuit?* A team with a good answer has usually thought about everything else too. A team that
has not considered the question has a release process that is going to surprise
them.<Cite src="ethereum-org-zkevm-roadmap" /><Cite src="halo-paper" />
