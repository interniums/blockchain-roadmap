The headline sentence of Succinct's SP1 Hypercube announcement, published 20 May 2025, is this: the
system "can prove over 93% of Ethereum blocks in under 12 seconds".<Cite src="succinct-sp1-hypercube" />

Four paragraphs further down, the same post says what that took: a cluster capable of real-time
proving more than 90% of mainnet blocks needs **roughly 160 RTX 4090 GPUs** and can be built for
**$300,000 to $400,000** — with an estimate that more cost-efficient hardware could bring a cluster
to around $100,000.<Cite src="succinct-sp1-hypercube" /> Two paragraphs above that, it says what the
number excludes: the measurements leave out the time to fetch Merkle proofs over Ethereum RPC, the
witness fetching that stateless execution
requires.<Cite src="succinct-sp1-hypercube" />

Same post. Same result. Three different facts, and only the first one travels.

This lesson is not about which zkVM is faster. It is about why that question, asked the way people
ask it, has no answer — and what to measure instead.

## The percentage is not a single number

Start with the specific claim, because it is the one you will be quoted.

The May 2025 post reports 93% of blocks proved in under 12 seconds, with an average proving time of
10.3 seconds.<Cite src="succinct-sp1-hypercube" /> This project's own conflict register records that
other Succinct posts state different percentages at different thresholds and different hardware
counts, and that those figures were **not reconciled**: the honest instruction is to name the
hardware and the source post rather than publish one
number.<Cite src="chainpath-conflict-register" />

So the correct form of the claim is not "SP1 proves 93% of blocks in 12 seconds". It is: *the post
of 20 May 2025 reported 93% under 12 seconds, averaging 10.3 seconds, on a cluster the same post
sizes at about 160 RTX 4090s, excluding RPC witness fetching.* That sentence is longer, and it is the
only version that survives contact with a second source.

### Cross-check against a board that publishes the whole tuple

There is one place that reports latency, hardware, cost and date together, and it is run by the party
with no product to sell. Ethproofs is the Ethereum Foundation's real-time-proving cohort board. Read
on **26 August 2026**, its front page showed:<Cite src="ethproofs" />

- **6 evaluated provers, 2 eligible.**
- Aggregate across **301,398 block slots**: **33.8%** proved in 10 seconds or less, against a stated
  target of **at least 70%**. The remainder is broken out as stunned, paralyzed and offline, with
  offline the largest share.
- Of **119,215 total proofs**, **101,939** were eligible — an **85.5%** eligible rate.
- Per-proof cost for the two eligible entries: **$0.0054** and **$0.0079**, on clusters the board
  labels respectively as sixteen machines of sixteen NVIDIA 5090s, and as an eight-GPU cluster.
- Current phase: a **security sprint**, working toward a milestone stated as **100-bit provable /
  600 KiB**.

Hold that against the vendor headline. Neither is dishonest. They are measuring different things: one
is a curated result on a purpose-built cluster at a moment in time, the other is a continuous
multi-team cohort where "offline" counts against you. A prover that is fast in a blog post and absent
on a Tuesday has a liveness number, and the board publishes it.

Two details are worth pulling out because they teach the discipline directly.

**The cost figures are dollars per L1 block proof.** Not per transaction. This project's conflict
register flags exactly this collision: a shard baseline of roughly $0.001 per proof sits beside
secondary claims of "0.5–1 cent per transaction", and those are different units over different
denominators. The register's verdict is that no number should be published without its unit and its
source.<Cite src="chainpath-conflict-register" /> A block proof amortised over a few hundred
transactions and a per-transaction figure are not comparable, and dividing one into the other
requires a transaction count nobody stated.

**The proof-size target moved.** The Foundation's July 2025 definition of real-time proving set proof
size at **300 KiB or less with no trusted setups**.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" /> The live board,
read fourteen months later, names its current milestone as **100-bit provable / 600
KiB**.<Cite src="ethproofs" /> That is not a contradiction to explain away — it is a target being
renegotiated in public as the security requirement tightened and the size requirement loosened to
pay for it. Quote the one you read, with the date you read it.

<Aside kind="warn">
**Latency claims span three orders of magnitude because they span three workloads.** Proving an L1
block is not proving an L2 batch, and neither is proving your application's guest program. The
figures collected for this project range from "seconds" for L1 block proving on a GPU cluster to
"30 minutes" and "75 minutes" for production L2 provers of different systems — across different
years, hardware and scopes, and explicitly **not reconciled**.<Cite src="chainpath-conflict-register" />
Any latency number you carry across workloads is a fabrication with a citation attached.
</Aside>

## The hardware requirement usually dominates

Here is the part that surprises people: the vendors do not hide this. They say it in their own
documentation, in the pages you would read on day one.

SP1's hardware-acceleration page states that CUDA on Nvidia GPUs "can provide dramatically better
latency and cost performance compared to using the CPU prover, **even with AVX
acceleration**".<Cite src="sp1-docs-hardware-acceleration" /> Read the concession in that clause:
the comparison is not GPU against a naive CPU path, it is GPU against a CPU path that has already
been vectorised with AVX256 or AVX512 through Plonky3.<Cite src="sp1-docs-hardware-acceleration" />
The gap survives the optimisation.

And the floors are specific. SP1's GPU path requires CUDA compute capability 8.0 or higher, at least
24 GB of VRAM, and Linux x86\_64 — with a recommendation of at least 4 CPU cores and 16 GB of RAM
alongside, just to keep the GPU
fed.<Cite src="sp1-docs-hardware-acceleration" /><Cite src="sp1-docs-hardware-requirements" />

For local proving without a GPU, the requirement varies by *proof type*, which is the detail that
wrecks single-number comparisons:<Cite src="sp1-docs-hardware-requirements" />

The final wrapping step alone is called out at roughly **14 GB for Groth16** and **60 GB for
PLONK**.<Cite src="sp1-docs-hardware-requirements" /> That is a four-fold memory swing produced by a
choice you make in one line of host code, on the same guest program, with the same inputs. Any
benchmark that does not name the proof type has not named its workload.

RISC Zero answers the same question from the other end. Its overview recommends at least 16 GB of RAM
for local proof generation — and then offers the alternative of not owning the hardware: proofs can
be generated on your own machines *or* requested from **Boundless**, where provers produce them on
your behalf.<Cite src="risc0-zkvm-docs" /> SP1 makes the parallel recommendation for non-trivial
programs, pointing at its own prover
network.<Cite src="sp1-docs-hardware-requirements" />

So the practical question about a zkVM is not "which is faster". It is two questions:

1. **What machine does this need to hit the latency my product requires?**
2. **Do I intend to own that machine, rent it, or buy proofs as a service?**

The second is a purchasing decision with an operational tail — a marketplace is a dependency with
availability, pricing and censorship properties, and a rack in your own facility is a capital
expense with a power bill and a replacement schedule. Neither shows up in a proving-time chart.

## What actually changed in SP1 Hypercube

It is worth knowing what the generational claim rests on, because it tells you which of your
workloads it applies to.

Previous SP1 versions, including SP1 Turbo, were built on a STARK architecture over **Plonky3**,
using univariate polynomials. Hypercube is built entirely on **multilinear** polynomials, with a new
polynomial commitment scheme — the **Jagged PCS**, described as a "pay only for what you use"
architecture — paired with an optimised implementation of **LogUp GKR**, a multilinear-friendly
sumcheck protocol.<Cite src="succinct-sp1-hypercube" /> SP1's documentation records this as the V6
generation: SP1 V6 introduces Hypercube, "a new multilinear proof system", with a migration guide
from V5.<Cite src="succinct-sp1-docs" /> The current release line at the time of writing is
**v6.5.0**, published 26 August 2026.<Cite src="sp1-releases" />

The speedup claim is stated with its workload attached, which is the right way to state one: up to
**5×** improvement over SP1 Turbo for compute-heavy workloads like loops and Fibonacci, and up to
**2×** for precompile-heavy workloads like Ethereum
proving.<Cite src="succinct-sp1-hypercube" /> Note the asymmetry. If your guest program is dominated
by hashing through accelerated precompiles, you are on the 2× curve, not the 5× one — and which curve
you are on is a property of your program, not of the zkVM.

<Aside kind="note">
**The 2026 emphasis moved from speed to soundness.** The Foundation's second post in the L1 zkEVM
series is about security foundations rather than latency, and the live cohort board's current phase
is a security sprint with a provable-security milestone rather than a performance
one.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" /><Cite src="ethproofs" /> You can see the same shift in
SP1's own security-model page, which is unusually explicit about what it assumes: Poseidon2 over the
Koalabear field treated as a random oracle to justify Fiat–Shamir, an assumption that recursion does
not lose security as depth grows, and — the notable one — that Hypercube does **not** rely on
proximity-gap *conjectures*, resting instead on proximity-gap theorems while operating in the unique
decoding regime.<Cite src="sp1-docs-security-model" /> A vendor page that distinguishes a theorem from
a conjecture is telling you which questions its competitors are being asked.
</Aside>

## The measurement you should actually run

Since no published comparison answers your question, here is the protocol that does. It is four
numbers, and every one of them carries its unit.

**Step 1 — get a cycle count before you spend anything.** Run your guest under the executor and read
`total_instruction_count`.<Cite src="sp1-example-fibonacci-host" /> This costs a normal program run
and tells you the order of magnitude of everything downstream. If the cycle count is enormous, the
answer is to change the program, not the prover.

**Step 2 — fix the proof type before you compare anything.** Core, compressed, Groth16 and PLONK are
four different products with different sizes, different on-chain costs and a four-fold memory
spread.<Cite src="sp1-docs-proof-types" /><Cite src="sp1-docs-hardware-requirements" /> Comparing two
systems at different proof types is comparing nothing.

**Step 3 — record four quantities, each with its unit:**

**Step 4 — stamp every row with the machine and the date.** CPU model and core count, GPU model and
count, VRAM, whether it was local or a network, and the day you ran it. A row without those is not
reproducible and therefore is not a measurement.

Do this on both systems, on *your* guest program. The published comparisons were run on Fibonacci and
SHA-256 by parties with an interest in the outcome; your program is neither, and the properties that
will actually decide it — memory behaviour on large programs, precompile coverage for the hashes you
use, and whether you can get the hardware — are workload-specific by construction.

> "We benchmarked both zkVMs. System A proved our workload in 41 seconds; System B took 96 seconds.
> System A is also about 100× cheaper per proof according to published figures. Recommendation:

<Answer>
**The two timings may be perfectly accurate and still not support the recommendation.** Four things
are missing, and each one can move the answer.

**1. Which proof type produced those seconds?** If A was measured at core or compressed and B at
Groth16, the comparison includes a wrapping step on one side and not the other — and that step alone
is the difference between roughly 14 GB and 60 GB of memory in SP1's own
table.<Cite src="sp1-docs-hardware-requirements" /> If the output has to be verified on-chain, only
the EVM-compatible regimes are candidates at all.

**2. Which hardware, and is it the hardware we will run on?** "The two spare CPU boxes" is the load-
bearing sentence in that memo. SP1's documentation states plainly that CUDA gives dramatically better
latency and cost than the CPU prover even with AVX acceleration, and that the GPU path wants compute
capability 8.0 or higher and 24 GB or more of VRAM on Linux
x86\_64.<Cite src="sp1-docs-hardware-acceleration" /> A benchmark run on a GPU and deployed on spare
CPU boxes is not a forecast of anything. Conversely, if the benchmark *was* on CPU, the 41 seconds may
collapse for both systems once a GPU exists, and possibly by different factors.

**3. Where does "100× cheaper per proof" come from, and per proof of what?** Circulating cost figures
for these systems are vendor-adjacent, undated, and stated in mismatched units — per proof against
per transaction — and this project explicitly refuses to resolve
them.<Cite src="chainpath-conflict-register" /> A ratio inherited from someone else's workload is not
evidence about ours. Our own delivered cost is measurable and nobody measured it.

**4. What is the liveness story?** A prover that is fast and sometimes absent is a different product
from one that is slower and always there. The Foundation's own cohort board grades performance and
liveness as separate columns, and on the day I read it a third of block slots met the latency bar
while offline was the single largest category.<Cite src="ethproofs" /> Ask what happens the week our
prover is down, and who else can produce the proof.

**The one experiment I would ask for.** Re-run both systems on our guest program, at the *same*
proof type, on the *exact* hardware we intend to deploy — including the case where that hardware is
"a proving service we buy from", since both vendors offer that as a first-class
option.<Cite src="risc0-zkvm-docs" /><Cite src="sp1-docs-hardware-requirements" /> Report four
numbers per system with units — wall-clock seconds, peak gigabytes, proof bytes, dollars per proof —
plus the machine and the date. Start by running the executor and reporting the cycle count, because
if that number is bad, neither system is the problem.<Cite src="sp1-example-fibonacci-host" />

**The sentence I would put at the top of the memo.** "A proving time without a machine attached is
not a number." Everything above is that sentence, applied four times.
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

You now have a defensible way to choose, and it is not a leaderboard. **Fix the proof type, run your
own guest, report four quantities with their units, and stamp the machine and the date on every row.**
Where a published figure is all you have, carry its qualifiers with it — the threshold, the cluster,
the workload, the post — or do not carry it.

You also have the habit this track will need repeatedly. Every headline in this field is a
measurement of something; the work is finding out of *what*, on *which machine*, at *which date*, and
whether that is your situation. The Foundation's real-time-proving definition is the model: six lines,
four of them about the machine and the money.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" />

One lesson remains in this module, and it closes the loop the track opened with. You have been
handed a zkVM and told that circuits are not where you start. That is a claim about defaults, and it
has an edge — there are workloads where a hand-written circuit is decisively the right tool. The next
lesson names them, and says what you would have to give up to go there.
