Open SP1's optimisation documentation and you find a table of patched crates: `sha2`, `sha3`,
`tiny-keccak`, `k256`, `p256`, `secp256k1`, `curve25519-dalek`, `bls12_381`, `substrate-bn`,
`crypto-bigint`, `rsa`. Swap the dependency in your `Cargo.toml` and your guest program's hashing and
signature checking gets faster without another line
changing.<Cite src="sp1-docs-precompiles" />

Read the sentence above the table. Those precompiles are described as "specialized, highly-optimized
**circuits** that execute directly within the zkVM".<Cite src="sp1-docs-precompiles" />

So circuits did not go away. They moved. A production zkVM is a general machine with a set of
hand-written circuits bolted into it at the points where generality is too expensive — and the list
of those points is not a design opinion, it is empirical. It is the hashes and the curve operations,
every time.

This lesson is about that boundary: what a circuit is, why the zkVM is the correct default anyway,
and what changes about your risk when you cross the line.

## Instructions versus equations

A zkVM proves a statement about a **machine**: that a RISC-V processor, starting from this program
and these inputs, took these steps and ended in this state. Everything the machine does gets proved,
including the parts you did not write — instruction fetch, decode, register file, memory consistency
— for every cycle, whether your program spends that cycle on business logic or on a loop counter.

A hand-written circuit proves a statement about **numbers**: that this set of field elements
satisfies this set of polynomial equations. There is no machine in it. If the relation you care about
is "these two curve points add to this third one", the circuit encodes exactly that and nothing else.

That difference is the entire cost gap, and it runs in one direction. For a fixed predicate, the
circuit is far cheaper to prove, because the zkVM has to pay for the generality you are not using.
The precompile table is that arithmetic made concrete: SP1's own framing is that the precompiles
"dramatically reduce execution time and proving costs" for cryptographic
operations.<Cite src="sp1-docs-precompiles" />

You saw the same shape from the other side last lesson: SP1 Hypercube reported up to 5× on
compute-heavy workloads and up to 2× on **precompile-heavy** ones.<Cite src="succinct-sp1-hypercube" />
A program already dominated by hand-written circuits gains less from a better general machine,
because less of it is running on the general machine.

## When a circuit is the right tool

The default is a zkVM, and the default is right for almost everything. A circuit earns its cost under
a specific and narrow combination:

- **The predicate is small and fixed.** One relation, not a program. A signature check. A membership
  check. A range check.
- **It is proved constantly.** Millions of times, or once per block forever, so a constant-factor
  saving compounds into a real bill.
- **You can afford the expertise, and keep affording it.** Not writing it once — maintaining it,
  reviewing it, and re-reviewing it every time the relation changes.

Notice that the precompile list satisfies all three by construction. Read what is actually on it — the
patched crates SP1 ships, each pinned to a specific upstream version:<Cite src="sp1-docs-precompiles" />

| Domain | Patched crates |
|---|---|
| Hashing | `sha2`, `sha3`, `tiny-keccak` |
| Big integers | `crypto-bigint` |
| Elliptic curves | `k256`, `p256`, `secp256k1`, `curve25519-dalek`, `curve25519-dalek-ng`, `bls12_381`, `substrate-bn` |
| Other | `rsa` |

That is not a list of interesting relations. It is **hashes and curve operations**, and nothing else
— identified empirically by whoever was paying the GPU bill, and routed through the precompiles
without changing your code beyond the dependency declaration.<Cite src="sp1-docs-precompiles" />

The heuristic that falls out: **if your candidate predicate is not a hash or a curve operation, it is
probably not hot enough to justify hand-writing it.** The ecosystem has already searched this space
with real money, and the answer it found is short.

The honest rule, then, is not "zkVM or circuit" but: **use a zkVM, and reach for a hand-written
circuit only for a predicate hot enough that someone would have written a precompile for it.**

## The failure mode is different, and it is worse

This is the part that should decide it, and the vendor's own documentation states it plainly. SP1's
safe-precompile-usage page lists assumptions the caller must satisfy. Two of
them:<Cite src="sp1-docs-safe-precompile-usage" />

- Field inputs must be in **canonical form** — the `u32` limbs must encode `1`, not `p + 1` or
  `2p + 1`. Non-canonical representations "may result in unverifiable SP1 proofs".
- The elliptic-curve precompiles **assume the inputs are valid curve points**. "Since this validity
  is not enforced within the precompile circuits, it is the responsibility of the user program to
  verify that the points lie on the curve."

Sit with the second one. A circuit that does not constrain curve membership is not broken — it is
*specified* not to check, for performance, with the obligation documented and pushed to the caller.
That is a reasonable engineering decision, and it is also the exact shape of every serious bug in
this field.

The general principle: **an under-constrained circuit still produces proofs that verify.** No crash,
no revert, no failing assertion — the proof is valid and proves a weaker statement than the one you
believed you wrote, which a prover who notices the gap can satisfy with values you never intended.
No test fails either, because an honest prover's inputs satisfy both statements at once.

Compare that with a bug in a zkVM guest program. That is a normal software bug in normal Rust. You
find it by running the executor and looking at the output, in a normal debugging session, without
proving anything.

<Aside kind="stop">
**The hazard has a name in circuit languages, and it is deliberately visible there.** Noir separates
constrained from **unconstrained** code, and its documentation is explicit that unconstrained results
are hints your constrained code must then check.<Cite src="noir-docs-unconstrained" /> That
separation exists because the mistake is common enough to need a keyword. When you descend into
circuits later in this track, that keyword is the one to read most carefully — and the exercise
attached to it is to write a circuit that accepts a false statement.<Cite src="noir-docs-quickstart" />
</Aside>

The rest of that page is more of the same species — pointer alignment, and a warning not to invoke
syscalls directly with `ecall`, with calling `HALT` yourself given as an example that leads to
vulnerabilities.<Cite src="sp1-docs-safe-precompile-usage" /> Every one is a constraint living in a
comment rather than in a type. That is what descending a layer costs: obligations move from the
compiler to the reader.

SP1's security model makes the general form of this explicit: it is critical for safe usage that the
program compiled into SP1 is correct.<Cite src="sp1-docs-security-model" /> At every layer, a proof
system's soundness is never a substitute for the correctness of the thing being proved — which is
what the Foundation's 2026 push on provable-security bits and formal verification amounts to at
protocol scale.<Cite src="ef-zkevm-security-foundations" />

## Why this track is ordered the way it is

You now have a working mental model of proving without having touched a polynomial, and it is
incomplete in one load-bearing way. Everything in this module rested on a claim made in the first
lesson and never justified: verification is cheap and roughly **constant**, no matter how large the
proved computation is. That is not how anything else in computing behaves, and until you know why it
holds you cannot predict when it stops holding.

So the rest of the track is a descent, each step answering the question the step above raises:

Circuits sit near the bottom of that ladder rather than the top, and that placement is the argument
of this whole module. They are what you go down to when you need to know why the proof is small — not
where a 2026 learner starts, and not where most production systems end up living.

<Check type="explain" concept="circuits-are-the-descent">
Your team proves a batch of application state transitions in a zkVM. Profiling shows that 70% of
cycles are spent verifying ECDSA signatures over `secp256k1`, one per user action. An engineer
proposes writing a hand-optimised circuit for the signature check to cut proving cost.

Rank the options and say what you would do first.

<Answer>
**First: check whether the circuit already exists.** `secp256k1` and `k256` are both in SP1's patched
crate table, which routes those operations through built-in precompile circuits with no code change
beyond the dependency.<Cite src="sp1-docs-precompiles" /> If the 70% was measured *without* the
patched crates, the proposal is to hand-write something that ships in the box. That is the first
question, it costs ten minutes, and it very often ends the discussion.

**Second: if the patches are already in use, read the assumptions before optimising.** The
safe-usage page requires canonical field inputs and — for the curve precompiles — that the user
program itself verify the points lie on the curve, because the precompile circuits do not enforce
it.<Cite src="sp1-docs-safe-precompile-usage" /> A 70% cycle share concentrated in signature
verification means those obligations are on your hot path. Confirm they are met before you make that
path faster; a faster incorrect check is worse than a slow correct one.

**Third: reconsider the workload before the machinery.** One signature per user action inside the
proof is a design choice, not a law. Batch verification, or aggregating signatures before proving,
changes the cycle count by more than a constant factor — and cycle count is the number that governs
everything downstream. Changing the program beats changing the prover, and it is the cheaper
experiment.

**Only then: the bespoke circuit.** It would be genuinely cheaper to prove, and it buys a permanent
new liability. An under-constrained signature circuit produces perfectly valid proofs of forged
signatures, with no failing test and no revert to warn you — the same failure class the precompile
docs are managing by documenting what they do *not* check.<Cite src="sp1-docs-safe-precompile-usage" />
That is not a reason never to do it. It is a reason to price the audit and the maintenance alongside
the GPU saving, and to require that the predicate be small, fixed and proved constantly enough to
amortise both.

**What I would say in the meeting.** "Before we take on a bespoke circuit, I want three numbers: the
cycle count with the patched crates in place, the cycle count with signatures batched, and the
delivered cost at both. If the bespoke circuit is still the answer after that, it will be an answer
we can defend — and we will budget the audit as part of it."
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

This module ends with an economic fact and a default. **Proving costs more than executing, the
saving is in verification, and it multiplies by the number of parties who stop redoing the work.**
For general computation the tool is a zkVM taking ordinary Rust; for a small, hot, fixed predicate, a
circuit — and the ones that qualified have mostly been written and shipped as precompiles already.

What you cannot yet do is explain why any of it works. The next module answers the question this one
kept deferring: what a proof system proves, what "succinct" means precisely, why an *argument* is not
a *proof*, and how a verifier's random challenges become a hash of a transcript. After that,
commitments — because the reason the proof is small is that nothing large is ever transmitted, only
committed to and opened at a few points.
