On 10 July 2025 the Ethereum Foundation published a working definition of "real-time proving". It is
six lines long, and only two of the six are about cryptography:<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" />

```text
Latency:     <= 10s for P99 of mainnet blocks
On-prem CAPEX: <= 100k USD
On-prem power: <= 10kW
Code:        fully open source
Security:    >= 128 bits
Proof size:  <= 300KiB with no trusted setups
```

Read the third line again. A cryptographic milestone with a **power budget** in it. The post gives
the reason plainly: the constraint on home proving is not silicon but electricity, most residential
homes have at least 10 kW entering from the street, and some have a circuit sized for an
electric-vehicle charger — so real-time proving has to be possible on hardware drawing 10 kW or
less.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" />

That is the honest shape of this subject, and it is why this track opens here rather than with a
polynomial. Proving is a machine you buy, power you draw, and seconds your user waits. Every number
in the next seven modules comes attached to a workload, a machine and a date, or it is not a number.
This lesson establishes the one economic fact that makes any of it worth paying for.

## A zkVM is a four-call interface

Start with what you would actually type. SP1's own documentation states the flow as four
steps:<Cite src="sp1-docs-what-is-a-zkvm" />

1. **Define** your function `f`.
2. **Setup** a proving key `pk` and a verifying key `vk` for the program, derived from its compiled
   ELF binary.
3. **Prove** the output using `prove(pk, x)`, producing a proof `π` that `f(x) = y`.
4. **Verify** with `verify(vk, x, y, π)`.

`f` is ordinary Rust. It compiles to RISC-V, which is the instruction set both production zkVMs
target, and the proof attests that *this* program, on *these* inputs, produced *this*
output.<Cite src="succinct-sp1-docs" /> The verifier learns that fact without re-running the program
and without seeing any input the program did not publish.

The load-bearing word in step 2 is **derived**. `vk` is a commitment to the compiled binary. Change
one line of the guest program, recompile, and `vk` changes; a proof made against the old key will not
verify against the new one. This is the mechanism that stops "I proved *something* ran" from being
the only claim on offer — you already have the intuition from
[hash functions](/c/hash-function), and this is the same idea applied to an executable.

What is *not* in that list is the entire skill people associate with this field. You do not write
constraints. You do not choose a field. You do not lay out a trace. The constraint system was written
once, by the zkVM's authors, for the VM's instruction set — and then every program anybody compiles
reuses it. SP1's documentation puts the pitch in one sentence: write provable programs in ordinary
code, with no need for custom circuit design or cryptography expertise.<Cite src="succinct-sp1-docs" />

  belief="Using zero-knowledge proofs means learning circuit programming."
  reality="The mainstream 2026 path for general computation is a zkVM that takes ordinary Rust. Hand-written circuits are a specialisation you descend into later, for small fixed predicates that are proved constantly."
  why="Circuit-first pedagogy predates viable zkVMs. Most tutorials that dominate search results were written before 2023, when writing the circuit yourself was the only option there was."

## Proving does not make computation cheaper

Here is the sentence that reorganises everything: **a proof system never reduces the cost of a
computation. It moves the cost.**

Producing a proof costs a large multiple of running the program directly. Treat that as an
order-of-magnitude intuition rather than a benchmark — the multiple depends on the workload, the
zkVM, the proof type and the hardware, it has fallen sharply over the last few years, and no source
in this project measures it uniformly across systems.<Cite src="chainpath-conflict-register" /> What
does not depend on any of that is the direction: proving is the expensive side, always.

The saving is entirely on the other side of the wire. Verification is cheap, and — this is the part
that matters — roughly *constant* in the size of the computation being proved. SP1's proof-type table
makes the shape concrete, as read on 26 August 2026:<Cite src="sp1-docs-proof-types" />

Now do the arithmetic that makes the asymmetry visible. Around 270,000 gas is the *whole* on-chain
cost of accepting a Groth16-wrapped SP1 proof.<Cite src="sp1-docs-proof-types" /> Price that against
something you measured directly in the toolchain track: a cold `SLOAD` is 2,100
gas.<Cite src="measured-gas-constants" /> So verifying a proof of arbitrarily large off-chain work
costs roughly what 128 cold storage reads cost. Not 128 reads' worth of *logic* — 128 bare reads,
before a single comparison or arithmetic operation.

That ratio is the entire product. Under it, proving is a bad trade. Over it, the gap widens without
bound, because the left-hand side does not grow.

<Aside kind="warn">
**The 270k figure is a verification cost, not a total cost.** It is what the L1 charges for checking
the proof. It says nothing about what producing the proof cost you in wall-clock seconds, GPU rental
or electricity, and those are separate line items on a separate bill paid by a different party. Any
comparison that puts on-chain gas beside "the cost of ZK" without naming the prover's bill is
comparing two different quantities.
</Aside>

### The bill on the other side, with its units attached

So what does the expensive side actually cost? The honest answer in August 2026 is: it depends, and
the published figures are not comparable to each other.

You can see the shape of the dependency in SP1's own hardware requirements page. For *local* proving
it distinguishes three regimes: mock and network mode wants one or more CPU cores and 8 GB of memory;
core and compressed proving wants 16 or more cores and 16 GB or more; and the EVM-facing wrapping
step wants 16 GB or more for Groth16 and 64 GB or more for PLONK, with the final wrap alone taking
roughly 14 GB for Groth16 and 60 GB for PLONK.<Cite src="sp1-docs-hardware-requirements" /> For GPU
proving it names a floor: CUDA compute capability 8.0 or higher, at least 24 GB of VRAM, Linux
x86\_64 only.<Cite src="sp1-docs-hardware-requirements" />

Notice what just happened. The same program, proved four different ways, has a memory requirement
that varies by a factor of roughly four and a hardware category that changes from "any laptop" to
"a specific GPU class on a specific operating system". A dollar-per-proof figure that does not say
which of those regimes it was measured in is not wrong so much as meaningless.

This is not a temporary state of the art. It is the reporting convention the field has not yet
settled, and this project has explicitly refused to resolve it: circulating cost claims for these
systems use mismatched units — "per proof" against "per transaction" — and circulating latency claims
span seconds to over an hour across different years, systems and
scopes.<Cite src="chainpath-conflict-register" /> None of them is quotable on its own.

There is one place that reports the whole tuple, and it is worth bookmarking now. Ethproofs is the
Ethereum Foundation's own board for L1 block proving, and its cohort table gives every prover with
its zkVM, its guest program, its cluster and its cost together. Read on 26 August 2026 it listed six
evaluated provers of which two were eligible, an aggregate of 301,398 block slots with 33.8% proved
in 10 seconds or less against a target of at least 70%, and per-proof costs of $0.0054 and $0.0079
for the two eligible entries — on clusters the board labels, respectively, as sixteen machines of
sixteen NVIDIA 5090s and as an eight-GPU cluster.<Cite src="ethproofs" />

Every one of those numbers is attached to a workload (one mainnet block), a machine (named), a
metric (dollars per block proof, not per transaction) and a date. That is the standard. It is also,
bluntly, a much less flattering picture than any single headline, and that gap is the reason the
standard exists.

## The saving is per verifier, and there is usually more than one

If proving costs more than executing, why does anyone do it? Because the cost you removed was being
paid many times.

Ethereum's own case is the cleanest version. Today every full node re-executes every block to know
that it is valid; the work is duplicated across the whole network, and the gas limit is set by what
the *weakest* participant can re-execute in time. The Foundation's plan replaces that with validators
who statelessly verify proofs instead of re-executing — and deliberately verify *several*, say three,
generated by different zkVMs proving different EVM implementations, on the stated reasoning that this
applies the same defence in depth as existing client diversity.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" />
Once a supermajority is comfortable running such clients, the gas limit can rise to a level that
would be beyond re-execution on reasonable hardware.<Cite src="ef-realtime-proving" />

Write the trade as a comparison rather than a slogan:

That inequality is the only question worth asking about any proposed use of this technology, and it
has three inputs you can actually estimate: how expensive the computation is, how much cheaper
verification is than re-execution, and — the input people forget — **how many independent parties
would otherwise have had to check the work.**

When N is one, proving is almost always the wrong tool. If your backend computes something and your
own contract needs to trust it, and nobody else will ever check, a signature from a key you control
is cheaper, simpler and has a smaller failure surface. Proofs earn their cost when N is large, when
the parties are mutually distrusting, or when N is one but that one party *cannot* re-execute at all
— which is the situation an L1 contract is permanently in.

<Aside kind="note">
**N is not always a count of machines.** For a rollup, N is closer to "everyone who will ever need to
be convinced that this batch was valid, forever" — including people who join the network years later
and cannot re-run history. A proof compresses that unbounded audience into one on-chain check. That
is a different argument from throughput, and it is the more durable one.
</Aside>

## What an on-chain verifier does, and what it does not

A proof matters on-chain because a contract can check it. Concretely, in SP1's recommended
arrangement, you call the `ISP1Verifier` interface on the `SP1VerifierGateway`, passing your
program's verification key, the public values and the proof bytes; the gateway routes the proof to
the correct verifier implementation.<Cite src="sp1-docs-solidity-verifier" /> The documentation adds
two operational facts worth reading as design constraints rather than footnotes: your program's
verification key should be **upgradeable within your contract**, so you can move to a newer SP1
version or change the program; and Succinct retains the ability to **freeze verifiers** on the
canonical gateway in the event of a security issue, while verifier deployment itself remains
permissionless so you can choose a different security configuration.<Cite src="sp1-docs-solidity-verifier" />

Both of those should feel familiar from the security track. A verification key that must be
upgradeable is an upgrade key. A gateway whose entries can be frozen by one party is a liveness
dependency. Neither is hidden — they are in the vendor's own docs — but neither shows up in the
sentence "the proof is verified on-chain, so it is trustless".

And then the sharper point, which is about the boolean itself.

<Misconception
  belief="If the on-chain verifier returns true, the requested action is authorised."
  reality="Verification establishes only that some witness exists making the statement true for the public values supplied. Binding those public values to the state you meant, to the caller you meant, and to a nonce that has not been used before is the calling contract's job, not the verifier's."
  why="A boolean return from a contract with 'Verifier' in its name reads like an access-control check, and the public values are passed in as an opaque byte string that nothing forces you to inspect."
  src="sp1-docs-solidity-verifier"
/>

A valid proof of "this program, run on some inputs, output these public values" is a true statement
about a computation. It becomes a statement about *your system* only when your contract checks that
the public values name the state root it is currently at, the recipient it intended, and a sequence
number it has not already honoured. That work is ordinary Solidity, it is where the money is lost,
and the last module of this track is about nothing else.

## "Zero-knowledge" is usually the wrong word for this

One more piece of vocabulary hygiene before the module proceeds, because it saves a category error.

Succinctness and zero-knowledge are **two independent properties**. Succinctness means the proof is
small and cheap to check relative to the computation. Zero-knowledge means the proof reveals nothing
about the witness beyond the statement's truth. A system can have either without the other.

In zkVM deployments the property being bought is nearly always succinctness. An Ethereum block is
public. A rollup's transaction data is published on purpose, because publishing it is what makes the
rollup a rollup. The zkEVM roadmap's stated motivation is verification cost and scale, not
confidentiality.<Cite src="ethereum-org-zkevm-roadmap" /> Nothing is being hidden; something is being
*compressed*.

<Misconception
  belief="Zero-knowledge means the computation is private."
  reality="In zkVM deployments the property purchased is almost always succinctness. Most proved computations — blocks, rollup batches, light-client updates — are entirely public, and privacy plays no part in them."
  why="The word 'zero-knowledge' is applied as a label to the whole field, including systems that use none of it, and 'ZK rollup' is the most-repeated phrase in the field."
  src="ethereum-org-zkevm-roadmap"
/>

Keep the distinction sharp, because the two properties have different costs, different failure modes
and different regulatory weight. When you reach [privacy applications](/c/zero-knowledge-property)
much later in this track, you will be turning on a property that everything before it deliberately
did not use.

<Check type="explain" concept="proof-vs-execution-cost">
A colleague proposes replacing a trusted off-chain service with a zkVM. The service reads a user's
last 90 days of activity from your own database, computes a loyalty tier, and today signs the result
with a backend key that a contract checks. Their argument: "signatures mean users have to trust us; a
proof means they do not."

Work the economics and say whether they are right, wrong, or right about the wrong thing.

<Answer>
**They are right about a real problem and wrong about which mechanism solves it.**

**Start with N.** Who else needs to be convinced? Today: one contract, which checks one signature.
If the answer stays "one contract, and it is our contract", the verification-savings side of the
trade is multiplied by one, and you are paying a large multiple of the computation's cost to save
roughly 270,000 gas' worth of checking that you were not doing
anyway.<Cite src="sp1-docs-proof-types" /> That is not a trade, it is a donation.

**Now find the thing they actually care about.** The complaint is not the signature's cost, it is
that the signature attests to *authorship*, not to *correctness*. A backend key says "we produced
this number". It does not say the number was computed by the published rule. That is a genuine and
important gap, and a proof does close it — the verification key is derived from the compiled program,
so a valid proof binds the output to a specific binary.<Cite src="sp1-docs-what-is-a-zkvm" />

**But check what is inside the proof boundary.** The tier is computed from your private database.
A proof can attest that the published rule was applied to some input, and it cannot attest that the
input was your real data rather than convenient data. Unless the database itself is committed to
something the chain already knows, you have proved correct arithmetic over numbers you chose. The
trust assumption moved; it did not leave.

**What I would say back.** "Proving is the right tool when many mutually distrusting parties would
otherwise re-do the work, or when a party who cannot re-do it must still be convinced. Neither is
true here yet. If what you want is that users can check we applied the published rule, the cheaper
first step is to publish the rule and commit to the input data — then a proof becomes worth
discussing, because at that point it would actually be proving something about data we cannot swap."

**And one number to bring.** Before anyone commits, measure the guest program: wall-clock seconds,
peak RAM in gigabytes, proof size in bytes and cost in dollars, each on named hardware, on a named
date. Do not carry a published per-proof figure across from another workload — the circulating ones
disagree on units, let alone on values.<Cite src="chainpath-conflict-register" />
</Answer>
</Check>

## What this unlocks

You now have the two facts the rest of the track is built on. **Proving costs more than executing,
and verification is cheap and roughly constant** — so the design question is never "is this cheap"
but "how many parties stop having to redo the work, and can one of them not redo it at all". And
**on-chain verification is what converts an off-chain claim into something a chain will act on**,
with the boolean it returns meaning strictly less than it appears to.

The next lesson opens the box you have been calling `f`. A guest program is not quite ordinary Rust:
it reads from an input stream rather than from the network or the clock, it commits its public
outputs explicitly, and everything it touches that it does not commit is a private witness. Getting
that boundary wrong is the first real bug in this field, and it is a bug about I/O rather than about
mathematics.<Cite src="sp1-repo" />

Then, two lessons later, the question this one leaves hanging: the whole trade rests on verification
being roughly constant no matter how large the proved computation grows. Nothing so far explains why
that would be true. The answer is that nothing large is ever transmitted — only commitments, and
openings at a handful of points — and chasing it down is what the rest of this track is.
