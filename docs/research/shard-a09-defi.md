## 08.4 — Lending design: collateral factors, health factor, rate curves, bad debt, isolated vs pooled risk

### Concepts
- lend-overcollateralisation — Permissionless lending has no credit scoring or recourse, so every loan must be backed by on-chain collateral worth more than the debt. | requires: [] | contrasts: [lend-undercollateralised-credit]
- lend-ltv-vs-liquidation-threshold — LTV (a.k.a. collateral factor) caps how much you may borrow at open time; the liquidation threshold is a strictly higher ratio at which the position becomes seizable, and the gap between them is deliberate safety buffer. | requires: [lend-overcollateralisation] | contrasts: [lend-health-factor]
- lend-health-factor — Health factor = sum(collateral_i x liquidationThreshold_i) / total borrow value; HF < 1 means liquidatable, and it is a ratio of *values*, so it moves with oracle prices and with accrued interest, not only with user actions. | requires: [lend-ltv-vs-liquidation-threshold, orac-price-feed] | contrasts: [lend-ltv-vs-liquidation-threshold]
- lend-emode — Efficiency mode raises LTV and liquidation threshold for baskets of assets the risk framework asserts are price-correlated (ETH/LST, USD stables), buying leverage by betting on correlation holding. | requires: [lend-ltv-vs-liquidation-threshold] | contrasts: [lend-correlation-break]
- lend-correlation-break — E-mode's high LTVs are only safe while the correlation assumption holds; an LST/stablecoin depeg converts a "safe" 93% LTV basket into instant mass insolvency. | requires: [lend-emode] | contrasts: [lend-emode]
- lend-utilisation — Utilisation U = totalBorrows / totalSupplied is the single state variable that lending pools use to price money; it is also the liquidity signal that decides whether suppliers can withdraw at all. | requires: [] | contrasts: [lend-rate-kink]
- lend-rate-kink — The two-slope ("kinked") borrow rate is base + slope1 x (U/U_opt) below the kink and base + slope1 + slope2 x ((U-U_opt)/(1-U_opt)) above it; slope2 is deliberately brutal so that rates, not a queue, ration the last units of liquidity. | requires: [lend-utilisation] | contrasts: [lend-supply-rate-derivation]
- lend-supply-rate-derivation — Supply rate is not set by governance: it is borrowRate x U x (1 - reserveFactor), so suppliers earn only on the borrowed fraction and the reserve factor is a protocol tax skimmed off the top. | requires: [lend-utilisation, lend-rate-kink] | contrasts: [lend-rate-kink]
- lend-index-accounting — Interest is not iterated per user; the pool stores a monotonically increasing liquidity/borrow index and each account stores a scaled balance, so balanceOf() = scaledBalance x currentIndex and accrual is O(1). | requires: [lend-supply-rate-derivation] | contrasts: []
- lend-caps-and-ceilings — Supply caps, borrow caps and isolation-mode debt ceilings are the crude but effective risk lever: they bound worst-case loss per asset regardless of how wrong the LTV parameter turns out to be. | requires: [lend-ltv-vs-liquidation-threshold] | contrasts: [lend-isolated-vs-pooled]
- lend-isolated-vs-pooled — A pooled ("monolithic") market shares one liquidity pot and therefore socialises every listing mistake across all suppliers; an isolated market (Morpho Blue, Euler v2 vaults, Aave isolation mode) confines a bad listing's loss to that market's suppliers, at the cost of fragmenting liquidity. | requires: [lend-caps-and-ceilings] | contrasts: [lend-caps-and-ceilings]
- lend-bad-debt — Bad debt (Aave calls it a "deficit") is debt with no collateral left behind it; unhandled it keeps accruing interest and inflates a phantom asset, which is why v3.3 added explicit deficit accounting and a zero-collateral/non-zero-debt check at end of liquidation. | requires: [lend-health-factor, liq-close-factor] | contrasts: [lend-backstop]
- lend-bad-debt-is-a-bank-run — Because withdrawals are first-come-first-served against a shared pool, discovering bad debt rationally triggers a race to withdraw, which spikes utilisation to 100% and traps the slow — the loss and the run are separate harms. | requires: [lend-bad-debt, lend-utilisation] | contrasts: []
- lend-backstop — Backstops (Aave Umbrella staked-aToken slashing, Maker/Sky surplus buffer, Compound reserves) socialise residual loss onto a pre-committed capital tranche; Umbrella is per-asset and per-network, so a WETH deficit only burns WETH-market stakers. | requires: [lend-bad-debt] | contrasts: [lend-bad-debt]
- lend-curated-vaults — On isolated-market protocols the risk decision moves to a curator running a vault that allocates supplier deposits across markets, which converts "trust the DAO's risk framework" into "trust this curator's mandate and caps". | requires: [lend-isolated-vs-pooled] | contrasts: [lend-isolated-vs-pooled]

### Current state (Aug 2026)
- **The April 2026 Kelp DAO / Aave event is now the canonical bad-debt teaching case.** 18 Apr 2026 ~17:35 UTC an attacker forged a message to Kelp DAO's LayerZero bridge and minted/released ~116,500 unbacked rsETH (~$293M). They supplied the unbacked rsETH to Aave as collateral and borrowed real WETH against it: reported ~52,834 WETH on Ethereum plus ~29,782 WETH and ~821 wstETH on Arbitrum. Aave was left with roughly $177M-$236M of bad debt depending on source and price mark. Aave TVL fell ~$6B, the WETH reserve pinned at 100% utilisation, AAVE token -18%. By 26 Apr 2026 ~$160M of the ~$200M had been pledged/raised (Mantle, the DAO, Stani Kulechov). Largest DeFi exploit of 2026 to that date.
- The teaching point: **the lending protocol's code was not exploited.** An oracle+collateral listing decision was. Unbacked collateral is indistinguishable from backed collateral to a pool that only reads a price feed and a token balance.
- Aave v3.3 (shipped Dec 2024/early 2025) added explicit on-chain **deficit** accounting and a check that a liquidation cannot leave an account with zero collateral and non-zero debt; that debt is instead moved into the reserve's deficit so it stops accruing phantom interest.
- **Aave Umbrella** went live on Ethereum 5 Jun 2025 and expanded to further networks through 2025-2026. It replaces the old Safety Module design: you stake aTokens (aUSDC/aUSDT/aWETH) or GHO, and a deficit in that specific asset on that specific network burns those stakers automatically, no governance vote. Slashing is scoped per asset per network. Commonly-taught "the Safety Module slashes up to 30% of staked AAVE" is now the *old* design — check what is actually live before teaching it.
- **Isolated-market lending is now mainstream, not exotic.** Morpho Blue (immutable market = loanToken, collateralToken, oracle, IRM, LLTV) and Euler v2 vaults dominate the "modular lending" category; the risk decision has shifted to vault curators. MetaMorpho factory v1.0 vaults realise bad debt into share price; v1.1 vaults do not, leaving the bad debt in the market indefinitely — same brand, opposite loss semantics.
- Commonly taught and now wrong: "Aave's close factor is always 50%." v3.2/v3.3 allow a 100% close factor when the position is small or health factor is deep below 1 (the HF < 0.95 rule), specifically to avoid leaving dust positions that can never be profitably liquidated. Verify the exact threshold against aave-v3-origin before teaching a number.

### Misconceptions
- Belief: LTV and liquidation threshold are the same number. | Reality: LTV gates borrowing at open; the liquidation threshold is a higher number that gates seizure. The band between them is the only thing standing between a user and instant liquidation on a 1-tick price move. | Why: most UIs show one "borrow limit" bar and hide the second parameter. | Source: https://aave.com/docs/developers/smart-contracts/pool
- Belief: Health factor 1.5 means you are 50% away from liquidation in price terms. | Reality: HF is a value ratio, so the price drop that kills you depends on which side of the position is volatile; a 1.5 HF on a stable-collateral/volatile-debt position can die on a smaller move than on a volatile-collateral/stable-debt one. | Why: HF is taught as a scalar without asking which leg moves. | Source: https://aave.com/docs/developers/smart-contracts/pool
- Belief: Suppliers earn the borrow rate. | Reality: supplyRate = borrowRate x utilisation x (1 - reserveFactor). At 50% utilisation with a 15% reserve factor, a 6% borrow rate pays suppliers ~2.55%. | Why: dashboards show both APYs side by side without the derivation. | Source: https://aave.com/docs/developers/smart-contracts/interest-rate-strategy
- Belief: High utilisation is good — it means the pool is efficient. | Reality: utilisation is also the withdrawal-availability metric. At U=100% no supplier can exit; the kink's slope2 exists precisely to price that away. A pool at 100% U is a pool in a run. | Why: "capital efficiency" framing. | Source: https://aave.com/docs/developers/smart-contracts/interest-rate-strategy
- Belief: Isolated markets eliminate contagion. | Reality: they isolate *accounting* loss, not *behavioural* contagion. A curator's vault spread over many isolated markets re-pools the risk, and shared oracles or shared collateral assets re-couple markets that are nominally isolated. | Why: "isolated" is read as "independent". | Source: https://docs.morpho.org/curate/concepts/security-considerations/
- Belief: Bad debt only happens when liquidators are too slow. | Reality: the largest 2026 case had nothing to do with liquidation speed — the collateral was unbacked from the moment it was deposited, so no liquidation price existed. | Why: curricula teach bad debt as a liquidation-latency problem. | Source: https://www.coindesk.com/tech/2026/04/20/aave-could-face-up-to-usd230-million-in-losses-after-kelp-dao-bridge-exploit-triggers-defi-chaos
- Belief: Interest is computed per user on each block. | Reality: pools store a global index and per-user scaled balances; nothing is iterated. This is why balanceOf() is a view that multiplies, and why a user's "balance" changes with no transaction touching them. | Why: mental model imported from off-chain ledgers. | Source: https://github.com/aave-dao/aave-v3-origin

## 08.5 — Liquidations & LLAMMA

### Concepts
- liq-why-liquidation-exists — Liquidation is not punishment; it is the protocol buying back its own solvency by paying a third party to retire under-collateralised debt before the collateral is worth less than the loan. | requires: [lend-health-factor] | contrasts: [liq-bad-debt-threshold]
- liq-incentive — The liquidation bonus/incentive is the discount at which the liquidator buys seized collateral; it must exceed the liquidator's total cost (gas + slippage on the exit trade + inventory risk) or nobody calls the function. | requires: [liq-why-liquidation-exists] | contrasts: [liq-incentive-is-a-loss]
- liq-incentive-is-a-loss — Every unit of liquidation bonus is value taken from the borrower's equity, so incentive sizing is a direct trade-off between "liquidations always happen" and "borrowers are not robbed on a wick". | requires: [liq-incentive] | contrasts: [llamma-soft-liquidation]
- liq-close-factor — The close factor caps how much of a position one liquidation call may repay (classically 50%), which limits borrower damage but can leave dust positions that are unprofitable to finish. | requires: [liq-incentive] | contrasts: [liq-bad-debt-threshold]
- liq-dutch-auction — Maker-style liquidation runs a descending-price (Dutch) auction: the ask starts above market and falls on a curve until someone takes it, so the discount is discovered rather than fixed by governance. | requires: [liq-incentive] | contrasts: [liq-fixed-discount]
- liq-fixed-discount — Aave/Compound-style liquidation uses a fixed governance-set bonus and a first-come race, which is simpler and atomic but hands the entire surplus to whoever wins the ordering fight. | requires: [liq-incentive] | contrasts: [liq-dutch-auction]
- liq-flash-loan-liquidator — A modern liquidator holds no inventory: flash-borrow the debt asset, repay, seize collateral, swap it, repay the flash loan, keep the spread — so the capital requirement is zero and the only real barriers are gas and transaction ordering. | requires: [liq-fixed-discount, mev-searcher] | contrasts: []
- liq-keeper-liveness-assumption — Every liquidation design silently assumes a competitive keeper set with working RPC, funded hot wallets and a profitable route; the design fails exactly when that assumption fails, which is precisely during a crash. | requires: [liq-incentive] | contrasts: [llamma-soft-liquidation]
- liq-gas-price-coupling — Liquidation profitability is inversely coupled to network congestion, and congestion peaks during crashes, so the incentive is smallest exactly when it needs to be largest. | requires: [liq-keeper-liveness-assumption] | contrasts: []
- liq-bad-debt-threshold — A position becomes unliquidatable when debt exceeds collateral value minus the incentive; past that point rational liquidators stop and the residual is bad debt regardless of how many keepers are watching. | requires: [liq-incentive, lend-bad-debt] | contrasts: [liq-why-liquidation-exists]
- liq-cascade — Liquidation selling pushes the price down, which pushes more positions under water, which forces more selling: a reflexive loop whose speed is bounded only by block time and available exit liquidity. | requires: [liq-bad-debt-threshold] | contrasts: [llamma-soft-liquidation]
- liq-cascade-cross-venue — Cascades are cross-venue: CEX perp liquidations move the oracle price that on-chain lenders read, so a perp-market deleveraging becomes a DeFi liquidation event even with no on-chain trigger. | requires: [liq-cascade, orac-price-feed] | contrasts: []
- llamma-bands — LLAMMA discretises a price range into N bands (user picks 4-50); collateral is spread equally across them, and each band is a concentrated-liquidity range holding either pure collateral, pure stablecoin, or a mix. | requires: [amm-concentrated-liquidity] | contrasts: [liq-fixed-discount]
- llamma-soft-liquidation — As price falls through the bands, arbitrageurs progressively swap the user's collateral into crvUSD band by band; there is no discrete liquidation event, no close factor and no liquidation bonus — the position de-risks continuously and reverses if price recovers. | requires: [llamma-bands] | contrasts: [liq-fixed-discount, liq-dutch-auction]
- llamma-A-parameter — LLAMMA's A sets band width and therefore max LTV: LTV = 100% - loan_discount - 100 x N/(2A), so N=4, A=100 gives roughly 89% LTV and N=50 gives roughly 66% — the user trades leverage against soft-liquidation smoothness. | requires: [llamma-bands] | contrasts: [lend-ltv-vs-liquidation-threshold]
- llamma-soft-liquidation-loss — Soft liquidation is not free: each oscillation through the bands sells low and buys high, so the borrower bleeds value proportional to realised volatility inside the range — a continuous cost replacing the discrete liquidation penalty. | requires: [llamma-soft-liquidation] | contrasts: [liq-incentive-is-a-loss]
- llamma-hard-liquidation — LLAMMA still has a hard liquidation: when health goes below zero (after soft-liquidation losses and accrued interest) anyone may repay the debt and take the collateral, so soft liquidation defers rather than abolishes the classic path. | requires: [llamma-soft-liquidation-loss] | contrasts: [llamma-soft-liquidation]
- llamma-oracle-coupling — Because LLAMMA's internal price is pinned to an external oracle and arbitrage does the rebalancing, a manipulable oracle lets an attacker force positions into soft liquidation and then push them under water on demand. | requires: [llamma-soft-liquidation, orac-manipulation-cost] | contrasts: []

### Current state (Aug 2026)
- **10 October 2025 is now the canonical cascade case study**, displacing Black Thursday for scale. Trigger: a 100%-tariff-on-China announcement into a market carrying ~$217B open interest with no circuit breakers. ~$19B liquidated in 24h across ~1.6M accounts, roughly 9x the previous single-day record. The violent phase reportedly began ~20:50 UTC with ~$6.93B liquidated in 40 minutes (~$10.4B/hour) per Amberdata. BTC ~-14% ($122k -> ~$105k), ETH ~-12% to ~$3,436, SOL briefly -40%+, several alts -70 to -80% intraday.
- The 10 Oct 2025 crash also produced **on-chain** liquidation failures. Curve's CRV-long LlamaLend market accumulated roughly $700K of bad debt because prices moved faster than keepers could act while gas costs spiked — the textbook liq-gas-price-coupling failure. In April 2026 Curve proposed a market-based recovery (a dedicated StableSwap pool) rather than a treasury bailout, with modelled partial recovery starting around CRV ~$0.957 and full modelled recovery around CRV ~$1.242. This is a deliberate contrast case to Aave's donation-funded bailout the same month.
- **2 March 2026 — sDOLA/crvUSD LlamaLend exploit** is the best available demonstration that soft liquidation is not oracle-immune. A two-pronged flash-loan attack first executed a large swap on the LLAMMA AMM to force every position in the market into soft-liquidation state, then called `DolaSavings.stake()` to inflate the sDOLA exchange-rate oracle, rendering the now-soft-liquidated positions insolvent and hard-liquidatable. Reported result: 27 active borrowers hard-liquidated, roughly $10.9M of debt. The lesson: a yield-bearing-token exchange rate is an oracle, and if it can be moved by a single unpermissioned call, it can be attacked.
- Commonly taught and now questionable: "LLAMMA means you can never be liquidated." Both the CRV-long bad debt and the sDOLA attack are hard liquidations inside LLAMMA markets. Soft liquidation changes the shape of the loss curve; it does not remove the tail.
- Also worth pinning: the perp-DEX layer (Hyperliquid and peers) now matters to DeFi liquidation teaching because auto-deleveraging (ADL) and mark-price/oracle design there are the mechanism by which a CEX/perp cascade transmits into on-chain lender oracles.

### Misconceptions
- Belief: liquidations are performed by the protocol. | Reality: protocols never sell anything; they expose a `liquidationCall` and hope someone profitable shows up. Solvency is outsourced to an incentive. | Why: "the protocol liquidated me" is how the UI phrases it. | Source: https://aave.com/docs/developers/smart-contracts/pool
- Belief: A bigger liquidation bonus always makes the system safer. | Reality: the bonus is paid out of borrower equity and accelerates the death spiral — bigger bonus means each liquidation dumps more collateral and moves price further, feeding the cascade. There is an optimum, not a monotone. | Why: incentive framing without the reflexivity. | Source: https://www.coindesk.com/research/market-spotlight-the-19-billion-liquidation-that-shook-crypto
- Belief: Auctions guarantee fair price discovery. | Reality: MakerDAO's original English auctions produced winning bids of ~0 DAI during the March 2020 congestion because competing keepers could not get transactions included; the auction cleared "fairly" at nothing. Auctions inherit the liveness assumptions of the chain. | Why: auction theory taught without the mempool. | Source: https://docs.makerdao.com/smart-contract-modules/dog-and-clipper-detailed-documentation
- Belief: Soft liquidation is strictly better for the borrower. | Reality: soft liquidation charges a continuous cost proportional to realised volatility inside the band range. A choppy sideways market can bleed a LLAMMA borrower more than a single clean liquidation would have. | Why: "no liquidation penalty" is marketed as "no cost". | Source: https://docs.curve.finance/developer/crvusd/llamma-explainer
- Belief: More bands is always safer. | Reality: more bands lowers soft-liquidation loss but also lowers max LTV (LTV = 100% - loan_discount - 100·N/(2A)), so it is a leverage/smoothness dial, not a free safety setting. | Why: the formula is rarely shown. | Source: https://docs.curve.finance/developer/crvusd/llamma-explainer
- Belief: Bad debt means the liquidators were asleep. | Reality: in both the Oct 2025 CRV-long case and the March 2026 sDOLA case the failure was structural — gas/price-velocity coupling in one, oracle manipulation forcing insolvency in the other. Perfect keepers would not have prevented either. | Why: keeper-blaming is the easy story. | Source: https://gov.curve.finance/t/llamalend-sdola-long2-post-mortem/11020
- Belief: Liquidation MEV is a bug. | Reality: it is the payment mechanism. The competitive bidding that extracts the bonus is what guarantees sub-block liquidation latency; removing the competition removes the liveness. | Why: MEV is framed uniformly as extraction. | Source: https://www.coindesk.com/research/market-spotlight-the-19-billion-liquidation-that-shook-crypto

## 08.6 — Oracles & manipulation cost

### Concepts
- orac-price-feed — A blockchain has no native notion of "the price of ETH"; every DeFi valuation is a number some off-chain process wrote into a contract, so the oracle is the trust boundary of the entire protocol. | requires: [] | contrasts: []
- orac-push-model — In a push oracle the operator writes updates on its own schedule and pays the gas; consumers just read a storage slot, so reads are cheap and the price is whatever was last written, however long ago. | requires: [orac-price-feed] | contrasts: [orac-pull-model]
- orac-deviation-and-heartbeat — A push feed updates on two triggers only: the price moved more than the deviation threshold, or the heartbeat interval elapsed; between triggers the on-chain price is legitimately and knowably wrong. | requires: [orac-push-model] | contrasts: [orac-staleness-check]
- orac-staleness-check — Consuming `latestRoundData()` without checking `updatedAt` against your own maximum age (and without handling a reverting/paused feed) is the single most common oracle integration bug. | requires: [orac-deviation-and-heartbeat] | contrasts: []
- orac-pull-model — In a pull oracle the network signs prices continuously off-chain and the *consumer* submits a signed update in the same transaction that uses it, paying the gas; this buys sub-second freshness at the cost of putting update liveness on the caller. | requires: [orac-price-feed] | contrasts: [orac-push-model]
- orac-pull-verification — A pull update is trusted because the transaction verifies a signature/attestation over (feed id, price, publish time) against a known validator set, not because a privileged address wrote it. | requires: [orac-pull-model] | contrasts: []
- orac-confidence-interval — Pull networks publish a confidence band alongside the price; ignoring it and using the midpoint discards the oracle's own statement about how much it disagrees with itself, which is exactly the signal you want during a crash. | requires: [orac-pull-model] | contrasts: []
- orac-spot-price-is-not-an-oracle — Reading `getReserves()` or `slot0` gives the instantaneous marginal price, which any flash loan can move to an arbitrary value within one transaction; this is the origin of most historical oracle exploits. | requires: [orac-price-feed, defi-flash-loan] | contrasts: [orac-twap]
- orac-twap — A TWAP integrates price over a window, so moving the reported price by X requires holding the manipulated price for a proportion of the window, converting a one-transaction attack into a sustained-capital attack. | requires: [orac-spot-price-is-not-an-oracle] | contrasts: [orac-twap-lag]
- orac-twap-lag — A TWAP's safety and its staleness are the same parameter: a longer window costs more to manipulate and is more wrong during real moves, which is why long-window TWAPs cause bad debt in genuine crashes. | requires: [orac-twap] | contrasts: [orac-twap]
- orac-manipulation-cost — Manipulation cost is not the capital deployed but the *mark-to-market loss* the attacker eats — mostly value paid to arbitrageurs and LP fees — so it scales with pool depth and with how long the distortion must be held. | requires: [orac-twap] | contrasts: [orac-multiblock-mev]
- orac-multiblock-mev — Under PoS a proposer knows in advance whether it also proposes the next slot; consecutive slots let an attacker distort and revert the price with no arbitrageur in between, collapsing the dominant term of TWAP manipulation cost toward zero. | requires: [orac-manipulation-cost] | contrasts: [orac-twap]
- orac-multiblock-is-probabilistic — Multi-block MEV is a probability, not a capability: consecutive-slot chances scale with stake share, so the honest framing is "expected cost per attempt weighted by P(back-to-back proposal)", not "TWAPs are broken". | requires: [orac-multiblock-mev] | contrasts: [orac-multiblock-mev]
- orac-lp-token-and-rate-oracles — Pricing an LP share or a yield-bearing wrapper by calling its own `convertToAssets`/`get_virtual_price` makes a protocol function into an oracle; if that function can be moved in one transaction, so can the price. | requires: [orac-spot-price-is-not-an-oracle] | contrasts: [orac-sanity-bounds]
- orac-sanity-bounds — Defensive integration means bounding the oracle: min/max price clamps, cross-checking two independent sources and reverting on divergence, and treating a feed's own min/max answer as a real circuit breaker. | requires: [orac-staleness-check] | contrasts: []
- orac-rate-limit — Rate limiting (max % move per update, or per unit time) turns an instantaneous manipulation into a multi-block one, buying human/governance response time — at the cost of guaranteed inaccuracy during a genuine gap move. | requires: [orac-sanity-bounds] | contrasts: [orac-twap-lag]
- orac-oev — Oracle updates are themselves MEV: whoever lands the transaction immediately after a price update captures the liquidation bonus, so the oracle's write is an auctionable right ("oracle extractable value"). | requires: [orac-push-model, liq-fixed-discount, mev-searcher] | contrasts: []

### Current state (Aug 2026)
- **Pull oracles are now the default for anything latency-sensitive.** Pyth (~400ms publish cadence, consumer-submitted signed updates verified via Wormhole attestation) and Chainlink Data Streams occupy the perps/high-frequency niche; Chainlink push Data Feeds remain the default for lending protocols where a deviation+heartbeat feed is adequate and reads must be cheap.
- The 2026 state of the art on manipulation cost is arXiv 2606.03548 (FC'26 DeFi Workshop). Its practical takeaway for curriculum: **aggregate multiple pools weighted by liquidity, not equally** — liquidity weighting provably maximises the minimum cost of manipulation for weighted medians at any distortion level. Equal-weight or naive-mean multi-source aggregation is now demonstrably a weaker design.
- Commonly taught and now wrong: "Uniswap v3 TWAP is a safe oracle." The honest 2026 statement is conditional: safe on deep pairs against non-proposers, unsafe in the consecutive-slot case, and unsafe on any long-tail pair at any time. The Uniswap Labs paper itself frames PoS as removing the back-run penalty that made PoW TWAPs safe. Most protocols that used to use v3 TWAPs as their primary oracle have moved to Chainlink/Pyth with a TWAP as a sanity cross-check rather than the source of truth.
- Also worth noting: Uniswap v4's singleton PoolManager does not ship the v3 built-in observation cardinality oracle in the core contract — TWAP observation is a **hook** concern in v4. Teaching "use the pool's built-in oracle" is now version-dependent. (Flagged as needing verification against the v4 core repo.)
- The **yield-bearing-wrapper rate oracle** is the live 2026 attack surface, not spot-price flash loans. The March 2026 sDOLA/crvUSD LlamaLend attack moved an exchange-rate oracle with an unpermissioned `stake()` call (see 08.5); the same shape recurs for ERC-4626 `convertToAssets`, LST exchange rates and LP virtual prices. Donation/inflation attacks on 4626 vaults are the same bug class.
- **OEV recapture is now productised.** Chainlink SVR (Smart Value Recapture) and competing OEV networks auction the right to act on a fresh oracle update and route part of the liquidation bonus back to the protocol instead of to searchers. Treat this as the bridge topic between 08.6 and 08.8. (Deployment scope with Aave NOT verified this session.)

### Misconceptions
- Belief: Chainlink prices update every block. | Reality: they update on deviation threshold or heartbeat, and a heartbeat can be hours. Between updates the on-chain answer is stale by design and your contract must decide how stale is too stale. | Why: dashboards display a live-looking price. | Source: https://docs.chain.link/data-feeds
- Belief: A TWAP is safe because it "averages out" manipulation. | Reality: a TWAP converts a cheap instantaneous attack into an expensive sustained one; it does not make attack cost infinite. The cost is finite, computable from pool depth and window length, and collapses if the attacker proposes consecutive blocks. | Why: "average" is heard as "robust". | Source: https://www.chainsecurity.com/blog/oracle-manipulation-after-merge
- Belief: Manipulation cost equals the capital the attacker needs. | Reality: cost is the attacker's mark-to-market *loss* — fees paid and value handed to arbitrageurs. Flash loans make required capital ~zero while leaving the loss intact, which is exactly why flash loans break spot oracles but not deep TWAPs. | Why: headlines quote loan size, not attacker loss. | Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03548
- Belief: Multi-block MEV means all TWAPs are now free to manipulate. | Reality: it requires proposing consecutive slots, whose probability scales with stake share; the Uniswap Labs study finds two-block manipulation of top pairs still prohibitively expensive. The correct framing is a probability-weighted expected cost, and it varies enormously by pair depth. | Why: the finding gets flattened into a slogan. | Source: https://blog.uniswap.org/uniswap-v3-oracles
- Belief: Using two oracles is strictly safer. | Reality: only if you define what happens on disagreement. Taking max, min, or average of two sources each has a distinct failure mode; the only safe default is to revert or pause on divergence beyond a bound. | Why: redundancy is assumed to compose. | Source: https://docs.chain.link/data-feeds
- Belief: Averaging N sources equally is a good aggregation. | Reality: the 2026 result is that liquidity-weighted aggregation maximises the minimum manipulation cost; equal weighting lets an attacker target the thinnest venue. | Why: equal weighting is the intuitive default. | Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03548
- Belief: A pull oracle is fresher, therefore safer. | Reality: pull shifts update liveness onto the caller. If your liquidation bot fails to attach a fresh update, the on-chain price is as stale as any push feed — and the attacker chooses when to submit theirs. | Why: "400ms" is read as a guarantee about your contract's state. | Source: https://docs.pyth.network/price-feeds/pull-updates
- Belief: Rate-limiting the oracle prevents manipulation losses. | Reality: it converts them. A rate limiter guarantees your price is wrong during a genuine gap move, which produces bad debt from the other direction. It buys response time, not correctness. | Why: circuit breakers are framed as pure upside. | Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03548

## 08.7 — Stablecoins: collateralised vs algorithmic, peg mechanics, depeg dynamics, redemption/arbitrage

### Concepts
- stable-peg-is-not-a-property — A stablecoin's price is not enforced by its code; it is the outcome of arbitrageurs acting on a redemption or minting right, so "how does it hold the peg" always means "what trade does a deviation create, and who can do it". | requires: [] | contrasts: [stable-algorithmic]
- stable-redemption-right — The strength of a peg equals the strength of the redemption right: who may redeem, at what size, at what fee, on what timescale, and whether the issuer can refuse. Retail-inaccessible redemption means retail-inaccessible peg. | requires: [stable-peg-is-not-a-property] | contrasts: [stable-secondary-market-peg]
- stable-secondary-market-peg — Most holders never redeem; they exit on a DEX or CEX, so the visible "price" is a secondary-market price that can deviate from the redemption value by exactly the cost and latency of arbitrage. | requires: [stable-redemption-right] | contrasts: [stable-redemption-right]
- stable-fiat-backed — Fiat-backed coins (USDT, USDC, USDtb) are IOUs on off-chain reserves; the peg risk is not mechanism risk but custody, banking and legal risk, and the on-chain contract typically has a blacklist/freeze function. | requires: [stable-redemption-right] | contrasts: [stable-cdp]
- stable-reserve-composition — Reserve quality is the whole story for a fiat-backed coin: T-bills and overnight repo behave differently from uninsured commercial bank deposits, which is exactly what the March 2023 USDC/SVB depeg demonstrated. | requires: [stable-fiat-backed] | contrasts: []
- stable-cdp — Crypto-backed (CDP) stablecoins are minted as over-collateralised debt against volatile collateral, so every unit in circulation is someone's leveraged long and the supply contracts when leverage demand falls. | requires: [lend-overcollateralisation, stable-peg-is-not-a-property] | contrasts: [stable-fiat-backed]
- stable-psm — A Peg Stability Module lets anyone swap a whitelisted asset (usually USDC) for the stablecoin 1:1 at near-zero fee, which pins the peg hard — and converts the CDP stablecoin into a partially fiat-backed one whose risk profile now includes the whitelisted asset's. | requires: [stable-cdp] | contrasts: [stable-cdp]
- stable-monetary-policy — CDP systems steer the peg with an interest-rate lever (stability fee / savings rate / crvUSD monetary policy): below peg, raise borrow rates to force repayment and burn supply; above peg, cut rates to encourage minting. | requires: [stable-cdp] | contrasts: [stable-psm]
- stable-pegkeeper — Curve's PegKeepers are the automated version: pre-authorised contracts that mint crvUSD into a StableSwap pool when it trades above peg and buy/burn when below, using pool imbalance as the trigger. | requires: [stable-monetary-policy, amm-stableswap] | contrasts: [stable-psm]
- stable-algorithmic — A purely algorithmic (seigniorage) stablecoin backs each unit with a claim on newly minted governance tokens rather than an asset, which works while the governance token has value and fails catastrophically when the peg break destroys that value. | requires: [stable-peg-is-not-a-property] | contrasts: [stable-cdp, stable-fiat-backed]
- stable-death-spiral — The algorithmic failure mode is reflexive: depeg -> redeem into governance token -> governance token supply inflates and price falls -> redemption backing falls -> deeper depeg. It is a run whose fuel is created by the defence mechanism itself. | requires: [stable-algorithmic] | contrasts: [stable-monetary-policy]
- stable-synthetic-delta-neutral — Delta-neutral synthetic dollars (Ethena USDe) hold spot crypto and an equal short perpetual position, so the dollar value is hedged and the yield is the funding rate; the backing is a *trade*, not a reserve. | requires: [stable-peg-is-not-a-property, perp-funding-rate] | contrasts: [stable-fiat-backed, stable-algorithmic]
- stable-synthetic-risks — A delta-neutral dollar's risks are exchange/custody risk, negative funding regimes, and forced deleveraging or ADL during a cascade — none of which look like the collateral risk of a CDP. | requires: [stable-synthetic-delta-neutral] | contrasts: [stable-cdp]
- stable-yield-bearing-is-not-a-stablecoin — Tokens like xUSD, deUSD and similar "yield-bearing dollars" are fund shares wearing a dollar's name; their price is a NAV, so a manager loss is a permanent write-down, not a temporary depeg. | requires: [stable-synthetic-delta-neutral] | contrasts: [stable-peg-is-not-a-property]
- stable-collateral-contagion — When lending protocols accept a yield-bearing pseudo-dollar as collateral, its NAV write-down becomes instant bad debt across every market that listed it, converting one fund's loss into system-wide insolvency. | requires: [stable-yield-bearing-is-not-a-stablecoin, lend-isolated-vs-pooled] | contrasts: []
- stable-depeg-asymmetry — Depegs are asymmetric: trading below peg is a solvency signal that triggers a run, while trading above peg is usually a liquidity/mint-capacity signal, and the two need completely different responses. | requires: [stable-secondary-market-peg] | contrasts: []
- stable-regulatory-boundary — Under the US GENIUS Act a compliant "payment stablecoin" must be fiat-reserve-backed and may not pay yield to holders, which structurally excludes CDP, algorithmic and synthetic dollars from that category and pushes issuers to run two separate products. | requires: [stable-fiat-backed, stable-synthetic-delta-neutral] | contrasts: [stable-cdp]

### Current state (Aug 2026)
- **Regulation is now the primary design constraint, not mechanism.** The GENIUS Act was signed 18 July 2025; most required rulemakings were due roughly July 2026. A compliant "payment stablecoin" must be fiat-reserve-backed and **may not pay yield to holders**. Crypto-collateralised, over-collateralised and algorithmic designs have no path into that category. Practical consequence: issuers now run split product lines — Ethena ships USDtb (GENIUS-aligned, no yield) alongside USDe (synthetic, yield-bearing), and Tether has moved to launch a separate US-domiciled token rather than restructure USDT.
- Supply picture (Q1 2026, vendor-sourced, treat as approximate): USDT ~$189B, USDC ~$77.6B. USDC overtook USDT on **annual transaction volume** in 2025 ($18.3T vs $13.3T) while remaining far smaller by supply — a good lesson that supply and usage are different metrics. USDe grew from under $6B at the start of 2025 to over $14B, roughly 5% of the stablecoin market.
- **The Stream Finance / Elixir collapse (Nov 2025) is the defining recent case and it is NOT an algorithmic-stablecoin story.** Sequence: the 10 Oct 2025 crash liquidated a fund manager's leveraged positions; on 4 Nov 2025 Stream disclosed ~$93M of losses and froze withdrawals; xUSD fell ~77% to around $0.26 intraday, stranding ~$160M. Elixir's deUSD, which had lent roughly 65% of its backing to Stream, fell ~98% to about $0.015 and was wound down (redemptions processed for ~80% of holders, then sunset). Stables Labs' USDX broke below $0.60 days later. Researchers traced roughly $285M of interconnected debt; Euler, Morpho, Silo and Gearbox had all accepted xUSD as collateral, so the loss propagated into lending markets. Roughly $1B exited DeFi yield within a week.
- Commonly taught and now wrong: "stablecoins are either fiat-backed, crypto-backed, or algorithmic." The 2025-2026 blow-ups came from a **fourth** category the taxonomy has no slot for — opaque yield-bearing fund shares marketed as dollars, whose "peg" is a self-reported NAV. Teaching the three-way taxonomy leaves learners unable to classify the actual failures.
- Also commonly taught and now stale: DAI as the canonical CDP stablecoin. MakerDAO rebranded to **Sky** with USDS alongside DAI; documentation URLs under docs.makerdao.com are unreliable. Check current Sky docs before teaching PSM parameters or the DAI Savings Rate.
- crvUSD's design remains the best teaching example of coupling peg defence to a rate lever: an EMA price aggregator across multiple Curve pools feeds the monetary policy contract, and PegKeepers act on pool imbalance.

### Misconceptions
- Belief: A stablecoin's smart contract enforces the $1 price. | Reality: no contract can set a market price. The contract creates a redemption or mint right; arbitrageurs create the price. Every peg question reduces to "what is the arbitrage, and what is its friction". | Why: "pegged" sounds like a hard constraint. | Source: https://docs.curve.finance/developer/crvusd/overview
- Belief: USDC depegged in March 2023 because of a flaw in its design. | Reality: it depegged because a portion of reserves sat in an uninsured deposit at a failing bank and redemptions were closed over a weekend. The mechanism worked exactly as designed; the reserve and the banking calendar did not. | Why: on-chain people look for on-chain causes. | Source: (SVB/USDC — verify a primary source before citing)
- Belief: Over-collateralisation makes a stablecoin safe. | Reality: over-collateralisation makes it *solvent at current oracle prices*. It does not make it liquid, and a PSM full of one fiat-backed asset re-imports that asset's risk wholesale. | Why: collateral ratio is the only number the dashboards show. | Source: https://docs.makerdao.com/smart-contract-modules/peg-stability-module
- Belief: Terra failed because it was under-collateralised. | Reality: it was *un*-collateralised by construction; the redemption gave you newly minted LUNA, so the defence mechanism inflated the asset backing the defence. The failure was reflexivity, not a shortfall. | Why: "under-collateralised" is the nearest familiar concept. | Source: (Terra post-mortems — URL unverified)
- Belief: Ethena's USDe is algorithmic and will Terra itself. | Reality: it is delta-neutral — real spot collateral plus an offsetting short perp. Its risks are counterparty/exchange risk, sustained negative funding, and forced deleveraging, which are different failure modes entirely. Conflating them teaches the wrong risk checklist. | Why: "synthetic dollar" pattern-matches to "algorithmic". | Source: https://www.forbes.com/sites/digital-assets/2026/06/15/ethenas-usde-pays-yield-legally-and-the-genius-act-has-no-answer-for-it/
- Belief: A token trading at $1.00 on a DEX is at peg. | Reality: it is at *secondary-market* peg. If redemption is whitelisted, gated, or suspended, the secondary price is a market's guess at eventual recovery value. xUSD traded near $1 right up to the withdrawal freeze. | Why: price charts are the only visible signal. | Source: https://blockeden.xyz/blog/2025/11/08/m-defi-contagion/
- Belief: A depeg is a temporary dislocation that arbitrage will close. | Reality: only if the deviation is a liquidity gap. If it is a solvency gap, arbitrage is precisely the mechanism that drains the remaining reserves to the fastest actors. Distinguishing the two in real time is the actual skill. | Why: "arbitrage closes the gap" is taught unconditionally. | Source: https://pharos.watch/learn/case-studies/stream-elixir-contagion-2025/
- Belief: Yield on a stablecoin comes from lending it out. | Reality: for the 2025 failures it came from an off-chain discretionary trading strategy with no on-chain proof of reserves. "Where does the yield come from, and can I verify it on chain" is the whole diligence question. | Why: DeFi-native yield intuition transfers badly to opaque funds. | Source: https://www.ccn.com/education/crypto/why-xusd-depegged-stream-finance-stablecoin-crisis-explained/

## 08.8 — MEV: sandwiches, arbitrage, liquidation MEV, private orderflow, intents/solvers, and 2026 reshaping

### Concepts
- mev-definition — MEV is the value a party can extract purely by choosing which transactions go in a block and in what order — it exists because ordering is a discretionary resource, not because of any bug. | requires: [] | contrasts: []
- mev-searcher — A searcher is a bot that finds an ordering-dependent profit and bids for the right to have its bundle placed at a specific position; searchers are price-takers on blockspace and price-makers on the opportunity. | requires: [mev-definition] | contrasts: [mev-builder]
- mev-atomic-arbitrage — Cross-DEX arbitrage is risk-free within a transaction: buy on the cheap venue, sell on the expensive one, revert if unprofitable — so it is pure competition on latency and bid, and it is the mechanism that keeps DEX prices consistent. | requires: [mev-searcher] | contrasts: [mev-sandwich]
- mev-arbitrage-is-lvr — The arbitrage profit is paid by liquidity providers: LVR (loss-versus-rebalancing) is the same value seen from the LP's side, which is why "arbitrage is harmless" is false — it is an ongoing transfer from LPs to searchers. | requires: [mev-atomic-arbitrage] | contrasts: [mev-atomic-arbitrage]
- mev-sandwich — A sandwich buys ahead of a victim's swap to push the price, lets the victim execute at the worse price, then sells behind it; the victim's slippage tolerance is the attacker's profit budget. | requires: [mev-searcher, amm-slippage] | contrasts: [mev-atomic-arbitrage]
- mev-slippage-is-the-attack-surface — Setting a wide slippage tolerance does not "make the trade succeed"; it explicitly authorises up to that much extraction. Slippage settings are a security parameter that UIs present as a convenience setting. | requires: [mev-sandwich] | contrasts: []
- mev-liquidation — Liquidation MEV is the race to be first after a health-factor breach or an oracle update; it is the payment that makes lending protocols solvent, so it is the clearest case where MEV is load-bearing rather than parasitic. | requires: [mev-searcher, liq-fixed-discount] | contrasts: [mev-sandwich]
- mev-toxic-vs-benign — The useful axis is not "MEV good/bad" but whether extraction requires a worse outcome for a specific user (sandwiching, some liquidations) or merely captures a public inconsistency (arbitrage, backruns); the two need different remedies. | requires: [mev-sandwich, mev-atomic-arbitrage] | contrasts: []
- mev-pbs — Proposer-builder separation splits "propose a block" from "order its transactions": builders assemble and bid, proposers blindly sell the slot to the highest bid, so validators no longer need in-house MEV expertise to earn MEV. | requires: [mev-definition] | contrasts: [mev-searcher]
- mev-builder — Builders are the actual ordering monopolists in practice; a small number of them win the vast majority of slots because exclusive orderflow deals compound into better bids, which win more slots, which attract more exclusive flow. | requires: [mev-pbs] | contrasts: [mev-pbs]
- mev-relay-trust — MEV-Boost interposes relays that hold the block body until the proposer signs the header; this is an out-of-protocol trust assumption that ePBS (EIP-7732) is designed to remove. | requires: [mev-pbs] | contrasts: [mev-epbs]
- mev-private-orderflow — Routing a transaction to a builder privately instead of the public mempool removes it from bot visibility, which protects the user and simultaneously entrenches the builders who receive that flow. | requires: [mev-builder, mev-sandwich] | contrasts: [mev-encrypted-mempool]
- mev-orderflow-auction — An orderflow auction (MEV-Share, MEV Blocker) sells the right to backrun a user's transaction and refunds part of the proceeds to the user, converting extraction into rebate. | requires: [mev-private-orderflow] | contrasts: [mev-sandwich]
- mev-intent — An intent expresses the desired outcome ("at least X of token B for exactly Y of token A, before time T") and is signed off-chain; the user never publishes an executable path, so there is nothing specific to front-run. | requires: [mev-private-orderflow] | contrasts: [mev-sandwich]
- mev-solver-competition — Solvers compete in a batch or Dutch auction to fill intents; whatever MEV exists in the fill is competed into the user's price rather than extracted around it, so the user's protection is the competitiveness of the solver set, not cryptography. | requires: [mev-intent] | contrasts: [mev-encrypted-mempool]
- mev-cow — Batch auctions enable coincidence-of-wants: two opposing intents in the same batch settle against each other with no AMM, no slippage and no MEV surface, and a uniform clearing price removes intra-batch ordering value entirely. | requires: [mev-solver-competition] | contrasts: [mev-atomic-arbitrage]
- mev-encrypted-mempool — Encrypting transactions until ordering is fixed (threshold decryption, TEEs, or delay encryption) attacks MEV at the information layer rather than the incentive layer; the trade-off is a new liveness/trust assumption on the decryption committee or hardware. | requires: [mev-private-orderflow] | contrasts: [mev-solver-competition]
- mev-tee-block-building — BuilderNet runs many operators executing the same builder inside TEEs so orderflow can be shared without any operator seeing it, aiming to decentralise building without giving up the value of aggregated flow. | requires: [mev-builder, mev-encrypted-mempool] | contrasts: [mev-builder]
- mev-oev — Oracle-extractable value is the special case where the profitable ordering is "immediately after the oracle update"; auctioning that position lets protocols recapture liquidation value that would otherwise go to searchers. | requires: [mev-liquidation, orac-oev] | contrasts: []
- mev-l2-sequencer — On a single-sequencer rollup the sequencer holds the entire ordering right, so L2 MEV is a policy question (FCFS, private mempool, encrypted ordering) rather than an auction; several rollups have effectively eliminated sandwiching by not having a public mempool. | requires: [mev-definition] | contrasts: [mev-pbs]

### Current state (Aug 2026)
- **Sandwiching on L1 has been substantially suppressed by private orderflow, not by protocol changes.** Reported sandwich extraction on Ethereum fell from roughly $10M/month in late 2024 to roughly $2.5M/month by October 2025, attributed to adoption of protected RPCs (Flashbots Protect, MEV Blocker) and intent-based routing. Academic measurement in early 2026 finds sandwiching endemic and profitable on L1 but rare and largely unprofitable on rollups that have no public mempool.
- **Intents are now mainstream execution infrastructure, not an experiment.** UniswapX (gasless, cross-chain, filler-competition), CoW Protocol (batch auctions with uniform clearing prices) and 1inch Fusion together handle billions in monthly volume. CoW Swap is reported among the top five Ethereum DEXs by volume with >$80B cumulative and a claimed 30-60 bps typical improvement versus routing the same trade directly through Uniswap (vendor-adjacent claim — see gaps).
- **Builder concentration remains the unsolved problem.** The shared baseline says top-2 builders ~73% (Titan, Quasar), top-4 >95%, MEV-Boost building ~90% of blocks. A 2026 source instead reports "roughly 80% of Ethereum blocks built by two builders: Titan and BuilderNet". These are inconsistent — see gaps. Either way the qualitative fact is stable: two entities order most of Ethereum.
- **BuilderNet is the notable structural response.** Many independent operators run bit-identical builder software inside TEEs; each instance shares received orderflow with the others (safe because no operator can read inside the enclave), blocks are submitted through normal relays, and the winning block's value is refunded to orderflow providers in proportion to measured contribution. It attacks exclusive-orderflow lock-in rather than MEV itself.
- **Protocol-level changes have NOT landed.** Per the shared baseline: Glamsterdam is not shipped (final devnet mid-June 2026, targeted H2 2026, no mainnet date); EIP-7732 (ePBS) and EIP-7928 (block-level access lists) are the headline EIPs; **FOCIL (EIP-7805) was declined for Glamsterdam and deferred to Hegota**. So the censorship-resistance leg of the MEV story is still pending. Do not teach inclusion lists as live.
- **Encrypted mempools moved from research to advocacy in 2026** (encryptedmempool.org, Shutter's threshold-encryption work, core-dev discussion of hiding pending trades). Still not an L1 protocol feature. Teach it as a live design debate with a real trade-off (decryption committee liveness and trust) rather than as a coming solution.
- **New in 2026: MEV infrastructure is itself an attack surface.** A large sandwich bot was drained in June 2026 — a reminder that searcher/builder contracts are unaudited high-value targets, and a nice narrative hook.
- Commonly taught and now wrong: "use Flashbots Protect and you're safe from MEV." Private RPCs stop *public-mempool* sandwiching; they do not stop the receiving builder from being the extractor, and they concentrate orderflow. The honest framing is a trust shift, not an elimination.
- Also commonly taught and now wrong: "MEV is a tax on users." Much of the largest MEV category (atomic arbitrage) is paid by *liquidity providers* via LVR, not by swappers. And liquidation MEV is a fee the protocol deliberately pays for solvency.

### Misconceptions
- Belief: MEV is caused by the mempool being public. | Reality: MEV is caused by ordering being valuable. Hiding transactions changes *who* captures it, not whether it exists — a private builder with exclusive flow captures it silently. | Why: sandwiching is the most visible form and does depend on visibility. | Source: https://ethereum.org/en/developers/docs/mev/
- Belief: Arbitrage MEV is victimless. | Reality: the arbitrageur's profit is the LP's LVR. Passive AMM LPs pay it continuously, which is a large part of why passive LPing underperforms holding. | Why: no single user's transaction gets worse, so no victim is visible. | Source: https://ethereum.org/en/developers/docs/mev/
- Belief: Higher slippage tolerance means your trade is more likely to succeed. | Reality: it means you have pre-authorised a larger sandwich. Slippage tolerance is the attacker's profit cap, chosen by the victim. | Why: wallets present it as a reliability dial. | Source: https://blog.shutter.network/how-to-protect-yourself-against-malicious-mev-in-2026/
- Belief: Intents eliminate MEV. | Reality: intents relocate it. The solver captures whatever ordering value exists and competes it back into the user's quote; if the solver set is small or colluding, the user gets a worse price and never sees why. Your protection is auction competitiveness. | Why: "MEV-protected" marketing. | Source: https://docs.cow.fi/cow-protocol/concepts/introduction/intents
- Belief: PBS decentralises block production. | Reality: PBS decentralises *proposing* while concentrating *building*. Two builders order most Ethereum blocks. PBS made validator income fairer and builder power more concentrated at the same time. | Why: "separation" is heard as "distribution". | Source: https://buildernet.org/blog/introducing-buildernet
- Belief: MEV-Boost is part of the Ethereum protocol. | Reality: it is out-of-protocol middleware with relays as trusted intermediaries; ethereum.org itself calls it a temporary implementation with higher trust assumptions. EIP-7732 is the attempt to enshrine it, and it has not shipped. | Why: ~90% adoption reads as canonical. | Source: https://ethereum.org/en/developers/docs/mev/
- Belief: Inclusion lists (FOCIL) are coming in the next fork. | Reality: EIP-7805 was declined for Glamsterdam and deferred to Hegota. Any 2025-vintage material saying otherwise is stale. | Why: it was widely discussed as a Glamsterdam candidate. | Source: shared baseline; verify at eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-7805
- Belief: L2s have less MEV because they are faster. | Reality: they have less *sandwich* MEV because a single sequencer with no public mempool removes the information the attack needs. The sequencer itself holds an unaudited ordering monopoly, which is a larger concentration than L1's. | Why: "no sandwiches observed" is read as "no MEV". | Source: https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2601.19570

### Practice ideas
- kind: implement — Write a sandwich simulator in TypeScript against a constant-product pool: given a victim swap size, pool reserves, fee and slippage tolerance, compute the attacker's optimal frontrun size and resulting profit. — Acceptance: your tool outputs the profit curve versus frontrun size, identifies the optimum, and shows profit going to zero as slippage tolerance approaches the true price impact.
- kind: measure — Take 100 recent large swaps from a public RPC/archive and classify each as sandwiched or not by inspecting the transactions immediately before and after within the same block from the same address. — Acceptance: you report an observed sandwich rate and total extracted value for your sample, and can explain why your L1 number differs from the same measurement on an L2.
- kind: implement — Build a backrun-only arbitrage bot on a fork: watch for a large swap, compute the post-swap price gap against a second pool, and submit an atomic arb. — Acceptance: the bot profits on a simulated large swap and reverts (costing only gas) when the gap is smaller than fees — proving atomic arbitrage's risk profile is fundamentally different from sandwiching.
- kind: measure — Compare execution for the same trade size across a direct Uniswap v4 route, a UniswapX/CoW quote, and a direct route via a protected RPC. — Acceptance: a table of realised output for each path at three trade sizes, with a statement of where the intent route wins and where it loses (it does lose — usually on small trades where solver fees dominate).
- kind: read — Read the MEV-Share protocol repo and the BuilderNet introduction back to back. — Acceptance: you can state precisely what each one privatises, who is trusted in each, and what each does with the extracted value.
- kind: break — Deploy a naive DEX-router contract that accepts `amountOutMin = 0` and demonstrate full extraction. — Acceptance: a Foundry test where a sandwich takes an arbitrarily large share of the trade, then a fixed version with a computed `amountOutMin` and a deadline that makes the same attack unprofitable.
- kind: write — Write the MEV supply chain as a one-page sequence: user -> wallet/RPC -> (public mempool | private orderflow | intent auction) -> searcher -> builder -> relay -> proposer, marking at each hop who can see the transaction and who can reorder it. — Acceptance: for each of MEV-Share, CoW batch auction, and a plain public-mempool swap, you can trace a different path through the same diagram.
