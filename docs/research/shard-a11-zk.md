## 10.2 — Proof systems intuition

### Concepts
- proof-system-relation — A proof system proves membership in a relation R(x, w): the verifier knows the public statement x, the prover claims to know a witness w with R(x,w)=true. | requires: [] | contrasts: [proof-system-not-magic]
- argument-of-knowledge — SNARKs/STARKs are *arguments*, not proofs: soundness only holds against computationally bounded provers, and "of knowledge" means a witness could in principle be extracted from a successful prover. | requires: [proof-system-relation] | contrasts: [information-theoretic-soundness]
- succinctness — Succinct means the proof size and verification time are sublinear (in practice near-constant or polylogarithmic) in the size of the computation being proved. | requires: [proof-system-relation] | contrasts: [zero-knowledge-property]
- zero-knowledge-property — Zero-knowledge means the proof reveals nothing about the witness beyond the truth of the statement; it is an *optional, orthogonal* add-on to succinctness. | requires: [proof-system-relation] | contrasts: [succinctness]
- zk-rollups-are-not-zk — Production "ZK rollups" use only succinctness for compression; their transaction data is public, so they are validity rollups, not privacy systems. | requires: [succinctness, zero-knowledge-property] | contrasts: [shielded-pool]
- arithmetization — Before proving, a computation is converted into polynomial constraints over a finite field (R1CS, PLONKish gates, or an AIR trace table); this translation step is where most engineering effort and most bugs live. | requires: [proof-system-relation] | contrasts: [evm-execution]
- non-interactivity-fiat-shamir — Interactive protocols are made non-interactive by the Fiat–Shamir transform, replacing the verifier's random challenges with hashes of the transcript; getting the hashed transcript wrong is a recurring real-world soundness bug. | requires: [argument-of-knowledge] | contrasts: [interactive-proof]
- trusted-setup — Pairing-based SNARKs need public parameters (a structured reference string) generated from secret randomness; anyone who retains that secret ("toxic waste") can forge proofs for false statements. | requires: [succinctness] | contrasts: [transparent-setup]
- universal-vs-circuit-specific-setup — Groth16 needs a fresh ceremony per circuit; PLONK/KZG-style systems use one universal, updatable SRS reusable by any circuit up to a size bound. | requires: [trusted-setup] | contrasts: [transparent-setup]
- transparent-setup — STARKs and FRI-based systems need no secret setup: their public randomness is publicly derivable, which also makes them plausibly post-quantum since they rest only on hash assumptions. | requires: [trusted-setup] | contrasts: [trusted-setup]
- proof-size-verify-cost-tradeoff — Groth16 proofs are ~200 bytes and cheapest to verify on-chain; STARK proofs are tens-to-hundreds of KB but need no setup — hence the standard pattern of proving with a STARK and wrapping in a SNARK for the final on-chain verification. | requires: [succinctness, transparent-setup] | contrasts: [onchain-verifier-gas]
- recursion — A verifier can itself be expressed as a circuit, so one proof can attest that other proofs verified; this enables constant-size proofs over unbounded computation, incremental proving, and parallel distributed proving. | requires: [arithmetization, argument-of-knowledge] | contrasts: [proof-aggregation]
- proof-aggregation — Aggregation combines many independent proofs into one for a batched verification; recursion is the general mechanism, aggregation is the specific batching use case that amortises on-chain verification cost. | requires: [recursion] | contrasts: [recursion]
- zkvm-vs-circuit — A zkVM proves the execution trace of a general-purpose VM (RISC-V etc.) so you write ordinary Rust; a hand-written circuit encodes one specific relation with far fewer constraints but far more expert effort. | requires: [arithmetization] | contrasts: [noir-circuit-model]
- prover-verifier-asymmetry — Proving is orders of magnitude more expensive than the original computation (historically ~10^5–10^6x overhead, now much lower for zkVMs but still large); verification is cheap and roughly constant. This asymmetry is the whole economic point. | requires: [succinctness] | contrasts: [reexecution-economics]

- [Post-quantum cryptography on Ethereum](https://ethereum.org/roadmap/future-proofing/quantum-resistance/) — tier: canonical-docs — published: 2026 (living) — Why the roadmap is drifting toward hash-only assumptions (STARKs) and away from pairing-based setups.

### Current state (Aug 2026)
- **Real-time proving is a solved-ish engineering problem, not a research one.** EF's stated bar: ~99% of L1 blocks proved within ~10s, 128-bit provable security (100-bit minimum at launch), proof size under 300 KiB, prover hardware under $100k and under 10 kW. Succinct's SP1 Hypercube reports proving >93–99% of blocks under 12s on 16 NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPUs. (Baseline said ">93% under 12s"; vendor blog also claims 99.7% under 12s and 95.4% under 10s on 16 GPUs — **these two figures are from different posts and I did not reconcile them; treat the exact percentage as unpinned.**)
- **The 2026 emphasis moved from speed to soundness.** EF shifted the roadmap to provable-security bits, requiring teams to use `soundcalc`, hit ≥100-bit by ~May 2026 and 128-bit with <300 KiB proofs by end of 2026. Conjectured-security claims that were common in 2023–24 marketing are now explicitly not acceptable.
- **Poseidon was dropped for L1.** Ethereum walked away from Poseidon as the L1 ZK-friendly hash, pivoting toward SHA/BLAKE-family, on conservatism/cryptanalysis grounds. Teaching "Poseidon is the standard ZK hash on Ethereum" is now wrong for L1 (it remains widely used inside L2/app circuits).
- **"Lean Ethereum" is the long-horizon frame:** hash-based validator signatures (leanXMSS) + a minimal zkVM (leanVM) to aggregate them, so the hash function becomes the single cryptographic assumption. Production leanVM targeted 2027, deployments 2028, full post-quantum posture ~2029. None of this is shipped; treat as direction, not fact.
- **Architecture shift inside provers:** from univariate-polynomial STARKs (FRI over Plonky3-style AIRs) toward multilinear/sumcheck-based IOPs (SP1 Hypercube, Binius-lineage work). Small fields (Mersenne-31, BabyBear, Goldilocks) plus GPU are the performance story.
- **The standard production shape is a pipeline, not a single system:** transparent STARK for the bulk proving, then recursive wrapping into a small pairing-based SNARK (Groth16/PLONK) for cheap on-chain verification.
- Baseline confirmed: SP1 vs RISC Zero cost gap (~$0.001 vs ~$0.10 per proof) and SP1's CPU-friendliness vs RISC Zero's GPU dependence remain the commonly cited comparison, though both are vendor-adjacent numbers.

### Misconceptions
- Belief: "ZK rollups are private." | Reality: Production validity rollups publish all transaction data; zero-knowledge is not used, only succinctness. | Why: The marketing name "ZK rollup" conflates two independent properties. | Source: https://ethereum.org/roadmap/zkevm/
- Belief: "A SNARK proves the computation is correct, absolutely." | Reality: It is an *argument* — soundness holds only against computationally bounded adversaries, and only up to the claimed security bits (which several systems set below 128). | Why: "Proof" in the name; conjectured vs provable security was rarely distinguished before EF's 2026 push. | Source: https://blog.ethereum.org/2025/12/18/zkevm-security-foundations
- Belief: "Trusted setup means one party you must trust." | Reality: Modern ceremonies are multi-party and 1-of-N honest: a single honest participant who deletes their contribution makes the SRS safe. | Why: The phrase "trusted setup" implies a trusted party. | Source: https://ceremony.ethereum.org/
- Belief: "STARKs are strictly better because no trusted setup." | Reality: They trade setup away for much larger proofs and higher verification cost; that is why nearly everyone wraps a STARK in a SNARK before touching L1. | Why: Transparency is easy to explain and marketable; the proof-size cost is not. | Source: https://blog.ethereum.org/en/2025/07/10/realtime-proving
- Belief: "Poseidon is the ZK hash function Ethereum uses." | Reality: Ethereum L1 dropped Poseidon in favour of SHA/BLAKE-family for the post-quantum roadmap. | Why: Poseidon dominated 2021–2024 circuit tooling and most tutorials. | Source: https://ethereum.org/roadmap/future-proofing/quantum-resistance/
- Belief: "Recursion and aggregation are the same thing." | Reality: Recursion is the general capability (a verifier expressed as a circuit); aggregation is one application of it (batch N proofs into 1). Aggregation can also be done without full recursion, e.g. by batching FRI low-degree tests. | Why: Both reduce to "many proofs become one" in casual explanation. | Source: https://www.maya-zk.com/blog/proof-aggregation
- Belief: "Proving is roughly as expensive as running the code." | Reality: Proving carries orders-of-magnitude overhead; the design point is that you pay it once, off-chain, so that many parties can verify cheaply. | Why: Real-time-proving headlines hide the hardware footprint behind them (16 GPUs, ~10 kW budget). | Source: https://blog.ethereum.org/en/2025/07/10/realtime-proving

## 10.3 — Commitments & KZG

### Concepts
- commitment-scheme — A commitment is a short binding-and-hiding stand-in for data: you publish `C`, cannot later change what it commits to (binding), and `C` alone reveals nothing (hiding). | requires: [] | contrasts: [hash-commitment]
- hash-commitment — A plain hash is a commitment to a whole blob, but proving anything about *part* of the data requires revealing it or a Merkle path whose size grows with the data. | requires: [commitment-scheme] | contrasts: [polynomial-commitment]
- polynomial-commitment — A polynomial commitment commits to a polynomial `p` such that you can later open `p(z) = y` at any point `z` with a proof, without revealing `p`. | requires: [commitment-scheme] | contrasts: [hash-commitment]
- data-as-polynomial — Arbitrary data is interpreted as the evaluations of a polynomial over a fixed domain; a blob is exactly 4096 field elements = 4096 evaluations. | requires: [polynomial-commitment] | contrasts: [data-as-bytes]
- kzg-scheme — KZG (Kate–Zaverucha–Goldberg) commits to a degree-d polynomial as a single elliptic-curve group element, with constant-size (48-byte) commitments and constant-size (48-byte) opening proofs regardless of d. | requires: [polynomial-commitment, pairing] | contrasts: [fri-commitment]
- kzg-opening-proof — An opening proof for `p(z)=y` is a commitment to the quotient polynomial `q(X) = (p(X) - y) / (X - z)`; the verifier checks the quotient identity with one pairing equation. | requires: [kzg-scheme, pairing] | contrasts: [merkle-proof]
- structured-reference-string — KZG needs a public SRS: encrypted powers of a secret τ, i.e. `[1]G, [τ]G, [τ²]G, …`. Knowing τ lets you open a commitment to any value you like, breaking binding. | requires: [kzg-scheme, trusted-setup] | contrasts: [transparent-setup]
- powers-of-tau-ceremony — The SRS is produced by an MPC where each participant multiplies in their own secret and discards it; the result is safe if at least one participant was honest (1-of-N trust). | requires: [structured-reference-string] | contrasts: [trusted-setup]
- kzg-ceremony-ethereum — Ethereum's KZG Summoning Ceremony ran Jan–Apr 2023 with ~141,000+ contributors, producing the SRS used by EIP-4844 blobs and intended to serve full Danksharding. | requires: [powers-of-tau-ceremony] | contrasts: [circuit-specific-ceremony]
- versioned-hash — The EVM never sees a blob or its 48-byte commitment directly; it sees a 32-byte versioned hash `0x01 || sha256(commitment)[1:]` returned by BLOBHASH, which keeps the commitment EVM-word-sized and future-proofs the scheme. | requires: [kzg-scheme] | contrasts: [blob-data-in-evm]
- point-evaluation-precompile — The precompile at address 0x0A takes 192 bytes (versioned hash, z, y, commitment, proof) and verifies both that the commitment matches the versioned hash and that the committed polynomial evaluates to y at z. | requires: [versioned-hash, kzg-opening-proof] | contrasts: [blob-data-in-evm]
- proof-of-equivalence — A ZK rollup commits to its data twice — once as a blob KZG commitment, once inside its own proof system — and uses the point-evaluation precompile at a random challenge point to prove both commit to the same data. | requires: [point-evaluation-precompile] | contrasts: [calldata-posting]
- erasure-coding-extension — Each blob is Reed–Solomon extended (2x) so that any 50% of the extended data suffices to reconstruct the original; this is what makes sampling meaningful. | requires: [data-as-polynomial] | contrasts: [replication]
- cell-and-column — PeerDAS splits each extended blob into cells (~2 KiB each) and defines a *column* as the same cell index taken across all blobs in the block; a node samples columns, not blobs. | requires: [erasure-coding-extension] | contrasts: [full-download]
- cell-kzg-proof — A cell proof attests that a given cell is the correct opening of the blob's KZG commitment at that cell's positions, so a sampler can verify a fragment without the blob. | requires: [kzg-opening-proof, cell-and-column] | contrasts: [merkle-proof]
- das-security-argument — Data availability sampling gives probabilistic assurance: an adversary withholding more than the reconstruction threshold will fail a random sample with overwhelming probability after enough queries. | requires: [erasure-coding-extension, cell-and-column] | contrasts: [committee-attestation]

### Primary sources
- [EIP-4844: Shard Blob Transactions](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-4844) — tier: spec — published: 2022-02 (final 2024-03) — Canonical definition of blobs, versioned hashes, the point-evaluation precompile, and its exact 192-byte input layout.
- [EIP-7594: PeerDAS — Peer Data Availability Sampling](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-7594) — tier: spec — published: 2024-01 (Fusaka, activated 2025-12) — Defines cells, columns, cell KZG proofs, the 2x extension, and the per-transaction blob cap.

- [ethereum/kzg-ceremony-specs](https://github.com/ethereum/kzg-ceremony-specs) — tier: spec — published: 2022–2023 — The MPC protocol, sequencer role, and contribution/verification format for the Summoning ceremony.
- [ethereum/kzg-ceremony FAQ](https://github.com/ethereum/kzg-ceremony/blob/main/FAQ.md) — tier: canonical-docs — published: 2023 — Plain-language 1-of-N trust argument and what "toxic waste" actually is here.
- [EIP-7918: Blob base fee bounded by execution cost](https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-7918) — tier: spec — published: 2025 — Relevant because blob pricing, not blob cryptography, is what rollup teams actually feel.
- [Consensys: Dencun Upgrade Part 5, EIP-4844](https://consensys.io/blog/ethereum-evolved-dencun-upgrade-part-5-eip-4844) — tier: secondary — published: 2024-02 — Readable walkthrough; use for framing, not for numbers.
- [a16z crypto: On-Chain Trusted Setup Ceremony](https://a16zcrypto.com/posts/article/on-chain-trusted-setup-ceremony/) — tier: primary-analysis — published: 2024 — Argues for running ceremonies with on-chain verifiability; good source for "why ceremony design is hard".

### Current state (Aug 2026)
- **Blob parameters (stable since Dencun):** 1 blob = 4096 field elements x 32 bytes = 131,072 bytes (128 KiB). Commitment and opening proof are each 48 bytes (compressed BLS12-381 G1 points). Versioned hash is 32 bytes, version byte `0x01`. Point-evaluation precompile at address `0x0A`, input exactly 192 bytes, fixed gas cost (50,000 in EIP-4844 — **verify against the final spec text before publishing**).
- **PeerDAS is live** since Fusaka (3 Dec 2025). Blob data is 2x Reed–Solomon extended and split into 128 columns; a node reconstructs from ≥50% of columns. Cell KZG proofs let a sampler verify a fragment against the blob commitment. EIP-7594 also caps a single transaction at 6 blobs.
- **The consumer-facing change is in the transaction wrapper, not the EVM.** Post-PeerDAS, blob transactions carry *cell* proofs rather than a single blob proof, which broke library assumptions (see ethers.js issue #5062). Any teaching material that shows `blobs + commitments + proofs` as a 1:1:1 triple in the network wrapper is now outdated.
- **Blob throughput is governed by BPO (blob-parameter-only) forks** scheduled after Fusaka, which step the target/max blob counts up without a full hard fork. **The current target/max blob numbers as of Aug 2026 are UNVERIFIED here — do not publish a specific pair.**
- **The EVM still cannot read blob contents.** Only `BLOBHASH` (the versioned hash) and the point-evaluation precompile are available. This is unchanged and is the single most load-bearing fact for app engineers.
- **The 2023 ceremony output is still the live SRS.** No re-ceremony has occurred. The long-run roadmap direction (hash-only assumptions, post-quantum) implies KZG is eventually replaced rather than re-ceremonied, but nothing has moved.

### Misconceptions
- Belief: "A smart contract can read blob data." | Reality: The EVM sees only the 32-byte versioned hash via BLOBHASH; any statement about blob contents must be proved through the point-evaluation precompile. | Why: Blobs are described as "cheap data on Ethereum", which sounds like calldata. | Source: https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-4844
- Belief: "KZG proofs prove the data is available." | Reality: KZG proves *consistency* — that a claimed piece matches the commitment. Availability comes from the sampling/networking layer (PeerDAS), not from KZG itself. | Why: The two are always introduced together. | Source: https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-7594
- Belief: "The trusted setup means Ethereum trusts a small group." | Reality: ~141,000+ people contributed; a single honest contributor who deleted their secret suffices. | Why: "Trusted setup" wording, plus memory of small 2016-era ceremonies. | Source: https://github.com/ethereum/kzg-ceremony/blob/main/FAQ.md
- Belief: "Blob proofs are one proof per blob." | Reality: Since PeerDAS, blob transactions carry per-cell KZG proofs so that fragments can be verified independently. | Why: The EIP-4844 mental model persisted for two years before Fusaka changed the wrapper. | Source: https://github.com/ethereum/EIPs/blob/master/EIPS/eip-7594.md
- Belief: "Erasure coding is about redundancy/backup." | Reality: Here it exists to make *withholding* detectable: to hide any part of the data you must hide more than half of it, which random sampling catches quickly. | Why: RS coding is taught in a storage context. | Source: https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-7594
- Belief: "KZG commitments are hiding, so blobs are private." | Reality: The blob itself is broadcast publicly; the commitment scheme's hiding property is irrelevant to blob privacy. | Why: "Commitment" implies concealment. | Source: https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-4844

## 10.4 — Elliptic curves & pairings

### Concepts
- ec-group — An elliptic curve over a finite field gives a group: points can be added, and repeated addition ("scalar multiplication") is the only operation, so `[a]G` is easy to compute but recovering `a` from it is hard. | requires: [] | contrasts: [rsa-factoring]
- discrete-log-hardness — Security rests on the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem: given `G` and `[a]G`, find `a`. Everything else is built on this one-way step. | requires: [ec-group] | contrasts: [hash-preimage]
- scalar-field-vs-base-field — A curve has two different fields: the base field `Fp` where coordinates live, and the scalar field `Fr` (the group order) where witnesses and circuit values live. Confusing them is the #1 source of ZK integration bugs. | requires: [ec-group] | contrasts: [single-modulus]
- pairing — A pairing `e(P, Q)` maps a point in G1 and a point in G2 into a third group GT, and is bilinear: `e([a]P, [b]Q) = e(P, Q)^(ab)`. It lets a verifier check one multiplication between hidden scalars. | requires: [ec-group] | contrasts: [hash-only-crypto]
- pairing-buys-one-multiplication — Without pairings you can check linear relations on hidden values; a pairing adds exactly one degree of multiplication, which is enough to verify quadratic constraint systems and KZG quotient identities. | requires: [pairing] | contrasts: [fri-commitment]
- pairing-friendly-curve — Most curves have astronomically large embedding degree, making pairings uncomputable; pairing-friendly curves are specially constructed families (BN, BLS) with small embedding degree. secp256k1 is *not* pairing-friendly. | requires: [pairing] | contrasts: [secp256k1]
- g1-g2-asymmetry — G1 elements are small and cheap (48 bytes compressed on BLS12-381), G2 elements are twice the size and much costlier to operate on; protocol designs deliberately put the frequently transmitted object in G1. | requires: [pairing] | contrasts: [symmetric-pairing]
- bn254 — BN254 (alt_bn128) is the curve behind Ethereum's original pairing precompiles; originally believed to give 128-bit security, it is now assessed at roughly 100 bits after improvements to the exTNFS attack. | requires: [pairing-friendly-curve] | contrasts: [bls12-381]
- bls12-381 — BLS12-381 uses a 381-bit base field, was designed with exTNFS in mind, and targets slightly under 128-bit security; it is the curve used by Ethereum consensus signatures and by EIP-4844 blob commitments. | requires: [pairing-friendly-curve] | contrasts: [bn254]
- curve-security-vs-speed — BN254 is faster to prove over (smaller field) but weaker; BLS12-381 is stronger but slower and more expensive on-chain. The choice is a security/cost trade, not a matter of taste. | requires: [bn254, bls12-381] | contrasts: [strictly-better]
- bn254-precompiles — Addresses 0x06 (ECADD), 0x07 (ECMUL) and 0x08 (ECPAIRING) implement BN254 operations; EIP-1108 cut their costs roughly an order of magnitude, which is what made on-chain Groth16 verification affordable. | requires: [bn254] | contrasts: [bls-precompiles]
- bls-precompiles — EIP-2537 (Pectra, May 2025) added seven BLS12-381 precompiles at 0x0b–0x11: G1ADD, G1MSM, G2ADD, G2MSM, PAIRING_CHECK, MAP_FP_TO_G1, MAP_FP2_TO_G2. | requires: [bls12-381] | contrasts: [bn254-precompiles]
- msm-multi-scalar-multiplication — MSM computes `Σ [a_i]P_i` in one call with a volume discount; it is both the dominant cost inside provers and the reason the precompiles expose it rather than plain multiplication. | requires: [ec-group] | contrasts: [single-scalar-mul]
- hash-to-curve — Mapping arbitrary bytes to a curve point must be done with a specified map (MAP_FP_TO_G1 / MAP_FP2_TO_G2), not by hashing-and-retrying, or you break the security proofs of the signature scheme built on top. | requires: [ec-group] | contrasts: [naive-hash]
- bls-signature-aggregation — BLS signatures over BLS12-381 can be aggregated: many signatures over the same message compress to one, verified with a constant number of pairings. This is why Ethereum consensus uses this curve. | requires: [pairing, bls12-381] | contrasts: [ecdsa]
- pairing-and-post-quantum — Pairing-based cryptography is broken by a sufficiently large quantum computer; hash-based systems (STARKs/FRI) are not, which is the structural reason Ethereum's long-run roadmap moves away from pairings. | requires: [pairing] | contrasts: [transparent-setup]

### Current state (Aug 2026)
- **Precompile map, current:** 0x06/0x07/0x08 = BN254 ECADD / ECMUL / ECPAIRING; 0x0a = KZG point evaluation (EIP-4844); 0x0b–0x11 = BLS12-381 (EIP-2537, Pectra); 0x100 = secp256r1 (EIP-7951, Fusaka, 6900 gas).
- **EIP-2537 gas, verified from the EIP:** G1ADD 375; G2ADD 600; G1MSM `(k * 12000 * discount(k)) / 1000`; G2MSM same shape with base 22500; PAIRING_CHECK `32600*k + 37700`; MAP_FP_TO_G1 5500; MAP_FP2_TO_G2 23800.
- **EIP-2537 encodings, verified:** Fp = 64 bytes (padded), Fp2 = 128 bytes, G1 point = 128 bytes, G2 point = 256 bytes, scalar = 32 bytes. Note these are *uncompressed and zero-padded* EVM encodings — different from the 48-byte compressed G1 form used for KZG commitments. This padding difference trips up nearly everyone integrating.
- **EIP-1108 gas (Istanbul-era, still current):** ECADD 150, ECMUL 6000, pairing 45000 + 34000·k. **The 45000/34000 pair is from memory and was NOT re-verified this session — check EIP-1108 before publishing.**
- **BN254 is not 128-bit.** Post-exTNFS assessments put it near 100 bits. This matters now because EF's zkEVM roadmap demands 128-bit provable security, which pushes L1-facing systems toward BLS12-381 or hash-based alternatives. Curricula that still say "BN254 = 128-bit security" are wrong.
- **The practical split as of 2026:** BN254 remains the default for cheap on-chain Groth16/PLONK verification because it has had precompiles since 2017; BLS12-381 is used for consensus signatures, blob commitments, and anything wanting real 128-bit security. EIP-2537 makes BLS12-381 verifiers viable on L1 for the first time, but adoption is still early.
- **Directionally, pairings are on the way out at L1.** The post-quantum roadmap (hash-based signatures + STARKs) treats pairing-based cryptography as a transitional dependency. Nothing has been removed; treat as a multi-year direction.

### Misconceptions
- Belief: "BN254 gives 128-bit security." | Reality: Roughly 100 bits after exTNFS improvements. | Why: The original BN paper's parameter choice targeted 128 bits and the label stuck for a decade. | Source: https://www.ietf.org/archive/id/draft-irtf-cfrg-pairing-friendly-curves-08.html
- Belief: "Ethereum uses one elliptic curve." | Reality: At least four are in play — secp256k1 (accounts), BN254 (SNARK precompiles), BLS12-381 (consensus + blobs), secp256r1 (EIP-7951, passkeys). | Why: "The Ethereum curve" is a common shorthand. | Source: https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-2537
- Belief: "You can do pairings on secp256k1." | Reality: secp256k1 has a huge embedding degree; pairings on it are not computable. Pairing-friendliness is a special construction. | Why: All curves look alike from an API surface. | Source: https://eth2book.info/latest/part2/building_blocks/bls12-381/
- Belief: "G1 and G2 are interchangeable." | Reality: G2 elements are ~2x the size and far more expensive; MSM in G2 costs nearly 2x the base gas of G1 in EIP-2537. Protocols place objects deliberately. | Why: Both are "curve points". | Source: https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-2537
- Belief: "A BLS12-381 G1 point is 48 bytes on-chain." | Reality: 48 bytes is the compressed serialization used by KZG/consensus; the EIP-2537 precompiles take 128-byte padded uncompressed G1 points. | Why: The same curve appears with two different encodings in two different Ethereum subsystems. | Source: https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-2537
- Belief: "Pairings make things zero-knowledge." | Reality: Pairings provide a verifiable multiplication; zero-knowledge comes from blinding the witness, and plenty of pairing-based proofs are not zero-knowledge at all. | Why: SNARK, pairing, and ZK are introduced as one package. | Source: https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-197
- Belief: "Hashing to a curve point is just hash-then-interpret-as-x." | Reality: Naive maps leak structure and break security proofs; standardised maps exist and are exposed as precompiles for a reason. | Why: The naive approach appears to work in tests. | Source: https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-2537

### Practice ideas
- kind: implement — In Foundry, write a contract that calls 0x0b (G1ADD) and 0x0f (PAIRING_CHECK) with vectors pulled from `execution-spec-tests`, and assert the expected outputs. — Acceptance: both calls succeed on a Pectra-or-later fork and you report measured gas against the EIP formula.
- kind: measure — Verify the same BLS signature two ways: via EIP-2537 precompiles, and via a pure-Solidity library. — Acceptance: a gas ratio, plus a statement of how many pairings each path performs.
- kind: break — Take a working EIP-2537 call and remove the zero-padding from an Fp element (send 48 bytes instead of 64). — Acceptance: precompile reverts; you can explain the encoding rule that was violated.
- kind: measure — Benchmark scalar multiplication and MSM (k = 1, 8, 64, 512) in G1 using `arkworks` or `blst` locally, and plot cost per scalar. — Acceptance: the curve flattens, and you can relate it to why EIP-2537 prices MSM with a discount function.
- kind: read — Diff EIP-196/197 encoding rules against EIP-2537 encoding rules and write a one-page integration checklist. — Acceptance: checklist names field size, padding, point-at-infinity handling, and subgroup-check expectations for each.
- kind: fix — Take a Groth16 verifier hard-coded for BN254 and enumerate exactly what must change to target BLS12-381. — Acceptance: a concrete list (field modulus, point sizes, precompile addresses, gas budget) without needing to run it.

## 10.5 — Noir circuits

### Concepts
- circuit-vs-zkvm-tradeoff — A hand-written circuit encodes one specific relation and can be 10–1000x cheaper to prove than the same logic inside a zkVM, at the cost of expert effort and a much narrower blast radius for bugs. | requires: [zkvm-vs-circuit] | contrasts: [zkvm-vs-circuit]
- when-circuit-wins — Circuits win when the statement is small, fixed, and proved constantly (Merkle membership, signature checks, range proofs, one app-specific predicate); zkVMs win when the program is large, changing, or already written in Rust. | requires: [circuit-vs-zkvm-tradeoff] | contrasts: [when-zkvm-wins]
- noir-language — Noir is a Rust-like DSL for writing ZK circuits, developed by Aztec, designed to hide field arithmetic and proving-system details behind ordinary-looking code. | requires: [arithmetization] | contrasts: [circom]
- acir — Noir compiles to ACIR (Abstract Circuit Intermediate Representation), a backend-agnostic constraint IR; this is the layer that makes Noir theoretically portable across proving backends. | requires: [noir-language] | contrasts: [r1cs]
- barretenberg-backend — Barretenberg (`bb`) is Aztec's C++ proving backend and the de-facto default for Noir; UltraHonk is the general-purpose scheme, MegaHonk targets Aztec's private contracts. | requires: [acir] | contrasts: [alternative-acir-backends]
- everything-is-a-constraint — In a circuit there is no control flow in the runtime sense: `if` becomes arithmetic on both branches, loops must have compile-time bounds, and every operation you write becomes constraints that the prover must satisfy. | requires: [noir-language, arithmetization] | contrasts: [imperative-execution]
- field-arithmetic-default — Noir's default numeric type is a field element modulo the backend curve's scalar field order; it wraps at the field modulus, not at 2^256, so ordinary integer intuitions about overflow are wrong. | requires: [scalar-field-vs-base-field] | contrasts: [uint256]
- private-vs-public-inputs — Circuit inputs are declared private (witness only) or public (visible to the verifier and part of what the verifier contract checks); getting this boundary wrong is a privacy bug, not a compile error. | requires: [noir-language, zero-knowledge-property] | contrasts: [all-public]
- unconstrained-functions — `unconstrained fn` runs outside the constraint system, letting the prover compute a hint cheaply (e.g. a square root, a division, a sorted order); the circuit must then *verify* the hint. | requires: [everything-is-a-constraint] | contrasts: [constrained-computation]
- compute-vs-verify-asymmetry — The central circuit optimisation pattern: never compute inside the circuit what you can compute unconstrained and cheaply check. Division, inversion, sorting, and search all follow this shape. | requires: [unconstrained-functions] | contrasts: [naive-porting]
- unconstrained-is-untrusted — Anything returned by an unconstrained function is attacker-controlled; if the circuit forgets to constrain the hint, the prover can prove false statements. This is the single most common Noir vulnerability class. | requires: [unconstrained-functions] | contrasts: [unconstrained-functions]
- constraint-counting — `nargo info` reports ACIR opcode counts and backend gate counts; gate count is the number that predicts proving time, memory, and (for recursion) whether the circuit fits. | requires: [acir, barretenberg-backend] | contrasts: [wall-clock-only]
- acir-opcodes-vs-backend-gates — ACIR opcodes and final backend gates are different measurements and do not move together; optimising one without checking the other misleads. | requires: [constraint-counting] | contrasts: [constraint-counting]
- noir-verifier-generation — `bb` can emit a Solidity verifier contract for a compiled circuit, which is how a Noir proof reaches an EVM chain; the generated contract's public inputs must line up exactly with the circuit's declared public inputs. | requires: [private-vs-public-inputs] | contrasts: [offchain-verification]
- noirjs — NoirJS runs proving in the browser via WASM, which is what makes client-side private proofs practical for consumer apps — and also what makes proving time a UX constraint rather than a backend one. | requires: [noir-language] | contrasts: [server-side-proving]
- noir-maturity — Noir has not shipped a stable 1.0: as of Aug 2026 it is on the `1.0.0-beta.x` series with daily nightlies, so breaking changes between betas are normal and pinned versions are mandatory. | requires: [noir-language] | contrasts: [production-stable]

### Current state (Aug 2026)
- **Version:** Noir is still pre-1.0. - **Toolchain shape:** `noirup` installs `nargo` (compiler/CLI); `bbup` installs `bb` (Barretenberg backend). These version independently and mismatches are a common first-hour failure. Any lesson must pin both.
- **Proving schemes:** UltraHonk is the general-purpose Barretenberg scheme for standalone Noir circuits; MegaHonk is used for Aztec private contracts. The older "UltraPlonk / plonk backend" naming in 2023–24 tutorials is outdated.
- **Recursion is supported natively in Noir** (verifying a Noir proof inside a Noir circuit), which is the mechanism behind proof aggregation in Noir-based apps.
- **The ecosystem's centre of gravity moved to zkVMs for general computation** (SP1, RISC Zero) while Noir occupies the app-specific-circuit niche: identity/attestation predicates, private voting, membership proofs, and Aztec contracts. Framing Noir as "the ZK language" is now overclaiming.
- **Aztec's own network status matters for Noir's positioning** and is **not verified here** — do not assert Aztec mainnet status in a lesson without checking.
- **Commonly taught and now wrong:** that Circom is the default way to learn circuits. Noir (and to a lesser extent Halo2/Rust DSLs) has largely displaced Circom for new work, though Circom's installed base and audit corpus are still larger.

### Misconceptions
- Belief: "Noir is Rust, so Rust performance intuitions apply." | Reality: Every line becomes constraints; a loop that runs 1000 times costs 1000x the constraints regardless of data, and both branches of an `if` are paid for. | Why: The syntax is deliberately Rust-like. | Source: https://noir-lang.org/docs/noir/concepts/unconstrained
- Belief: "Unconstrained functions are just a performance optimisation." | Reality: They are a trust boundary. Their outputs are prover-chosen; a missing constraint on the returned hint is a soundness hole. | Why: Docs frame them as an optimisation because that is the motivation. | Source: https://aztec.network/blog/unconstrained-functions-in-noir
- Belief: "Field elements behave like uint256." | Reality: They wrap modulo the curve's scalar field order (~254 bits for BN254), so overflow, comparison, and bit operations all behave differently. | Why: Both are "big numbers". | Source: https://noir-lang.org/docs/
- Belief: "Fewer ACIR opcodes means a faster proof." | Reality: ACIR opcode count and backend gate count are different measures; proving cost tracks gates. | Why: `nargo info` shows both and the first column is read first. | Source: https://noir-lang.org/docs/reference/nargo_commands
- Belief: "If a value isn't marked public, it's private end-to-end." | Reality: Privacy also depends on what the *application* publishes — nullifiers, amounts, timing, and the verifier's public inputs all leak. The circuit boundary is not the privacy boundary. | Why: The `pub` keyword looks like the whole story. | Source: https://noir-lang.org/docs/
- Belief: "Noir 1.0 is out." | Reality: Still `1.0.0-beta.x` with daily nightlies as of Aug 2026. | Why: The `1.0.0-` prefix reads as 1.0 in changelogs and package managers. | Source: https://github.com/noir-lang/noir/releases
- Belief: "You should hand-write circuits for anything ZK." | Reality: For general computation, a zkVM is usually the correct default in 2026; circuits pay off for small, hot, fixed predicates. | Why: Circuit-first pedagogy predates viable zkVMs. | Source: https://blog.succinct.xyz/sp1-hypercube/

## 10.6 — On-chain verification

### Concepts
- verifier-contract — A verifier is a stateless pure function on-chain: it takes a proof plus public inputs and returns true/false. All the application logic lives in whatever contract calls it. | requires: [succinctness] | contrasts: [reexecution]
- public-inputs-binding — The verifier only attests to the statement encoded in the public inputs; if the calling contract fails to bind those inputs to the thing it cares about (a state root, a recipient, a nonce), a valid proof can be replayed or repurposed. | requires: [verifier-contract, private-vs-public-inputs] | contrasts: [proof-validity]
- proof-replay — Because proofs are just bytes, the same valid proof can be submitted twice unless the caller enforces a nullifier or state-progression check; "proof verified" never means "action authorised". | requires: [public-inputs-binding] | contrasts: [verifier-contract]
- groth16-verifier-cost — A Groth16/BN254 verifier costs roughly 200–250k gas, dominated by the pairing precompile at 0x08, plus a per-public-input term. | requires: [verifier-contract, bn254-precompiles] | contrasts: [plonk-verifier-cost]
- plonk-verifier-cost — PLONK/FFLONK verifiers land in a similar ~200k range but scale much better with public-input count, trading a slightly larger proof for a universal setup. | requires: [verifier-contract] | contrasts: [groth16-verifier-cost]
- pairings-dominate-gas — Nearly all verifier gas is pairing operations; every other step (field arithmetic, calldata, hashing) is noise by comparison, which is why every optimisation targets pairing count. | requires: [pairing, groth16-verifier-cost] | contrasts: [calldata-cost]
- batch-pairing-check — n independent pairing checks can be combined into one randomised check requiring n+2 pairings instead of ~3n, which is the mathematical basis for cheap batch verification. | requires: [pairings-dominate-gas] | contrasts: [sequential-verification]
- recursive-aggregation-onchain — Alternatively, N proofs are recursively folded off-chain into one proof, so L1 verifies exactly once regardless of N; cost per proof falls toward zero as N grows, at the price of aggregation latency. | requires: [recursion, verifier-contract] | contrasts: [batch-pairing-check]
- aggregation-layer — Third-party aggregation services (Aligned, NEBRA, zkVerify) sit between app provers and L1, batching heterogeneous proofs; they introduce a latency and, for some designs, an additional trust assumption. | requires: [recursive-aggregation-onchain] | contrasts: [direct-l1-verification]
- verify-vs-reexecute — The economic core: re-executing a computation on L1 costs gas proportional to the work, while verifying a proof costs a near-constant ~200k gas. Proofs win once the proved computation exceeds the verification floor. | requires: [prover-verifier-asymmetry, verifier-contract] | contrasts: [reexecution]
- verification-floor — Because verification has a fixed floor (~200k gas), proving is uneconomic for small computations; the break-even is not "always use ZK" but "use ZK above N gas of work". | requires: [verify-vs-reexecute] | contrasts: [always-prove]
- data-cost-vs-proof-cost — For a rollup, proof verification is often a minor line item compared with data availability; blobs changed rollup economics far more than proof-cost improvements did. | requires: [verify-vs-reexecute] | contrasts: [pairings-dominate-gas]
- proving-cost-not-gas-cost — On-chain verification gas is only one of three costs: prover hardware/time, DA for the data, and L1 verification. Optimising only the gas number misreads the P&L. | requires: [verify-vs-reexecute] | contrasts: [verification-floor]
- native-rollups — EIP-8079 proposes an EXECUTE precompile letting L1 verify an L2 state transition using Ethereum's own execution rules, removing the need for each rollup to maintain a bespoke proof system and verifier. | requires: [verify-vs-reexecute] | contrasts: [verifier-contract]
- verifier-upgradeability-risk — A verifier contract behind an upgradeable proxy with centralised keys nullifies the cryptographic guarantee, which is why L2BEAT places proof-carrying rollups at Stage 0 when upgrade keys remain centralised. | requires: [verifier-contract] | contrasts: [proof-validity]

### Current state (Aug 2026)
- **Single-proof verification cost is roughly stable and roughly commoditised.** Groth16 on BN254 lands around 200–250k gas (commonly modelled as ~207k + ~7.2k per public input); FFLONK is modelled around ~200k + ~0.9k per public input, i.e. much flatter in public-input count. **Both formulas come from a 2024 Orbiter Finance analysis, not from a spec — treat as indicative and re-measure.**
- **Batching is the standard answer to verification cost.** Batch pairing checks need n+2 pairings instead of ~3 per proof; vendor benchmarks put a 20-proof batch near ~40k gas per proof. That figure is vendor-published (Aligned) and should be re-derived.
- **Aggregation services are live but not universal.** NEBRA's aggregator reached Ethereum mainnet in 2024; Aligned offers both a restaked-AVS verification layer and a full-security aggregation service; zkVerify markets a dedicated verification chain. Choosing one is a security decision (does the batch inherit Ethereum security or an AVS's?), not just a pricing one.
- **The 2026 structural story is native rollups.** EIP-8079's EXECUTE precompile was demonstrated in a March 2026 proof-of-concept built on the Ethrex client with EF researchers. If it lands, individual rollup verifier contracts and bespoke proof systems become optional infrastructure. It is a prototype: no fork, no date.
- **What actually changed rollup economics was data, not proofs.** EIP-4844 blobs and then PeerDAS moved the dominant cost line; verifier gas has been a minor term for most rollups since 2024. Curricula that present "proof verification cost" as the rollup bottleneck are teaching the 2022 picture.
- **Baseline confirmed and load-bearing here:** no Stage 2 rollups exist as of mid-2026, and zkSync Era and Linea remain Stage 0 *despite* having validity proofs, because upgrade keys are centralised. Verifiers are necessary but nowhere near sufficient for trustlessness.
- **Fusaka raised the L1 gas limit to 60M with a ~16.78M single-transaction cap (EIP-7825).** A verifier call is far below that cap, but proof-heavy batched transactions should be checked against it.

### Misconceptions
- Belief: "If the verifier returns true, the action is authorised." | Reality: Verification says only that *some* witness exists for the given public inputs. Replay protection, nullifiers, and binding public inputs to the intended state are the caller's job. | Why: The verifier's boolean return reads like an authorisation check. | Source: https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-8079
- Belief: "ZK proofs are always cheaper than doing the work on-chain." | Reality: There is a ~200k gas verification floor. Below that, re-executing is cheaper, and you also pay prover cost that never appears in the gas number. | Why: "Constant-time verification" is taught without the constant. | Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.16581
- Belief: "Proof verification is the main cost of a ZK rollup." | Reality: Since blobs, data availability and prover infrastructure dominate; verification gas is often a small fraction. | Why: The 2021–22 pre-blob picture is what most explainers were written against. | Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.22709
- Belief: "A rollup with validity proofs is trustless." | Reality: L2BEAT keeps zkSync Era and Linea at Stage 0 despite validity proofs because upgrade keys can replace the verifier. | Why: The proof is the visible mechanism; the governance is not. | Source: https://l2beat.com/scaling/summary
- Belief: "Aggregation is free savings." | Reality: It buys gas with latency (waiting for a batch) and, for AVS-style layers, with a different security assumption than Ethereum's. | Why: Marketing leads with the gas multiple. | Source: https://blog.alignedlayer.com/proof-verification-layer-vs-aggregation-service/
- Belief: "Native rollups are shipping soon." | Reality: EIP-8079 has a March 2026 proof-of-concept on Ethrex and no fork assignment; Glamsterdam's headline EIPs are ePBS and block-level access lists. | Why: The demo got heavy coverage. | Source: https://www.theblock.co/news/ecosystems/2026-03-11-ethereum-researchers-demo-native-rollups-prototype-that-could-simplify-layer-2-verification-393160
- Belief: "More public inputs barely matters." | Reality: For Groth16 each public input adds a scalar multiplication (thousands of gas); for FFLONK the term is far smaller. Public-input count is a real design lever. | Why: Proof size is constant, so people assume cost is too. | Source: https://hackmd.io/@Orbiter-Research/S1nat__m0

## 10.7 — Privacy applications

### Concepts
- account-model-leaks-everything — Ethereum's account model publishes sender, recipient, amount, and timing for every transfer; privacy cannot be retrofitted at the app layer without changing the data model. | requires: [] | contrasts: [note-commitment-model]
- note-commitment-model — Private systems replace balances with a set of *notes* (UTXO-like), each published only as a commitment in an append-only Merkle tree; the tree is public, the note contents are not. | requires: [commitment-scheme, account-model-leaks-everything] | contrasts: [account-model-leaks-everything]
- nullifier — Spending a note publishes a nullifier — a deterministic, unlinkable one-way function of the note's secret — which the contract records to prevent double-spends without revealing which note was spent. | requires: [note-commitment-model] | contrasts: [spent-flag]
- nullifier-must-be-deterministic-and-hiding — A nullifier must be uniquely determined by the note (or double-spends become possible) *and* uncorrelatable with the commitment (or privacy breaks); both failure modes have occurred in real systems. | requires: [nullifier] | contrasts: [nullifier]
- shielded-pool — A shielded pool is the contract holding all deposited value, where the ZK proof asserts "I own an unspent note in this tree worth X" without revealing which; the pool is the privacy boundary. | requires: [note-commitment-model, nullifier] | contrasts: [zk-rollups-are-not-zk]
- anonymity-set — Privacy is relative to the set of deposits you could plausibly be: a pool with 5 users gives essentially no privacy regardless of the cryptography. Anonymity is a liquidity property, not a math property. | requires: [shielded-pool] | contrasts: [zero-knowledge-property]
- fixed-denominations — Early mixers used fixed deposit sizes precisely because unique amounts destroy the anonymity set; variable-amount pools must hide amounts too. | requires: [anonymity-set] | contrasts: [arbitrary-amounts]
- metadata-leakage — Even with perfect note privacy, timing, IP address, gas payment, RPC provider, and the funding source of the withdrawing address routinely deanonymise users. Most real deanonymisation is metadata, not cryptography. | requires: [anonymity-set] | contrasts: [zero-knowledge-property]
- relayer — Because a fresh withdrawal address has no ETH for gas, a relayer submits the transaction for a fee; this solves the gas leak but introduces a censorship point and a party that sees the withdrawal early. | requires: [metadata-leakage] | contrasts: [account-abstraction-paymaster]
- private-execution-vs-private-state — Hiding *what state you touched* is a different problem from hiding *what code ran*; systems differ in whether contract logic, function selector, or only balances are concealed. | requires: [shielded-pool] | contrasts: [note-commitment-model]
- viewing-key — A viewing key lets a chosen third party (auditor, tax authority, counterparty) decrypt a user's transaction history without granting spend authority — the basic primitive of selective disclosure. | requires: [shielded-pool] | contrasts: [full-transparency]
- selective-disclosure — Rather than revealing history, a user proves a *predicate* about it ("this came from a non-sanctioned source", "I paid the tax owed"), which is the compliance story ZK systems actually offer. | requires: [viewing-key, proof-system-relation] | contrasts: [viewing-key]
- association-set — Privacy Pools' mechanism: a user proves membership in a chosen subset of deposits (an association set) curated by an Association Set Provider, so honest users can voluntarily exclude themselves from tainted funds. | requires: [selective-disclosure, anonymity-set] | contrasts: [full-pool-membership]
- privacy-compliance-equilibrium — The claim behind association sets: if honest users can cheaply prove separation from illicit deposits, the equilibrium is that they do, leaving bad actors in a small and conspicuous set — privacy and compliance without a backdoor. | requires: [association-set] | contrasts: [backdoor]
- asp-centralisation-risk — An Association Set Provider is a trusted curator with de-facto censorship power over who counts as clean; it moves the trust problem rather than eliminating it. | requires: [association-set] | contrasts: [privacy-compliance-equilibrium]
- developer-liability-risk — Writing and publishing privacy software has produced criminal exposure in the US (the Tornado Cash prosecutions), which is a real engineering-decision input, not a hypothetical. | requires: [shielded-pool] | contrasts: [neutral-tooling]
